ABSTRACT
In the pursuit of developing high-temperature alloys with superior ability to fulfill the performance requirements 
of next-generation energy and aerospace demands, integrated computational materials engineering (ICME) has 
played a key role. A physics-assisted machine-learning (ML) approach, utilizing a bilinear-log model together 
with its extensions, is presented, in this paper. The bilinear-log model and the associated trilinear-log model are 
capable of predicting the temperature-dependent ultimate strengths of superalloys, medium-entropy alloys 
(MEAs), and high-entropy alloys (HEAs). In these models, the parameter break temperature, T
break
, is introduced. 
The break temperature serves as an upper boundary for operating conditions, ensuring acceptable mechanical 
performance. In contrast to conventional black-box ML approaches, the underlying fundamental physics are built 
directly into the bilinear- and the trilinear-log models. These models are here being further extended such as to 
analytically account for dependence on the strain rate and composition parameters, in addition to the analytical 
dependence on the temperature. The results presented extend the authors‘ earlier work on MEAs and HEAs and 
offer further support for the bilinear- and trilinear-log models and their applicability for modeling the 
temperature-dependent strength behavior of superalloys as well as of MEAs and HEAs.

In terms of the application of physics-assisted machine learning,

physics-induced data driven models to alloy design or manufacturing, 
the reviews by Guo et al. and by Kouraytem et al. stand out [ 5 , 6 ]. In 
Ref. [ 5 ], Guo et al. note that physics-informed machine learning (PIML) 
has emerged as a hybrid machine-learning paradigm that imbues ML 
models with physical domain knowledge, such as thermomechanical 
laws and constraints. A distinguishing aspect of PIML is the synergistic 
integration of data-driven methods that reflect system dynamics in 
real-time with the governing physics underlying additive manufacturing 
(AM). In Ref. [ 6 ], Kouraytem et al. present a collection of physics- and 
data-driven methods and examples of their application for understand -
ing the linkages in the process-structure-property (p- s -p) relationships 
(in any of the links) in widely used metal AM techniques. Even though 
physics-driven methods advance the understanding of the p- s -p re -
lationships, they may face challenges of high computing cost as well as 
need for the calibration of input parameters. Data-driven methods are 
capable of predicting complex phenomena without the need for tradi -
tional calibration, but also present drawbacks in the form of interpret -
ability being difficult and validation complicated.
In terms of important contributions (novelty), this paper presents a 
bilinear-log model for accurately predicting the ultimate strengths of 
superalloys across temperature. This bilinear-log model consists of 
separate exponentials, for a low-temperature and a high-temperature 
regime, with a break temperature, T
break
, in between [ 7 – 9 ]. The model 
accounts for the underlying physics, in particular, diffusion processes 
that are required to initiate phase transformations in the 
high-temperature regime [ 10 ]. We have, moreover, shown in Ref. [ 7 ] 
how a piecewise-linear regression can be employed to extend the model 
beyond two exponentials and yield accurate fit, in the case of a 
non-convex objective function caused by hump(s) in the data. Previous 
models for the temperature dependence of ultimate strengths (US) only 
accounted for a single exponential [ 11 , 12 ], This means there was no 
break temperature, T
break
. We view the break temperature especially 
important for the optimization of the high-temperature properties of 
alloys. For reliable operations, the temperature of turbine blades, disks 
or other components made from refractory alloys may need to stay 
below T
break 
(not accounting for coatings). The alloy may lose its 
strength rapidly, due to rapid diffusion, once the alloy temperature ex -
ceeds T
break
, leading to the easy dislocation motion and dissolution of 
strengthening phases [ 10 ]. We regard T
break 
a fundamental parameter 
for the design of alloys with attractive high-temperature properties, one 
warranting the inclusion in alloy specifications. It is important, there -
fore, to be able to accurately estimate T
break
, e.g., using the global 
optimization approach for medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) and 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs) presented in Refs. [ 7 – 9 ].
In terms of impact, this paper addresses a physics-assisted, i.e., not a 
black box, approach to ML and data analytics. Such physics-assisted ML 
models may be preferred in materials science, first because they involve 
fewer parameters than the black-box ML models, and thus require fewer 
input data for the accurate parameter estimation. With experiments 
usually being time-consuming and expensive, at least in the case of 
materials science, high-quality input data tend to be a scarce resource. 
Furthermore, the physics-assisted approaches to ML help establish 
causal links between output observations and the behavior of the un -
derlying material system.

Superalloys can be strengthened through solid-solution strength -
ening (and its chemistry), but also through the addition of strengthening 
phases (usually precipitates) [ 9 , 22 ]. In the case of Co-based superalloys, 
there may be a tendency towards the transformation of the FCC matrix 
phase to stable lower-temperature phases. But in the case of Ni-based 
and Fe – Ni-based superalloys, the FCC matrices may exhibit favorable 
characteristics for precipitation of uniquely effective strengthening 
phases [ 9 , 22 ]. The predominant precipitate used for strengthening in 
Ni-based superalloys is the γ’ phase, which generally causes the ultimate 
strength of many nickel-based superalloys to be relatively insensitive to 
temperature [ 9 , 23 ]. This strengthening effect arises from dislocation 
locking on secondary {100} slip planes caused by cross-slipping of pri -
mary dislocations on {111} slip planes in the γ’ phases during plastic 
deformation. Both for the γ and the γ’ phases, ordinary dislocation slip 
occurs on the {111} < 110 > plane [ 9 ]. Nevertheless, there is a tendency 
for dislocations in the γ’ phase to cross-slip on to secondary {100} 
planes, where they can have a lower anti-phase domain boundary en -
ergy (APB). Similar to the APB energy, these secondary slip planes are 
thermally activated, which is crucial for maximizing the strengthening 
provided by the presence of γ’ phases [ 9 ]. With an increase in temper -
ature, strengthening contributions from both the γ ʹ phase and the APB 
energy simultaneously decrease. These observations correlate with 
findings in the results presented, such as the signature peak in the flow 
stress, seen for γ’ -strengthened superalloys [ 9 , 24 , 25 ]. Models for the 
APB energy exist, that incorporate compositions, grain parameters and 
precipitate properties, and that show that concentrations of Ti, Ta, and 
Nb in the γ’ precipitate phase, in particular, are crucial for controlling 
the APB energy [ 26 , 27 ]. In addition to the predominant precipitate used 
for strengthening in Ni-based superalloys, the γ ′ phase, the definition of

3.1.1. Background
In [ 64 ], Varvenne et al. develop and validate a mechanistic theory, 
for the yield stress of general FCC random alloys at arbitrary composi -
tion, hence including FCC HEAs. The authors demonstrate predictive 
capability of the theory for the FCC Ni – Co – Fe – Cr – Mn alloy system, 
which has been well studied [ 11 , 64 , 66 , 67 ]. In an effort to understand 
and explain recent experiments, Varvenne et al. present a mechanistic, 
parameter-free, and predictive theory for the temperature-, composi -
tion-, and strain-rate-dependence of the plastic yield strength of FCC 
HEAs. According to Varvenne et al. [ 64 ], the theory rationalizes 
experimental results, quantitatively and qualitatively, and identifies 
underlying material properties that control strength, hence serving as a 
basis for design and optimization of new FCC HEAs.

HEA is considered a solute embedded in effective matrix of the sur -
rounding alloy [ 64 ].

microstructure is assumed.
4. Quasi-static loading is assumed.

3.2. Accounting for screw or edge dislocations in alloys with BCC 
microstructures
While the FCC-based γ phase (matrix) is fundamental to the micro -
structure of most superalloys, there also exist BCC-based superalloys, 
such as refractory metal BCC-superalloys (e.g., for W), BCC refractory 
high-entropy superalloys, Naka + Khan-type refractory HEAs, Beta-Ti 
superalloys, Cr BCC-superalloys, Ferritic superalloys as well as A2-B2 
eutectics/composites.
3.2.1. Background and primary assumptions
The analytical, dislocation-based models by Curtin et al. build on the 
Labusch model [ 71 , 72 ], in important ways, resulting in a well-defined, 
general models valid for random solutions with arbitrary numbers of 
components and compositions. The theory of strengthening for edge 
dislocations in BCC, presented in Ref. [ 12 ], is an extension of the anal -
ysis for diluate alloys and FCC HEAs presented in Refs. [ 60 , 64 ]. As in the 
case of [ 60 , 64 ], random distribution of solutes in the lattice leads to 
local fluctuations in the solute concentrations. A solute is assumed to 
distort the core of a dislocation, but not change it topologically, and 
solutes outside the core of the dislocation are assumed to interact with 
the dislocation through nominally elastic interactions [ 62 ]. The dislo -
cation is attracted to fluctuations in the overall solute configuration that 
lower the system energy and is repelled by fluctuations that increase the 
system energy. Unlike the BCC screw dislocations, a long BCC edge 
dislocation line is very flexible, and hence can form a wavy configura -
tion as it finds energetically-favorable regions of solutes. As indicated in

3.3. Accounting for short-range ordering
In the most recent analytical, dislocation-based models by Nag and 
Curtin [ 45 ], the previous theory that accounts for solute-dislocation and 
solute-solute interactions in random alloys is extended in a rigorous 
fashion to include strengthening due to short-range ordering (SRO). Nag 
and Curtin present closed-form expressions for the yield strength and 
energy barrier of dislocation motion, in alloys with SRO, based on inputs 
of atomic misfit volumes, average lattice and elastic constants, the SRO 
parameters, and effective pair interactions between solutes [ 45 ]. These 
most recent analytical, dislocation based models can both account for 
long-established athermal strengthening effect of SRO as well as for a 
notable effect of SRO on the misfit volume strengthening, which can 
increase or decrease the alloy strength [ 45 ]. For specifics on the models 
by Nag and Curtin, and for additional background on SRO, refer to 
Supplementary Note 26.
The previous analytical, dislocation-based models by Curtin et al. on 
the temperature-dependent alloy strength properties [ 12 , 20 , 58 – 64 ] 
only seemed to account for short-range ordering to a limited extent. In 
essence, these analytical, dislocation-based models seemed to account 
for interactions of solutes with dislocations through an average envi -
ronment (an average chemical neighborhood; an average matrix) [ 64 ], 
so the effects of short-rnage ordering seem to be averaged out, for most 
parts. Then, chemically-controlled (non-Hall-Petch) strengthening in 
MEAs or HEAs predominantly involves strengthening of a random solid 
solution with 100 % solute concentration in the average matrix [ 64 ]. 
This fundamental approximiation of solutes in an effective matrix 
seemed to be the foundation for the earlier model of solute strength -
ening at arbitrary solute concentrations [ 64 ]. Curtin et al. then rein -
troduced local variations in solute chemical environment and structure 
around the average matrix environment. Starting from the effective 
medium approximation, the theory by Curtin et al. first averaged out the 
effects of all the solutes and then reintroduced the effects of solute 
fluctuations in attracting and repelling a dislocation [ 64 ]. The theory 
did account for effects due to local variations in solute chemical envi -
ronment and structure around the average matrix environment. More 
specifically, the theory assumed that a long straight dislocation of 
length, L , will reduce its total potential energy by adopting a wavy 
configuration, where some segments of characteristic length ξ

regions of favorable solute fluctuations [ 64 ]. These dislocation segments 
lie at the minima in a potential energy landscape having typical minima 
and maxima spaced by some glide distance w

not consider atomic fluctuations at the scale of b < ξ

expected to be easily overcome at finite temperature [ 64 ].
Curtin et al. did provide a justification for their approach. To first 
order, each elemental alloy component could be seen as a “ solute ” 
embedded in the average effective medium “ matrix ” of the surrounding

been well-established in different fields, such as in electronic structure 
theory [ 64 ], with Virtual Crystal and Coherent Potential Approxima -

3.4. Accounting for multi-phase properties, precipitates, metastability, or 
heterogenous microstructures
The analytical, dislocation-based models by Curtin et al. on the 
temperature-dependent alloy strength properties [ 12 , 20 , 58 – 64 ] assume 
single-phase MEAs or HEAs. Multi-phase properties, precipitates meta -
stability or heterogenous microstructures are yet to be accounted for. 
The models do not account for precipitates, or even local clustering, in 
any way yet. The models are for statistically homogeneous microstruc -
tures. In essence, the analytical, dislocation-based models by Curtin 
et al. [ 12 , 20 , 58 – 64 ] model interactions of solutes with dislocations 
through an average enviornment (an average matrix), as noted above. 
The dislocations are assumed to interact with solvent atoms and the 
overall solute configuration, but within a single phase (not with pre -
cipitates comprising a separate phase).

4. Future research
The aforementioned models may be extended such as to account for 
precipitates in superalloys, MEAs or HEAs (multi-phase alloys), meta -
stability or even hierarchical microstructures. Most precipitate- 
strengthening models in the literature may be considered somewhat 
ad-hoc (may employ fitting parameters, may heavily emphasize fitting, 
but with limited prediction involved). For insights into the complexity of 
a realistic system, refer to relatively recent papers by Hu and Curtin on 
modeling of precipitate strengthening in Al – Mg – Si alloys [ 85 , 86 ]. 
Ref. [ 86 ] contains atomistic simulations and analysis of number of 
critical features, in the context of a “ pure ” matrix (no solutes). Reference 
[ 86 ] presents a path to address the daunting complexity of microstruc -
ture- and chemistry-dependent mechanisms affecting precipitation 
strengthening through a combination of modeling methods and theories, 
using the Al – Mg – Si alloys (the Al-6xxx series) as demonstration alloys. 
This demonstrated path for state-of-the-art modeling of alloy perfor -
mance can be extended to many other alloy systems, including specific 
MEA or HEA systems, to guide both understanding and design. This 
demonstrated path starts with first-principles calculations of properties 
and then continues along two branches: (i) development of 
chemically-accurate ML potentials for direct atomistic simulations of 
essential strengthening mechanisms and (ii) application of theory and 
calibrated mesoscale simulations to capture both qualitative and quan -
titative trends in the relationship between underlying material proper -
ties and macroscopic alloy-performance [ 86 ].

include much physical, atomic-level details, and often require extensive

applies to other alloy systems. So for specific MEA or HEA systems, 
existing precipitation-strengthening models can be used as a starting 
point.
More specifically, Ref. [ 86 ] motivates particular directions for future 
research likely relevant to specific MEA and HEA systems. 
1. Model extensions to MEAs and HEAs with moderate to significant 
lattice mismatches and thus moderate-to-significant Resolved Shear 
Stress (RSS) fields. Reference [ 86 ] notes that RSS fields in both the 
matrix and the precipitate can play an important role here.
2. Extensions addressing precipitate size distributions, simulation size 
scales, and finite-temperature shearing. Reference [ 86 ] notes that 
such extensions will likely reduce the predicted critical resolved 
shear stress (CRSS) toward the experimental values measured at 
room temperature.
3. Extensions involving the use of finite temperature shearing energies 
and better microstructure representations (both larger sizes and with 
a realistic precipitate size distribution). Such extensions seek to 
address overestimation of uniaxial tensile yield strength at room 
temperature [ 86 ].
4. Extensions of models involving Discrete Dislocation Dynamics, such 
as to include shearing directly, along with the execution of first- 
principles DFT to compute accurate precipitate properties (lattice 
mismatch with the matrix and shearing energies of relevant slip 
planes in the precipitate). Reference [ 86 ] notes that the generally 
good agreement among atomistic simulations, mesoscale simula -
tions, and theories indicates that expensive atomistic simulations 
may be replaced by higher-scale models and theories with limited 
loss of essential physical information.
5. Further extensions and improvements of the ML potentials. ML-based 
methods are extremely valuable for capturing complicated atomistic 
interactions with near-chemical accuracy, as demonstrated by the 
neural network potentials used in Ref. [ 86 ] for direct atomistic 
simulations of the essential strengthening mechanisms.

6.2. Methodology for modeling US at elevated temperatures
Physics-based foundation for the bilinear log model is provided by 
Senkov et al. [ 10 ]. Senkov et al. note that the solid solution strength -

effects and offers good agreement with experimental data only at rela -
tively low temperatures, at which diffusion-controlling deformation 
mechanisms can be ignored. Senkov et al., however, indicate that earlier 
analysis has revealed at least two strengthening mechanisms: (1) 
thermally-activated, diffusionless dislocation glide at low temperatures, 
and (2) diffusion-controlled dislocation climb/glide at high tempera -
tures [ 10 , 93 ]. According to Senkov et al. [ 10 ], the diffusion processes 
required to initiate phase transformations generally become noticeable 
at temperatures T > 0.4 T
m

m 
the phase trans -
formations are kinetically restricted. In other words, when T < 0.4 T
m
, 
the atoms are generally not able to move out of the lattice, and no phase 
transformations can take place. This tendency applies to low-entropy 
alloys, medium-entropy alloys, high-entropy alloys, and superalloys, 
and serves to explain a model comprising of the two exponentials [ 7 – 9 ]. 
A single-exponential model does not take into account diffusion effects, 
as suggested by Senkov et al. [ 10 ], and agrees well with the experi -
mental data only at relatively low temperatures, where 
diffusion-controlling deformation mechanisms can be ignored. But as 
the temperature increases, the chemical bonds between the elements 
become weaker. A diffusion-controlled regime is usually observed above 
~0.5 T
m

m

yield stress phenomenon in select superalloys, such as Inconel 903

mean squared error (MSE) tapers off. This practical approach assumes 
the model selected is the one yielding MSE corresponding to the on-set of 
the tapering.
Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6 of [ 7 ] capture an application of piece -
wise linear regression, a classical technique, presented to address chal -
lenges imposed by non-convexity of the objective function possible, in 
cases of tri-linear or quad-linear log models. Here, one can expand the 
parameter set such as to explicitly account for the break temperatures. In 
this case, the extra parameters can help in terms of maintaining 
dependence across temperature regimes during concurrent optimization 
across the temperature regimes. Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8 of [ 7 ] 
provide Matlab ® pseudo-code for the bilinear log model (a convex case 
with no piece-wise linear regression needed) and a tri-linear log model 
(a possibly non-convex case, one benefitting from piecewise linear 
regression).
As further explained in the Supplementary Note 8 of [ 7 ], at least ( n 
+ 1) data points are needed, for accurate estimation of a model 
comprising of n exponentials, where n = 1, 2, 3, … , assuming individual, 
piecewise linear fits (no concurrent optimization). Yet, in the case of 
concurrent optimization over the low-temperature and 
high-temperature regimes, utilizing an algorithm based on the Matlab ® 
pseudo-code in Supplementary Fig. 7 of [ 7 ] for the bilinear log model, 
four data points are needed for full definition of the problem (since the 
bilinear log model contains four parameters). Analogously, for concur -
rent optimization over the individual-temperature regimes, using an 
algorithm based on the Matlab ® pseudo-code in Supplementary Fig. 8
[ 7 ] for the trilinear log model, eight data points are needed for full 
definition of the problem (since the model now contains eight 
parameters).

motivated by the models of Maresca and Curtin [ 12 , 20 ]. Supplementary
