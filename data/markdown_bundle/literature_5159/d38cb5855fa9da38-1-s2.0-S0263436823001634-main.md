ABSTRACT 
Most refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) with Al possess excellent strength but low elongation. Low ductility 
is generally induced by the formation of brittle phases after long-term heat treatment. Here, we demonstrate a 
new strategy to optimize strength-ductility in RHEAs through cold rolling-annealing treatments. The

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new class of metallic materials that 
usually consist of at least five main elements, each with concentrations 
between 5 – 35 at. % [ 1 ]. HEAs based on a variety of refractory elements 
(Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo, and W), sometimes including non -
refractory elements such as Al, Si, Co, and Ni, are named refractory high- 
entropy alloys (RHEAs) [ 2 ]. RHEAs can obtain excellent performance, 
including high strength [ 3 , 4 ], high ductility [ 5 , 6 ] and corrosion resis -
tance [ 7 , 8 ], through reasonable alloy design, which gives RHEAs great 
application potential. 
Despite their complex compositions, RHEAs with equiatomic or 
nonequiatomic ratios usually have a single BCC structure, which gua -
rantees room-temperature and high-temperature strength. However, 
poor room-temperature ductility limits the engineering applications of 
RHEAs. For example, the room-temperature compression ductility of 
NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW RHEAs is less than 2% [ 9 ]. The ductility at 
room temperature of TiZrHfNbTa RHEA does not exceed 10% [ 10 , 11 ]. 
To obtain good ductility, Al was alloyed to RHEAs to introduce B2 
nanoprecipitates and regulate the synergistic effect of B2 nano -
precipitates and the body-centered cubic (BCC) matrix phase [ 12 , 13 ]. 
For example, Soni et al. [ 14 ] improved the room-temperature 
compressive ductility by forming an “ inverted ” BCC + B2 microstruc -
ture in Al-containing RHEAs to disperse B2 precipitates within a 
continuous BCC matrix. Although the introduction of B2 nano -
precipitates can optimize ductility, it is difficult to obtain a stable BCC/ 
B2 coherent microstructure in these alloy systems composed of Al and 
early transition metals [ 15 ]. After long-term high-temperature heat 
treatment, the B2 nanoprecipitate phase in Al-containing RHEAs will 
disappear and be replaced by other intermetallic compounds [ 15 ], 
which will destroy the ductility and toughness [ 16 ]. 
Several new approaches to optimize the strength-ductility trade-off 
in Al-containing RHEAs by thermomechanical processing treatments 
have been reported recently. For example, Wu et al. [ 17 ] improved the 
overall strength and ductility of AlHfNbTiZr RHEA by cold rolling and 
simple annealing. Cold rolling can achieve dislocation strengthening 
[ 18 , 19 ] and ultrafine grain-induced strengthening [ 20 – 22 ]. After cold 
rolling, a short annealing treatment can retain the high-density dislo -
cations generated by cold rolling. As the annealing time is short, the 
brittle LAVES phase has no time to precipitate. In addition, annealing 
can optimize the grain size to facilitate grain boundary sliding [ 23 ] and 
obtain a high strength-ductility combination [ 24 , 25 ]. However, the 
mechanisms of cold rolling-annealing treatments in Al-containing 
RHEAs have not yet been systematically investigated.

4.2. Effect of microstructure on ductility 
After annealing at 850 
◦
C, there are uniformly distributed high- 
density dislocation entanglements and dislocation accumulation bands 
in the matrix ( Figs. 3 c-d). Compared with the R850 alloy, the uniformly 
distributed high-density dislocation entanglement in the R1050 alloy 
disappears, and only dislocation accumulation bands exist ( Figs. 4 c-d). 
The dislocation density is obviously reduced. In general, the reduction in 
dislocation density is conducive to the improvement in tensile ductility. 
However, compared with the R850 alloy, the tensile ductility of the 
R1050 alloy decreases from ~20% to ~15%. This abnormal phenome -
non is caused by changes in dislocations and grain sizes. Asgari et al. 
[ 54 ] reported that a uniformly distributed high-density dislocation 
network facilitates the interaction between dislocation segments, which 
is beneficial to uniform strain hardening and ultimate ductility. In 
addition, as the annealing temperature increased, the grain size 
increased from d = ~4.03 μ m to d = ~22.53 μ m by more than 5 times 
( Figs. 3 a-b and Figs. 4 a-b). On the one hand, in a certain volume, the 
finer the grains, the greater the number of grains, and the more grains 
with favorable orientation during plastic deformation. In this case, the 
deformation can be more evenly distributed to each grain, and the dis -
tribution of internal stress will be more uniform, thereby obtaining a 
higher ductility [ 55 ]. On the other hand, the resistance of grain 
boundaries to plastic deformation increases with the increase in the 
fraction of grain boundaries caused by grain refinement. For example, 
Guo et al. [ 56 ] found that the reduced grain size could explain the 
improved strength and ductility of nonisoatomic HEA due to the for -
mation of a finer dislocation unit structure. It is difficult to obtain a 
stable BCC/B2 coherent microstructure in an alloy system composed of 
Al and transition metal elements. After long-term high-temperature heat 
treatment, the B2 nanoprecipitates in Al-containing RHEAs will gradu -
ally coarsen, and brittle LAVES phases will inevitably form [ 15 ]. Large 
precipitates result in a decrease in the tensile ductility of the alloy [ 51 ]. 
After cold rolling-annealing treatment, the sizes of B2 nanoprecipitates 
in the R850 and R1050 alloys remain almost unchanged. The B2 
nanoprecipitates are dispersed in the BCC matrix, and there is no brittle

In addition, this work further analyzes the relationship between the 
work hardening rate and true strain of the R850 and R1050 RHEAs, as 
shown in Fig. 9 . Both RHEAs exhibit multistage work hardening be -
haviors. In both alloys, there is a sharp decrease in the work hardening 
rate in the beginning, which corresponds to the elastoplastic transition 
(stage I). The work hardening rate of the R1050 alloy decreases slowly 
(stage II) and then accelerates (stage III). In sharp contrast, the work 
hardening rate of the R850 alloy increases in the second stage and then 
begins to decrease in the third stage. Obviously, the R850 alloy with a 
high-density dislocation network and fine grain size consistently main -
tains a higher work-hardening rate, which possesses a better deforma -
tion capacity before tensile fracture. 
The work hardening behavior of the two alloys was further analyzed 
by TEM characterization of the tensile fracture of the R850 and R1050 
alloys ( Fig. 10 ). No TWIP or TRIP effect was observed, but deformation 
microbands (MBs) and cross-slip of dislocations were present in both 
alloys, as shown in Fig. 10 . Fig. 10 (a) and Fig. 10 (d) show the TEM 
images of the local MBs of the R850 and R1050 alloys, respectively. 
Compared with the R1050 alloy, the microbands in the R850 alloy are 
wider. Microbands consisting of geometrically necessary dislocations 
lead to a high total dislocation density during deformation [ 57 ], and the 
R850 alloy maintains a superior work-hardening state without local 
necking. Fig. 10 (b) and Fig. 10 (e) show the presence of high-density 
dislocation walls (HDDWs), dislocation pinning and dislocation cross- 
slip in both alloys. Fig. 10 (c) and Fig. 10 (f) are partial enlargements 
with dislocation cross-slip being more frequent in the R850 alloy. On the 
one hand, cross-slip and dislocation pinning promote dislocation 
appreciation and Frank-Read dislocation sources, which leads to the 
alloy possessing higher work hardening ability ( Fig. 9 ). On the other 
hand, HDDWs can further promote work hardening and retard the 
generation of necking, which ultimately leads to higher ductility. Yang 
et al. [ 58 ] found that microbands are another important ductile mech -
anism controlling the deformation stability of alloys by the microband- 
induced plasticity (MBIP) effect. The microbands act as another 
important deformation mode to provide an additional work-hardening 
source to accommodate the imposed strain, and MBIP can enable the 
alloy to maintain an unusually high work-hardened state without local 
necking, resulting in a more stable plastic deformation. In addition, Lei 
et al. [ 59 ] found that double cross-slip can promote dislocation multi -
plication during deformation, which can maintain the work hardening 
ability and achieve continuous and stable plastic deformation. Thus, the 
cross-slip of dislocations and the presence of deformation microbands 
contribute continuous and stable plastic deformation in stage II for both 
alloys. However, the relatively narrow microbands of the R1050 alloy 
make the work hardening rate of the alloy begin to decrease after 6% 
plastic deformation in stage II. The unstable work-hardening rate leads 
to early necking.
