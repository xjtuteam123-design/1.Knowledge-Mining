High temperature magnetic strengthening
in iron-based alloys: Magnetic effects
on deformation and fracture, revisited
Dedicated to Professor Dr. Günter Gottstein on the occasion of his 65th birthday
The importance of magnetic effects upon mechanical prop-
erties of ferromagnetic materials, discussed by Zener al-
most a half century ago, has been revisited in order to stim-
ulate researchers’ interest in this research subject. It is
shown that effects of magnetism and magnetic transforma-
tion can affect creep deformation, tensile deformation and
ductility in a-Fe-based solid solution binary alloys in the
ferromagnetic state. The magnetic alloy strengthening ob-
served in the ferromagnetic state has been quantitatively de-
scribed and discussed by using a new parameter related to
magnetic properties of solute atoms such as Co, Cr and sol-
vent Fe. The prospect of future application of the magnetic
strengthening is given for high temperature materials with
excellent strength, ductility and thermal stability.
Keywords: Magnetic effects; Magnetic alloy strengthen-
ing; High temperature strength; Iron-based solid solution
alloys; Grain boundary engineering
1. Introduction
In 1955, Zener published an important paper entitled “Im-
pact of Magnetism upon Metallurgy” focusing his discus-
sions on the role of magnetism on phase boundaries and
mechanical properties in ferromagnetic metals and alloys
[1]. At the beginning of the 21
st century, a half century after
the paper, it will be useful and meaningful to renew our
interest in the effect of magnetism upon metallurgical
phenomena and properties of ferromagnetic materials, by
examining recent progress in some research areas related
to ferromagnetism, which is one of the important physical
or electromagnetic properties of advanced materials. In the
four decades since the 1960s, the effect of ferromagnetism
on phase boundaries has been taken into account in the con-
struction of the phase diagrams in iron-based alloys by
Kaufman et al. [2], Nishizawa et al. [3], particularly in con-
nection with the formation of a new phase due to spinodal
decomposition theoretically predicted by Cahn [4], and
then up to the present Lin and Chang [5], Ohnuma et al.
[6], Oikawa et al. [7] have seriously studied and made suc-
cessful achievements, as Zener expected.
On the other hand, as for the effect of magnetism on me-
chanical properties, what progress has been made in this re-
lated research area? In the author’s opinion, unfortunately,
until recently the subject of the effect of magnetism on
mechanical properties has not drawn much interest from
researchers and much remains to be done. This is the moti-
vation of this paper. The author wishes to convey to
researchers some important messages on the effect of
ferromagnetism on mechanical properties, by showing
characteristic features of magnetism-induced strengthen-
ing, simply “magnetic strengthening”, reported so far. This
paper has been written paying particular attention to the
magnetic effect on high temperature deformation and frac-
ture in iron and its solid solution alloys, to reveal the origin
of magnetic strengthening which is not replaceable by any
existing strengthening mechanisms proposed previously.
The importance and high potential of magnetic strengthen-
ing are discussed for future development of advanced high
temperature materials with excellent strength, thermal sta-
bility and fracture toughness.
2. Magnetic effect on high temperature mechanical
properties
2.1. Brief survey of historical background
When magnetic transformation occurs at a specific tem-
perature, called the Curie temperature, Tc, in metals or al-
loys, it is interesting to see whether any change of mechan-
ical properties in them can occur with increasing tem-
perature from the ferromagnetic region to paramagnetic
region below and above Tc, respectively. In the 1940s,
Köster made a systematic investigation of the temperature
dependence of the elastic modulus in pure metals and al-
loys. He found a discontinuity in the elastic modulus char-
acterized by a gradual increase with decreasing temperature
below T
c. in alpha iron, nickel and cobalt [8] and iron – car-
bon and iron – silicon alloys [9]. The elastic modulus tends
to increase more rapidly than that predicted by extrapola-
tion of temperature dependence of the modulus in the para-
magnetic or non-magnetic region. This means that metals
and alloys in the ferromagnetic state are more elastically ri-
gid and stronger than in the paramagnetic state. Sometime

plastic deformation and fracture in ferromagnetic metals

it is urgent to answer the question, at least, for iron, nickel

formation process. Landon et al. have reported that the acti-
vation energy for creep in pure nickel shows a peak around
600 K which almost corresponds to Tc [13].
In the 1960s and 1970s, systematic studies were exten-
sively performed by many groups on high temperature
creep deformation and fracture in order to collect funda-
mental data for development of high temperature materials,
as seen in excellent reviews on creep deformation by those
pioneers such as Conrad [14], Garofalo [15], Sherby and
Burke [16], Mukerjee et al. [17]. In the mid 1960s the effect
of ferromagnetism on creep deformation was found for al-
pha iron [18, 19], and then more extensively studied until
the early 1980s for pure iron [20 – 22] and iron-based alloys
[23 – 30] and nickel – cobalt alloy [31]. In the following sec-
tions we revisit some previous works in the literature, in-
cluding our early work on the magnetic effect on creep de-
formation [19, 24, 29, 30] and recent work on tensile
deformation and fracture [32] in alpha iron-based solid so-
lution alloys.
2.2. Magnetic effect on creep deformation in a-Fe, and
Fe – Mo and Fe – Co alloys.
In 1966, we reported a new finding that temperature depen-
dence of the steady state creep rate at constant stress can
change at the Curie point T
c (1043 K) so that the activation
energy for creep deformation becomes greater in the ferro-
magnetic region below Tc than in the paramagnetic region,
due to magnetic transformation in pure alpha iron with bcc
structure [19], as seen from the slope of curves shown in
Fig. 1. Ishida et al. also reported a magnetic effect that the
activation energy for creep determined by rapid tempera-
ture-change testing shows a high peak around Tc due to
magnetic transformation in alpha iron [18], as similarly ob-
served for creep in nickel [13]. The new finding on the ef-
fect of ferromagnetism on creep deformation in alpha iron
was almost explained by changes in diffusion rate and
elastic modulus due to magnetic transformation. This seems
feasible because we know that high temperature creep de-
formation is a diffusion-controlled process, even though
there is some difference in the activation energy for diffu-
sion, depending on whether volume diffusion or grain
boundary diffusion is dominant for the temperature range
of deformation. Normally, an activation energy for creep
which is almost equal to that for self-diffusion has been ob-
served for metals and alloys. In fact, the activation energy
for self-diffusion in alpha iron in the ferromagnetic state is
slightly greater than that in the paramagnetic state, because
the formation energy of vacancies becomes greater in the
former by excess energy for disordering of magnetic spins.
Now let us examine creep behavior of some iron-base
solid solution alloys, paying particular attention to the ef-
fect of ferromagnetism and magnetic transformation, taken
from our early work reported in the 1960s. We studied sev-
eral a-Fe-based binary alloys containing solutes such as
Mo, Co. Si, Mn, and Cr. In particular we observed strong
magnetic effect on creep deformation for the Fe – Mo and
Fe – Co alloy systems, as shown below. Figure 2a and b

2.3. Inverse square root concentration dependence of
magnetic strengthening
It is interesting to see how the solute concentration can af-
fect the magnetic effect resulting in a decrease in the creep
rate, in a-Fe – Co and Fe – Mo solid solution alloys in the
ferromagnetic state. The whole characteristic feature of the
magnetic effect on creep deformation observed in the
Fe – Co and Fe – Mo alloy system are shown in Fig. 3 and
Fig. 4, respectively. In Fig. 3a and Fig. 4a the steady-state
creep rates determined at given temperature and creep
stress are plotted as a function of the solute concentration
of Co or Mo. Now let us begin with the Fe – Co alloy sys-
tem. We recognize the tendency that the level of the creep
rate generally goes up with increasing temperature and
creep stress. Regarding the four curves from the top, the
creep rate begins to go down beyond a certain critical con-
centration, c* beyond which Fe – Co alloys are in the ferro-
magnetic state at test temperature. As is well known from
the phase diagram, the Curie point for magnetic transforma-
tion goes up with increasing cobalt concentration in the
Fe – Co alloy system. In other words, Fe – Co alloys remain
in the ferromagnetic state beyond the critical cobalt concen-
tration c*. Accordingly it is evident that creep deformation
becomes more difficult with increasing cobalt concentra-
tion only beyond c*, i. e., in the ferromagnetic state. More-
over it is surprising that the creep rate can be lowered by al-
most two orders of magnitude within the range of studied
solute concentration. On the other hand, in the paramag-
netic state, the creep rate likely remains unchanged with co-
balt concentration. For the Fe – Co solid solution alloy sys-
tem with bcc structure, we cannot explain the observed
high temperature alloy strengthening by any existing the-
ories of solid solution strengthening [16, 33], such as Cot-
trell locking effect, Suzuki chemical effect, and Fleisher’s
shear modulus effect, because the atomic size and shear
modulus are almost the same between iron and cobalt. Ac-
cordingly, there is no doubt that the origin of high tempera-
ture alloy strengthening of the Fe – Co alloy system can be
ascribed to the effect of ferromagnetism on creep deforma-
tion.

that alloy strengthening can occur even at paramagnetic
temperatures (1073 K, 1123 K), although the magnitude of
observed alloy strengthening in the paramagnetic state is
smaller than in the ferromagnetic state. This is more clari-
fied when the data are plotted as a function of the square

of solid solution alloy strengthening observed in the
Fe – Mo alloy system. Firstly we should remember that al-
loy strengthening can occur even at the paramagnetic tem-
peratures above the Curie point. This means that some
mechanism of alloy strengthening is operative even in para-
magnetic Fe – Mo alloys with bcc structure. In fact, there
are several possibilities that Fe – Mo alloy are strengthened
by well known mechanisms such as Cottrell locking and
Fleisher's shear modulus effect, because the differences in
atomic size and shear modulus are very large between iron
and molybdenum. The atomic size of Mo atom is 10.2 %
larger than iron and the shear modulus difference even at
room temperature is *2.27 % to iron [34], suggesting being
greater at high temperature. Accordingly ordinary alloy
strengthening mechanisms due to the Cottrell locking and
Fleisher’s shear modulus effect [35] are very likely opera-
tive for movement of screw dislocations, particularly in
bcc Fe – Mo alloy, in nonmagnetic or paramagnetic state.
Now we should answer the next question: what is the
origin of alloy strengthening observed in Fe – Mo alloys in
the ferromagnetic state? We observed much greater alloy
strengthening in the ferromagnetic state than the paramag-
netic state. Again, simply speaking, the origin of the addi-
tional alloy strengthening should be ascribed to the mag-
netic origin. If we assumed that any alloy strengthening
due to several possible mechanisms are simply additive
without any interaction between them, we would be able to
estimate the magnetic alloy strengthening by deducting the
observed alloy strengthening in the paramagnetic state from
that in the ferromagnetic state, expecting that the remaining
part may give the magnitude of magnetic strengthening.
From such analysis of the data, it was found that the mag-
netic strengthening in the Fe – Mo alloy system is greater
than in the Fe – Co alloy system [30]. Intuitively this result
is surprising because we know that the effect of cobalt addi-
tion is always to produce almost the strongest among solute
elements to iron, as predicted from the Slater – Pauling
curve for 3d transition metals and alloys which shows the
variation of net atomic magnetic moment as a function of
alloy composition [36, 37]. From the literature we know
that in the Fe – Co alloy system, the magnetic moment on
cobalt atoms does not appear to be affected by alloying but
that on the iron atom increases up to about 2.5 l
B,( lB: Bohr
magneton) with increasing cobalt content, and that cobalt is
the only solute element which increases of the magnetic
moment of the iron matrix (2.2 lB) [38]. Unfortunately, to
the author's knowledge, there is no available information
about the magnetic moment of Mo atoms in the Fe – Mo al-
loys system. There is a considerable lack of fundamental
data on electromagnetic properties of Mo and Fe – Mo al-
loys. For this reason, the magnetic alloy strengthening ob-
served in Fe – Mo alloys cannot be fully understood.
3. Magnetic effect on tensile deformation and
fracture in Fe – Co and Fe – Cr alloys
In the previous chapter we revisited early works performed
in the period of 1960s to the 1980s to refresh our memories
about the high temperature magnetic strengthening in pure
alpha iron and iron base alloys, particularly a-Fe – Co and
a-Fe – Mo alloys. It might be said that creep deformation
is more static in comparison with ordinary tensile deforma-
tion at constant strain rate. So it is interesting to see the ef-
fect of magnetic transformation on more dynamic tensile
deformation “in a sense” than creep deformation, in order
to obtain more general information on “high temperature
magnetic alloy strengthening”. For this purpose, an experi-
mental study of magnetic strengthening was conducted by
the author’s group by tensile testing for alpha iron-based
Fe – Cr and Fe – Co alloys at high temperatures [32]. The
reason for choice of the Fe – Cr and Fe – Co alloy systems
was that the Cr atom has a larger negative magnetic mo-
ment ( – 3.4l
B ) among the 3d transition elements dissolved
in iron, resulting in a decrease in the mean magnetic mo-
ment of Fe – Cr alloys, while the Co atom has a large posi-
tive magnetic moment (2.1 lB) resulting in a increase in the
mean magnetic moment of Fe – Co alloys, as seen from the
Slater – Pauling curve. Moreover it is known that the Fe –
Cr alloy system [39] shows higher diffusivity than in the
Fe – Co alloy system [40] in the ferromagnetic state, as dis-
cussed in excellent reviews on the effect of magnetic trans-
formation on diffusion in iron and its alloys [41, 42]. Ac-
cordingly it was expected that some useful information
would be obtained by comparison of the results from the
two alloy systems, in order to reveal the origin and possible
mechanism of magnetic alloy strengthening, in addition to
our previous works on creep deformation in Fe – Co and
Fe – Mo alloys [19, 24, 29, 30].

temperatures, by a combined way of the above-discussed
magnetic alloy strengthening and the grain boundary engi-
neering which the author’s group has been deeply involved
in and successfully developed during the past two decades.

4.2. Excellent strength and thermal stability of magnetic
alloy strengthening
Now let us examine general features of magnetic alloy
strengthening in ferromagnetic iron and its alloys. Firstly,
the magnetic alloy strengthening is a new type of alloy
strengthening originating from the effects of magnetism on
high temperature plastic deformation, at least, under the in-
fluence of ferromagnetic effects on diffusion and elasticity
in ferromagnetic a-Fe-based solid solution alloys with bcc
structure. The extent of magnetic alloy strengthening very
systematically increases with increasing solute content,
irrespective of alloy system, although the effectiveness of
alloying was found to depend on the species of alloying ele-
ment or the magnetic moment of the solute atom.

spin alignment and some variation in magnetic moment.
So far several researchers have discussed the interaction be-

on magnetization processes in ferromagnetic single crystals
[49, 50], but now we need to discuss the problem of the in-
teraction in the opposite direction from the view point of
the effect of magnetism upon mechanical properties, as
Zener initially directed and pointed out its importance [1].
5. Prospect of ductility improvement by grain
boundary engineering
Finally we briefly mention the possibility of improvement
of ductility of ferromagnetic materials strengthened by
magnetic alloy strengthening discussed above. Quite re-
cently the dilemma of high strength and low ductility in
polycrystalline materials has been solved to some extent
by the application of the concept of grain boundary engi-
neering [44 – 47]. It has been already demonstrated that se-
vere intergranular brittleness and low ductility of so called
“intrinsic brittle” materials such as refractory metal Mo
[46], intermetallic Ni
3Al [51, 52] and ceramics SiC [53],
can be drastically improved by the introduction of a high
fraction of low-energy special grain boundaries which are
very resistant to intergranular fracture [46, 53]. For this pur-
pose, several processing methods for grain boundary engi-
neering have been successfully applied [54] including grain
boundary engineering by magnetic field application [47,
55] which may be particularly applicable to ferromagnetic
materials discussed in this paper.
6. Conclusions
We have revisited the effect of magnetism and magnetic
transformation upon mechanical properties, discussed by
Zener almost half century ago, paying our particular atten-
tion to high temperature creep and tensile properties and
fracture in iron and its solid solution alloys, on the basis of
previous studies from the literature and recent work by the
author’s group. It has been confirmed that the extent of
magnetic alloy strengthening systematically increases with
increasing solute content for the Fe – Mo, Fe – Co and
Fe – Cr alloy systems in the ferromagnetic state. A new type
of analysis has been proposed which can relate the observed
magnetic alloy strengthening to the magnetic moment of
solute elements and solvent a-Fe, in order to quantitatively
evaluate the extent of magnetic alloy strengthening. The
prospect of strong and tough ferromagnetic iron-based al-
loys has been discussed on the basis of recent knowledge
of both magnetic alloy strengthening and grain boundary
engineering in which the present author’s group has been
deeply involved.
