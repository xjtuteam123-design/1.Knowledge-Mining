abstract
To develop a new type of light-weight, high strength, high-temperature structural material, cast bars of a
NiTi–Al-based alloy with low Nb and Hf contents were prepared by DS (directional solidiﬁcation). The
cast bars prepared by optimal DS were homogenizing treatment at 950 /C176C for different times. The tensile
strength of the homogenized test samples at elevated temperatures was tested. The results reveal that
the DS microstructure is a cellular–dendrite or acicular cellular structure that preferentially grows along
the [0 0 1] orientation. With an increase in the drawing velocity, the cellular crystals and cellular arm
spacing gradually decrease, but the directionality of the structure weakens. With an increase in the
homogenization time, the cellular structure becomes increasingly coarse, and the precipitated phases
are more dispersed and distributed more homogeneously. At 800 /C176C and 850 /C176C, the highest tensile
strengths occur with 12 h of homogenizing treatment, resulting in strengths of up to 340 MPa and
263.6 MPa, respectively; at 900 /C176C, the tensile strength is 171.5 MPa. This alloy is expected to become
a high-temperature structural material for applications at above 800 /C176C. The highest elongation at
800 /C176C is 42.4%, and at 850 /C176C and 900 /C176C, the highest elongation is 47.2% and 70.8%, respectively. The
alloy exhibits good plasticity at high temperatures.

1. Introduction
To meet the demand of aeronautic and astronautic devices for
new-style lightweight high-temperature structural materials, sci-
entists have focused on the lightweight and high-strength interme-
tallics. However, most intermetallics have not been widely used
because of their poor room-temperature ductility [1–4]. c-TiAl
based intermetallics have been attracted great attention by scien-
tists for decades for their low density and high-temperature high
strength. However, besides the poor room temperature ductility,
c-TiAl based alloys can only be used for lowpressure blades, and
generally, the service temperature is lower than 800 /C176C [5]. There-
fore, seeking a low density, good room temperature ductility and
high-temperature high strength of structural material to substitute
the large speciﬁc weight of nickel base superalloy has great
signiﬁcant.
NiTi-based intermetallics are excellent alloys and are favored by
the materials ﬁeld for their low density (6.2 g/cm 3), excellent
shape memory, superelasticity and high room temperature ductil-
ity [6–10]. Since Koizumi et al. [11] added different amounts of Al
to NiTi alloys as a substitute for Ti in nearly equal atomic ratios and
found that the compression strength improved dramatically at
room temperature and at high temperature, NiTi–Al-based alloy
is considered as a potential high temperature structural material.
In recent years, research has focused on adding alloying elements
to NiTi–Al alloys in an attempt to further improve their high-tem-
perature mechanical properties. Meng et al. [12,13] found that Nb
can improve the compression strength of a NiTi–Al alloy from
room temperature to 700 /C176C and that the compression yield
strength and speciﬁc strength of the Ti 50Ni40Al8Nb2 alloy are as
high as 1237 MPa and 216 MPa g /C01 cm3, respectively, at 600 /C176C,
exceeding the properties of the René 95 superalloy. In addition,
Zhao et al. [14] reported that Nb can improve the oxidation resis-
tance of NiTi–Al-based alloys at approximately 800 /C176C. Song et al.
[15] researched the strengthening effect of elemental Mo on
NiTi–Al-based alloys and found that the highest compression yield
strength at 800 /C176C is associated with the Ni 50Ti43Al6Mo1 alloy. At
present, NiTi–Al-based alloys have been deemed a potential high-
temperature aerospace structural material for applications on top
of the mesothermal interval (800–900 /C176C).
In addition, basic researches of using new preparation technic
(such as DS (directional solidiﬁcation)) to prepare NiTi–Al-based
alloy are reported. Pan et al. [16] studied the effect of casting
temperature on microstructure in a directionally solidiﬁed
Ni–44Ti–5Al–2Nb–1Mo alloy, which found that the [1 0 0] orienta-
tion is signiﬁcantly enhanced and the solid/liquid (S/L) interface

2. Experimental procedures
High purity Ni (99.95%), Ti (99.99%), Al (99.99%), Nb (99.98%)
and Hf (99.9%) were used as the raw materials to prepare master
alloy ingots with nominal compositions of Ni–43Ti–4Al–2Nb–2Hf
(at.%). The master alloy ingots were prepared by arc-melting on a
water-cooled copper crucible in an argon atmosphere and were
melted four times to ensure composition homogeneity. Before
the DS experiment, the bar stock for DS was cut by wire-electrode
cutting to 14 mm in diameter and 220 mm in total length from the
master alloy ingots. The surface oxide skin of the bar stock was
lathed off, and the bar stock was ultrasonically washed in acetone
solvent and then dried. Finally, the bar stock was packed into a
Y
2O3/Al2O3 double-layer ceramic tube ( £14.5 /C2240 mm) similar
to that reported by Zhang et al. [19], which acted as the melting
crucible, and then placed in a laboratory–scale Bridgman-type
directional solidiﬁcation furnace (LMC, liquid metal cooling tech-
nology). Before each experiment, the furnace chamber was vacuu-
mized to 3–5 /C210/C03 Pa and then backﬁlled with high purity argon
to a pressure of 0.05 MPa. The holding temperature and holding
time were 1550 /C176C and 20 min, respectively. After reaching the
holding time, the ceramic tube and the melt inside were drawn
into the Ga–In–Sn cooling liquid at different drawing velocities
(2 mm/min, 18 mm/min, 30 mm/min and 60 mm/min). In order
to eliminate the nonuniformity of the intercrystalline chemical
composition and structure, the DS bars were vacuum homogeniz-
ing annealing treated at 950 /C176C for 12 h, 50 h and 100 h, (furnace
cooling), and they were then processed into tensile specimens with
the dimensions £5 /C235 mm (parallel length) (shown in Fig. 1 ).
The microstructural samples were sampled from the centers of
the DS bar stock, which are the steady-state growth regions. The
samples were cut in longitudinal sections and transverse sections
and then ground and polished. The microstructure and composition
of the samples were analyzed with a JXA8100 electro-probe micro-
analyzer (EPMA) (JEOL, Japan), which was equipped with energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The phase types were ana-
lyzed with a D/max2200pcX-type X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with
Cu K a radiation (voltage: 40 kV; current: 40 mA; scanning speed:
6/C176/min; scanning range: 20–90 /C176). The Cambridge 3400 scanning
electron microscope (SEM) was used to observe the fracture surface
morphology. The IAS8 metallographical analysis software was used
to the phase volume statistical analysis. The high temperature
(800 /C176C, 850 /C176C and 900 /C176C) tensile tests were performed with a Shi-
madzu IS-10T electronic testing machine in atmospheric condi-
tions. The holding time before stretching was 20 min, and the
tensile rate was 0.5 mm/min. The elongations were measured with
an electronic digital caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. XRD analysis of the samples
The XRD diffraction spectra of the samples are shown in Fig. 2.I t
can be observed that the master alloy sample and the DS samples
are composed of the NiTi, b-Nb and Ti
2Ni phases. The phase types
do not change after DS. The phase types are the same as the Ni 45.5-
Ti45.5Al6Nb3 alloy that was investigated by Jian Xu et al. [20], the
Ni50Ti48Nb2 and Ni 49.5Ti46.5Nb4 alloys that were reported by Xiao
Fu et al. [21], and the Ni 50.1Ti46.9Nb3 alloy that was researched by
He et al. [22]. With the increase in the drawing velocity, the NiTi
phase is inclined to preferentially grow along the (1 0 0) and

of the b-Nb phase and the Ti 2Ni phase are very weak. In some sam-

the drawing velocity. We assume that these results are related to
the low volume content of the b-Nb phase and the Ti 2Ni phase in
the alloy. Based on the volume fraction statistics shown in Table
1, the volume fraction of the b-Nb phase is approximately 3–6%,
and the Ti 2Ni phase is less than 1%.

3.3. Microstructures of directional solidiﬁcation samples
The microstructures of the DS samples are shown in Fig. 4 (left:
longitudinal section, right: transverse section). We can see that the
microstructures are still composed of the dark grey and light grey
NiTi matrix phase, the white b-Nb phase and the black Ti 2Ni phase
after directional solidiﬁcation. However, the solidiﬁed structure
preferentially grows along the [0 0 1] orientation that parallels
the drawing direction. When the drawing velocity is 2 mm/min,
the solidiﬁed structure is cellular–dendrite crystalline with short,
thick secondary dendrite arms (shown in Fig. 4a and a’). The cellu-
lar crystal trunk and the second branch at the side are dark grey
NiTi matrix phases, and the interdendritic phases are the light grey
NiTi matrix phase. The white b-Nb phases and black Ti 2Ni phases
are banded, massive or granular and are randomly distributed
along the [0 0 1] orientation between the dendritic crystals. Most
black Ti 2Ni phases and white b-Nb phases display an intergrowth
state, but some Ti 2Ni phases distribute in isolation in the light grey
NiTi matrix phases. When the drawing velocity increases to
18 mm/min, 30 mm/min or 60 mm/min, the short, thick secondary
dendrite arms degenerate and disappear. The solidiﬁed structure
becomes vimineous and exhibits an acicular cellular structure.
The dark grey matrix phases and light grey phases distribute in
an alternating pattern. With an increase in the drawing velocity,
the sizes of the cellular crystals diminish gradually, but many dis-
continuous and irregular groove nets are produced (shown in
Fig. 4 c and d), which indicates that the directionality of the solid-
iﬁed structure weakens. Meanwhile, the sizes of the b-Nb phases
and Ti
2Ni phases decrease, and they transform from the original
coarse-banded or massive structures into a long and thin or gran-
ular structure. The volume of the light grey matrix phases and
white b-Nb phases increases gradually, whereas the black Ti 2Ni
phases and dark grey matrix phases decrease (statistical data
shown in Table 1). The transverse section shows that the structures
are massive dendritic crystals or equiaxial crystals, which appear
similar to the equiaxial structure of the as-cast sample. In addition,
with an increase in the drawing velocity, both the white b-Nb
phases and black Ti 2Ni phases become more dispersed and are dis-
tributed more homogeneously, which seems to improve the
mechanical properties.
Generally, the mechanical properties of an alloy depend on its
microstructure. With a constant composition, the grain size, mor-
phology and distribution of the precipitated phases have a signiﬁ-
cant effect on the properties. The strengthening effect is increased
when the hardness, dispersion, and thermostability of the disperse
phase particles are higher. From the microstructure of the DS sam-
ples, we can see that the cellular–dendrite crystals are coarse and
that the directional effectiveness is high when the drawing velocity
is 2 mm/min. This is beneﬁcial for the improvement of the creep
strength and creep-rupture strength at high temperatures. How-
ever, the b-Nb (Ti, Ni) and Ti 2Ni particles are coarse and sporadi-
cally distributed in the intercellular regions, and the dispersion is
very low, which makes it difﬁcult for these phases to act as disper-
sion-strengthening phases for the matrix phase at high tempera-
tures, so the high-temperature mechanical properties may be
poor. When the drawing velocity is 18 mm/min, 30 mm/min or
60 mm/min, the microstructure is much ﬁner than that of the
2 mm/min samples, which may be bad for the high-temperature
strength. However, the b-Nb (Ti, Ni) and Ti
2Ni phase particles are
very ﬁne and distribute in a more disperse and homogeneous man-
ner, which is more beneﬁcial to the high-temperature strength.

lid solubility of the solute atoms increases. The cellular crystalline
grain coarsens very little for 12 h of homogenizing treatment,
whereas coarsening is obvious for 50 h and 100 h of treatment.
The white b-Nb phase is partly dissolved and diffused into the ma-
trix phase, which becomes smaller in size, whereas the black Ti
2Ni
phase becomes coarser in size and increases in quantity. The two
phases disperse more and are distributed more homogeneously,
which seems to improve the alloy strength.
3.5. High-temperature tensile properties
The high-temperature tensile properties are shown in Table 3 .
At 800 /C176C, the highest tensile strength occurs after 12 h of homog-
enizing annealing treatment and is equal to 340 MPa; for the 50 h
and 100 h treatments, the strengths are 339.9 MPa and 336.9 MPa,
respectively. The three tensile strengths are nearly identical, which
indicates that the homogenizing time has no obvious effect on the
tensile strength at 800 /C176C. At 850 /C176C, the highest tensile strength is
found after the 12 h heat treatment and is equal to 263.3 MPa; for
the 50 h and 100 h treatments, the tensile strength decreases to
approximately 225 MPa. At approximately 165 MPa, the tensile
strengths at 900 /C176C are nearly identical. The tensile strength de-
creases noticeably with the increase in the stretching temperature.
Of the three treatment times, the 12 h homogenizing treatment
process produces the best mechanical properties at high tempera-
ture. This result can be attributed to a good match between the
cellular crystal size, the dispersion of second phase particles and
the directional effectiveness. However, the high-temperature
mechanical properties are low in comparison with those of the

precipitated phase but are mainly inﬂuenced by the low melting

strengthening phase at room temperature, however, we think it
becomes a harmful phase at temperatures greater than 800 /C176C.
According to the Ni–Ti binary alloy phase diagram reported by Ot-
suka adn Ren [24], we know that the Ti 2Ni phase is produced by
the peritectic reaction that is caused by the composition segrega-
tion in the nearly equal atomic ratio of the NiTi alloy during the
solidiﬁcation process. The melting point of the pure Ti 2Ni phase
is approximately 984 /C176C, which does not change signiﬁcantly when
Nb, Hf and Al atoms are dissolved in it. When stretching is per-
formed at temperatures over 800 /C176C, the temperature has exceeded
0.8 Tm (melting point) of the Ti 2Ni phase. At this time, the Ti 2Ni
phase is in the dynamic recovery and recrystallization stage and
it begins to soften, which cannot hinder the slipping of the disloca-
tions. Meanwhile, the Nb, Hf and Al atoms that dissolved in the
Ti2Ni phase can no longer provide shear resistance and tensile
resistance. As a result, fractures ﬁrst appear in the Ti 2Ni phase re-
gions under the action of stress. As observed the fracture surface
morphology from Fig. 7 a, the fracture surface is composed of lots
of equiaxial dimples, which is typical of the ductile fracture mode.
The dimples form from the ‘‘inner’’ necking of the plastic deforma-
tion of the cellular matrix phase. According to formation character-
istics of the dimples, here, we believe that the crack sources are
ﬁrst generated from the intercellular regions or grain boundaries
(shown in Fig. 7 b) for the existing of the softened Ti 2Ni phase,
and then it rapidly expand to the b-Nb phase and at last occur in
the NiTi matrix phase. Although the transverse grain boundaries

The elongation data are given in Table 3 ; the elongation of the
high-temperature tensile specimens (the fractured tensile test
specimens are shown in Fig. 9 ) is generally very high. At 800 /C176C,
the largest elongation is 48.4%; at 850 /C176C and 900 /C176C, the largest
elongation is 47.2% and 70.8%, respectively. Compared with those
of some high temperature alloys as given in Table 4 , we can see
that either at an almost the same strength or at an almost the same
testing temperature, the current elongations far surpass the elon-
gations list in Table 4. For example, the elongation of IN-738 alloy
is 10% with strength of 343 MPa at 980 /C176C, it is far below the cur-
rent result of 42.4% with strength of 340 MPa at 800 /C176C, and also
the elongation of IN-738 at 815 /C176C is far below the current result.
Although the current tensile strengths are below those of listed
in Table 4 at the same testing temperature, this alloy really exhibits
good pyroplasticity. We believe such good ductility is ﬁrst a result
of the bcc structure of the NiTi matrix phase at high temperatures,
which has many slip systems that provide the advantage of plastic
deformation. Second, the transverse grain boundaries have been
eliminated to a great degree after DS, which also improves the duc-
tility. Finally, the deposition hardening of the b-Nb phase and the
solution strengthening of the Al, Nb and Hf atoms in the matrix
phase are important factors in enhancing the high-temperature
ductility. The homogenization of the structural and chemical com-
positions after the homogenizing treatment is also a very impor-
tant factor.
4. Conclusions
The microstructure and high-temperature tensile properties of
directionally solidiﬁed Ni–43Ti–4Al–2Nb–2Hf alloy for different
homogenizing treatment times were investigated. The DS micro-
structure is a cellular–dendrite or acicular cellular structure that
preferentially grows along the [0 0 1] orientation. With an increase
in the drawing velocity, the cellular crystals and cellular arm spac-
ing gradually decrease, but the directionality of the structure
weakens. With an increase in the homogenization time, the cellu-
lar structure becomes increasingly coarse, and the precipitated
phases are more dispersed and distributed more homogeneously.
