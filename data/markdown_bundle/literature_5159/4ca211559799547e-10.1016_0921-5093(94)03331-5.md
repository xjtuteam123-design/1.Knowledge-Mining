Effects of thermal exposure on the properties of two y alloys 
D.S. Lee, M.A. Stucke, D.M. Dimiduk 
Materials Directorate, Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, OH 45433, USA 
Abstract 
Ti-Al alloys with various alloying additions show promise as high temperature materials because of their high temperature strength 
and modulus. Recent strides in processing and microstructural control produced alloys which exhibit an acceptable balance of 
properties for aerospace applications. However, little is known about the influence of the high temperature exposures, which these 
materials will experience in service, on the mechanical properties. Therefore, a study was undertaken to examine the effects of thermal 
exposure on the tensile properties of two wrought alloys: Ti-47AI-2Mn-2Nb and Ti-47Al-l.oCr-0.9V-2.4Nb. The effects of 
microstructure and exposure at temperatures spanning the anticipated use range were investigated. 
Keywords: Titanium; Aluminium; Alloys; Thermal exposure 
1. Introduction 
Titanium aluminides are currently being studied for 
a variety of military and commercial applications. The 
alloys of most interest combine some mixture of y 
and a2 phases in a duplex microstructure [l]. Many 
researchers have sought a balance of properties by 
varying alloy additions, grain refinement, and micro- 
structure. While advances in processing and alloy 
development have made these alloys contenders for 
applications up to 1500 OF, some fundamental aspects 
of their performance remain uncharacterized. 
One of the most basic problems with this class of 
alloys is their lack of room temperature ductility. While 
room temperature ductility is not a component design 
criterion for the applications in question, it typically 
provides some safety margin against possible damage 
encountered in service, or in a maintenance shop. 
Given that the low-temperature ductility of y materials 
is typically less than 3%, it is desirable that this 
property is not diminished in service or during process- 
ing. As a result, studies have begun which examine the 
effect of thermal exposure on the room temperature 
ductility of y alloys. 
The first such studies were conducted on wrought 
material of composition Ti-48Al-lV-O.2C [2]. This 
study did not show any adverse effects of exposure to 
air on tensile ductility or fracture behavior. However, 
this material was not in an optimum microstructural 
condition thereby yielding low initial ductilities. There- 
fore, the effects of exposure to air on ductility may have 
been masked. Studies by Dowling and Donlon [3,4] on 
the same wrought alloy in a duplex condition showed a 
dramatic effect of exposure on tensile ductility. Specifi- 
cally, when exposed at 775 or 815 “C, tensile elonga- 
tion was reduced by about 75%, from 1.5% to less than 
0.5%. A lower carbon material also experienced the 
loss in ductility, but to a lesser degree. Dowling and 
Donlon found a surface layer of T&Al, and surmised 
that this layer may be partially responsible for the loss 
in ductility after conducting tests on material machined 
from exposed blanks. Recently Kelly et al. [S], using 
cast Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb, showed the same loss in 
properties after exposure in air for as little as 16 h at 
650 “C. Kelly et al. attributed the loss in ductility to a 
brittle surface layer which cracked in the presence of 
atmospheric moisture. Finally, a study by Liu and Kim 
examined a Ti-47A1-2.5Nb-2Cr-O.SMn-O.5Si alloy 
at room temperature in the duplex condition in air, 
vacuum, and pure oxygen. Liu and Kim concluded that 
hydrogen embrittlement resulting from moisture in air 
caused low plastic strain to fracture in this alloy. 
Because the studies by Dowling and Donlon, Kelly 
et al., and Liu and Kim examined only the duplex struc- 
ture, exposure effects on the lamellar structure are 
unknown. In this study, thermal exposure effects on

2. Approach 
The materials used in this study have the nominal 
compositions of Ti-47A1-1.6Cr-O.9V-2.4Nb (the Cr 
alloy) and Ti-47Al-2Mn-2Nb (the Mn alloy). The Cr 
alloy was induction skull melted, and the Mn alloy was 
vacuum arc remelted. Both were cast into a cylindrical 
ingot and then two-step, isothermally forged into a 9 in 
diameter by 0.4 in thick pancake. Sections of each 
pancake were heat treated to two microstructural 
conditions (lamellar and duplex) and stabilized with a 
direct age treatment as shown in Table 1. Tensile speci- 
mens with a 16 mm gage length and 3.5 mm diameter 
were cut from the heat-treated wedges. Machined 
specimens were exposed for 100 h in air at 600, 800, 
and 1000 “C to promote formation of an oxide layer. 
Specimens were tested at room temperature under a 
strain rate of 1.2 x 10 -3 s- ’ in the as-exposed condi- 
tion. After the initial tests, additional specimens were 
prepared and tested as follows: ( 1) wrapped in Ta foil, 
and exposed at 800 “C for 1900 h in evacuated quartz 
tubing backfilled with Ar, with Ti chips added to the 
tube to getter oxygen; (2) mechanically polished using 
600 grit paper after exposure; or (3) tested at 400 “C 
after exposure at 800 “C for 100 h. All test results 
reported are for duplicate specimens at each condition. 
A summary of the testing done can be found in Table 2. 
After testing, specimens were sectioned and analyzed 
using optical microscopy, backscattered electron 
microscopy, energy-dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) 
analysis, and wavelength-dispersive spectroscopic 
(WDS) analysis techniques.

Fractographic analysis yielded typical fracture 
surfaces expected for such y alloys as shown with the 
Mn alloy in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b). The fracture surface for

a mixture of cleavage of the y grains and translamellar 
fracture of the lamellar colonies. 
Several tests were conducted in attempts to isolate 
the mechanism responsible for the strain-to-failure 
debit. Duplicate specimens were tested at room tem- 
perature after exposure at 800 “C for 100 h in a 
protected atmosphere. In this case, there appears to be 
no effect on the strain-to-failure debit (Fig. 8), as there 
is no difference between the protected samples and the 
samples exposed in air at the same temperature. Speci- 
mens were also tested after removing the oxide layer 
from the exposed surfaces with 600 grit paper. As 
shown in Fig. 9, removal of the oxide layer restored 
part, but not all, of the strain to failure lost upon 
thermal exposure. Finally, tests were conducted at a 
moderately elevated temperature, 400 “C, in an 
attempt to eliminate any atmospheric moisture which 
might be present during testing. Fig. 10 shows the com- 
parison of samples with and without exposure and 
tested at room temperature and 400 “C. The exposed 
samples were treated at 800 “C for 1900 h and tested 
at 400 “C. There is no apparent difference in strain to 
failure between the unexposed specimens tested at 
room temperature and tested at 400 “C. However, 
there was a marked increase in strain to failure for the 
exposed specimen when tested at 400 “C vs. the un- 
exposed specimen. Yield strengths, however, did not 
change despite the increase in strain to failure.

5. Conclusions 
(1) Exposure to air at elevated temperatures has a 
deleterious effect on room temperature strain to failure 
regardless of microstructure or alloy composition. 
(2) Loss in failure strain is a result of the combina- 
tion of an embrittling surface layer and a moisture 
effect. The exact mechanisms of each component have 
yet to be precisely explained. 
(3) Tensile testing at elevated temperature improves 
the strain-to-failure values as compared to unexposed 
materials. This may be the result of the elevated-tem- 
perature exposure serving as a ductilizing heat treat- 
ment.
