abstract
The intrinsic mechanical and interfacial characteristics of precipitates in Mg eSneY alloys
were calculated to predict strengthening precipitates and to design novel Mg alloys. The
ﬁrst-principle calculations results indicated that the Sn
3Y5 phase had a higher bulk and
shear moduli than the Mg 24Y5 and Mg2Sn phases. The Mg/Sn 3Y5 interface was more stable
and possessed a higher adhesive strength than the Mg/Mg 2Sn and Mg/Mg 24Y5 interfaces.
Four as-cast alloys (S10Y3 (Mg-0.5Sn-0.16Y), S5Y3 (Mg-0.43Sn-0.25Y), S1Y1 (Mg-0.36Sn-
0.37Y) and S3Y5 (Mg-0.27Sn-0.48Y) alloy (at.%)) were then designed according to the
different Sn to Y atomic ratios (10:3, 5:3, 1:1 and 3:5). The lower Sn to Y atomic ratio pro-
moted the formation of the Sn
3Y5 phase in the S3Y5 alloy, whereas the S10Y3 alloy with a
higher Sn to Y atomic ratio mainly consisted of the Mg 2Sn phase. Compared with the S10Y3
alloy, the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of the S3Y5 alloy were increased by
70% and 16% without sacriﬁcing the elongation at room temperature, which was greatly
improved by 127% and 104% at elevated temperatures simultaneously. These results sug-
gested that the calculations of intrinsic mechanical and interfacial characteristics of pre-
cipitates may provide an effective tool to design high-strength Mg alloys.

1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) is green and environmental-friendly natural
resource with abundant reserves [ 1,2]. Meanwhile, Mg alloys
are known to have high speciﬁc strength [ 3,4]. Due to these
advantages, Mg alloys play an important role in the aerospace
and transportation industries [ 5]. The increasing re-
quirements for lightweight parts on automobiles and growing
interest in environmental protection have stimulated wide-
scale interest in developing Mg alloys [ 6,7]. However, the
restricted elevated-temperature mechanical properties of
conventional Mg alloys, such as AZ91 [ 8] and AM60 [ 9] alloys,
severely limit their wider applications, although these alloys
perform great mechanical properties at room temperature
(RT). So, it is of great signiﬁcance to design and develop novel
Mg alloys with excellent mechanical properties both at RT and
elevated temperatures.
In recent years, Mg eSn-based alloys have attracted much
attention owing to their excellent mechanical properties
regardless of RT or elevated temperature [ 10,11]. For example,
The ultimate tensile strength of 140 MPa and percentage
elongation after fracture of 9.7% were achieved in the as-cast
Mg-8.23 wt.% Sn-2 wt.% Nd alloy at RT [ 12]. Liu et al. [ 13]M g -
10 wt.% Sn alloys with the existence of Mg
2Sn phase exhibit
greater creep resistance with a lower indentation creep rate of
0.02 mm/min at 150
/C14 C. In addition, it was reported that the
rare-earth element yttrium (Y) can further improve the me-
chanical properties of Mg alloys [14]. Zhao et al. [15] noted that
the addition of 3 at.% Y promoted the formation of Sn
3Y5
phases and improved the ultimate tensile strength to 305 MPa
in as-extruded MgeSn alloys at RT. Hu et al. [ 16] reported that
as-aged Mge6Zne1Mne4Sn alloys containing 0.5 wt.% Y pre-
sented a favourable yield strength and ultimate tensile
strength of 371 and 376 MPa, respectively at RT. Another study
discovered that the Mg-0.4Sn-0.7Y alloys consisted of Sn
3Y5
and MgSnY phases and displayed a better homogenous
microstructure and higher ductility of ~33% than AZ31 alloys
[17] at RT. About the study of elevated-temperature mechan-
ical properties, Luo et al. [ 18] revealed that the formation of
the Mg
2Sn and MgSnY phases reﬁned the grain size below
6 mm and increased the tensile strength from 131 to 256 MPa at
200 /C14 C in as-extruded Mg-5.0Sn-1.5Y-0.1Zr alloys. Thus, the
MgeSneY alloy has great potential in the application both at
RT and elevated temperatures. Based on all the above litera-
ture reports, a variety of precipitates can be formed in
MgeSneY alloys due to the different contents of Sn and Y. It is
well-known that precipitation strengthening is an important
reinforcement mechanism for the macroscopic mechanical
properties of Mg alloy. Also, different types of precipitates in
MgeSneY alloys show different strengthening effects. Ac-
cording to the Mg-rich region of the Mg eSneY phase diagram
[14], Sn
3Y5,M g 2Sn, Mg 24Y5 and MgSnY precipitates can be
formed in equilibrium with the a-Mg phase. However, the ef-
fect of intrinsic physical properties of mentioned above pre-
cipitates on the RT and elevated-temperature mechanical
properties of Mg eSneY alloys lack in-depth and systematic
investigation. The physical properties of these precipitates are
difﬁcult to be measured experimentally. On the other hand,
ﬁrst-principles calculations based on quantum mechanics
have been demonstrated as a valid simulation tool to estimate
the performance of crystalline materials [ 19e21]. The detailed
bulk structure and interfacial structure between precipitates
and the matrix on the atomic scale can be visually observed,
and the total energy for the corresponding crystal structure
can also be obtained by ﬁrst-principles calculations. Using
this, the elastic constants and interface adhesion strength can
be calculated, which can be used to comprehend the me-
chanical properties of the materials [ 22].
As illustrated in numerous studies, the elastic constant is
used to describe the response stiffness of a system to a given
strain [23]. By calculating the elastic constants of precipitates,
relevant parameters of mechanical properties in bulk mate-
rials can be obtained, including the bulk and shear moduli
[24,25]. More importantly, the effect of the interface between
precipitates and the matrix on the mechanical properties are
recognized as one of the most crucial factors [ 26]. It has been
reported that the tensile strength of SiCw-reinforced AZ91
composites improved by 355 MPa due to a great interfacial
bonding strength between the MgO phase and the matrix [ 27].
Also, Masakazu et al. [ 28] used the ﬁrst-principles calculations
to indicate the small elastic mismatch between the a-Mg and
LPSO phases in two-phase Mg eZneY alloy, which caused a
little effect on the stress partitioning from Mg matrix to LPSO
phases during the deformation.
Therefore, an intensive comprehension of intrinsic prop-
erties for precipitates from the atomic view will help to design
the alloys with high strength that is appropriate for the RT
even at elevated temperatures. In the present work, the
intrinsic characters of the precipitates in Mg eSneY alloys
were systematically evaluated by ﬁrst-principles calculations
and the corresponding evolution of mechanical properties is
discussed by experiments. The Mg eSneY phase diagram [ 29]
shows that in the Mg-rich region, precipitation rarely occurs in
a-Mg after solidiﬁcation except for the Sn
3Y5,M g 2Sn, Mg 24Y5
and MgSnY phases. However, it should be noted that the
crystal structure of the MgSnY ternary phase is not well un-
derstood [ 30,31] and the corresponding model has not been
calculated. Therefore, the elastic constants of the Sn
3Y5,
Mg2Sn and Mg24Y5 phases, as well as the interfacial properties
between the precipitates and the matrix phase, were calcu-
lated. Then, based on these calculations, the corresponding
MgeSneY alloys were designed and prepared, and, due to the
dissimilar atomic ratios of Sn to Y, different types of pre-
cipitates were obtained in the Mg eSneY alloys. Finally, the
mechanical properties of as-cast Mg eSneY alloys were
measured by RT and elevated-temperature (350
/C14 C) tensile
tests.
2. Theoretical and experimental procedures
2.1. Calculation details
The Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP) [ 32]
employing density functional theory (DFT) [ 33] was used to
explore the phase stability and interface properties in crys-
talline materials in the present study. Ultra-soft pseudopo-
tentials (USP) [ 34] were chosen to describe the electron eion
interactions and obtain the lowest value of cut-off energy. The

2.2. Materials and experimental procedures
According to the phase diagram in [14], four different Sn and Y
atomic ratios of 10:3, 5:3, 1:1 and 3:5 were designed to achieve
different types of precipitates in Mg eSneY alloys. For
simplicity, the experimental alloys were deﬁned as S10Y3 (Mg-
0.5 at.% Sn-0.16 at.% Y), S5Y3 (Mg-0.43 at.% Sn-0.25 at.% Y),
S1Y1(Mg-0.36 at.% Sn-0.37 at.% Y) and S3Y5 (Mg-0.27 at.% Sn-
0.48 at.% Y) according to their Sn to Y atomic ratios of 10:3, 5:3,
1:1 and 3:5. It should be noted that the total mass content of Sn
and Y remained at 3 wt.% to ensure a consistent alloying
element content.
The raw materials in this study were pure commercial Mg
(99.99 wt.%), pure Sn (99.9 wt.%), and Mg-30 wt.% Y master
alloy, which are supplied by the Yuhua company in Yueyang
city. First, the pure Mg ingot was heated to 740
/C14 C and kept in
melts for 10 min. The pure Sn and Mg-30 wt.% Y alloys were
immediately added. After being completely melted in a
furnace protected by CO
2 and SF6 gas mixture atmosphere, the
melt was poured into a metallic mould, which was preheated
to 200 /C14 C. Next, the ingots were homogenized at 500 /C14 C for 10 h
and air-cooled to eliminate casting defects. The actual com-
positions of the alloys were obtained by inductively coupled
plasma emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES), as seen in Table 1.
Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, INCA Energy, Oxford
Instruments) equipped on a backscatter scanning electron
microscope (BSEM, TESCAN VEGAII LMU) was used to identify
the distribution and precipitates types. To further quantify the
alloy phases, X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/MAX-1200) with
Cu(Ka) irradiation at 30 mA and 40 kV was chosen. The
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, the relevant
selected area diffraction patterns (SAED) and energy disperse
X-ray spectroscopy (EDXS) mapping, used the FEI Talos F200S
G2 operating at 200 kV. The TEM foils with a thickness of
around 55 nm were prepared by mechanical grinding and then
were ion-polished by using a Gatan Precision Ion Polishing
System (GATAN PIPS II 695). The hardness was measured
using the HVS-30 digital display Vickers hardness tester
(980 N). For tensile testing, dog-bone samples of 64 mm gauge
length and 5 mm diameter were machined, and uniaxial
tensile tests were carried out on a universal testing machine
(CMT5105) at a speed of 10
/C0 2/s at RT and 350 /C14 C.

3.2. Interface properties
3.2.1. Establishment of the interface model
To further analyse the interface properties between pre-
cipitates and the a-Mg matrix, the interfaces were modelled.
Before interfaces were modelled, appropriate surfaces were
chosen to guarantee the reliability of the subsequent results.
According to our previous work [ 53], the crystal orientation
relationships (ORs) between the Sn
3Y5 phase and a-Mg matrix
in Mg eSneY alloys were predicted by an edge-to-edge
matching (E2EM) model and validated by TEM. The ORs
showed that the [0001] Mg direction was parallel to that of
[1123]Sn3Y5, and the (10 10)Mg and (11 22)Sn3Y5 planes matched.
An OR for the Mg 2Sn phase and the Mg matrix (10 11)Mg/
(220)Mg2Sn and [2113]Mg//[001]Mg2Sn is similarly obtained by the
E2EM model and veriﬁed by Hao et al. [ 54]. Also, the OR be-
tween the Mg 24Y5 phase and a-Mg matrix is [11 20]Mg//[111]
Mg24Y5 and (0002) Mg/(330)Mg24Y5 through the E2EM model and
the corresponding crystal orientation relationship is proved
by Zhang et al. [ 55].

5. Conclusions
The mechanical stability, mechanical properties and interfa-
cial properties of precipitates were determined by ﬁrst-
principles calculations based on density functional theory.
Then, the RT and elevated-temperature mechanical proper-
ties of as-cast Mg eSneY alloys with different precipitates
were studied. The major conclusions are summarized as
follows:
(1) The elastic constant values illustrated that Sn
3Y5,
Mg2Sn and Mg 24Y5 all maintained their mechanical
stability. In particular, the bulk and shear moduli
showed that the Sn
3Y5 phase possessed stronger
deformation resistance than the Mg 2Sn and Mg 24Y5
phase.
(2) Based on the surface energy, a seven-layer (220) Mg2Sn
slab is placed on a four-layer (10 11)Mg slab in the Mg/
Mg2Sn interface model, For the Mg/Mg 24Y5 interface
model, a 9-layer (3 30)Mg24Y5 slab is placed on a 4-layer
(0002)Mg slab. For the Mg/Sn 3Y5 interface model, a
four-layer (10 10)Mg slab is located on a four-layer
(1122)Sn3Y5 slab. The interface adhesion strength of the
(1010)Mg/(1122)Sn3Y5 interface, which possessed lower
interfacial energy, was higher than the (0002) Mg/
(330)Mg24Y5 and (1011)Mg/(220)Mg2Sn interface and showed
greater stability and higher deformation resistance.
(3) When the atomic ratio of Sn to Y decreased, the yield
strength and ultimate tensile strength improved at RT
as well as elevated temperature (350
/C14 C). The elevated-
temperature yield strength and ultimate tensile
strength of the S3Y5 alloy drastically improved by 127%
and 104%, respectively, when compared with that of the
S10Y3 alloy. The enhanced strength of the S3Y5 alloy
might be attributed to the uniform distribution of the
Sn
3Y5 phase with higher moduli and better
particleematrix interface properties than those of the
Mg2Sn or Mg 24Y5 phase.
