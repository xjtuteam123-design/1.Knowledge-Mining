abstract
The high temperature tensile properties of solution treated, cold worked and peak aged Cu–1.5 wt.%Ti
and Cu–4.5 wt.%Ti alloys have been investigated in the temperature range from room temperature (RT) to
550
◦C. Yield strength (YS) and tensile strength (TS) of Cu–1.5Ti alloy were found to be independent of test
temperature up to 350 ◦C and decreased thereafter, up to 550 ◦C. In the case of Cu–4.5Ti alloy, behaviour
similar to Cu–1.5Ti alloy was observed. The decrease in strengths beyond 350◦C is attributed to coarsening
of /H9252′ precipitate particles at 450 ◦C and precipitation of equilibrium phase /H9252at 550 ◦C. TEM analysis of
the alloys conﬁrms the presence of metastable and coherent Cu 4Ti /H9252′ precipitate when tested at 450 ◦C
and equilibrium precipitate Cu 3Ti, /H9252at the test temperature of 550 ◦C. It is interesting to note that in the
cold worked and peak aged Cu–Ti alloys tested at elevated temperatures, ductility (% elongation) was
found to increase with the test temperature up to 550
◦C which is attributed to dynamic recrystallisation.
The large elongation (160%) observed in Cu–4.5Ti alloy at the test temperature of 550 ◦C is an indication
of superplasticity. We are reporting the discovery of superplasticity in Cu–4.5Ti alloy for the ﬁrst time.
Ductile mode of fracture was observed in these alloys tested at elevated temperatures, i.e. at 350 and
450
◦C. High temperature tensile properties of Cu–Ti alloys are found to be superior to those of Cu–Be
alloys (C1 7200 and C1 7510).

1. Introduction
Cu–Ti alloys have been developed with the aim of substituting
them for Cu–Be alloys. Though Cu–Be alloys are not toxic by them-
selves, they do emit invisible toxic fumes during hot working, heat
treatment, machining and pickling operations due to the toxicity of
Be, which causes health hazards such as serious chronic pulmonary
illness [1]. As a result, these operations demand special safety mea-
sures in order to protect the health of the people, which makes the
alloys highly expensive. Therefore, worldwide research has been
aimed at developing a substitute for the noxious and costly Cu–Be
alloys. Literature survey indicates that Cu–Ti alloys have very good
potential as substitute for Cu–Be alloys [2].
Earlier studies on Cu–Ti alloys reported mechanisms of age hard-
ening and effects of prior cold deformation on ageing [2–7] and
electrical and thermal conductivities [8,9]. The variation of tensile
properties with test temperature of a Cu–1.5Ti alloy has been inves-
tigated [10], which concludes that the tensile properties of the alloy
at elevated temperatures are better than those of Cu–0.65Be–2.7Co

3.2. Elongation
Variation of elongation with test temperature in cold worked
and peak aged Cu–Ti alloys is shown in Fig. 3 . In Cu–1.5Ti alloy,
elongation was constant at 1 1% up to 350
◦C and increased with
increasing temperature, to 18% at the test temperature of 450 ◦C
and 33% at 550◦C. In the case of Cu–4.5Ti alloy, the elongation, 2% at
room temperature, started increasing marginally up to 8% at 350◦C
and signiﬁcantly thereafter, to 40% at 450 ◦C and 160% at the test
temperature of 550 ◦C. The increase in elongation is attributed to
recovery and dynamic recrystallisation during testing at 450 ◦Ca s
well as 550 ◦C. Our observation is supported by the microstructure
illustrated in the succeeding section (Figs. 4d and 5c), which shows
very ﬁne grains (<1 /H9262m) and no traces of any deformation at all.
This is responsible for enormous increase in the elongation. In the
absence of cold work prior to aging, the elongation was reported
to decrease with increasing test temperature up to 550

Cu–1.5Ti [10] and Cu–4.5Ti alloys [1 1]. Since cold worked and peak
aged Cu–4.5Ti alloy exhibited very large elongation (160%) after
testing at 550
◦C, it may be an indication of superplasticity in this
material.
Superplasticity is the ability of a material to withstand very
large deformations in tension with out necking [15]. The require-
ments for a material to exhibit superplastic behaviour are ﬁne
grain size (less than 10 /H9262m) and the presence of a second phase
which inhibits grain growth at the elevated temperatures. Testing
at high temperatures and low strain rates accentuates superplas-
tic behaviour. In the present study, the grain size obtained in cold
worked and peak aged Cu–4.5Ti alloy during testing at 550
◦Ci s
less than 1.0 /H9262m, which is conﬁrmed in the following section. Fur-
ther, the alloy contains a second phase Cu 3T i ,a t5 5 0◦C and was
tested at a low strain rate of 10−3 s−1. Therefore, it is concluded that
cold worked (90%) and peak aged (at 400◦C) Cu–4.5Ti alloy exhibits
superplastic behaviour when tensile tested at 550◦C at a strain rate
of 10−3 s−1.
However, it was earlier reported that neither Cu–4.5Ti alloy
[16] nor Cu–1.5Ti alloy [1 7] displayed any sign of superplastic-
ity in peak aged condition without any prior cold work. This was
explained on the basis that considerable grain reﬁnement (grain
size < 1.0/H9262m) did not occur during testing at high temperatures
in either Cu–4.5Ti alloy [16] or Cu–1.5Ti alloy [1 7] even though
a precipitate ( /H9252
′,C u 4Ti) was present in the peak aged condition.
Further, superplastic behaviour has not been observed in the cold
worked and peak aged Cu–1.5Ti alloy of the present study where
the elongation during testing at 550
◦C was only 33%. Therefore,
the main reason for superplasticity in Cu–4.5Ti alloy is due to the
ﬁne scale precipitation in the form of modulations formed dur-
ing quenching itself. Formation of ﬁne scale precipitation cannot
be suppressed during quenching and has been reported to occur
in Cu–Ti alloys containing Ti > 4.0 wt.% [3–5,7,18]. Therefore, when

alloy.

4. Conclusions
(1) Yield and tensile strengths of cold worked and peak aged
Cu–1.5Ti alloy were found to be independent of test tem-
perature up to 350
◦C and decreased thereafter, up to 550 ◦C.
Coarsening of /H9252′precipitate particles at 450◦C and precipitation
of equilibrium phase /H9252at 550 ◦C decrease the strengths.
(2) In the case of Cu–4.5Ti alloy, variation of yield and tensile
strengths with test temperature is found to be similar to that of
Cu–1.5Ti alloy.
(3) The large elongation (160%) observed in Cu–4.5Ti alloy at the
test temperature of 550
◦C is an indication of superplasticity in
this alloy, which is discovered and being reported ﬁrst by us.
(4) TEM analysis of the cold worked and peak aged Cu–Ti alloys
conﬁrms the presence of metastable and coherent Cu4Ti /H9252′pre-
cipitate in Cu–1.5Ti alloy when tested at 450◦C and equilibrium
precipitate Cu3Ti, /H9252in Cu–4.5Ti alloy at the test temperature of
550◦C.
