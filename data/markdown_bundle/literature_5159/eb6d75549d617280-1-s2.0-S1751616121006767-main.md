ABSTRACT 
The mechanical properties and residual stress of dental Co – Cr – Mo (CCM) alloy depend on the manufacturing and 
post-processing methods, which affect the prognosis of dental prostheses. Two CCM alloys manufactured by 
casting and selective laser melting (SLM) were compared, and the effect of heat treatment temperature for CCM 
alloys manufactured by SLM method was evaluated. Specimens were fabricated by casting (Cast Co – Cr) and SLM 
(SLM Co – Cr). SLM Co – Cr specimens were heat treated at 750, 950, and 1150 
◦
C to compare their properties. 
Microstructures were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS), and electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD), and the residual stress was measured via x-ray diffraction 
(XRD). Mechanical properties were evaluated by a Vickers hardness test and a tensile test; fractography was 
performed after this. The SLM Co – Cr group exhibited a decrease in porosity, grain size, increase in solid solution 
limit, and high residual stress compared to Cast Co – Cr; the ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, and hardness 
were also higher. The microstructures, residual stresses, and mechanical properties differed significantly 
depending on the heat treatment, and the strength and hardness showed a tendency inverse to that of the 
elongation. Type I residual stresses mostly decreased after 750 
◦
C heat treatment, however type II and III residual 
stresses remained even after 1150 
◦
C heat treatment. SLM presented superior mechanical properties to casting. 
Considering the reduction of tensile residual stress and increased ductility, CCM alloys should be heat treated at a 
temperature of 950 
◦
C or higher. 
1. Introduction 
Because Co – Cr – Mo (CCM) alloys are relatively inexpensive and 
exhibit superior mechanical properties and biocompatibility, they are 
widely used as metal substructures for dental prostheses ( Al Jabbari, 
2014 ; Hedberg et al., 2014 ; Suleiman and Vult von Steyern, 2013 ). 
Prostheses made of CCM-based materials are generally manufactured 
using a casting method, which, however, can cause solidification 
shrinkage, internal defects, increased porosity, and the formation of 
coarse microstructures; this can lead to reduced mechanical properties 
and the premature failure of the prosthesis ( Bae et al., 2015 ; Zhou et al., 
2018 ). 
Recently, additive manufacturing has been employed to manufacture 
prostheses. Powder bed fusion is most often used when manufacturing 
metal structures due to its excellent surface quality and mechanical 
properties of the resultant specimen. Among the powder bed fusion 
methods, selective laser melting (SLM) is the most commonly used 
method for manufacturing metal structure of dental prosthesis ( Tulga, 
2018 ). The metal powder is locally heated by high energy laser beam, 
completely melted, and rapidly cooled before adding more powder. By 
repeating this process, the structure created with the CAD program can 
be fabricated ( Konieczny et al., 2020 ; Koutsoukis et al., 2015 ). A cooling 
rate as high as 1 × 10
6 
K/s can be achieved using SLM, creating a fine 
microstructure ( Wang et al., 2019 ). Several studies have reported on the 
improved microstructure and mechanical properties of CCM alloys 
manufactured by the SLM method ( Al Jabbari et al., 2014 ; Kim et al., 
2016 ; Koutsoukis et al., 2015 ). Unlike casting, it is possible to produce 
CCM alloy with a high density, complex geometric shape, and low 
porosity through SLM ( Li et al., 2015 ; Xiang et al., 2012 ; Zhou et al., 
2020 ). In addition, the grains of the alloy are fine; the solid solution limit 
of the alloying element is increased, thus reducing precipitation and 
segregation ( Zhou et al., 2018 ), and there are few secondary phases or 
intermetallic compounds ( Suleiman and Vult von Steyern, 2013 ; Tonelli 
et al., 2020 ). In the case of most CCM alloys, the transformation rate of a

the FCC phase is present after the SLM process due to the rapid melting

On the contrary, SLM causes the accumulation of a large amount of 
residual stress due to the layer-by-layer fabrication and rapid heating 
and cooling, which adversely affects mechanical properties ( Edwards 
and Ramulu, 2014 ; Kajima et al., 2018 ; Mengucci et al., 2016 ). The 
accumulation of such a large amount of residual stress may cause 
distortion of specimen ( Li et al., 2015 ). Therefore, heat treatment is 
required to improve their microstructures and mechanical properties, as 
well as to reduce residual stress ( Lu et al., 2015 ; Mengucci et al., 2016 ). 
The heat treatment conditions affect the size, shape, and distribution of 
precipitates ( Mengucci et al., 2016 ; Takaichi et al., 2020 ; Wei et al., 
2020 ), size and shape of grains ( Kittikundecha et al., 2019 ; Seki et al., 
2019 ; Takaichi et al., 2020 ), and phase fraction of the FCC and HCP ( Wei 
et al., 2020 ; Zhou et al., 2020 ), which also changes the mechanical 
properties of the specimen. Kajima et al. (2016) found that CCM alloy 
produced by SLM exhibited a higher fatigue strength than CCM alloy 
produced by the casting. Seki et al. (2019) and Kittikundecha et al. 
(2019) reported that the fatigue strength of CCM alloy manufactured by 
SLM was improved after heat treatment. However, it has been reported 
that a long heat treatment time promotes excessive grain growth 
( Kajima et al., 2018 ) and increases the size of the precipitate and 
thickness of the oxide layer, which adversely affects the mechanical 
properties ( Kittikundecha et al., 2019 ). As such, studies on the effect of 
heat treatment on the microstructure and mechanical properties of CCM 
alloy manufactured by SLM have been reported; however, little has been 
reported on the resultant change in residual stress. Therefore, it is 
necessary to study heat treatment conditions that can improve the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of CCM alloy produced by 
SLM while also reducing the residual stress. 
The purpose of this study was to compare and evaluate the micro -
structure and mechanical properties of CCM alloys produced by the 
casting and SLM. In addition, CCM alloy produced by SLM was further 
studied to determine the conditions for optimal residual stress removal 
and to evaluate the changes in microstructure and mechanical proper -
ties before and after heat treatment.

2.4. Mechanical properties 
The tensile strength of the specimens was measured using a universal 
testing machine (Model 5982, Instron Co., USA) with a 100 kN load cell 
on a dumbbell shaped specimen with a diameter of 3 mm and a gauge 
length of 15 mm (as specified in ISO specification 22674). The crosshead 
speed was set to 0.5 mm/min, and tensile force was applied at room 
temperature (20 ± 5 
◦
C). All specimens were fractured at the gauge 
length, and the ultimate tensile strength, 0.2% yield strength, and 
elongation at break were measured. After the tensile test, the fracture 
surface was analyzed using the representative fractured specimens of 
each group using FE-SEM (Quanta FEG 250, USA). The microhardness of 
a disk shaped specimen was measured using a Vickers indenter of a 
hardness tester (HM-200, Mitutoyo, Japan). The test conditions were set 
to 500 g of test force, 10 s each of load time, storage and maintenance 
time, no-load time, and 60 μ m of access speed. After 12 measurements 
for each specimen, the minimum and maximum values were excluded, 
and 10 values were used for analysis. 
2.5. Statistical analysis 
The results obtained from the tensile and microhardness tests were 
analyzed using the SPSS statistical program (IBM SPSS 25.0, IBM, USA), 
and the results from the Cast Co – Cr and SLM Co – Cr groups were 
compared. Shapiro-Wilk tests and Levene ’ s tests were performed to test 
the normality and equal variance of the measured values. Because the 
measured values did not satisfy both normality and equal variance, the 
Kruskal-Wallis test - which is a nonparametric statistical method - was 
used, and a post hoc analysis was performed using the Bonferroni 
correction ( α = 0.05). In addition, the results of the tensile and hardness 
tests of the SLM Co – Cr groups were compared according to the heat

Elemental analysis was performed on the microstructures of the Cast 
Co – Cr and As-SLM groups using EDS ( Fig. 4 ). The secondary phase 
dispersed in the Cast Co – Cr contained Mo and W in higher ratios than 
that of the substrate. However, the As-SLM group was identified as a 
single phase that was rich in Co and Cr, without precipitates. In the case 
of the SLM 750, SLM 950, and SLM 1150 groups, precipitates of similar 
composition were observed. 
3.1.2. XRD analysis 
The results of XRD analysis showed that all specimens contained 
both FCC and HCP phases, and the FCC ↔ HCP transformation was 
confirmed according to the heat treatment temperature ( Fig. 5 ). The 
ratio of FCC and HCP phases was calculated by the Rietveld method 
based on the XRD data ( Table 3 ). The FCC phase fraction was higher in 
the As-SLM group than in the Cast Co – Cr group. Heat treatment at 
750 
◦
C caused an increase in the ratio of the HCP phase; however, this 
decreased as the heat treatment temperature increased further; the ratio 
of the FCC phase varied correspondingly.

Using the EBSD scan data, the grain size distribution was analyzed,

observed in the grain boundary maps of all groups, with no distinct

although a slight decrease was observed in the SLM 750 and SLM 1150 
groups. 
3.2. Residual stress 
The peak residual stress of the Cast Co – Cr group could not be ob -
tained accurately owing to the large grain size. Inadequately inter -
spersed peak values may be caused by the inhomogeneity of the internal 
structure, and they were excluded from the results because they were 
not accurate enough to depict residual stress. Residual stress was 
measured in the SLM Co – Cr groups, calculated by the Sin2 Ψ method 
( Table 5 ). Heat treatment reduced the residual stress of the specimens. 
The residual stress of the SLM 750 group was approximately 93% less 
than that of the As-SLM group for Ψ = 0. Further, the compressive re -
sidual stress of this group in the Ψ = 90 direction rapidly increased. SLM 
950 group had little residual stress. However, the residual compressive 
stress values of the SLM 1150 group increased as the heat treatment 
temperature increased further. However, in case of the SLM Co – Cr 
groups, the trend of the measured residual stress did not match the KAM 
values determined by EBSD analysis.

3.4. Fracture surface analysis 
After tensile testing, fracture surface analysis was performed to 
analyze the toughness characteristics of the specimens ( Fig. 8 ). When 
the fracture surface was magnified by 70, an irregular fracture surface 
shape and a very large wedge type crack were observed within the Cast 
Co – Cr group. When observed at × 5000 magnification, a mixed pattern 
of cracks, cleavage patterns, and dimples was observed in the Cast Co – Cr 
and As-SLM groups. Large cleavage facets, cleavage steps, and cracks 
were observed in the SLM 750 group. A large number of dimples were 
observed in the SLM 950 and SLM 1150 groups. 
4. Discussion 
The ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, and hardness of the As- 
SLM group were found to be significantly higher than those of the Cast 
Co – Cr group, as indicated from tensile and hardness tests. No significant 
difference in elongation between these groups was observed; however, 
the As-SLM group did exhibit a greater elongation. The difference in 
mechanical properties due to these manufacturing methods was related 
to the porosity, grain boundary strengthening, and solid solution 
strengthening. A high porosity was observed in the BSE image of the Cast 
Co – Cr group, which was related to the casting shrinkage and dendrites 
formed during solidification, which are known limitations of the casting 
method ( Al Jabbari et al., 2014 ; Koutsoukis et al., 2015 ). A porous 
structure can be formed if the shrinkage of the specimen is not 
compensated for during the formation of the dendritic structure. The 
CCM alloy of the porous structure showed inadequate outcomes in the 
oral environment of repeated loading, temperature change, and humid 
environment compared to the mechanical properties of the raw material 
for dental prostheses ( Lewis, 1978 ). On the contrary, Porosity was not 
observed in the SLM Co-Cr alloys. Complete melting and rapid cooling 
process in the local area, a characteristic of the SLM method, minimized 
porosity and improved mechanical properties by producing an alloy 
with near perfect density ( Kim et al., 2016 ; Koutsoukis et al., 2015 ). 
Therefore, when manufacturing dental prostheses using the SLM 
method, unexpected failure due to porosity in the oral environment is 
prevented, and a more predictive treatment is achieved. From the BSE 
image of the Cast Co – Cr group, several particles were precipitated from 
the substrate and existed in the interdendritic space and grain bound -
aries. Through EDS analysis, the precipitates in the Cast Co – Cr group 
were confirmed to be intermetallic compounds with a Mo or W rich 
phase. However, distinct precipitates were not observed in As-SLM 
group. It is presumed that the solidification process by rapid cooling 
of the molten alloy powder creates precipitate that cannot be measured 
within the resolution of the analyzer because the solubility limit of the 
dissolved element increases and forms a supersaturated state or very fine 
precipitate. The supersaturated state with an increased solid solution 
limit can increase the solid solution reinforcement effect, which is 
thought to have contributed to the strength improvement of the As-SLM 
group. Additionally, the average grain sizes of the Cast Co – Cr and 
As-SLM groups were 158 and 36 μ m, respectively. Compared to casting, 
that of the SLM Co – Cr groups was small. As the grain size decreases and 
area of the grain boundaries increases, the effects of the grain bound -
aries increase, thus improving the mechanical properties ( Sing et al., 
2020 ; Song et al., 2014 ; Zhou et al., 2018 ). Further, grain boundaries act 
as obstacles for dislocation movement and crack propagation. The re -
sults of this study also showed that the hardness, strength, and elonga -
tion of the As-SLM group were higher than those of the Cast Co – Cr 
group. In addition, it can be expected that the survival rate of prostheses 
manufactured by SLM method can be increased due to superior me -
chanical properties.

Residual stress was measured using XRD; however, it was impossible 
to measure that of the Cast Co – Cr group as it had a large grain size in this 
study. Measuring residual stress in this way is suitable for polycrystalline 
materials with grain sizes of approximately 10 – 100 μ m ( Conroy and 
Tanner, 2016 ; Fitzpatrick et al., 2005 ). However, residual stress can be 
indirectly evaluated using the KAM value, which corresponds to lattice 
distortion ( Bang et al., 2021 ). In case of the Cast Co – Cr group, very little 
residual stress could be inferred through the low KAM value. The 
As-SLM group had a large KAM value, consistent with the residual stress 
results measured using XRD. However, estimating the residual stress 
distribution using the KAM value may be inaccurate because the KAM 
value can be high if there is an in grain orientation gradient or if the 
grain size is excessively small ( Yamanaka et al., 2019 ). Therefore, the 
residual stress must be evaluated using an additional method. In this

Lutterotti et al., 2004 ; Raju et al., 2018 ; Rossini et al., 2012 ; Withers,

treatment and residual stress measured using XRD tended to be incon -
sistent. It is thought that the high KAM value was due to type II micro 
residual stress at the grain level and type III residual stress at the atomic 
level in the grain. The residual stress measured using XRD is a type I 
residual stress associated with volume change ( Conroy and Tanner, 
2016 ; Simson et al., 2017 ). Because this type of stress decreased 
significantly after the high temperature heat treatment, it is estimated 
that the type II and III microscopic residual stresses, which are implied 
by the high KAM values, have little effect on the volume change. In this 
study, it was possible to predict the microscopic residual stress, which 
was difficult to measure before, by comparing the difference between 
the measurement of residual stress using the sin2 Ψ method and KAM 
value ( Bartlett and Li, 2019 ; Li et al., 2018 ; Sames et al., 2016 ). The 
microscopic residual stress would adversely affect the strength of the 
dental prosthesis even if it does not cause shrinkage or distortion ( Wang 
et al., 2021 ). Therefore, it is important to predict microscopic residual 
stresses. The prediction of microscopic stress, which was attempted in 
this study, requires further research to establish a correlation and 
calculation method. 
In this study, it was confirmed that the CCM alloy manufactured by 
SLM method has different residual stresses depending on the building 
direction. In the As-SLM group, tensile residual stress values were 
observed. The residual stress in the direction perpendicular to the 
building ( Ψ = 90) was observed to be approximately 2.4 times higher 
than that in the building direction ( Ψ = 0). The Ψ = 90 direction is the 
direction in which rapid melting and cooling are repeated quickly. It is 
presumed that this caused the high residual stress because it was a di -
rection in which heat could not be transferred, and the stress was 
relieved after rapid cooling. On the contrary, the residual stress was low 
in the building direction, where residual stress can be resolved through 
heat transfer when the next layer was built, with sufficient time until the 
following layer was built. The residual stress for Ψ = 0 dramatically 
decreased in the SLM 750 group. However, in the direction perpendic -
ular to the building ( Ψ = 90), it was reversed, and compressive stress 
was observed. For the SLM 950 group, most of the residual stress was 
lost regardless of the measurement direction. In contrast, the residual 
compressive stress of the SLM 1150 group increased slightly compared 
to that of SLM 950. During initial cooling after heat treatment, the 
surface was cooled relatively quickly. It is estimated due to the 
compressive stress of the surface generated through rapid cooling pro -
cess; however, it is believed to have a negligible effect on strength and 
hardness. In this study, most of the residual stresses observed in the SLM 
manufactured CCM alloy observed as tensile residual stresses. These 
tensile residual stresses are known to accelerate the generation and 
progression of cracks ( Edwards and Ramulu, 2014 ). However, a 
decrease in the tensile residual stress and a change in the compressive 
residual stress were observed according to the heat treatment. Dental 
prostheses are more vulnerable to cracks on the metal surface due to 
prolonged exposure to the oral environment. Therefore, heat treatment 
at 950 
◦
C or higher is recommended for manufacturing dental prosthe -
ses, which can remove as much as possible the tensile residual stress that 
accelerates crack generation and progression. The phenomenon of 
abrupt reversal of residual stress occurring perpendicular to the building 
direction in SLM 750 group requires additional analysis, and further 
research is needed on the effect of this phenomenon on the mechanical 
properties and deformation of CCM alloy. 
Because the anisotropy of mechanical properties may appear 
depending on the building direction of the SLM Co – Cr alloy, all speci -
mens of the mechanical properties test were prepared parallel to the 
building direction, which is a limitation of this study. In addition, re -
sidual stress measurement using XRD is limited to the surface and has a 
penetration depth of approximately 50 μ m. Therefore, it is necessary to 
further study the residual stress change according to the measurement 
site and thickness using depth profiling. 
5. Conclusion 
SLM is considered to be a superior manufacturing method to casting 
if appropriate heat treatment is included. Most of the residual stress, 
except the micro residual stress, was reduced during heat treatment at 
750 
◦
C. It is necessary to consider the heat treatment temperature ac -
cording to the type of dental prosthesis since the mechanical properties 
of CCM alloy change according to the heat treatment temperature. 
However, considering the reduction of tensile residual stress and in -
crease of ductility, specimens should be heat treated at a temperature of 
950 
◦
C or higher.
