In this work, the effects of deformation on the microstructure and mechanical behaviors of
TiAl alloy were investigated. Deformed microstructure observation was characterized by
scanning electron microscopy, electron back scattered diffraction technique, transmission
electron microscopy and DEFORM-3D software. Results indicated that the core area of the
TiAl pancake was characterized by completely dynamically recrystallized microstructures,
however some residual lamellar colonies can be observed near the edge area, which
are primarily caused by a temperature drop and inhomogenous plastic flow. The main
softening mechanism is dynamic recrystallization of γ grains. The as-forged alloy exhibited
excellent mechanical properties at both room temperature and high temperature. Tensile
test results showed that the ultimate tensile strength of the alloy increased from 832 MPa at
room temperature to 853 MPa at 700 °C, while the elongation increased from 2.7% to 17.8%.
Even at the temperature of 850 °C, the ultimate tensile strength maintained 404 MPa, and
the elongation increased to 75%. The as-forged alloy also exhibited remarkable low-
temperature superplasticity at 850 °C, with an elongation of 120%.

1. Introduction
Recently, there are increasing interests in high Nb-containing
TiAl alloys, which are regarded as a new generation of alloys
for high temperature structural applications. This group of
titanium aluminides not only has excellent oxidation resis-
tance and creep strength, and retention of tensile strength at
elevated temperatures, but also has high tensile strength
at room temperature [1–4]. Ingot metallurgy (IM) of the
high Nb-containing TiAl alloys often is encountered with
macrosegregations of B2 phase (an ordered structure of β
phase at low temperatures), which cause the brittleness
of the alloy [5,6]. In comparison to IM processes, powder
metallurgical methods can decrease the retention of B2
phase in the microstructure [7], and fine homogenous
microstructure can be obtained.
Since the microstructural refinement and pore elimination
can be effectively achieved by subsequent hot working, an
enhanced overall performance of TiAl alloys is expected. It
is therefore greatly interesting to investigate the as-forged

attention is paid to the study on the pack forging of high

pancake with a size of Ф145 mm × 10 mm was prepared

properties at room temperature and elevated temperature

2. Experimental
The pre-alloyed powders with a nominal composition of Ti –
45Al–7Nb–0.3W (at.%) were prepared by plasma rotating elec-
trode processing (PREP) under the protection of an inert gas. The
powders with particle sizes ranging from 100μmt o1 5 0μmw e r e
filled into a steel can, degassed at 400 °C and then hot isostatic
pressed (HIPed) at the temperature of 1240 °C under a pressure of
140 MPa for 4 h. The as-HIPed billet had a density of 4.2 g·cm
−3.
Cylindrical billet with dimensions ofФ50 mm × 70 mm was cut
from the as-HIPed billet by electric-discharge machining and
the surface was ground. The billet was further canned with a
304 steel with a thickness of 10 mm, and was sealed by
vacuum welding. Quasi-isothermal forging was performed
on the canned billet at 1280 °C and 0.1 s
−1, with a total
engineering strain of 80%.
The specimens for scanning electron microscopical (SEM)
observations were mechanically polished, and then etched in
Kroll's reagent. The specimens for transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) were mechanically ground to about 70 μm
firstly, and then thinned using a twin-jet polishing method in
a solution of 21% perchloric acid, 29% n-butyl alcohol and 50%
methanol with a voltage of 22.5 V at −30 °C. The TEM observa-
tion was undertaken on a JEOL-2100F transmission electron
microscope operated at 200 kV. To statistically and detailed-
ly analyze the deformed microstructures, the electron back
scattered diffraction (EBSD) technique was used. The EBSD
sample was cut from the core of t he deformed samples vertical
to the compression axis, and then prepared using the same
process as TEM samples, except that the jet electropolishing
time was 15 s. The data collection was conducted at intervals of
0.3 μm. Finally, the collected EBSD data were treated with TSL
OIM Analysis software.
Tensile samples were cut from the center of the pancake,
with a gauge section of 3 × 1.2 mm cross-section and 8 mm
gauge length. The tensile tests were conducted on an Instron
universal test machine in air at temperatures ranging from
room temperature to 900 °C with nominal strain rate of 3.0 ×

3. Results
3.1. Initial microstructure
Fig. 1 shows the microstructures of the as-HIPed specimens
prior to isothermal compressive tests. The dark phase in
Fig. 1 (a) is γ phase, and the gray phase is α
2. The α2 phase is
dispersed at grain boundaries. Meanwhile, from the EBSD
phase map in Fig. 1(b), it can be found that microstructures are
mainly composed of the γ phase, and the volume fraction is
approximately 91.5%. Most grain boundaries are of high
angle boundaries (98%), and the mean grain size of γ grains
is estimated to be 7 μm. The microstructures are clearly near
the γ-phase structure, and few β(B2) can be observed. This is
different from the as-cast microstructure with similar com-
positions, such as Ti –45Al–7Nb–0.4W–0.15B alloy, which has a
high amount of residual B2 phases [13]. The oxygen content of
the alloy is determined to be 800 ppm.
After the sample was heated at 1280 °C (in the ( α + γ)
phase field), the microstructure consisted of fine and almost
equiaxial γ and α
2 grains, as shown in Fig. 2 . There are four
crystallographically equivalent {111} planes in the γ grain, and
α laths could precipitate at four different orientations in each
γ grain, all satisfying the orientation relationship of (111) γ//
(0001)α2 and 〈110〉γ//〈1120〉α2 [14]. This means that each
gamma grain can be refined by the formation of alpha grains
with 4 different orientations, and the alpha grains transform
to lamellar colonies after cooling. However, there are still
some pores and large residual lamellar colonies found after
the heat treatment. It is necessary to homogenize the micro-
structure further through the hot deformation. Fig. 2(b) shows
the lamellar colony existing among the γ gains, and nearly
no dislocations or subgrains found in the γ gains or lamellar
colony.
3.2. Deformation microstructures
Fig. 3 shows the deformation microstructures of different
positions in the TiAl pancake. Fig. 3(a) shows that the
effective strain of the pancake decreases along the radius
direction, indicated by the arrow. The strain was 1.9 at the
central area while 1.0 near the edge area. As shown in
Fig. 3(b), an incompletely DRX microstructure with dominant
coarse and elongated residual lamellar colonies can be
observed near the edge area. While, at the midpoint of the
radius ( Fig. 3(c)), it can be clearly seen that the microstruc-
tures are characterized by fine DRX grains and some refined
lamellar colonies and some white particles smaller than
1 μm precipitating along the grain boundary. The core area
exhibits a fully dynamic recrystallization (DRX) microstruc-
ture consisting of equiaxed γ grains (the black areas), as
shown in Fig. 3 (d), the lamellar colonies are further refined,
and there is no white particles found in this area.

3.3. Tensile properties of the as-forged TiAl alloy
Fig. 6 shows the tensile properties of the as-forged Ti –45Al–
7Nb–0.3W alloy tested at a speed of 2 × 10 −4 s−1. At room
temperature (RT), the ultimate strength (UTS) of the alloy is
832 MPa, with an elongation ( δ) of 2.7%. At the temperature
of 700 °C, the UTS and δ increase to 853 MPa and 17.8%,
respectively. However, with the temperature increasing,
above 700 °C, the UTS values drop sharply while the δ values
increase rapidly. At 850 °C, the UTS drops to 404 MPa, and the
δ value increases up to 75%.

4. Discussions
4.1. Deformation mechanism
The microstructural variations from the center to the edge in
the as-forged pancake are generally ascribed to inhomogenous
plastic flow. The heat loss and temperature drop during
forging, as well as the friction between the can and TiAl
billet, are inevitable, especially at the ends of the billet [10].
Moreover, plastic deformation is a typically thermally activated
process. Consequently, during canned forging, the effective

When the TiAl alloys are deformed in the ( α + γ) phase
field, the main softening mechanism is DRX. In the work
hardening stage of the hot deformation process, the density
of dislocations increased significantly as the strain increased,
providing a driving force for DRX. As the deformation pro-
ceeded, DRX started, and the recrystallized γ grains will result
in the fragmentation of lamellar colonies. Meanwhile, a small
amount of Ti

refine the γ grains. On the other hand, mechanical twinning
also improves the deformability; this is probably very helpful
for large-scale deformation.
4.2. Tensile properties
At RT, the plastic elongation of the currently forged alloy at RT
(2.7%) is almost the same as that of PM Ti –46Al–9Nb (at.%)
alloy (2.5%), and is higher than that of as-cast high Nb TiAl
alloys, such as Ti –45Al–(8–9)Nb–(W, B, Y) [19] and Ti –43Al–7
(Nb, Mo) –0.2B alloys [20]. This may be due to the fact that
PM materials have more homogenous microstructures than
as-cast materials. Nb has a solubility of about 20 at.% in the γ
phase of TiAl alloys at 1200 °C [21], and the formation of large
amount of β(B2) phase due to the chemical segregation in PM
materials can be avoided. It is reported that the B2 phase is a
brittle phase at low temperatures, and will cause deterioration
of the mechanical properties [13,22]. Therefore, the absence of
much B2 phase in the present TiAl alloys also contributes to
the high tensile ductility at RT.
The strain-rate sensitivity index m indicates the resistance
of necking during superplastic deformation. Ti –45Al–7Nb–
0.3W (at. %) alloy exhibits a relatively high m value of 0.6 at
850 °C, compared with that of other Nb-containing TiAl alloys
at about the same temperature. The m value was determined
to be 0.3 for the as-forged Ti –43Al–4Nb–2Mo–0.5B alloy at
850 °C [23], and 0.5 for PM the Ti –47Al–2Cr–1Nb–1Ta alloy at
800 °C [24]. This is mainly due to the fact that the as-forged PM
TiAl alloy in this work possesses fine grain size, smaller
than 5 μm. Fine grains are beneficial for alloys to display
superplastic behavior [25] for the facility of grain sliding and
diffusion. Meanwhile, small cavities are usually formed for
the heterogenous deformation between the γ and α
2 phases,
and play a role in relieving the localized stress concentration
in grain boundaries. Clemens [26] suggested that the β phase
distributed on the grain boundary will promote grain bound-
ary sliding effectively during low-temperature superplasticity.
Thus, the segregation of a small amount of β particles with a
size less than 1 μm from the α
2 phase may also contribute to
the low-temperature superplasticity of the as forged Ti –45Al–
7Nb–0.3W (at.%) alloy.

5. Conclusions
(1) The as-forged PM Ti –45Al–7Nb–0.3W pancake has a
duplex (DP) microstructure. The core area of the TiAl
pancake was characterized by completely dynamically
recrystallized microstructures, while some residual
lamellar colonies can be observed near the edge area,
due to the temperature drop and inhomogenous plastic
flow during the forging process.
(2) The as-forged alloy exhibits excellent mechanical proper-
ties at both room and high temperatures. The ultimate
tensile strength (UTS) of the alloy increased from
832 MPa at room temperature to 853 MPa at 700 °C,
while the elongation ( δ) increased from 2.7% to 17.8%.
The elongation ( δ) at RT is higher than that of other
high Nb-containing TiAl alloys. The forged alloy also
exhibited superplasticity at 850 °C.
(3) At RT, cleavage and transgranular (translamellar and
interlamellar) cracking were the dominant fracture
modes. At 800 °C, the formation of dimples occurred,
leading to ductile fracture.
