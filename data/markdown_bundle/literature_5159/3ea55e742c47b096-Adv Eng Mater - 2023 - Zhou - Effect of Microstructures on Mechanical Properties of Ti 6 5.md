1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are the important struc-
tural materials in the aerospace industry
due to their high strength-to-weight ratio,
as well as their superior combination of
strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance
at elevated service temperatures. [1– 3]
However, with the rapid development
of aeroengines, the requirements for
high-temperature strength and thermo-
mechanical stability in extreme working
environments are increasing. [4] Therefore,
numerous studies have been carried out
on high-temperature titanium alloys, in
which Ti – 6.5Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 4Mo– 1W– 0.2Si
(wt%, Russian grade is BT25y) alloy is one of the competitive
alloys.[5,6] It is a near- α-type titanium alloy based on BT25 alloy. [7]
Compared to BT25 alloy, Al content is slightly reduced and Zr
and Mo contents are increased, and the alloy has a better com-
prehensive performance.[8] Due to its excellent mechanical prop-
erties at 550 °C, BT25y alloy has attracted extensive attention and
has been used to manufacture high-pressure compressor disks
and rotor blades in aeroengines. [9]
For near- α titanium alloys, three typical microstructures, that
is, lamellar, bimodal, and equiaxed microstructures, can be
obtained by varying heat treatment processes after thermal defor-
mation, and then different mechanical properties arise. [10] Fine
lamellar microstructure normally exhibits improved strength and
limited ductility, [11,12] while equiaxed microstructure exhibits
excellent ductility and acceptable strength. [13– 15] Bimodal micro-
structure, which combines the former two kinds of microstruc-
tures, exhibits the balance between strength and ductility. [16– 21]
In addition, the α’ phase appears in some near- α titanium alloys,
but it never occurs in the ﬁnal state of the alloy, which eventually
transforms into a stable α phase by heat treatment. [22– 24]
Many studies have been conducted to correlate microstruc-
tural morphology and the resultant mechanical properties in
titanium alloys. [25– 28] In TA19 alloy fabricated by laser metal
deposition, α þ α’ phase was found, and the Widmannstätten
microstructure and double-lamellar special bimodal microstruc-
ture were obtained after heat treatment, where the perfect
combination of strength (1112 /C6 13 MPa) and ductility
(12.6 /C6 0.35%) was obtained by tailoring the content and size
of primary α phase ( αp), secondary α phase ( αs), and α’.[29]

were investigated through three-stage heat treatments. [30] The
tensile results showed that the increase in the volume fraction

The tensile properties at both room temperature and 550 °C are investigated in
Ti–6.5Al–2Sn–4Zr–4Mo–1W–0.2Si with three typical microstructures, which are
lamellar, bimodal, and equiaxed microstructures. The results show that the yield
and ultimate tensile strengths decrease while the ductility increases as the
volume fraction of primary α phase increases at both temperatures. The above
phenomena result from the decrease in the β transformed microstructure, which
consists of ﬁne α and β lamellae with high-density α/β interfaces blocking
dislocation slipping. The yield and ultimate tensile strengths at 550 °C are lower
than that at room temperature, and the ductility at 550 °C is higher than that at
room temperature, both of which are attributed to the activity of multislipping
systems at high temperature.

2. Experimental Section
The wrought BT25y alloy forged in the α þ β phase ﬁeld was used
in the present work. The measured composition was Ti – 6.48Al–
1.94Sn– 3.88Zr– 4.05Mo– 0.73 W– 0.25Si (wt%) and the β-transus
temperature ( Tβ) measured by the metallographic method was
about 980 °C. Double annealing (the detailed heat treatment
regimes are shown in Table 1 ) was used to obtain three kinds
of microstructures. All the heat treatment processes were
conducted in air by an electrical resistance furnace.
For OM imaging, the samples were sectioned by electrical dis-
charge machining, ground with SiC sandpapers, polished with
oxide polishing suspension (OP-S) solution, and then etched
by Kroll solution (1%HF, 4% HNO 3, and 95%H 2O) for /C25 10 s.
The OM images were received from the Lecia DMi8. To further
observe the microstructural morphology, TEM observation was
conducted on the Talos F200X G2 TEM operated at 200 KV.
The TEM samples were machined into thin sheets with a thick-
ness of 0.1 mm and then mechanically ground to 0.04 – 0.05 μm.
The foils 3 mm in diameter were twin-jet electrolyzing polished
in solution (6%HClO 4, 35%CH 3(CH2)3OH, and 59%CH 3OH).
Different scale features of α phase were quantitatively character-
ized on OM and TEM images by the image analysis software.
After tensile testing, the facture surface features of the samples

3. Results
3.1. Microstructures
The wrought alloy shows a bimodal microstructure, which
consisted of 20% equiaxed αp in volume fraction distributing
in the β transformed microstructure consisting of ﬁne αs and
β lamellae, and the average size of αp is 6.14 /C6 0.59 μm, as shown
in Figure 1 a.
Lamellar microstructure consists of alternating α lamellas
segregated by the ultra ﬁne residual β phase. The solution
treatment above β transus made the equiaxed αp and lamellar
αs transform into β phase entirely and then transform into
lamellar microstructure during cooling. TEM results showed
that the average thickness of α lamella was 0.13 /C6 0.04 μm, as
shown in Figure 2 a,b. The selected-area diffraction pattern in
Figure 2c showed the presence of the α phase and β phase.
Finer α phase lamellae couldn ’t be observed in TEM images
in Figure 2a,b.
Compared with the microstructure in the as-received state, the
αp and αs of bimodal microstructure became larger, with the
average size of αp measured as 8.74 /C6 0.74 μm, as shown in
Figure 1c. TEM results showed that the average thickness of
αs in β-transformed microstructure ( βt) is 1.52 /C6 0.33 μm.
Furthermore, a few ultra ﬁne tertiary α phase ( αt) was observed
with average thickness as 0.14 /C6 0.013 μm, as shown in
Figure 3 b,d. The residual β phase between the αs lamellas is
stable enough without further decomposition in the aging pro-
cess. Some silicides are also observed, as shown in Figure 3c.
Generally speaking, there are two types of silicides in high-
temperature titanium alloys, that is, (TiZr) 5Si3 (S1, a = 0.773 nm,
c = 0.532 nm) and (TiZr)6Si3 (S2, a = 0.70 nm, c = 0.368 nm), both
of them are hexagonal crystal structures. [32] The selected-area
electron diffraction (SAED) of the silicide in Figure 3e
indicates that this silicide is S 2-type (TiZr)6Si3.

3.3. Fractographic Analysis
The fractographic analysis is performed to elucidate the fracture
mechanisms of three typical microstructures. LM ( Figure 6 a,b)
shows the highest strength but poor ductility. The fractographic
analysis is a brittle fracture, as indicated by the facets and inter-
granular cracks. These are signi ﬁcant characteristics of brittle
fractures. Moreover, the crack propagation path is relatively
smooth compared with BM and EM, showing less deformation
energy dissipation before the ﬁnal fracture, which is consistent
with tow ductility. The formation of facets and the intergranular
fracture mode are thought to be associated with the appearance
of the continuous αGB (Figure 1b), which is widely considered to
be a low-energy path for crack propagation.
The fractographic analysis of BM, with the best trade-off
between the strength and ductility, is shown in Figure 6c,d. It
reveals typical ductile fracture characteristics, indicated by the
dense equiaxed dimples and crack propagation paths. Actually,
numerous ultra ﬁne αs laths (Figure 4) are sheared and bent
in shear bands to facilitate compatible deformation, which
should be more dif ﬁcult than the deformation of individual αs
laths. Therefore, it is recommended to accommodate the large
substantial deformation strain to improve strength and ductility
before the ﬁnal fracture.
The fractographic analysis of EM shows the typical ductile
fracture characteristics including cup- and cone-shaped fracture
surfaces (consisting of ﬁbrous regions and shear lips) and tortu-
ous cross-grain crack propagation paths, as shown in Figure 6e,f.
Due to the mismatch in mechanical properties between α and
parent β phases, additional interaction stresses are inevitably
introduced and present as stress gradients way away from the
interface.[33,34] Microvoids unavoidably nucleate at the peak of
the α/β interfaces or the inside of the αp particles through the
aggregation of microvoids (Figure 6e,f ), acting as the starting
points of the microcrack, leading to transgranular fracture with
high ductility.
Figure 7 shows the fractography of the BT25y alloy after 550 °C
tensile testing. Small ﬁsh-scale dimples appeared in LM, which
indicated that the ductility is enhanced. In addition, the tearing
edge and crevices are consistent with the low elongation, as
shown in Figure 7a,b. The fracture mechanisms of BM and
EM (Figure 7c,d) are obviously ductile fracture, consisting of
deep and large dimples, which indicate high ductility.
4. Discussion
In this work, three typical microstructures of BT25y alloy were
obtained by designing different heat treatments. LM was solution
treated at 1100 °C, above the β-transus temperature, resulting in
the complete transformation of αp to β phase. During the air cool-
ing process (large cooling rate), the β phase was totally trans-
formed into α lamellae. BM was solution treated at 950 °C,
below the β-transus temperature, increasing the size of αp and
αs. BM precipitated α lamella in the air-cooling process (small
cooling rate). EM was solution treated at 965 °C, exhibiting an
increase in the size of equiaxed αp. Compared with BM, αp grew
further in the furnace cooling process in EM, and the new αp
nucleated and grew at the β grain boundary, increasing the vol-
ume fraction of αp. To ensure the comprehensive properties of
the alloy, samples were carried out with the secondary annealing
treatment, and no decomposition occurred again during the
aging treatment. After the different heat treatments, the bimodal
in as-received samples transformed into the lamellar microstruc-
ture in LM, the bimodal microstructure in BM, and the equiaxed
microstructure in EM.

crystallographic orientations led to high density of α/β interfaces,
which could be the potential locations for dislocation pileup. The
α phase dispersion strengthening was considerably effective
because the high density of α/β interfaces markedly blocks the
dislocation motion.[35] As deformation proceeds, a large number
of dislocations pile up on the α grain boundary ( αGB) and were
difﬁcult to pass through. The phenomena resulted in high back
stress, which signi ﬁcantly prevented dislocation motion. At the
same time, the localized dislocation pileup caused stress concen-
tration around αGB, leading to the early crack generation and poor
ductility.
In BM, αp, αs, and αt appeared in the β matrix, as shown in
Figure 3. The thickness of αs and αt was larger than in LM, result-
ing in the strength lower than that in lamellar microstructure. αs
and αt provided some α/β interfaces that caused the dislocation
pileup, allowing the strength to be maintained. In addition, αp
and αs allowed more plastic deformation to occur before the local
cumulated stress was high enough to induce cracks. Therefore,
the increasing volume fraction of αp could ameliorate the ductil-
ity. Both BM and EM included equiaxed αp whereas equiaxed
microstructure had better ductility. This was partially attributed
to the higher volume fraction of equiaxed αp in EM ( /C25 95%) than
in BM ( /C25 60%), which can accommodate strains better. The
width of the αs laths was also essential for ductility because it
not only had to be ﬁne enough to supply precipitation strength-
ening but also enable deformation compatible with the parent-
β-grain to ensure ductility. [16] As the deformation proceeds,
the slip occupies more α grains and expands to the surrounding
β-transformed microstructures, which does not cause cracks
from stress concentration in a single grain. Therefore, the forma-
tion and development of microvoids were relatively late before
the ﬁnal fracture. [36,37]
During tensile deformation at 550 °C, a large number of slip
traces appeared at the α/β interfaces. This behavior can be
explained by the possible slip systems in α and β phases. At room
temperature, the ⃑a-type Burgers vector <1120> had the smallest
critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) values in α phase. But non ⃑a-
type slip with <⃑c þ ⃑a> Burgers vector had remarkably high
CRSS values, and its activation required high tensile stress. [38,39]
The values of CRSS deeply depended on the experimental
temperature in titanium alloys. The CRSS values reduced with
increasing temperature; this was reported by Wang et al. [40]
and Li et al. [38] During high-temperature tensile deformation,
<⃑c þ ⃑a> slip was more predominant as compared to <⃑a> slip.
Therefore, it was very likely that both <⃑a> slip and <⃑c þ ⃑a> slip
were activated at 550 °C. The activation of multiple slip systems
including <⃑a> slip and <⃑c þ ⃑a> slip was the main reason for
higher ductility tested at 550 °C. [38,40]
5. Conclusion
In this work, different microstructures, which are lamellar,
bimodal, and equiaxed, were obtained by designed heat treat-
ment processes in Ti – 6.5Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 4Mo– 1W– 0.2Si alloy,
investigated, and correlated with both room temperature and
550 °C tensile properties. The deformation and fracture mecha-
nisms were investigated at room temperature and 550 °C to elu-
cidate the inﬂuence of microstructures on mechanical properties
and to understand the effect of the volume fraction of equiaxed αp
on the mechanical properties. The conclusions that can be drawn
from the present work are as follows. 1) The strength of the alloy
is dominantly controlled by the high-density α/β interface, which
effectively blocked dislocation glide. The ductility was regulated
by tailoring the equiaxed αp and β transformed microstructure. A
large volume fraction of equiaxed αp facilitated substantial strain
strengthening and promotes ductility. 2) The strength at 550 °C is
lower than that at room temperature while the ductility is higher,
due to the activity of multislip systems at 550 °C.
