Abstract 
Molybdenum alloys are used in a number of commercial, defense, nuclear, and aerospace 
applications that take advantage of their high melting temperature, good creep resistance, and 
decent mechanical properties at elevated temperature. Titanium-Zirconium-Molybdenum (TZM) 
alloys are the most commercially important Mo alloy as they possess substantially higher 
strength than pure molybdenum while still maintaining strong mechanical performance at 
elevated temperature. High density components of these alloys are of interest for high 
performance defense and aerospace applications, however obtaining high relative densities by 
sintering has proven difficult. Here we report nano-titanium carbide addition as an effective 
method of improving TZM sintered density as well as increasing alloy hardness and flexural 
strength. Pure TZM was sintered by Field Assisted sintering Technique (FAST) to over 98.5% 
density at 2000°C under 55 MPa applied pressure, while density was improved to >99% using 
TiC addition. TiC addition was found to result in reduced grain size and improved hardness of 
as-sintered alloys, including over a 95% reduction in grain size and 75% increase in hardness for 
TZM with 10 vol%TiC addition. 
Introduction 
The combination of a ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) below or near room 
temperature1, high melting temperature, good creep resistance2, high electrical conductivity3, 
high strength at elevated temperatures4,5, and low thermal neutron cross section6 make 
molybdenum alloys highly attractive for use in the nuclear, defense7, and aerospace industries8. 
In the nuclear industry, molybdenum is of particular interest for use as a structural component in 
gas cooled reactor applications (GCR) and in accelerator applications6,9. The most commercially 
important molybdenum alloy is known as TZM and consists of Mo alloyed with small quantities 
of titanium and zirconium (generally below 0.1 wt%) in order to gain advantage of solid solution 
strengthening and second phase precipitation strengthening mechanisms while still maintaining

high melting temperature10. Fabrication of components from molybdenum based alloys remains 
challenging, as molybdenum processing requires the use of elevated temperatures or extreme 
plastic deformation to produce components of high density11. Furthermore, molybdenum 
commonly displays substantial recrystallization and grain growth at elevated temperatures12 that 
can lead to premature failure of TZM alloys in high-temperature applications13. 
To improve the mechanical properties of Mo and TZM alloys, small additives of oxides or 
carbides can be added to these alloys. These additives act as grain refinement agents which 
result in smaller grain size than unalloyed products. Oxide additives include Al2O3 to that in 
addition to acting as grain refiners improve oxidation and wear resistance14,15,16, spinel 
(MgAl2O4) additives which have been demonstrated to improve specimen ductility1, and La2O3 
and other rare earth oxide additives17,18. Carbide additives have been less explored for use in 
these alloys and have largely been confined to TiC additives19. 
Field Assisted Sintering Technique (FAST), also commonly referred to as Spark Plasma 
Sintering (SPS), is a promising technique for the cost-effective production of refractory metal 
components. While similar in design to hot pressing (HP) due to its use of simultaneous 
application of elevated temperatures and pressures to obtain powder densification, FAST makes 
use of electrical resistive heating. In FAST, current is passed directly through the sintering die 
and sample itself during densification, which allows for increased heating rates in comparison to 
HP and other conventional sintering techniques. This increased heating rate and volumetric 
heating of the sample has been shown to lead to reduced sintering cycle times and consequently 
reduced grain growth of samples in comparison to HP. Investigation of the sintering of pure 
molybdenum using FAST was performed by Mouwad et al. who found that full densification of 
the powder was possible 20. Work was also performed on Mo-TiC alloys by Ohser-Wiedemann 
et al. using a combination of high energy ball milling and spark plasma sintering to produce 
high-density TZM-TiC composites that displayed superior hardness and recrystallization 
resistance in comparison to pure TZM 13. Characterization of these of alloys was unfortunately 
limited to hardness measurements in this study, providing little information as to the effect of 
TiC on the plasticity of TZM. In this study we report the use of low-energy ball milling with 
TZM powder to produce TZM-TiC composites. These composites featured reduced grain size 
and improved hardness and flexural strength in comparison to pure TZM samples, while still 
maintaining room temperature ductility. 
Experimental Procedure 
TZM (Mo-0.5%Ti-0.08%Zr, 0.01-0.04% C, 0.025% 0.40%O) powder acquired from H.C. Starck 
Inc. and titanium carbide (TiC) powder (99+%, 40-60 nm manufacturer specification by APS) 
powder acquired from U.S. Research Nanomaterials were used for all sintering investigations. 
For pure TZM sintering experiments, powder was used as-received while for TZM-TiC alloys 
were produced using rotary ball milling at 150 RPM for 4 hrs using a 4:1 ball to powder ratio 
(BPR). Bragg-Brentano X-ray diffraction (XRD) between angles of 20-80 ° 2θ analysis using an

Empyrean PANalytical Pro diffractometer with a Cu Kα source was used to perform powder 
phase analysis. Powders were analyzed using an FESEM LEO 1530 scanning electron 
microscope to determine powder size and morphology. 
TZM powder was sintered using a 25 ton FCT Systeme GmbH Spark Plasma Sintering Furnace 
under a variety of temperatures and pressures to determine optimal sintering conditions. Once 
conditions were found, TZM-5vol%TiC and TZM-10vol%TiC powders were also synthesized 
using the same conditions to allow for comparison in densities and grain size as a result of alloy 
contents. Samples were sintered using a 40 mm graphite die lined with graphite foil and powder 
densities targeted at producing samples 6 mm in thickness. Samples were cut, mount, and 
polished to a final polish of 0.05 μm from each disc. Samples were etched using Murakami’s 
reagent and imaged by optical microscopy. Average grain size of samples was determined from 
samples using the ASTM line intercept method. A carbide layer was found to form on the 
outside of each sample which was removed using the diamond saw prior to examination with x-
ray diffraction to determine changes in sample composition in comparison to the pre-sintered 
powders. Hardness measurements were performed on samples using a Vicker’s microhardness 
indentor with 1 kg load and 15 s dwell time following ASTM E384-11. 
In order to further investigate mechanical properties, 10.16 mm x 10.16 mm (4”x4”) tiles with 
thickness of approximately 6 mm were sintered using a 250 ton FCT Systeme GmbH Spark 
Plasma Sintering Furnace. Bend testing was then performed on bend bars cut from tiles by wire 
EDM following the ASTM C1161 testing standard. Seven bars were tested for each alloy using 
an Instron 5866 testing apparatus system to obtain statistical significance in results.

Relative densities of all samples were found to vary with respect to both sintering temperature 
and pressure, with sample density increasing with both increased sample temperature and 
pressure. It should be noted that a carbide layer was found to form on the outside of sintered 
samples due to the formation of Mo2C as a result of reaction of TZM powder with the outer 
graphite surface. Density measurements were performed on materials following removal of this 
carbide layer for comparison. Generally, sample density was found to increase with both 
increasing temperature and increasing pressure for samples measured both with and without the 
carbide layer present. Densities of samples were found to range between approximately 95.5-
98.5% relative density. While pressure was found to produce substantially improved 
densification at lower sintering temperatures, density of samples above 2000 °C was found to be 
independent of pressure. This suggests the existence of a limit to maximum sample density 
obtained by modification of SPS sintering parameters alone. Additional powder processing or 
alloying additions are therefore needed to improve sample density beyond this level.

Despite the substantially different grain size noted between samples in Figure 5, hardness values 
near 190 VHN were found for all samples. This in disagreement with that expected from the 
Hall-Petch effect, which states that decreased grain size should result in increased hardness. The 
independence of hardness with respect to processing conditions coincides well with previous 
reported hardness values for sintered TZM and pure Mo alloys which have been found to very 
between approximately 190-210 VHN13. Pure molybdenum has previously demonstrated 
hardness values independent of sintering conditions21. This suggests that increases in sample 
density are offset by increases in sample porosity for TZM alloys as well, making hardness 
improvement unobtainable by variation of sintering parameters within reasonable values of 
sintering temperature and pressure. Examination of the density and grain sizes obtained for 
samples suggested optimal sintering conditions for TZM of approximately 1900 °C, 55 MPa, 15 
min for optimal sintering density with minimal grain growth. TZM-5vol%TiC and TZM-
10vol%TiC were therefore sintered at these conditions. Optical micrographs showing the 
characteristic microstructure of each of these alloys is shown in Figure 7.

One possible explanation for the improvement in sample density with respect to sintering lies 
reaction of TiC with the surrounding matrix. Examination of the Ti-Mo system reveals the alloy 
system to be fully miscible, while quasibinary Mo-TiC diagrams show miscibility of TiC in the 
Mo matrix up to values of approximately 5 at% TiC. This suggests that TiC particles will 
partially decompose during sintering within the matrix. It can also be noted that partial or even 
complete oxidation of TiC is expected at the elevated temperatures used during the sintering 
procedure. Therefore, it is likely that TiC particles will at least partially react with Mo in the 
TZM matrix during the sintering following the equations: 
3Mo + TiC → MoTi + Mo2C (Eq. 1) 
and:

Conclusions 
Sintering experiments investigating the consolidation of unalloyed TZM and TZM alloyed with 
nano-titanium carbide powder were successfully conducted using FAST. Results from this work 
include: 
 Production of pure TZM resulting in alloys of densities in excess of 98.5% density and 
alloys of greater than 99.5% density using nano titanium carbide addition. 
 5 and 10 vol%TiC addition were found to improve sample densification leading to alloys 
of increased density at relatively lowered sintering temperatures (as low as 1800 ° C). 
 5 and 10 vol% TiC addition resulted in over a 95% reduction in sample grain size in 
comparison to pure TZM for TZM sintered at 2000 °C, 55 MPa, 15 min. 
 10vol%TiC addition was found to produce alloys 90% higher in hardness than pure TZM 
alloys at the cost of displaying brittle failure during flexural testing.

Sintering experiments investigating the consolidation of unalloyed TZM and TZM alloyed with 
nano-titanium carbide powder were successfully conducted using FAST. Results from this work 
include: 
 Production of pure TZM resulting in alloys of densities in excess of 98.5% density and 
alloys of greater than 99.5% density using nano titanium carbide addition 
 5 and 10 vol%TiC addition were found to improve sample densification leading to alloys 
of increased density at relatively lowered sintering temperatures (as low as 1800 ° C) 
 5 and 10 vol% TiC addition resulted in over a 95% reduction in sample grain size in 
comparison to pure TZM for TZM sintered at 2000 °C, 55 MPa, 15 min. 
 10vol%TiC addition was found to produce alloys 90% higher in hardness than pure TZM 
alloys 
 5 vol% TiC addition increased flexural strength of TZM by approximately 45% while 
still demonstrating measurable sample ductility, while the DBTT of pure TZM was found 
to be lower than that of room temperature.
