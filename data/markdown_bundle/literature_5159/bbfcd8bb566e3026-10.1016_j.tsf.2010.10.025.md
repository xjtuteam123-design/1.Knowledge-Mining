The effects of Ti/TiN bi- and multilayered ﬁlms on the fatigue performance of the Ti46Al8Nb alloy were
investigated. Ti/TiN ﬁlms with a total thickness of 1 μm were deposited on the Ti46Al8Nb alloy substrate by
the hollow cathode deposition method. The samples were examined with various analytical techniques
including nanoindentation, scratch test, stripping layer substrate curvature test and scanning electron
microscopy. The results show that multilayered Ti/TiN ﬁlms can enhance the fatigue strength of the
Ti46Al8Nb alloy, whereas bilayered ﬁlms have no obvious effect. Compared with the bilayer, the multilayer
exhibits higher hardness, higher residual compressive stress and higher adhesion strength to the substrate. It
is also demonstrated that the multilayer is responsible for retarding fatigue crack growth. All the superior
properties make the hard Ti/TiN multilayer to be an effective protection coating for the enhanced fatigue
strength of the brittle substrate.

1. Introduction
It has been recognized that the service life of ductile materials can
be improved by applying thin hard ceramic ﬁlms, such as nitride and
carbide ﬁlms on the surfaces. TiN, one of the ceramic ﬁlms has been
reported to improve the fatigue strength of many ductile substrate
materials, such as steels [1–6], titanium alloys [7] and copper [8].
Berríos et al. [3,4] found that a single layer of the TiN x ﬁlm on 316L
stainless steel can signi ﬁcantly increase fatigue strength, which was
mainly associated with the compressive residual stresses present in
the coating and the good adhesion of the coating to the substrate. Herr
et al. [5] reported that the bene ﬁcial effect of the TiN coating on the
fatigue properties of steel and grey cast iron was due to high hardness
combined with good adhesion to the substrate. The hard ﬁlms have
demonstrated in suppressing the formation of the critical persistent
slip band (PSB) surface morphology, but their impact on fatigue life
has been limited because cyclic loading can initiate cracks by another
mechanism [9]. In most cases involving hard surface coatings, this
secondary crack initiation mechanism is the rupture of the ﬁlm.
The effects of hard ﬁlms on fatigue behaviour may also depend on
the ﬁlm thickness. Su et al. [1,2] reported that the application of TiN
ﬁlms decreased the fatigue strength of AISI 1045 steel in the regions of
cycles b105. Kolkman [10] investigated the Ti6A14V alloy coated with
TiN monolayer and TiN/Ti multilayer with a thickness of 50 μm. The
experiments found that both ﬁlms decreased the fatigue strength of
the substrate, which was due to the introduced tensile stress in the
substrate as a compensation of compressive stress in the ﬁlms during
coating application. These results suggest that control of ﬁlm
thickness is important in order to improve fatigue strength.
Based on a thorough review of the literature on fatigue crack ini-
tiation, Stoudt et al.[8] proposed that for optimum resistance to fatigue
crack initiation, a combination of optimistic properties including
hardness, toughness, cyclic hardenability, residual compressive stres-
ses, and adherence is required. According to this assumption, it was
hypothesized that nanometer-scale, multilayered coatings will pos-
sess a combination of these required properties enabling signi ﬁcant
increases in fatigue crack initiation resistance. Stoudt et al. [8]
conducted fatigue experiments on Cu samples coated with nanoscale
Cu–Ni multilayer. The experiments concluded that the fatigue lives of
the multilayer coated samples were signi ﬁcantly higher than those of
uncoated samples, while a single layer had no obvious effect.
Up to now, the effect of the nanoscale multilayer on fatigue per-
formance was not extensively studied. In addition, the hard ﬁlms were
mainly applied on the ductile substrate. Little attention was paid on
the brittle substrate [10]. TiAl based intermetallics, one of the typical
brittle materials, have many applications in the aerospace and
automobile industries such as exhaust valve and turbine wheel due
to their low weight and high strength [11]. There are three typical
microstructures of TiAl, i.e., lamellar microstructure, equiaxed γ
microstructure and duplex microstructure [12]. Among them, TiAl
intermetallic compounds with a lamellar microstructure have the
highest fracture toughness, fatigue properties as well as high
temperature strength [13–15]. Ti46Al8Nb is one of the used TiAl
intermetallic compounds [16]. In order to further increase the fatigue

2. Experimental details
2.1. Specimen preparation
The substrate is the Ti46Al8Nb alloy which has a full lamellar
structure after hot isostatic pressing (HIP) as shown in Fig. 1 .
Microstructural investigations were observed by scanning electron
microscopy (FEI XL30 SEM) at an operating voltage of 15 kV. The ﬁlms
were deposited to the substrate by the hollow cathode deposition
method (IPB30/30 T, Japan). Before deposition, the specimens were
machined to shapes as schematically shown inFig. 2. Each specimen was
mechanically polished to a roughness of 0.5 μm( R
a), ultrasonically
cleaned in acetone, and then put into a depositing chamber at room
temperature. Then the chamber was heated up to a temperature of
400 °C. For both bi- and multilayered ﬁlms, a soft Ti layer was ﬁrstly
deposited on the substrate followed by a hard TiN layer. In this way, a
multilayer with alternating soft Ti layer and hard TiN layer was
produced. The multilayered ﬁlm has a 100 nm repeat thickness, a 9:1
(TiN to Ti) constituent ratio, and contains 10 interfaces (Fig. 3). For the
bilayered ﬁlm, the thickness of the Ti and TiN layer is approximately
100 nm and 900 nm, respectively. The total thickness of both bi- and
multilayered ﬁlms is approximately 1 μm.
2.2. Fatigue test
Fatigue tests were carried out in a load-controlled mode on the
Instron 8562 fatigue machine. The specimens were axially loaded in a
triangle waveform with a stress ratio of 0 and a frequency of 1 Hz.
Fatigue tests were carried out up to 3 × 10 6 cycles.
2.3. Residual stress test
The residual stress of Ti/TiN bi- and multilayeredﬁlms was measured
by the stripping layer substrate curvature technique (SLSCT) developed
by Zhao [17]. Compared with the conventional substrate curvature
technique, the advantage of the stripping layer substrate curvature
technique is that additional stresses induced by a mechanical method
can be avoided. To achieve this, the ﬁlm on one side was gradually
stripped by chemical etching with a mixture solution of hydrogen
peroxide and potassium hydroxide at a ratio of 5.3:1 at room
temperature. Then a precise optical system was designed to measure
the curvature. A detailed description can be found in [17].
2.4. Hardness test
Since the total thickness of the Ti/TiN ﬁlm is only 1 μm, in order to
eliminate the effect of the substrate, a Nano Indenter (MTS Systems
Inc., USA) equipped with a diamond (Berkovich) was used to evaluate
the hardness of the ﬁlm. Loading of the indenter on ﬁlm was
performed at a constant displacement rate until 1 μm depth was
achieved. The dwell period at the peak depth was 10 s. The adjacent
indents were separated by at least 5 μm. The hardness was averaged
with more than three measurements. Hardness was calculated
according to Oliver and Pharr's method [18].
2.5. Adhesion test
A scratch test apparatus with a Rockwell-shaped diamond
indenter of 200 μm radius was used to evaluate the adhesive strength
between the ﬁlm and the substrate. The test was carried out by
continuous loading with a normal force from 1 to 200 N. The samples
were scratched by increasing the normal load at a rate of 7.5 N/min
and using a scratching speed of 6 mm/min. The critical load was
determined by microscopic evaluation of the crack pattern around the
scratch channel and by the acoustic emission (AE) signal along the
scratch. The AE signal is induced by elastic waves generated by the
sudden cracking in materials when external load is applied.

3. Results
3.1. Stress amplitude versus numbers of cycle to failure curves
The stress amplitude versus numbers of cycle to failure i.e. S –N
curve of uncoated and coated specimens is shown in Fig. 4. The results
show that the multilayered Ti/TiN coating can increase the fatigue
strength of the Ti46Al8Nb alloy signi ﬁcantly, while the bilayered Ti/
TiN coating has no obvious effect. For example, the fatigue strength of
multilayered and bilayered coated samples at 3 million cycles is
approximately 300 and 280 MPa, respectively.

stepping distance for both bi- and multilayered ﬁlms. Based on the
measured curves, the residual stress of bi- and multilayered ﬁlms can

3.3. Hardness of the coating
The hardness of the bi- and multilayered ﬁlms was measured by
nanoindentation using the Oliver and Pharr method. The results are
shown in Fig. 6 . The load and unload curves on both ﬁlms were
repeated for three times, which exhibited good reproducibility as
shown in Fig. 6 (a). The relationship between hardness and indenta-
tion depth is shown in Fig. 6(b). The result shows that the multilayer
exhibits a higher hardness than the bilayer for the indentation depth
less than 200 nm. At a depth of more than 200 nm, it was found that
there was no obvious difference in hardness between both ﬁlms
which was probably due to the in ﬂuence of the substrate [20]. In the
present study, the Vickers hardness of the substrate is 317. It can be
deduced from Fig. 6(b) that both bilayer and multilayer have a higher
hardness than that of the substrate. At an indention depth of 100 nm,
the hardness of the multilayer and the bilayer was 23 GPa and 20 GPa,
respectively. The maximum hardness for the multilayer and bilayer
was approximately 35 GPa and 25 GPa, respectively.
3.4. Adhesion strength between coating and substrate
The adhesion strength between the ﬁlm and the substrate was
determined by a scratch test. Acoustic signal produced by the
delamination of the ﬁlm was used to characterize the critical load.
Since the fracture of the ﬁlm at the sides of the scratch trace may also
generate acoustic signals, in order to obtain a more precise result both
acoustic technique and the microscopic observations were used. The
critical load is deﬁned at the onset of cracks on the sides of the scratch.
The typical scratch traces observed by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) are present in Fig. 7 . It can be seen that much more cracks
formed at the sides of the scratch for the bilayered ﬁlm, while there
were less cracks formed at the scratch of the multilayer as shown in
Fig. 7 . Acoustic emission during the scratch test of the bilayer and
multilayer coated samples is shown in Fig. 8 . For bilayer coated
samples, obvious acoustic signals can be detected at a load larger than
70 N, while there are no obvious acoustic signals before the load
reaches 200 N. Both acoustic signal measurements and microscopic
observations demonstrate that the adhesion strength between the
substrate/multilayer is higher than that between the substrate/bilayer.

4. Discussion
Fatigue tests demonstrate that application of Ti/TiN multilayered
ﬁlms (9 alternating layers, total thickness of 1.1 μm) on the Ti46Al8Nb
alloy can signi ﬁcantly improve the fatigue strength, whereas the
bilayered ﬁlms have no obvious effect. By using various analytical
techniques (such as nanoindentation, scratch test and stripping layer
substrate curvature test), it discloses that the multilayered ﬁlms have
higher hardness, residual stress and adhesion strength than bilayered
ﬁlms. The effects of these properties on the fatigue performance of the
Ti46Al8Nb alloy are discussed as follows.
4.1. Effect of the residual stress on fatigue performance
Similar to the shot-peening process, residual compressive stress
can be introduced into the ﬁlms by ion bombardment during
deposition [21]. Residual stress measurements by SLSCT indicate
that the calculated residual stress in multilayered ﬁlms is higher than
that in bilayered ﬁlms after application of ﬁlms on the Ti46Al8Nb alloy
(Fig. 5). Many authors demonstrate that compressive stress can retard
fatigue crack propagation by reducing the effective stress intensity
factor. The residual compressive stresses present in the ﬁlm were
found to increase the fatigue strength of the 316L stainless steel [3,4].
The crack initiation and propagation in multilayered ﬁlms can be
delayed because the residual stress present in the multilayered ﬁlms
is higher than that in the bilayered ﬁlms. Kolkman reported that the
compressive stress in the coating can be balanced by a residual tensile
stress in the substrate in order to maintain the equilibrium of forces
[10]. It was reported that fatigue crack initiation may occur in the
interface between ﬁlms and substrates owing to the steep stress
gradient where compressive stress changed to tensile stress [22].
Therefore, the bene ﬁcial effect of residual compressive stress in the
ﬁlm may be limited due to the balanced residual tensile stress in the
substrate.
4.2. Film hardness
It is well known that the hardness of the ﬁlm can increase with
decreasing modulated periods. Hardness measurements by nanoin-
dentation indicated that the hardness of the multilayered sample was
higher than that of the bilayered sample ( Fig. 6 ). This result can be
associated with one or more of the following strengthening mech-
anisms that operate in bulk materials:(a) Orowan-type strengthening
[23], resulting from a layer thickness that impedes the formation and
motion of dislocation loops within the layer, (b) image force strength-
ening [24], resulting from modulus differences at the interfaces
between the two materials, and (c) Hall –Petch type strengthening
[25], resulting from the resistance to dislocation transmission across
the interfaces within the ﬁlm.
It was reported in the literatures that the hard ﬁlm can suppress
slip deformation in the ductile substrate [5,8,26]. Subsequently,

delayed crack initiation can occur. As for brittle materials, Sastry [27]
reported that even if the material was tested at stress levels close to
the fracture stress, no well de ﬁned dislocation substructure develops
and no persistent slip band formation is found in this material at low
temperatures. Huang [28] also demonstrated that there were no
obvious large-scale persistent slip bands in intermetallic TiAl alloys
with only micron or sub-micron persistent slip bands formed ahead of
the fatigue crack tips. In this study, there was no obvious large-scale
slip band found on the surface of the TiAl substrate. Therefore, the
beneﬁcial effect of the hard ﬁlm on suppressing slip deformation is
limited for the brittle Ti46Al8Nb alloy and the “suppress slip
deformation” mechanism by the hard ﬁlm may not apply for brittle
materials.
Nevertheless, the hard ﬁlm can have another bene ﬁcial effect
(stress shielding mechanism) to reduce the effective stress amplitude
of the substrate close to the ﬁlm/substrate interface during fatigue
tests. In order to achieve this, the adhesion strength between the
multilayered ﬁlms and the substrate should be high enough so that no
cracking can occur at the ﬁlm/substrate interface. In addition, the
elastic modulus of the hard ﬁlm should also be higher than that of the
substrate [29]. As shown in Fig. 8 , the adhesion stress for the
multilayered sample was 200 N which was much higher than 90 N for
the bilayered sample, indicating that the multilayered ﬁlm has a
higher adhesion strength to the substrate. Since the elastic modulus of
both ﬁlms is higher than that of the substrate, it can be expected that
the fatigue strength of both coated samples can be improved. The
“stress shielding ” mechanism can explain the observed high fatigue
strength for the multilayer coated samples. It should be mentioned
that the bene ﬁcial stress shielding effect would be lost if fracture
occurs in the ﬁlms. Thus the stress shielding effect of the hard ﬁlm also
depends on ﬁlm resistance to cracking, which is discussed in the
following section.
4.3. Nanoscale layer structure
As demonstrated in Fig. 10 , the cracks initiated at the ﬁlm/
substrate interface de ﬂected and were arrested by the multilayer. On
the contrary, there was no crack to de ﬂect in the bilayered ﬁlms as
shown in Fig. 9. This result demonstrates that resistance to cracking of
the multilayered ﬁlms is higher than that of bilayered ﬁlms. Similar
results were found by Stoudt et al. [8]
who reported that the nanoscale
Cu–Ni multilayer can signi ﬁcantly increase the fatigue strength of Cu
samples. Several factors may be responsible for the investigated
results [8]. First, this multilayer is composed of layers of ductile
metals, thus it should retain suf ﬁcient toughness so that brittle
fracture should not occur easily. Second, the high density of interfaces
in the multilayer forms a barrier to slip that is not readily defeated by
the passing of dislocations thereby minimizing the likelihood of
localized cyclic softening. Third, the interfaces in the multilayer
should help to disperse slip near the surface thus creating more a
homogeneous change in morphology during fatigue.
As for the bilayer structure, the through cracks formed on the ﬁlms
(Fig. 9 ) can cause stress concentration which would have a
deleterious effect on the fatigue strength [30]. As a result, bilayered
ﬁlms have a less effect on the high fatigue strength even though it has
a relatively high residual compressive state and has a higher hardness
than the substrate.
In summary, the bene ﬁcial effect of multilayered ﬁlms on fatigue
performance is more likely that the multilayered ﬁlms can reduce the
cyclic stress amplitude of the substrate surface of brittle materials. As
a result, the crack initiation life of the substrate can be improved. To
achieve this high hardness and good adhesion properties are required.
In addition, the multilayered structure and residual compressive
stress can retard fatigue crack initiation and propagation that initiated
at the ﬁlm/substrate interface, which can delay the cracking of the
protective ﬁlms.
5. Conclusions
The effects of Ti/TiN bi- and multilayered ﬁlms on the fatigue
performance of the brittle Ti46Al8Nb alloy were investigated. Several
techniques have been used to characterize the ﬁlms. Based on
adhesion, hardness and residual stress measurements on both ﬁlms,
it can be concluded that the multilayered Ti/TiN ﬁlms can signiﬁcantly
enhance the fatigue strength of the Ti46Al8Nb alloy, whereas the
bilayered ﬁlms have no obvious effect. Compared with bilayered ﬁlms,
multilayered ﬁlms exhibit higher adhesion strength to the substrate,
higher hardness and higher residual compressive stress. It is therefore
proposed that for the brittle substrate, the main role of the
multilayered ﬁlms on fatigue performance is that it can reduce the
effective stress amplitude on the surface of the brittle substrate, i.e.,
stress shielding effect. In addition, the multilayered structure can
further increase fatigue strength by retarding fatigue crack initiation
and propagation as demonstrated by SEM observations.
