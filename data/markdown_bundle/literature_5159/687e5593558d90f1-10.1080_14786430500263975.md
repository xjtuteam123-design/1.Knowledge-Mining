Intermetallic alloys with B2 structure and dual-phase alloys of the B2 phase and
an icosahedral (i-) phase have been produced, and their mechanical properties
have been investigated. Vickers hardness measurements and compression
experiments have shown that the dual-phase alloys have considerably higher
strength than that of the B2 single-phase alloy up to a high temperature of
around 750 K. The dual-phase alloys have also exhibited much better ductility
than i-single-phase alloys. One possibility to be pointed out is that we can further
design a high-strength and good-ductility alloy by tuning the volume fraction,
size and morphology of the i-phase precipitates.
1. Introduction
In general, quasicrystals are very hard at room temperature; Al-transition metal
type quasicrystals, such as icosahedral (i-) Al–Pd–Mn, i-Al–Cu–Fe, decagonal (d-)
Al–Ni–Co and d-Al–Cu–Co exhibit Vickers hardness values of about 10 GPa [1, 2],
which is comparable to those of covalent crystals such as silicon. In spite of the high
hardness, quasicrystals by themselves are not suitable for applications as structural
materials due to their extreme brittleness. However, making use of their hardness,
applications as surface-coating materials [3, 4] and dispersion-strengthened materials
[5–9] with quasicrystalline particles embedded in more ductile material are
prospective. As for the latter, systems have been studied where i-phase particles
are embedded in the fcc-Al phase [5, 6] or in amorphous phases [7–9]. Some of
them are now close to commercialization.
On the other hand, Al-transition metal intermetallic compounds such as
NiAl, Ni
3Al, TiAl, Fe 3Al and FeAl oﬀer advantages for engineering uses due to a
high melting temperature, high oxidation and corrosion resistances, high strength-
to-weight ratio and relatively good ductility. Among them, the FeAl B2 phase is
known to show peculiar mechanical properties such as a yield strength very sensitive
to alloy composition and heat-treatment conditions [10–13]. The FeAl B2 phase

The Al–Cu–Fe system has a composition area where a B2 phase and the i-phase

In ﬁgure 3a and b, a scanning electron micrograph of the alloy 5 before

In ﬁgure 4, the results of the hardness measurements are presented, where open
circles indicate the alloys 1–4 after annealing, solid square indicates the alloy 5 before
annealing and solid circle indicates the alloy 5 after annealing. Here, the hardness
value for an i-Al–Cu–Fe single-phase alloy previously reported by Koester et al . [2]
is indicated by open square for comparison. All the data are plotted against the
Cu concentration. For the alloys 1–4, which consist exclusively of the B2 phase,
a rather gradual increase in the hardness with increasing Cu concentration is seen.
The increase in the hardness with increasing Cu concentration can be attributed to a
solid solution hardening by Cu and/or vacancy hardening due to increase in vacancy
concentration by the addition of Cu. The present result is consistent with the
compositional dependence of the friction coeﬃcient in the B2 phase reported by
Zhang et al. [17]. The two data for the alloy 5, which consist of the B2 and i phases,
are located at the positions higher than the curve. Comparing the hardness values
for the two dual-phase alloys, we ﬁnd that the hardness for the alloy containing more
i-phase is higher by about 1 GPa. We note here that the hardness values for the
dual-phase alloys are lower than that for the i single-phase alloy by about 2–3 GPa.

Figure 5 shows the temperature dependences of the yield stress for the two dual-
phase alloys and a B2 single-phase alloy. Here the data for a i-Al–Cu–Fe single-
phase alloy is reported by Giacometti et al. [18] are also plotted for comparison. For
the B2 single-phase alloy, the yield stress value at 700 K is about 0.25 GPa.
It increases with decreasing temperature and reaches 0.75 GPa at 400 K. At about
600 K, a hump is observed in the curve. This type of anomaly in the temperature
dependence of the yield stress has often been observed for the B2 phase and the
mechanism for it has been discussed in relation with vacancy hardening [13–15].
For the two dual-phase alloys of B2 and i, more rapid increase in the yield stress
with decreasing temperature is observed than for the B2 single-phase alloy. For the
alloy 5 after annealing, the yield stress could not be measured due to fracture at
temperatures below 630 K while the alloy 5 before annealing exhibited yielding
without fracture at temperatures down to 400 K. The yield stress for this alloy
reaches 1.4 GPa at 400 K. At present it is unclear as to why the annealing enhanced
the brittleness for the alloy 5. More detailed observation of the i-phase precipitation
by transmission electron microscopy would be needed to clarify it. Anyway, ﬁgure 5
shows that the B2 phase was strengthened considerably by the i-phase precipitation
up to a high temperature of about 750 K. The yield stress for the i single-phase alloy
is much higher than those for the dual-phase alloys. However, the former is much
more brittle than the latter; for the former the yield stress has not been obtained
due to fracture at temperatures below 750 K.
As shown in ﬁgures 4 and 5, the dual-phase alloys exhibit higher strength than
the B2 single-phase alloys and better ductility than the i single-phase alloy.
