Abstract 
The microstructure and mechanical properties of an Fe-Al alloy containing 16 at.% Al and additions of small quantities of other 
elements have been examined. The alloying additions ensure that high strength with limited, but reasonable, ductility can be obtained 
for test temperatures from ambient to above 500 “C. Despite the low Al level, this alloy is found to show a two-phase 
disordered + ordered structure below about 550 “C, and it is the age hardening that occurs as the fine DO,-ordered particles form that 
is responsible for the high strengths found. 
Keywords: Iron; Aluminium; Alloys 
1. Introduction 
Iron aluminides containing about 25 at.% Al have 
the DO,-ordered structure in equilibrium at tempera- 
tures below about 550 “C [1,2], and are currently 
receiving renewed attention because potential 
materials for applications where good strength and oxi- 
dation resistance at high temperatures are required [3]. 
Their good mechanical properties are significantly 
impaired, however, by a problem of environmental 
sensitivity, which leads to a loss of tensile ductility [4]. 
The development of alloys of lower Al content, con- 
taining sufficient Al to ensure good oxidation resist- 
ance but without producing long-range ordering, is a 
potential solution to this problem [5-81. It has been 
shown, for example [7,8], that good tensile ductility and 
reasonable strength can be obtained in alloys contain- 
ing about 16% Al (throughout this report, alloying 
additions will be quoted as atomic percentages) and 
that such alloys are essentially insensitive to the testing 
environment. 
The alloys described above [7,8] have reasonable 
strength at room temperature (yield stress of about 
500 MPa) but lose their strength at temperatures 
above 200 “C (for example, a yield stress below 400 
MPa at 200 “C) and are unlikely to be satisfactory for 
many engineering applications. It is for this reason that 
an alloy development programme has been carried out 
in an attempt to improve the strength, both at room 
temperature as well as at high temperature, and also to 
improve the oxidation and erosion resistance of the 
materials examined. The alloys considered are based 
on the binary composition Fe-16% Al, in order to 
ensure that ordering to the DO, state with its asso- 
ciated environmental sensitivity is avoided [ 1,2]. The 
alloys contain additions of elements such as Si and Nb 
to improve the strength and oxidation and erosion 
resistance, and additions of Ti and B to produce stable 
dispersoids for grain boundary pinning to retain a fine 
grain size during processing. This report describes one 
such promising alloy that has been developed by ABB 
[91.

3. Results 
3.1. Cast and rolled microstructures 
The cast material showed a grain size of about 
200-500 ,um, elongated along the solidification direc- 
tion, with a solidification cell size of the order of 30 pm 
(see Fig. 1). The cell walls were decorated with 
particles about 2-5 pm in size, which were generally 
rather blocky in morphology, and were identified by 
energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) using a window- 
less detector as containing essentially only Ti and B. In 
addition, the cell interiors contained uniformly dis- 
persed particles about 0.5 pm in size and 5-10 pm 
apart, which were shown by EDS to be rich in Nb. 
Neither type of particle was obviously affected in size 
or morphology by any of the heat treatments given. A 
longitudinal section through the rolled material is also 
shown in Fig. 1, where little change in the microstruc- 
ture is seen, except for an elongation of the grains along 
the rolling direction. 
Preliminary annealing studies on this rolled material 
showed that recrystallization occurred on heating to 
1000 “C for 1 h, and that grain growth at higher tem- 
peratures was severely limited by the stable dispersoid 
particles present. It is for this reason that the standard 
recrystallization heat treatment of 1 h at 1000 “C, fol- 
lowed by air cooling, was adopted. Table 2 shows the 
grain sizes obtained after a series of heat treatments at 
temperatures in the range 1000-1300 “C, as well as the 
hardness of the resulting materials. Both the grain size 
and hardness evolve only slowly after the initial re- 
crystallization stage. The observation of a significant 
grain size reduction from that of the rolled state to that 
of the recrystallized states implies that significant strain 
energy is retained after the warm working process.

3.3. Microstructural studies 
Detailed studies of the microstructure and of the dis- 
location structures produced by deformation have 
been carried out by TEM to understand the mechani- 
cal behaviour of the materials examined here. A few 
preliminary studies were carried out by DSC and by 
X-ray diffraction, and the results of these will be 
described first. 
DSC studies were carried out by cycling a small 
sample of the alloy between room temperature and 
700 “C at a rate of 10 “C min- l. Two complete cycles 
were carried out, each of which showed essentially the 
same behaviour. A weak endothermic peak was seen 
during the heating-up period at a temperature of about 
550-560 “C, with a much stronger peak near 630 “C. A 
study conducted using a magnetic balance confirmed 
that the peak near 630 “C was due to the loss of 
ferromagnetism. Based on the earlier binary Fe-Al 
equilibrium data [ 1,2], extrapolated to an Al content of 
about 17%, there should be no DO, or B2 order at any 
temperature, and no change is expected at tempera- 
tures near 550-560 “C. In contrast the Curie tempera- 
ture is expected to lie somewhere near 650-700 “C, in 
reasonable agreement with the experimental observa- 
tion. 
X-ray diffraction examinations of the air-cooled cast 
or rolled materials showed essentially no diffraction 
intensities near the positions of superlattice peaks for 
the B2 or DO, structures. Annealing for 24 h near 
400-500 “C showed very weak and broad peaks at 
positions corresponding to DO, order (for example, 
111 and 200 peaks). (Throughout this report, the inde- 
xes are given in relation to the DO, lattice, unless spe- 
cific mention is made.) The peak intensity was so low 
and the peaks so broad and poorly defined that it was 
difficult to define a long-range order parameter. In fact, 
it is difficult to determine whether the broad intensities 
observed actually correspond to long-range or to 
short-range order. The value of a long-range order 
parameter is estimated as being below 0.1.

as well as the dislocations corresponding to deforma-

was expected from the binary Fe-Al phase diagram [2] 
that an alloy with about 17% Al - or even one with an 
effective Al content less than about 20% - should 
show no such long-range order. A quantitative analysis 
of the dark-field TEM images of materials annealed at 
temperatures of 500 “C and below shows that approxi- 
mately 25%-40% by volume of the material is present 
as DO,-ordered phase, lying in a matrix which is 
apparently disordered b.c.c. phase. The present study 
shows clearly that, at temperatures above about 
600 “C, this DO, order slowly disappears and, hence, a 
temperature of approximately 550 “C is proposed as 
the limiting temperature for the DO, phase in this 
material. Such order is present in the initial cast and 
rolled materials, since it forms during the sufficiently 
slow cooling after the final heat treatments are given. 
The reason why&is material shows such order is 
not clear. Two possibilities can be proposed: (1) that 
the binary Fe-Al phase diagram is not sufficiently 
defined in the temperature and composition range of 
relevance, or (2) that the alloying additions in the 
matrix (5% Cr, 1% Si and 0.4% Nb) sufficiently modify 
this phase diagram. It is not expected that the 5% Cr 
will be of relevance to the present discussion, since 
such amounts of this element are known to modify the 
critical temperature for DO, ordering of alloys con- 
taining 25%-28% Al by only a few degrees for each 
per cent of Cr [12]. Furthermore, it seems unreason- 
able to expect a noticeable effect on the composition 
range where the two-phase b.c.c. + DO, state is found. 
However, Si and Nb are both strong stabilizers of the 
DO, state and this may be expected to mean both an 
increase in the critical temperature for retaining DO, 
order near the 25% Al composition as well as displac- 
ing the region where DO, order appears to increase the 
temperature and lower the Al content in the 15-20% 
Al range.

state can be set to 42 “C higher [ 131 and 56 “C higher 
[ 141 with additions of 1% Si and Nb respectively.

the expected effect of 1% Si and 0.4% Nb in solution 
on modifying the temperature-composition range 
where DO, ordering will be found as follows: based on 
the effect of 1% solute additions on changing the criti- 
cal temperature for ordering, we can expect 1% Si and 
0.4% Nb to raise this critical temperature by about 
64 “C. In the composition range around 20-22% Al, 
the critical temperature varies by about 100 “C for 
each per cent Al; therefore, the addition of the Si and 
Nb solute should produce a similar effect for about the 
same amount, or less, of Al addition. According to this 
argument, the present alloy matrix will have an Al 
equivalent composition of about 18% and no DO, 
order should be expected during the present experi- 
ments. An alternative argument can note that [ 13,141 Si

[13], by about 14.5 “C for each per cent Al; hence, the 
effect of 1% Si and 0.4% Nb may be taken as equiva- 
lent to an Al addition of 4.4% Al. The present alloy 
matrix will then have an Al equivalent composition of

5. Conclusions 
The microstructure and properties of an Fe-Al alloy 
containing about 16% Al, designed as a disordered 
(b.c.c.) alloy and to show good strength and ductility, 
without suffering from the environmental cracking 
sensitivity of the DO,-ordered alloys containing 
25%-28% Al, have been examined in detail. Additions 
of Ti and B lead to the formation of stable dispersoid 
particles which retain a fine grain size during heat treat- 
ments after recrystallization, while Si and Nb remain in 
solution in the matrix. 
The present alloy is much stronger than a previous 
similar alloy developed at OBNL, but with reduced 
ductility. 
At annealing temperatures below about 550 “C, a 
DO,-ordered phase precipitates out and coarsens 
slowly during periods of hours and days at about 
400-500 “C. Such order precipitation leads to signifi- 
cant age hardening. 
The appearance of a DO,-ordered phase in the 
present alloy is attributed to the Si and Nb in solution, 
which modify the phase diagram significantly, allowing 
the formation of this order. It is clear that the ordered 
state and mechanical properties of such Fe-Al alloys 
depend sensitively on the precise alloying additions 
present. 
The strengthening caused by the ordered particles 
may be a logical contribution to the reduction of the 
overall ductility of the material. It is not clear whether 
the reduced environmental sensitivity reported for dis- 
ordered binary Fe-Al alloys is maintained in the 
present, low Al content material, or whether the pres- 
ence of some order will induce such sensitivity. Further 
work is required to examine this aspect.
