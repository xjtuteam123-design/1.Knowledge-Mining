For a replacement of nickel based superalloys by two-phase
titanium aluminide alloys as light weight materials in some
high temperature aero-engine applications, the mechanical
properties of the titanium aluminides have to be assessed
against the high standards set by superalloys. At an intended
service temperature of 700
◦C the strength and creep resis-
tance of the titanium aluminides are still inferior to those of
superalloys. In an attempt to overcome these problems several
studies have been made on titanium aluminides which have
been subjected to precipitation hardening treatments.
Of the doping elements investigated, carbon seems to be
the most promising [1–4]. Precipitation hardening by carbon
has been characterized by mechanical testing and transmis-
sion electron microscopy (TEM) observations [4]. Carbon
rich precipitates of perovskite type form strong glide obsta-
cles at which dislocations are effectively pinned. Thermal
stability of the precipitates is important for application of the

1 Experimental
The investigated alloy was Ti-48 .5a t. % Al-0.37 at. %C ,
which was supplied by Krupp Corp., Germany. In order to
improve structural and chemical homogeneity, the ingot was
subjected to hot isostatic pressing for 4 h at 1185
◦Ca n d
140 MPa and then isothermally forged at 1000 ◦C. A sample
of dimensions 3 × 15 × 15 mm3 was cut from the forged bil-
let. The sample was homogenized for 4 hours at 1250◦C, fol-
lowed by quenching in oil, and subsequently aged at 750 ◦C
for 24 hours and 7 days. TEM examination and diffraction
analysis revealed perovskite-type precipitates with L1
2 crys-
tal structure and a needle-like shape with an aspect ratio of
about 7 after 24 h at 750 ◦C[ 4 ] .
SANS measurements were carried out with the SANS-2
instrument at the Geesthacht Neutron Facility (GeNF). Only
one sample was used which was measured after the different
heat treatments to avoid any inﬂuence due to sample prepar-
ation. Selector-monochromated neutrons with a mean wave-
length of λ = 0.57 nm and a wavelength spread of ∆λ/λ =
20% were used. No anisotropy was observed in the SANS
signal that was recorded with an area detector, therefore the
intensity was azimuthally averaged. Macroscopic differential
scattering cross sections were obtained by calibration against
a Vanadium standard.

2 Results and discussion
The scattering of the sample in the as-quenched state results
from incoherent scattering (mainly of titanium) at large scat-
tering vectors q (isotropic background), and from scattering
at large inhomogeneities, such as grains of α2 phase, oxides
or nitrides, at small q (Fig. 1). During annealing at 750 ◦C,
the only change in the microstructure should be the formation
of perovskite precipitates throughout the matrix, leading to an
additional scattering contribution (Fig. 1).
For an analysis of the scattering curves of the annealed
states, ﬁrst the isotropic scattering contribution, as observed
at largeq, was subtracted from the measured scattering curves
and then the difference between the scattering curve of the an-
nealed sample and the scattering curve of the sample in the
as-quenched state was calculated (Fig. 2). The resulting error
bars after these subtractions are plotted in Fig. 2, however,
they are smaller than the symbol size, the largest relative error
at high q is about 15%. Fitting calculated scattering curves

A quantitative correlation of the scattering data and the
change in yield strength after overageing is not well founded,
because no information about the strength of the coarsened
glide obstacles could be deduced from the present study.
However, it might be speculated that a reduction of the glide
resistance due to the particle coarsening is partly compen-
sated for by an increase of the obstacle strength so that the
total effect on the yield strength is relatively small, thereby
indicating that suitable particle dispersions can be maintained
over relatively long service times.
