Abstract 
A numerical model to predict mechanical properties of Ni-base superalloys for turbine disk 
applications is developed by modifying and combinin g previous models that consider various 
microstructural factors. Modifications are made so that as many necessary model parameters as 
possible, whose values have been obtained from mech anical measurements and microstructural 
observation, can be determined using a thermodynami c calculation and a precipitation kinetics 
simulation. The model’s predictions of tensile prop erties, creep resistance and fatigue damage 
tolerance have good correlation with experimental d ata. The applicability and limits of the present 
model as an alloy design tool for new Ni-base disk superalloys are discussed in this paper.

1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys have good mechanical properties at temperatures 650 ≤ T ≤ 850 °C and 
therefore are used for turbine disk applications in aero-engines (Locq and Caron 2011; Zhou et al., 
2011; Crudden et al., 2013; Evans and Saxana, 2013) . When new Ni-base superalloys are being 
developed for turbine disk applications, several me chanical properties should be considered (e.g., 
such as tensile properties, creep resistance, and f atigue damage tolerance). To improve these 
properties, various alloying elements (e.g., B, C, transition metals) have been added to Ni-Al alloys 
(Crudden et al., 2013; Reed et al., 2016). However, quantification of these mechanical properties takes 
time; an alternative approach is to use a computati onal technique that predicts these mechanical 
properties before the superalloys are fabricated. 
All existing models of these mechanical properties have some limitations. Microstructure-based 
physical models based on continuum mechanics can predict mechanical properties in functional forms 
of not only stress and temperature but also microst ructural factors (Sabnis et al., 2013; Le Graverend 
et al., 2014; Ghorbanpour et al., 2017; Barba et al ., 2018). These models are practically suitable for 
analyses of relevant experimental properties, but r equire that some model parameter values be 
optimized experimentally, so application to new all oys is limited if the necessary experimental 
information is not available for those new alloys. Mechanical properties can be also predicted using 
material informatics approaches such as statistics and machine learning (Hou et al., 2010; Broderick 
et al., 2016; Conduit et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2 018), but these methods cannot readily represent 
changes in mechanical properties that occur when va rious microstructural factors change. Empirical 
models can consider a variety of microstructural fa ctors, and use experimental data to predict 
mechanical properties (Kozar et al., 2009; Osada et al., 2013; Ahmadi et al., 2014; Galindo-Nava et al., 
2015; Kassner, 2015). If model parameters in empiri cal models can be determined not from 
experimental data but from computational techniques , mechanical properties can be predicted before 
manufacturing new alloys. 
In fact, several authors have attempted to solve this problem. Collins and Stone (2014) reported that 
yield strength, /g=0=−/g<0=−, could be controlled from the size and fraction of γ′ phases, which were calculated 
using a precipitation kinetics simulation program, PrecipiCalc (Jou et al., 2004). Similarly, Reed et al. 
(2016) showed that changes in /g=0=−/g<0=− and creep resistance have been predicted using emp irical models 
that consider the antiphase boundary (APB) energy, fraction of γ′ phases, and diffusivity, which were 
obtained using thermodynamic calculation. However, these methods (Collins and Stone, 2014; Reed 
et al., 2016) are insufficient to estimate /g=0=−/g<0=− and creep resistance, because these properties are affected 
by many factors in addition to APB energy, fraction of γ′ phases, and diffusivity (Kozar et al., 2009; 
Osada et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2016). Jmatpro software (Jmatpro) overcomes these shortcomings; i t

model, and Section 5 concludes the paper. 
 
2. Experimental procedure 
The seven designed alloys (Table 1) were used to ev aluate the validity and applicability of the 
present model. These alloys were fabricated using argon gas atomization, powder sieving (-270 mesh), 
and hot isostatic pressing at sub-solvus temperatur es and 103 MPa for 4 h. The heat treatment 
procedure entailed sub-solvus solution annealing fo r 1 h, salt quenching or air cooling, and two-step 
aging heat treatments at 870 °C for 1 h, air cooling, 650 °C for 24 h, then air cooling. 
For scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-42 00) investigations, alloys that had been salt 
quenched and aged were electro-etched at a voltage of 6 V DC in a reagent composed of 5 ml HClO 4 
in 95 ml of ethanol, chemically etched in a reagent composed of 5 g CuCl 2 in 100 ml of HCl and 
100 ml of methanol at room temperature. For transmi ssion electron microscope (TEM, Jeol-2100F 
HR) observations, discs of 3-mm diameter were punch ed out of the aged samples. Thin foils were 
prepared by electrochemical polishing using a twin- jet polisher and a solution of 90% acetic acid and 
10% perchloric acid at 60 V and room temperature. A dditional analyses for microstructural 
observations were performed using an electron probe microanalyser (EPMA, JXA-8100) and an 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS).

3. Numerical modeling and simulation 
Turbine disks operate at 400 ≤ T ≤850 °C, and require high strength, long service lif e and high 
fatigue damage tolerance due to the increase in the risk of burst during longtime rotation (Locq and 
Caron 2011; Zhou et al., 2011; Crudden et al., 2013 ; Evans and Saxana, 2013). Ni-base superalloys, 
the main material for disk applications, have multi -modal γ′ size distributions in the γ′ fraction range 
of 30 ~ 60 %, and undergo deformation by several me chanisms (Chang et al., 1984; Ducrocq et al., 
1988; Krueger et al., 1990; Jain et al., 2000; Gabb et al., 2002; Hardy et al., 2004; Guédou et al., 
2008). 
Here we develop a numerical model to predict tensil e properties, creep resistance and fatigue 
damage tolerance for Ni-base disk superalloys, for a grain size that can be elaborately controlled 
during the fabrication process and thus is provided as input data. The present model was achieved by 
combining previous empirical models with modificati on. While developing the model we tried to 
consider as many microstructural factors as possibl e based on deformation mechanisms. Once the 
alloy composition and the heat treatment procedure are given, all model parameters except the grain 
size are computed. In this section, we explain the development of the model.

3.2. Creep model 
Ni-base disk superalloys should have a long service life under continuous pressure at 650 ≤ T ≤

dislocation creep, the Orowan or shearing mechanism (microtwinning and isolated faulting) can occur

mean size of γ′ precipitates increase simultaneously, the Orowan m echanism occurs. When the inter-
particle spacing of γ′ precipitates narrows, the shearing mechanism occur s. The interaction between a 
dislocation and a γ′ precipitate is called a back stress /g=0=−
/g<0+<; it is highly dependent on creep mechanisms. 
An existing creep model that considers the differen ce in the back stress caused by these mechanisms

3.3. Fatigue damage tolerance model 
Ni-base superalloys for turbine disk applications s hould not burst easily (Gabb et al., 2002; 
Kitaguchi et al., 2013). Disk superalloys rotate an d stop repeatedly, so fatigue damage tolerance 
should be high (Ducrocq et al., 1988; Chang et al., 1996; Kearsey et al., 2000; Kitaguchi et al., 2013). 
The fatigue damage tolerance is evaluated by examin ing fatigue crack growth rate. However, fatigue 
crack growth rate cannot be readily obtained using computational techniques, if relevant experimental 
data are not provided. In the present study, we established an alternative model to evaluate the fatigue 
crack growth rate. During propagation of fatigue cr acks at 650 ≤ T ≤ 850 °C, intergranular fracture 
generally occurs (Sadananda and Shahinian, 1980; Carpenter et al., 1997; Hardy et al., 2004; Kearsey 
et al., 2000; Xiao et al., 2005; Kitaguchi et al., 2013; Li et al., 2015; Németh et al., 2017). Hence, the 
main purpose of the present fatigue model is to eva luate the resistance to intergranular fracture unde r 
the fatigue environment, instead of estimating the fatigue crack growth rate. 
Intergranular fracture is affected by grain size (T elesman et al., 2004; Gao et al., 2005), grain 
boundary strengthening (Gao et al., 2005), stress-a ssisted grain boundary oxidation (Sadananda and 
Shahinian, 1980; Gao et al., 1995; Gao and Wei., 19 97; Wei and Huang, 2002), and the stress-
relaxation effect (Telesman et al., 2008). The effe cts of these factors on intergranular fracture depe nd 
on the condition of the fatigue test. Fatigue crack growth can be tested without dwell time, or with i t 
(Hardy et al., 2004; Gao et al., 2005; Telesman et al., 2008; Li et al., 2015). In both types of test, 
fatigue damage tolerance increases as grain size increases (Telesman et al., 2004; Gao et al., 2005).

3.4. Summary for numerical modeling and simulation 
The present numerical model calculates tensile prop erties, creep resistance, and fatigue damage 
tolerance by using computational techniques. In mos t previous numerical models, the model 
parameters had to be obtained by fitting relevant e xperimental data or from new experimental 
measurements. Since it was not easy to determine mo del parameter values for a wide range of alloy 
systems, most of the previous models showed limitat ions when being utilized for alloy design of 
unknown alloy compositions. In contrast, in our mod el all parameters are calculated from 
computational techniques or from relevant equations established from already available experimental 
data for a wide range of commercial alloys and the present alloys. Accordingly, no further 
experimental measurements are required to estimate mechanical properties of new alloy compositions. 
This is the biggest difference between our model and previous ones. 
Our model has two limitations: First, it does not u se a computational approach to estimate grain 
size, which is highly dependent on process conditio ns such as powder size, hot isostatic pressing 
temperature and solutioning temperature (Jackson an d Reed, 1999; Locq et al., 2000; Huron et al., 
2004; Qiu et al., 2013). To our best knowledge, no existing model estimates grain size from a wide 
range of process conditions with an accuracy level that is suitable for a practical alloy design. 
Therefore, grain size is left as an input parameter or a target value when predicting mechanical

during deformation, and therefore should be applied only at 650 ≤ T ≤ 850 °C, in which 
microstructural changes are relatively minor. Furth ermore, tensile properties are calculated only at

information that was collected at that temperature. 
 
4. Calculation of mechanical properties 
In this section, we discuss the reliability and app licability of our model by comparing the 
calculations and experimental information for the seven designed alloys (Table 1) and the commercial 
alloys (Table 3). All model parameters except grain size are obtained using a thermodynamics 
calculation and a precipitation kinetics simulation, so the numerical model is highly dependent on the 
CALPHAD thermodynamic calculation and the precipita tion kinetics simulation. The 
thermodynamics software used in this study were The rmo-Calc (database: Ni-DATA (ver.6) (Thermo 
Calc, Saunders et al., 2000)) and Jmatpro (Jmatpro) ; the precipitation kinetics simulation software 
was MatCalc (thermodynamic database: Ni-DATA (ver.6 ) & diffusion database: MC_Ni ver.2.004) 
(Saunders et al., 2000; MC_Ni ver.2.004; Kozeschnik et al., 2004; Svoboda et al., 2004; Radis et al., 
2009). The reliability of the thermodynamics calculation has been demonstrated (Kim et al., 2016). In 
the present study, the reliability of the precipita tion kinetics simulation was evaluated by comparing 
the predictions of the size and fraction of γ′ precipitates to relevant experimental observations. 
In the seven new alloys considered in the present w ork (Table 1), the average grain size was 4 ~ 
5 µm (Fig. 8). Analyses using TEM, EPMA, and EDS detec ted Cr-rich M 23 C6 carbides, Ti-rich MC 
carbides, borides, and primary γ′ precipitates (1 ~ 2 µm) at the grain boundaries of individual alloys, 
but did not detect topologically close-packed (TCP) phases such as σ and µ. Inside the grains, 
secondary (300 ~ 500 nm) and tertiary (40 ~ 100 nm) γ′ precipitates were commonly observed, but η 
phases based on the Ni 3Ti crystal structure were observed only in Alloys 1 and 3. 
The first area examined to confirm the reliability of the precipitation kinetics simulation was the 
size and fraction of secondary γ′ precipitates (Fig. 9). In the MatCalc simulation, the dislocation 
density was 10 11 m-2, the number of size classes was 250, and the nucleation site was bulk considering 
the homogeneous nucleation. The interfacial energy was estimated from the generalized broken bond 
(GBB) approach while taking into account interfacia l curvature size effects (Sonderegger and 
Kozeschnik, 2009; 2009). The precipitate growth kin etics was simulated with the SFFK mean-field 
model (Kozeschnik et al., 2004; Svoboda et al., 200 4) for multi-component multi-phase systems. In 
addition, the volume misfit was ignored due to the very small volume misfit between γ and γ′ . The 
precipitation kinetics simulation reproduced the size and fraction of secondary γ′ precipitates well.

5. Conclusion 
A numerical model was developed to predict tensile properties, creep resistance, and fatigue 
damage tolerance in Ni-base disk superalloys. Simul ation results agree reasonably with experimental 
data. 
The present model does not consider the microstruct ural changes during deformation process, and 
is therefore only applicable at 650 ≤ T ≤ 850 °C, in which microstructural changes are relatively mild. 
Furthermore, the prediction of tensile properties can be performed only at 650 °C. 
The advantage of the present model is that it uses computational approaches to obtain the material-
dependent model parameters, and does not require fu rther experiments to obtain them. Although the 
model still has limitations, it can be used to guid e design of new Ni-base disk superalloys to improve 
their mechanical properties.

Title: A numerical model to predict mechanical properties for Ni-base disk superalloys 
 
Authors: Young-Kwang Kim, Donghoon Kim, Hong-Kyu Kim, Eun-Yoo Yoon, Youngseon Lee, Chang-
Seok Oh and Byeong-Joo Lee 
 
Highlights 
 
/circle6 We propose a numerical model to predict tensile properties, creep resistance and fatigue damage 
tolerance. 
/circle6 The model has been achieved by combining earlier empirical models with modifications. 
/circle6 The model considers various microstructural factors based on deformation mechanisms. 
/circle6 The model enables to predict mechanical properties without any further experimental 
measurement due to calculating all model parameters from computational approaches.
