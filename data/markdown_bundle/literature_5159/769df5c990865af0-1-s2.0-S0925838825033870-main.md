ABSTRACT
The high-temperature strengthening mechanism of the L1
2 
phase in AlCoCrFeMo
0.05
Ni ₂ high-entropy alloy (HEA) 
remains unclear. This study investigates the high-temperature mechanical response of the L1
2 
phase in this HEA 
using atomistic simulations. A 2 × 2 × 2 supercell was used to represent L1
2
-structured regions in the quinary 
HEA, circumventing the limitations of modeling it directly within a 4-atom unit cell. The ordered (OM) and 
disordered (DM) models, with similar compositions, were compared under high-temperature deformation. The 
results revealed stress-induced phase decomposition in the OM, consistent with experimental observations in the 
alloy. The DM, conversely, exhibited superior phase stability. Furthermore, the OM exhibited significantly higher 
strength and enhanced ductility compared to the DM. Specifically, the OM showed a significant increase in the 
length of Shockley partial dislocations and stair-rod dislocations. Additionally, the OM exhibited a reduced 
dislocation density during tensile deformation. These observations collectively demonstrate that the OM pos -
sesses lower resistance to dislocation expansion and a greater propensity for cross-slip and dynamic recovery. 
This resulted in a lower work-hardening rate and improved uniform elongation, ductility, and toughness in the 
OM.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have garnered significant attention as a 
frontier research area in advanced materials, owing to their innovative 
compositional design strategies and exceptional mechanical properties 
[1 – 3] . Ordered structures significantly influence alloy phase stability 
and performance. This is particularly evident in L1
2
-type strengthening 
[4 – 7] . Tian et al. [4] enhanced the performance of an FCC HEA through 
nanoscale L1
2 
precipitation. This resulted in a 57.4 % increase in dy -
namic compression yield strength. Importantly, ductility and strain 
hardening were not compromised. The L1
2 
phase plays a crucial role. It 
effectively pins dislocations, hindering their movement. This leads to 
increased cross-slip and activation of multiple slip systems. Conse -
quently, high-strength and high-toughness alloys can be developed for 
dynamic applications. Charkhchian et al. [5] observed that L1
2 
nano -
precipitates in the AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 
eutectic high-entropy alloy form via 
solid-state phase transformation within the FCC matrix, rather than 
direct solidification. While these L1
2 
nanoprecipitates enhance the 
alloy ’ s strength, their specific impact on fracture toughness warrants 
further investigation to fully understand their behavior under complex 
stress states. Hamdi et al. [6] highlighted the work-hardening capabil -
ities of high-entropy alloys during low-temperature deformation. They 
elucidated the contributions of various mechanisms, including 
deformation-induced martensitic transformation, deformation twin -
ning, and stacking fault formation, to the synergistic enhancement of 
both strength and plasticity. Furthermore, the generation of geometri -
cally necessary dislocations (GNDs) significantly influences the defor -
mation behavior of these materials at cryogenic temperatures.

However, the underlying microscopic mechanisms governing these 
mechanical behaviors remain to be fully elucidated. The matrix of this 
alloy is characterized by an L1
2
-type ordered phase. However, Con -
ventional theoretical models treat HEAs as disordered solid solutions. 
They employ random atomic substitution to populate fixed-size unit 
cells [12 – 14] . This approach fails to capture the characteristic features 
of the ordered structure, thus leading to deviations in the mechanistic 
analysis. Furthermore, the classical L1
2 
structure [15 – 17] (with a 
4-atom unit cell) presents challenges in accurately representing the 
actual compositions of multi-component HEAs containing five or more 
principal elements, thus posing a significant challenge in constructing 
accurate atomic models.
This study introduces an ordered model construction strategy to 
overcome limitations inherent in traditional disordered alloy models. 
The approach entails generating and evaluating a set of 10,000 random 
atomic configurations within a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell. The configuration 
exhibiting the lowest energy is then selected as the minimal repeating 
unit for constructing the L1
2 
ordered phase model (OM). In contrast to 
disordered model (DM), this OM captures stress-induced phase decom -
position, a phenomenon corroborated by experimental evidence. A key 
finding is the elucidation of a dual strengthening mechanism that gov -
erns high-temperature mechanical behavior. Specifically, the OM en -
hances both strength, via lattice distortion and chemical bond 
strengthening, and plasticity, through unique dislocation slip paths and 
interfacial coordinated deformation. These results provide a funda -
mental theoretical framework for the development of HEAs with 
improved high-temperature performance and enhanced ductility.
2. Methods
The compositions and proportions of the FCC (L1
2
) phase used in the 
modeling are listed in Table 1 . The phase compositions were determined 
using Thermocalc thermodynamic calculations, with an equilibrium 
temperature set at 1473.15 K. The TCHEA4.2 database was employed 
for the calculations and has been reported in the literature to be reliable 
[18 – 20] . Regarding structural selection, since the conventional 4-atom 
L1
2 
ordered structure cannot achieve the desired composition ratios 
through atomic substitution, a 2 × 2 × 2 minimum repeating unit 
containing 32 atoms was adopted in this study. Considering the 
extremely low content of Mo in the L1
2 
phase, its influence on the phase 
structure can be neglected during the modeling process.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the 
LAMMPS package [21] . The interatomic interactions in the 
multi-component alloy were described using the embedded atom 
method (EAM) potential. The potential parameters were obtained from 
the FeNiCrCoAl.eam.alloy file [22] , which covers the interactions of Fe, 
Co, Cr, Ni, and Al elements. A neighbor list cutoff radius of 2.0 Å was 
used and updated every 10 steps to balance computational efficiency 
and accuracy.
To eliminate unreasonable initial atomic overlaps, energy minimi -
zation was performed using the conjugate gradient algorithm. The 
convergence criteria were set to an energy tolerance and a force toler -
ance of 1 × 10 ⁻ ¹ ⁵ eV, with a maximum of 10,000 iterations. During the 
relaxation process, the simulation box was allowed to expand freely in 
the x, y, and z directions until the system reached a global energy 
minimum. The temperature in the tensile simulations was set to 
973.15 K. Initial atomic velocities were generated using a Gaussian 
distribution corresponding to a temperature of 0.1 K, ensuring low 
initial thermal perturbation. The system was heated from 0.1 K to 
973.15 K under the NPT ensemble, using a Nose-Hoover thermostat for 
temperature control and an isotropic pressure control of 0 bar. This stage 
ran for 10 ps to achieve smooth heating. The system was further relaxed 
at 973.15 K under the NPT ensemble for 50 ps until the standard devi -
ation of the system ’ s energy fluctuation was less than 0.1 eV/atom, 
ensuring a thermodynamic equilibrium state was reached. Uniaxial 
tensile deformation was applied along the y-direction, with a simulated 
strain rate of 1 × 10 ⁹ s ⁻ ¹ (corresponding to 0.001 ps ⁻ ¹ in LAMMPS in -
ternal units). A constant strain rate was imposed using the fix deform 
command, while maintaining the pressure at 0 GPa in the x and z di -
rections. To maintain a constant temperature of 973.15 K, the Nose- 
Hoover thermostat (damping coefficient of 0.001 ps) was employed. 
The total simulated strain was 30 %, and atomic trajectories were output 
every 1 ps. The engineering strain in the y-direction and the true stress 
were recorded in real-time using the virial stress formula. The Common 
Neighbor Analysis (CNA) module within the Ovito software [23] was 
used to identify FCC, BCC, HCP, and amorphous atoms, with a neigh -
borhood cutoff radius of 3.5 Å. The Dislocation Extraction Algorithm 
(DXA) was used to track Burgers vectors and dislocation line densities, 
allowing for the statistical analysis of dislocation motion and multipli -
cation behavior.
An AlCoCrFeMo
0.05
Ni
2 
experimental ingot with a weight of 5 kg was 
prepared using vacuum arc melting. From this ingot, specimens with 
dimensions of 10 mm × 0.5 mm × 10 mm were sectioned using wire 
electrical discharge machining. These specimens were then mechani -
cally ground and subsequently electropolished using a twin-jet electro -
polishing technique to prepare TEM samples. A 6 % perchloric acid 
alcohol solution served as the electrolyte. The electrolytic temperature 
was maintained at 258.15 K, and a constant current density of 90 mA 
was applied. TEM observations were performed using a JEOL JEM- 
2100F TEM.

The stress field exerts a dual influence on the decomposition kinetics. 
Thermodynamically, the stress gradient-induced equivalent chemical 
potential drives the directional migration of Al atoms along dislocation 
networks, reducing the characteristic decomposition time. Kinetically, 
the rapid diffusion pathways provided by dislocation cell walls and sub- 
grain boundaries enhance the elemental diffusion coefficient. These 
findings provide a basis for designing high-temperature alloys with 
stress-modulated phase transformation behavior.
Uniaxial tensile simulation results ( Fig. 3 a) indicate that the OM 
exhibits superior strength and stiffness compared to the DM material. 
Notably, the OM demonstrates a more pronounced stress drop near the 
yield point, suggesting an enhanced stress relaxation capability. Plastic 
deformation involves multiple mechanisms, such as intragranular 
dislocation slip, twinning, and grain boundary sliding/rotation. These 
mechanisms collectively facilitate stress release. Consequently, 
enhanced stress relaxation generally promotes increased material 
plasticity.
To investigate the influence of dislocations on plasticity in detail, 
DXA was performed, and the distributions of phases and dislocation 
lengths in the OM and DM are depicted in Fig. 3 (b-c) and Fig. 3 (d-e), 
respectively. During the initial elastic loading phase, atomic dislocations 
arise within the FCC lattice. Increased strain drives a phase trans -
formation, wherein dislocated atoms transition to an alternative, un -
identified phase. This transformation correlates directly with the linear 
region observed in the stress-strain curve ( Fig. 3 a). As the material ap -
proaches the yield point, the content of the “ other ” phase reaches its 
maximum, signifying the highest degree of lattice distortion. Beyond the

As illustrated in Fig. 3 c and (e), the evolution of the 1/6 < 112 >
Shockley partial dislocation density during plastic deformation exhibits 
significant differences between the OM and DM, directly affecting their 
macroscopic plastic behavior. In FCC crystals, 1/2 < 110 > full disloca -
tions and 1/6 < 112 > Shockley partial dislocations are primary defor -
mation carriers. Full dislocations promote plastic deformation via slip. 
Partial dislocations, formed through the dissociation of full dislocations, 
create stacking faults and extended dislocations. The presence of 1/ 
3 < 111 > Frank partial dislocations and 1/3 < 100 > Hirth dislocations 
makes the material stronger but more brittle. 1/6 < 110 > dislocations 
strengthen the material by hindering dislocation motion at low tem -
peratures. However, at high temperatures, thermally activated processes 
increase their mobility, potentially causing softening, recovery, and 
recrystallization. In the DM material, the 1/6 < 112 > Shockley partial 
dislocation density increases continuously, indicating the persistent 
generation of stacking faults during deformation. This accumulation of 
stacking faults effectively hinders full dislocation glide, substantially 
increasing the work hardening rate. However, excessive work hardening 
can suppress further dislocation motion and coordinated deformation, 
thereby limiting the overall ductility of the material. In contrast, the 1/ 
6 < 112 > Shockley partial dislocation density in the OM reaches a peak 
and then rapidly decreases. This phenomenon suggests the activation of 
various dislocation recovery and plastic deformation mechanisms, such 
as stacking fault recovery induced by increased stacking fault energy, 
deformation twinning, dynamic recovery processes, and potentially 
stress-induced phase transformations or vacancy cluster nucleation. 
These mechanisms can consume or redistribute dislocations through 
dislocation annihilation, dislocation rearrangement, and modifications 
to the material ’ s microstructure, thereby reducing the work hardening 
rate to some extent and potentially promoting new deformation modes, 
ultimately improving the material ’ s ductility.
Fig. 4 presents the statistical distributions of 1/6 < 112 > Shockley 
partial dislocation lengths in both DM and OM at various strain stages, 
with corresponding dislocation morphologies depicted in Fig. 5 . Spe -
cifically, Fig. 4 (a – c) (morphologies in Fig. 5 a – c) and Fig. 4 (d – f) (mor -
phologies in Fig. 5 d – f) illustrate the dislocation length distributions in 
DM and OM, respectively. They depict the distributions at the yield 
point (DM: 0.09 strain, OM: 0.10 strain), post-yield (0.12 strain), and 
within the plastic deformation regime (0.25 strain). The results 
demonstrate a statistically significant increase in the proportion of 
longer dislocations within the OM compared to the DM under equivalent 
deformation conditions.
This observation suggests that, under the specified chemical 
composition and high-temperature deformation conditions, dislocations 
within the OM exhibit a pronounced tendency for glide and extension, 
leading to the formation of elongated dislocation line structures. The 
dislocation length distribution is intrinsically linked to the activation of 
dynamic recovery mechanisms. In the OM, dislocations may more 
readily overcome lattice friction stresses, facilitating movement via 
glide or cross-slip. This enhanced mobility allows for sustained dislo -
cation line extension during deformation, thereby minimizing pinning 
or entanglement events. This implies a lower effective stress for dislo -
cation motion in the OM, favoring dynamic recovery through mecha -
nisms such as climb and cross-slip.

ing from the reaction of two Shockley partial dislocations: 
1 / 6 < 2 11 > + 1 / 6 < 12 1 > → 1 / 6 < 110 > . During the initial stages of 
plastic deformation, the applied stress surpasses the critical resolved 
shear stress. These 1/6 < 110 > stair-rod dislocations facilitate strain 
delocalization by activating multiple slip systems, effectively mitigating 
stress concentrations. The high-temperature environment further ac -
centuates the role of 1/6 < 110 > stair-rod dislocations in regulating 
plastic deformation. Elevated temperatures enhance the rate of dislo -
cation climb, enabling sustained dislocation glide within the matrix. 1/ 
6 < 110 > stair-rod dislocations with significant length facilitate cross- 
slip. Cross-slip, in turn, promotes the sustained release of accumulated 
dislocation strain energy. Dynamic recovery mechanisms, including 
dislocation climb and annihilation, are key actors in this release. 
Consequently, the work-hardening rate diminishes, leading to a post -
ponement of necking. This delayed necking is critical for enhanced 
ductility. Moreover, the abundance of 1/6 < 110 > stair-rod dislocations 
in the OM sample is strongly correlated with its superior plastic 
deformability. This contributes significantly to the higher fracture 
toughness, relative to the DM sample.
4. Conclusions
This study presents a novel computational investigation into the 
high-temperature strengthening mechanism of the L1
2 
phase in the 
AlCoCrFeMo
0.05
Ni ₂ HEA. By constructing an OM based on a 2 × 2 × 2 
FCC supercell, this work overcomes the limitations of conventional 4- 
atom L1
2 
unit cells, which are often inadequate for simulating the 
complex chemical environments of multi-component alloys. The simu -
lations reveal a synergistic interplay between stress-induced Al-rich 
phase decomposition (consistent with experimental observations) and 
distinctive dislocation dynamics in enhancing high-temperature me -
chanical properties within the ordered structure. Specifically, compared 
to the DM, the OM exhibits a significant increase in strength. Further -
more, the OM promotes the expansion and dynamic recovery of 
Shockley partial and Stair-rod dislocations, leading to reduced resis -
tance to dislocation glide and enhanced cross-slip activity. Conse -
quently, a favorable balance between strength and ductility is achieved 
at 973 K. A novel dual mechanism, involving ordered structure- 
mediated phase decomposition and cooperative dislocation slip, has 
been identified. This mechanism offers a promising strategy for 
designing high-temperature, high-strength, and high-toughness high- 
entropy alloys for demanding aerospace applications.
