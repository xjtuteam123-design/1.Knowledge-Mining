ABSTRACT 
The excellent mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys rely on their unique two-phase microstructure. Here 
we probe the influence of interfacial dislocation network on strain-rate sensitivity in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys. The interfacial dislocation could generate a weak long-range interaction to the matrix dislocations at 
low stress, and react with the incoming dislocation to form junctions at high stress. The weak interaction results 
in the dislocation pile-up around the interphase boundary, while the strong dislocation reaction makes the 
gradual loss of interface coherency. With the activation energies, we predict the dependence of strain-rate 
sensitivity on temperature in a regime with two bounds correlated with these two kinds of interactions. The 
predicted values match well with that measured in experiments at elevated temperatures. 
Ni-based superalloys have an exceptional combination of high- 
temperature strength, excellent creep resistance and anti-corrosion 
properties. They typically can work at elevated temperature exceeding 
1000 
◦
C for thousands of hours. Because of these extraordinary me -
chanical properties, they are widely used in the components of aircraft, 
such as turbine blades and discs [1] . 
The Ni-based single crystal superalloys have the characteristic two- 
phase microstructure, i.e. , the γ’ (L1
2 
phase based on Ni
3
Al) pre -
cipitates are embedded in a solid solution γ matrix [2 – 5] . The lattice 
misfit between two phases results in the formation of misfit dislocation 
network at γ / γ’ interface [6] . The pattern of interfacial dislocations 
varies with the orientation of the interphase boundary. Previous atom -
istic simulations have revealed that the pattern is square at the {100} 
interface and rectangle at the {110} interface [6] . The dislocation 
network morphologies evolve to relieve the misfit stress during thermal 
exposure [7] . The dislocation network takes a crucial role in improving 
mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys. Huang et al. found that 
the interfacial dislocation networks could hinder the dislocations mo -
tion in the matrix channel [8] . It could also block the matrix dislocations 
to cut into γ’ precipitates. Zhu et al. unveiled the formation of 〈 100 〉 -type 
dislocation junctions as a 1/2 〈 110 〉 screw dislocation reacts with inter -
facial dislocation using atomistic simulations [9] . Experimentally, Tang 
et al. found plenty of dislocation pairs in precipitates using transmission 
electric microscope [10] . Prakash et al. studied the interaction of a 
single and a pair of 60
◦
mix dislocations with the dislocation network, 
and found that the depinning of reacted superdislocation behaves like an 
Orowan process [11] . Dislocations at γ channels become more difficult 
to cut through the interface with a much denser dislocation network 
[12] . Huang et al. observed the formation of interfacial dislocations with 
non-compact core structures at the protrusion tip of γ / γ’ interface with 
the aid of dislocation climb in experiments and proposed that the for -
mation of non-compact core structures takes a prominent role in stabi -
lizing dislocation network [13] . However, a recent study proposed that 
denser dislocation networks may exist a softening process involving 
enhanced interfacial dislocation climbing and γ’ precipitate cutting by 
the 〈 100 〉 superdislocations from the dislocation network [14] . 
Strain rate could influence the deformation behavior pronouncedly 
in Ni-based superalloys. For example, the plastic flow [15] , damage 
failure [16] , plastic deformation bonding behavior [17] and their un -
derlying mechanisms are highly related to the strain rate in Ni-based 
superalloys. The strain-rate sensitivity, the change of corresponding 
stress with varying the strain rate in the log-log axes, can reflect the 
characteristics of deformation mechanism [18] . Gopinath et al. studied 
the strain rate sensitivity at different temperatures in 720Li superalloy 
[19] . The alloy shows a much weaker strain-rate sensitivity at low 
temperatures due to the heterogeneous planar slip of dislocations.

However, at higher temperatures, diffusion promotes the plastic defor -
mation, making the alloy exhibit a strong strain-rate sensitivity [19] . A 
recent work carried out by Ding et al. shows that the strain sensitivity 
coefficients of a single crystal superalloys are around 0.1 at different 
strain levels at 1050 
◦
C, i.e. , 0.105, 0.093 and 0.101 with strain of 0.2 %, 
2 % and 10 % [20] . The manners of dislocations accumulation were 
observed to vary under different strain rates [20] . Although some ex -
periments have studied the dependence of strain rate on the deformation 
behavior, the indispensable interface and interfacial dislocations in 
Ni-based superalloy are not taken into account. 
In this study, we correlate the strain-rate sensitivity of Ni-based su -
peralloy with the interaction between the incoming screw dislocation 
and interfacial dislocation network at interphase boundary. Many 
experimental studies have shown that the plasticity in matrix is domi -
nated by screw dislocations in Ni-based superalloys [ 2 , 10 , 21 – 23 ]. For 
example, Pollock and Field [21] demonstrated that the narrow matrix 
channels serve as a filter in the incubation period of creep and only those 
screw dislocations enabling extensive cross slips can continue to glide. 
We found that the interfacial dislocation could generate a long-range 
repulsive stress to the incoming screw dislocation at low stress and 
react with it at higher stress. The activation energy reaches a few eV for 
the first event and tens of eV for the second event. With the activation 
energies, we further predict the strain-rate sensitivities and found that 
the calculated values are comparable to that measured experimentally. 
Our work provides the mechanistic insight on the origin of strain-rate 
sensitivity at microscopic scale in Ni-based superalloys.

processes for the binary Ni-Al system. Various properties reproduced by 
our ML potentials, such as elastic constants, stacking fault energies,

Tables S2 and S3). We also provide the relevant data on the equation of 
state, Ni/Ni
3

several atomic layers with a thickness ~1 nm at the top and the bottom

out using LAMMPS code [26] . The atomic configurations were displayed 
by OVITO [27] .

Fig. 4 shows the dependence of predicted strain-rate sensitivity m on 
temperature with the values of the first and second activation barriers. 
The two bounds correspond to two kinds of dislocation-interface in -
teractions above. The upper bound is related to the dislocation pile-up 
near the interphase boundary due to the long-range repulsive stress 
generated by interfacial dislocation to the incoming dislocations. In the 
incubation period of creeping at high temperatures, dislocations prop -
agate and accumulate at γ / γ’ interface [21] . The dislocation pile-up 
prevents the following dislocation to react with the interfacial disloca -
tions and develop to nodal networks. For the lower bound, it corre -
sponds to reaction between travelling dislocation and interfacial 
dislocation. At the end of the primary creep, the high density of dislo -
cations accumulated around the interphase boundary generates high 
local stress. The dislocation could overcome the local barrier and meet 
with the interphase boundary, making the dislocation reaction to occur 
at the interface. With more dislocations absorbed by the interface, the 
semi-coherency could be gradually degraded. At this state, the strong 
interaction between dislocation and interfacial dislocation network 
could result in a lower bound of strain-rate sensitivity m ( Fig. 4 ). 
In real experiments, the two kinds of dislocation-interface in -
teractions above are both involved. Thus, the strain-rate sensitivity 
could lie in the regime with the two predicted bounds according to our 
calculations (blue shadow in Fig. 4 ). To show the validity of our model, 
we noticed that a recent experiment has measured the strain rate 
sensitivity of Ni-based single crystal superalloy. The value m is ~0.1 at 
1050 
◦
C (red symbol in Fig. 4 ) [20] . This value stays right in this regime. 
In addition, the model also has a good prediction to m for the poly -
crystalline Ni-based superalloys (see Supplementary Note 6). Here we 
correlate strain-rate sensitivity with dislocation-interface interaction 
because this interaction provides the key mechanism responsible for the 
mechanical behavior in Ni-based superalloys. It should be noticed that 
some other mechanisms could also operate. For example, as the massive 
dislocations accumulate around interphase boundary and cannot pene -
trate the interface, the screw dislocations may undergo cross-slip [7] . 
Previous NEB calculations have shown that the activation volume is 
~50 b
3 
at a shear stress ~100 MPa for screw dislocation cross-slip in Ni 
[30] . The occurrence of this event might also take certain contribution 
to the strain-rate sensitivity m . 
To sum up, our present work reveals that the interfacial dislocation 
takes two kinds of interaction with the incoming dislocations in Ni 
channels, i.e. , the long-range repulsive interaction to induce dislocation 
pile-up at the interphase boundary and dislocation reaction to make 
coherency loss of interphase boundary. The energy barrier for the first 
and second events could reach a few eV and tens of eV, significantly 
larger than that for a coherent interface without interfacial dislocation. 
With the activation barriers, we predict the dependence of strain-rate 
sensitivity m on temperature in a regime with two bounds correlated 
with the two events above. The value of ~0.1 measured experimentally 
at 1050 
◦
C in Ni-based single crystal superalloys lies in this regime as we 
predicted.
