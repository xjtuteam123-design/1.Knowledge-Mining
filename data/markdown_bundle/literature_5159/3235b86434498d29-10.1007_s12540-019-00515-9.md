New Eutectic High‑Entropy Alloys Based on Co–Cr–Fe–Mo–Ni–Al: 
Design, Characterization and Mechanical Properties
Hakan Gasan1  · Akin Ozcan2

Abstract
New eutectic high-entropy alloys based on novel (CoqCrvFewMoyNiz)100−xAlx alloy systems were designed using both ther-
modynamic and computational approaches. After considering 324 equilibrium diagrams, ten potential eutectic compositions 
were determined to possess a eutectic point comprising FCC and B2 phases. (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82,2Al17.8 was found to 
have a fully eutectic structure through experimental analysis, which had a negligible error (0.23%) compared to that of the 
computational modeling. The XRD patterns showed that the alloy was composed of only FCC and B2 phases (with volume 
fractions of 73.4% and 26.6%, respectively) and did not contain σ phase, which was predicted by the computational model 
to appear at low temperatures. Among all other alloys, the hypereutectic (18 at% Al) alloy exhibited the highest compressive 
yield strength (729 MPa) and ultimate compressive strength (2844 MPa), and the hypoeutectic (16 at% Al) alloy had the 
highest compressive ductility (~ 39%). For all fabricated alloys (hypoeutectic, eutectic, and hypereutectic), the compressive 
strength and strain exceeded 2514 MPa and 27%, respectively.
Keywords Eutectic high-entropy alloys · Alloy design · Microstructure · Mechanical properties
1 Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), a novel and new type of alloy 
system, are presently of great interest in material science. 
These alloys possess high strength and good high-tempera-
ture creep and softening resistance due to their severe lattice 
distortion and sluggish diffusion, respectively [1–6]. Despite 
their outstanding mechanical properties, scientists are still 
dealing with fundamental problems with single-phase 
HEAs. Generally, HEAs with FCC crystal structures show 
good ductility but poor strength where HEAs with BCC 
crystal structures have excellent strength but low plasticity 
[6]. Although it is very difficult to improve the ductility of 
HEAs with BCC crystal structures, scientists have succeeded 
in carrying out promising work to strengthen HEAs with 
FCC solid solutions [7–12].
To address the problem of single-phase HEAs, the con-
cept of eutectic high-entropy alloys (EHEAs) has recently 
emerged. The experimental results show that EHEAs (with 
eutectic lamellar-type or rod-type structures) with a mixture 
of a soft FCC phase and a hard BCC or an intermetallic 
phase (such as a hard Laves phase) could offer an ideal com-
bination of strength and ductility [13]. In addition, eutectic 
structures have advantages of high-temperature creep resist-
ance, low-energy phase boundaries, and controllable micro-
structures [14]. Furthermore, most HEAs usually possess 
weak liquidity and castability and considerable composi-
tional inhomogeneity, mostly because of their multielement 
structures with high concentrations of the alloying elements. 
However, EHEAs have experimentally shown the potential 
to achieve high-quality industrial-scale ingots with uniform 
composition and structure and, more importantly, good 
mechanical properties [14, 15].
Although EHEAs possess good castability as well as 
being able to solve casting segregation problems of HEAs 
in general, designing and particularly establishing the eutec-
tic composition in a EHEA is still a big challenge. In this 
alloying system, where there are many possible eutectic

modeling of HEAs: theoretical modeling and computa-

In addition to computational approaches, such as den-
sity functional theory (DFT) calculations [24] and ab initio 
molecular dynamics (AIMD) [ 25] simulations, that can be 
used to study phase formation in HEAs, a semi-empirical 
computational approach, generally termed calculations of 
phase diagrams (CALPHAD), has been used to predict equi-
librium phases in HEAs [26]. The CALPHAD approach 
basically determines the most stable equilibrium state by 
minimizing the total Gibbs energy of the system, mostly for 
a known atmosphere at different temperatures and composi-
tions [27]. The strength of the CALPHAD method is that the 
descriptions of the constituent subsystems can be combined 
to describe a multicomponent system.
In this study, the phase formation rules of HEAs and 
computational approaches were used and a group of eutectic 
compositions based on Al–Co–Cr–Fe–Ni (as 3d transition 
metals) and Mo (as a refractory metal) was designed to 
achieve a FCC/B2 dual-phase EHEA with excellent mechan-
ical properties at both room temperature and above. The 
designed EHEAs were fabricated by vacuum arc melting and 
casting, and the microstructure and mechanical characteriza-
tions were carried out to better understand the mechanical 
properties.
In summary, this paper is concerned with the thermo-
dynamic and computational design of AlCoCrFeMoNi 
EHEAs. The objective of this work was to find the possi-
ble eutectic compositions of the system, calculate related 
thermodynamic parameters and phase diagrams for each 
possible EHEA and also investigate the validity of the cal-
culations using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Rietveld analysis 
and scanning electron microscopy/energy dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) to analyze as-cast samples and 
demonstrate whether the predicted phases were formed. In 
the last step, mechanical tests were applied to the fabricated 
EHEAs to study their mechanical properties.

It was indicated in the literature that the cooling rate has a

which is mostly performed in water-cooled copper crucibles, 
have shown that the rate of cooling of melted samples is very 
high. This encourages the formation of the first solidified 
phase or phases in alloys in high-entropy systems, which are 
known for their sluggish diffusion [29, 30].

3 Experimental Procedures
The determined HEAs were produced by vacuum arc 
melting of high-purity elements (Al, Co, Cr, Fe, Mo and 
Ni; Alfa Aesar, > 99.95%) in a high-purity argon atmos-
phere. The ingots were flipped and remelted three times to 
ensure chemical homogeneity. Molds with dimensions of 
6 mm × 12 mm × 60 mm were used to fabricate plates and 
cylindrical samples with a diameter of 8 mm and length 
of 50 mm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Rietveld analyses 
were performed to determine the types and quantities of the 
phases in the alloys. XRD analyses were performed on PAN-
alytical EMPYREAN instrument at a scanning rate of 2°/
min and from 30° to 100° using Cu-Kα radiation. The phase 
volume fractions were obtained by Rietveld analysis of the 
XRD patterns with the Materials Analysis Using Diffraction 
(MAUD) program [31]. The alloy microstructure was exam-
ined by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-
SEM) on an FEI Nova NanoSEM operating in backscattered 
electron (BSE) mode and equipped with energy disperse 
spectroscopy (EDS). Thermal analysis of the eutectic alloy 
was performed using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA 
8000) in a controlled nitrogen atmosphere within a tempera-
ture range of 25–1500  °C and a heating rate of 10  °C min
−1. 
The hardness of the new alloys was measured under a 200 g 
load that was held for 10 s (Future Tech FM800 microhard-
ness tester). Measurements were made at a minimum of 
five points on each specimen, and the average values were 
reported in the paper. The compression tests were conducted 
on a Shimadzu AG-IS 250 kN testing machine at a strain rate 
of 10
−3 s−1 at room temperature.
4 Results and Discussion
4.1 Microstructural Characterization (Establishing 
the Exact Eutectic Composition)
First, to establish the exact eutectic composition of the alloy, 
the microstructure of the (Co40Cr10Fe5Mo 5Ni40)84Al16, 
 (Co 40 Cr 10 Fe 5 Mo 5 Ni 40 ) 82.43 Al 17.57 , and 
 (Co40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82Al18 hypoeutectic, calculated eutec-
tic and hypereutectic HEAs, respectively, were investigated 
using SEM. Figure  1 shows SEM-BSE images of these 
alloys.
Figure  1a and b indicate that the microstructure of the 
alloy containing 16 at% Al contains a very low amount 
of the eutectic structure, so it is a hypoeutectic HEA. 
As seen in Fig.  1c and d, even though the alloy contain-
ing 17.57 at% Al contains more of the eutectic struc-
ture than the alloy containing 16 at% Al, it still contains 
the proeutectic FCC phase. Comparing the results of the 
 (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82Al18 alloy (Fig.  1e, f) to those of 
the alloys with 16 at% and 17.57 at% Al content illustrates 
that this alloy has the closest composition to the eutectic 
point but contains the proeutectic B2 phase, which makes 
it a hypereutectic HEA. As a result of the microstructure 
analyses of the (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)100−xAlx system, it 
was concluded that the exact eutectic composition should 
contain between 17.57 at% and 18 at% Al. To determine the 
exact eutectic composition, three additional alloys contain-
ing 17.75 at%, 17.8 at% and 17.85 at% Al were prepared, and 
their microstructures were examined. Figure  2 presents the 
SEM images of these alloys.
From the microstructure images of these alloys (Fig. 2), it 
is evident that the alloys containing 17.75 at% and 17.85 at% 
Al possess some proeutectic, FCC and B2 phases, whereas 
the alloy containing 17.8 at% Al consists of a 100% eutectic 
structure. Therefore, (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82,2Al17.8 was 
selected as the EHEA for the (Co40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)100−xAlx 
system. The results show a 0.23% fallibility compared to that 
of the calculated eutectic composition, so this result was 
considered to be successful and reliable.
The results of the EDS analysis of the eutectic 
 (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82,2Al17.8 alloy are given in Figs. 3, 4, 
and 5 and Table 5. 
The EDS results indicate that the experimental com-
position is close to the nominal composition of the alloy. 
Moreover, phase analysis is also in good agreement with the 
Scheil simulation results, which indicated that the alloy is 
composed of the (Al–Ni)-rich B2 phase and (Co–Cr–Mo)-
rich FCC phase.
The DSC curve of the (Co
40Cr10Fe5Mo5Ni40)82,2Al17.8 
alloy during heating is given in Fig. 6. It is obvious that the 
heating curve has only a sharp endothermic peak at 1362 °C, 
which indicates a eutectic structure.

it has a hardness lower than that of the eutectic alloy. This 
is thought to be the result of a fine-grained structure in the 
eutectic alloy; it has a very narrow range of solidification, 
which leads to elevated hardness values.
