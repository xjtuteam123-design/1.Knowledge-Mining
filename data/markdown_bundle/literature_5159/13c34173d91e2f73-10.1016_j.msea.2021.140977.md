ABSTRACT 
In this paper, the creep deformation mechanisms are investigated in nickel-based single crystal superalloys. Two- 
dimensional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are conducted to model various temperatures, stress condi -
tions, and phase interface crystal orientations. Ni-based single-crystal superalloys are of great importance in the 
aircraft industry due to their excellent high temperature creep resistance. This characteristic mainly originates 
from two features considered in their structure; firstly, their two-phase micro-structure comprising gamma γ and 
gamma prime γ
′
, and secondly the nature of this superalloy itself, which is a single-crystal. MD is a powerful tool 
to gain insight into creep behavior at small scales, where dislocations and high-temperature diffusional phe -
nomena are the most critical deformation agents. The parameters considered in the creep deformation are 
temperature, stress, and phase interface crystal orientation. The simulations are observed in various temperature 
conditions including 1100, 1200, 1400, 1600, and 1700 K. Stress levels are applied from 0.5 to 5.0 GPa with a 
sequence of 0.5 GPa, and phase interface crystal orientations are imposed on (001), (011) and (111). Various 
mechanisms are detected, including the γ
′
precipitate shearing, micro-twinning, the diffusion-mediated climb 
that allows the dislocations to bypass the γ
′
precipitates, and rafting that refers to directional γ
′
coarsening. The 
results are shown to be in good agreement with available experimental data. The steady-state creep phase is 
associated with a constant strain rate, which is calculated for each model. The power-law equation is employed 
to predict the steady-state strain rate as a function of stress, temperature, the required activation energy, and the 
stress exponent parameter. Finally, a deformation map is presented for different phase interface models based on 
the stress exponent parameter values. 
1. Introduction 
Superalloys ’ evolution is much needed to meet the dramatic increase 
in gas turbines and aero-engines demand in last decades. Many re -
searchers have proposed methods to improve the thermodynamic effi -
ciency of gas turbines. The practical process is to prepare a higher 
temperature environment for the gas path, such that the combustion 
occurs at a higher temperature with less amount of gas being used. In 
this case, the interior components, such as the turbine blades, are 
frequently in contact with the hot gas and have to withstand complex 
stress and strain conditions at high temperatures. The materials resisting 
the hostile environment should perform well in various conditions, 
including fatigue, creep, hot corrosion, and oxidation [ 1 ]. The preferred 
choice among the available materials is the superalloys, which are 
defined as “ the alloys based on group VIIIA-base elements developed for 
elevated-temperature service, which demonstrate combined mechanical 
strength and surface stability ” [ 2 ]. The development of superalloys is 
closely related to the history of processing techniques. In the first half of 
the twentieth century, polycrystalline superalloys were employed, 
where the grain boundary strengthening elements such as boron and 
carbon were used to enhance creep resistance. Afterward, the next 
generation gas turbines were introduced in the 1970s, during which the 
advent of vacuum induction melting and directional solidification 
improved the creep performance of the alloys. The directional solidifi -
cation technology eliminates the transverse grain boundaries; then, 
removing all the grain boundaries resulted in single crystal blades. Thus, 
by limiting the dislocations activity, significant strengthening occurred 
[ 3 , 4 ]. By then, Ni-based single crystal superalloys have been chosen 
among other superalloys to be extensively used in high-temperature 
environments, such as power-generation gas turbines and aircraft en -
gines, because of their high-temperature strength, toughness, and 
resistance to degradation in the corrosive or oxidizing environment [ 5 ,

Creep is defined as a time-dependent phenomenon resulting in strain 
when the applied stress is lower than the yield point. The creep defor -
mation is permanent and in response to extreme temperature conditions. 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys used in gas turbine engines experi -
ence creep at high temperatures. The creep deformation process com -
prises of three phases. The primary creep is interpreted as an adaptation 
stage during which the strain rate decreases with time. In contrast, the 
secondary creep is the useful life period in which the superalloy expe -
riences a steady strain rate. Finally, at the tertiary creep stage, the strain 
rate increases dramatically until the final failure. Since the industrial 
components are functioning most of their life in the secondary creep 
stage, its characteristics are of interest to designers. The knowledge of 
the constant strain rate associated with the steady-state creep phase 
allows estimation of the time interval, which takes for a component 
reaches a particular strain value and becomes incompatible with the 
designed geometry. The creep deformation mechanisms in each phase 
are highly dependent on the superalloy ’ s environmental conditions, 
including temperature, stress, and direction of the applied stress [ 2 , 3 , 
9 – 13 ]. Also, the changes in the volume fraction, size, shape, and 
orientation of the γ
′
precipitates with respect to the γ channels signifi -
cantly affect the active creep deformation at a temperature and stress 
regime. The alloying elements and heat treatment usually control the 
precipitate size, and it affects the mode of precipitation hardening

energy to be cut with strongly coupled dislocations. Therefore, a higher 
volume fraction of γ

optimized γ

To date, existing Ni-based superalloys, used in high-temperature 
environments, have been designed empirically and based on trial tests 
that are costly due to high safety factors. So far, extensive experimental 
research has been conducted to observe the mechanical properties and 
the creep behavior of Ni-based superalloys. On the other hand, computer 
simulations can provide a more determined understanding of the atomic 
processes and elucidate the details of creep deformation mechanisms 
that an experimental study might not access. Consequently, the novel 
information obtained through computer simulations can be employed to 
improve creep properties, develop industrial gas turbines and aero- 
engines, and eventually prevent using high safety factors in their 
design. There are various methods for simulating the mechanical 
behavior of nano-materials, including the molecular dynamics (MD), 
molecular mechanics (MM), density functional theory (DFT), quantum 
mechanics (QM), continuum mechanics (CM), and finite element 
method (FEM) [ 15 – 19 ]. However, based on the importance of γ / γ

2. Creep behavior in single crystal Ni-based superalloys 
Creep is a time-dependent phenomenon that induces permanent 
strain at high-temperature conditions under a constant applied stress, 
lower than the yield strength. Ni-based single crystal superalloys expe -
rience creep at high temperatures in power-generation gas turbines and 
aircraft engines. To better understand their creep behavior and extend 
their creep lives, a deep comprehension of how these alloys deform and 
what causes the deformation is required. The FCC crystal structure of γ 
and γ
′
results in 12 active slip systems along which the dislocations can 
glide and induce plastic deformation [ 31 ]. Dislocation ’ s movement 
causes plastic deformation and occurs only if the motion results in 
lowering the structure ’ s energy state. As a dislocation nucleates in the 
channel between two precipitates, it either bends, cuts through the 
precipitate, cross-slips or climb over them, or cease propagation. 
Determining which of the mentioned motions are taken depends on the 
amount of force. It is noted that the first three require greater force to 
proceed. Also, it is found that dislocations can dissociate and alleviate 
their motion, such that it requires less energy to slip. A perfect dislo -
cation dissociates in two partial dislocations such that adding up the 
smaller Burgers vectors of the partials produces the original disloca -
tion ’ s Burgers vector. For example, a / 2 < 1 ​ 1 ​ 0 > = a /

( 111 ) plane. Based on the Frank ’ s rule, the energy of a dislocation is

structure ’ s energy level, which prepares the required force for disloca -

The creep phenomenon is highly dependent on the conditions of the 
environment containing the superalloy, including the temperature, the 
stress, and the direction of the applied stress with respect to the phase 
interface crystal orientation. Based on the factors, a specific creep 
mechanism activates and dominates the deformation causing strain 
growth. Creep deformation phases comprise primary, secondary or 
steady-state, and tertiary, as shown in Fig. 2 (a). Primary creep is inter -
preted as an adaptation stage during which the strain rate decreases with 
time, and it is mostly observed at low temperatures. Whereas, secondary 
creep is the useful life period in which the superalloy experiences a 
steady strain rate. Finally, the tertiary creep is mainly active at inter -
mediate temperatures and stress regimes during which the strain rate 
increases dramatically until the final failure, as shown in Fig. 2 (b) [ 31 , 
32 ]. The creep mechanisms are illustrated in Figs. 3 – 6 such that the 
results from the current study (b) are validated with experimental data

2.2. Secondary creep 
After primary creep, secondary creep or steady-state creep appears, 
which is the longest phase of creep, and the alloy spends most of its life 
in this stage in single-crystal superalloys. During secondary creep, strain 
hardening and softening occur simultaneously. At this step, γ channel 
saturation with dislocations act as strain hardening, and softening 
mechanisms such as γ
′
precipitate shearing, cross-slipping, and climb of 
dislocations occur in thermal activation, which may release the stress 
concentration for maintaining the process of creep [ 6 , 31 ]. Once the 
strain hardening and recovery softening get to equilibrium, the alloy 
experiences a constant creep strain rate and enters the steady-state 
phase. 
The knowledge of the constant strain rate associated with the steady- 
state creep phase allows estimation of the time interval, which takes for 
a component reaches a specific strain value and becomes incompatible 
with the geometry. For example, in an aero engine, blades are elongated 
due to creep deformation at high temperatures and damage the casting ’ s 
inner wall. On the other hand, the gap between the tip of the blade and 
the casting should be kept at a minimum to reduce the required gas and 
enhance the engine ’ s efficiency. Hence, it is crucially important to know 
the rate at which the blade is to creep so that the blades could be 
replaced at the end of their life span before irreparable damage happens.

associated with vacancy absorption, a flux of vacancies toward the 
dislocation. Conversely, the downward climb of dislocations requires 
atoms to be constantly absorbed to the dislocation core, associated with 
an atom flux from the adjacent channels toward the dislocation core.

5. Results and discussion 
According to the molecular dynamics simulations, it has been 
observed that the single crystal superalloys can exhibit primary creep, 
secondary or steady-state creep, and tertiary creep in response to creep 
load. The mechanisms causing the creep strain are highly dependent on 
temperature, stress level, and phase interface crystal orientation. The 
following results determine the active creep mechanisms at each tem -
perature and stress regime for the phase interface models.

6. Conclusions 
The present paper describes a promising research area that can 
produce significant discoveries in the development of gas turbines and 
aero-engines in the near future where the possibility of significant ad -
vancements can still be made. In the first part of this paper, the two- 
dimensional MD simulations are conducted by the LAMMPS program 
on the Ni-based single crystal superalloys using the Mishin potential to 
investigate the creep deformation mechanisms. The creep strain evolu -
tion and changes in the active creep deformation mechanisms were 
analyzed, varying different parameters including temperature, tensile 
stress, and γ / γ
′
phase interface crystal orientation. The conditions being 
assessed comprise of temperature conditions including 1100, 1200, 
1400, 1600, 1700 K, and different stress levels ranging from 0.5 to 5.0 
GPa with a common difference of 0.5 GPa, and three γ / γ
′
phase interface 
crystal orientations including (001), (011) and (111). Characterization 
using the CSP and DXA by employing OVITO was used to identify and 
correlate the deformation mechanisms to creep behavior. 
Several different deformation mechanisms were operative for Ni- 
based single crystal tensile creep at temperatures and stresses ranging 
between 1100 and 1700 K and 0.5 – 5.0 GPa, respectively, and three 
phase interfaces including (001), (011) and (111). The first model with 
(001) as its phase interface orientation, activates the greatest number of 
slip systems. In this model, the active creep deformation mechanism is 
observed with DXA at an intermediate temperature and stress regimes, 
which is shearing of the γ
′
precipitate. It is in reasonable agreement with 
experimental data. It is observed that by increasing the temperature and 
applied stress, the CSP increases in the horizontal channels because of 
the superposition of lattice misfit and applied stresses. Consequently, 
this causes the screw dislocations to move from one slip plane to another 
and cross slip over the precipitates, and the edge dislocations to glide 
and climb to bypass the precipitates. Eventually, the diffusion of atoms 
required for the dislocation climb mechanism causes rafting of the 
precipitates perpendicular to the stress direction. Hence, the tertiary 
creep is observed as well. In the second model, (011), no creep strain is 
observed at low-temperature and low-stress regimes. However, by 
increasing either the temperature or applied stress to high values, it is 
detected that the diffusion of atoms from vertical into horizontal chan -
nels results in the glide - climb of dislocations and creep strain growth. 
Accordingly, the micro-structure is deformed into a rafted structure. 
Meanwhile, the strain rate increases dramatically until the final failure 
on account of the tertiary creep. Finally, in the last model, which sim -
ulates the (111) phase interface crystal orientation, the creep strain 
initiates with micro-twinning as the dominant creep deformation
