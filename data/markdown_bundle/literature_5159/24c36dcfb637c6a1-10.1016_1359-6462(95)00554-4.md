Introduction 
A coherent precipitate can encounter successive shape changes while increasing in size during either coar- 
sening (at constant volume fraction) or growth (at increasing volume fraction). This phenomenon has been 
widely studied in the case of y-y’ Nickel-based superalloys (see ref.( 1) for a review). It has been unam- 
biguously related to the existence of internal stresses resulting from the small lattice discrepancy between 
y matrix and y’ precipitates. “Sphere-to-cube” and “cube-to-plate” shape transitions have been reported, 
together with a phenomenon sometimes called “reverse coarsening”, which is the splitting of a single 
cuboidal precipitate into a pair of plates (a “doublet”) (2), a group of eight cuboids (an “octet”) (3-7) or 
chaplets of cuboids aligned along the cl1 l> crystallographic directions (3). With regard to the wide range 
of morphologies reported in the literature, it appears that the shape evolution strongly depends on the alloy 
composition and on the heat treatment parameters. 
Theoretical analyses of these “spontaneous” shape changes are mostly based on the calculation of the 
internal energy associated with a mismatching coherent precipitate, as a function of precipitate size and 
for different simple shapes chosen a priori to be as close as possible to the observed ones (1,4,8- 10). At 
a given size, the morphology of lowest energy is assumed to be the most favourable. These analyses differ 
by their simplificative assumptions and by their calculation methods, but they all converge to conclude 
that splitting is expected at realistic precipitate sizes in the case of two-phase systems with high lattice 
mismatch, hi& elastic anisotropy and low interfacial energy density, as it is the case for y-y’ alloys. 
However, they often fail in quantitatively predicting the critical sizes for shape transitions, as well as in 
forecasting more complex precipitate morphologies such as the “star” (or “butterfly”) ones illustrated in 
Figure 1. These limitations can be partly overcome if taking kinetics effects into account in the models 
(11,12). In the case of superalloys with a high amount of y’ phase, we also believe that the elastic inter- 
action between precipitates is an other major effect to be considered. 
In the present paper, we utilize a 3-D finite element approach to estimate the internal elastic energy 
associated with coherent y’ precipitates of different shapes. Firstly, we validate this approach by com- 
paring its results with those available from analytical calculations, using appropriate simplificative as-

Numerical Modelling 
3-D Finite Element Model 
As a basic assumption, we consider an ideal y-y’ alloy in which y’ precipitates are periodically distributed 
on a cubic array (Figure 2a). This allows to limitate the analysis to a cubic unit cell representative of the 
whole material. As illustrated in Figure 2b, this unit cell encloses one eighth of a single precipitate and one 
half of two channels of the surrounding matrix. Within this unit cell, different y-y’ microstructures were 
modeled, using different y’ phase volume fractions and shapes. The y’ volume fractions tested ranged 
between 1 % and 30 %. The different y’ shapes are illustrated in Figure 3. The cubic shape is close to the 
most current y’ morphology after standard heat treatment (cf Fig. 1’). “Octet” and “star” morphologies are 
observed during the y’ precipitation process, while an evolution towards “infinite plate” morphology oc- 
curs during high temperature creep tests. The unit cells were discretized in finite element meshes such as 
the one showed in Figure 2b. In fact, the meshes depended on the y-y’ configuration but they were 
typically constituted of about 2500 elements. The lattice mismatch between y and y’ phases was intro- 
duced as an isotropic initial deformation of the y’ elements. Symmetry and periodicity were imposed by 
constraining three contiguous faces of the cell to deform along their initial plane, while the three other 
faces had to remain parallel to their opposite face. The mechanical behaviour of both y and y’ phases were 
assumed to be elastic and anisotropic.

The results of Table 2 clearly show that the precipitate volume fraction strongly influences the level 
of internal energy, via the effect of elastic interaction between precipitates. For instance, for a volume fi-ac- 
tion of 20 %, the energy is about 30 % lower than the one expected if neglecting the interaction between 
precipitates. This effect is still more dramatic if one focuses on the gain of elastic energy associated with 
the cube-to-octet transition, which is strongly lowered as volume fraction increases. This means that an 
increase of precipitate volume fraction, i.e. an increase of elastic interaction between precipitates, results 
in a decrease of the driving force for splitting. As shown in the following, the consequences of this inter- 
action effect will be different, depending on whether one focuses on the coarsening or on the precipitation 
process. 
The case of y’ coarsening is treated in Figure 5, which reports the evolution of the splitting energy as 
a function of precipitate size (same representation as in Fig. 4 ), for different precipitate volume fractions. 
The curve noted “1 %” in Fig.5 is similar to the one of Fig.4, the slight differences being due to the fact 
that the former reports the results of calculations assuming different elastic properties for the two phases 
while the latter refers to identical properties. Fig. 5 shows that the reduction of elastic driving energy for 
splitting results in a drastic increase of the critical size for cube-to-octet transition. The calculations per- 
formed for V, values equal to 1 %, 10 %, 19.5 % and 30 % led to &_,, values of 0.44 pm, 0.59 pm, 0.74 
pm and 2.55 urn, respectively (for legibility purpose, the corresponding part of curve “30 %” is not repre- 
sented ). These results are in agreement with experimental observation. Indeed, whenever precipitate split- 
ting is reported in the literature, the critical size at which it occurs is often noticeably higher than the one 
expected from usual theoretical analyses (1 ,S). The reasons currently invoked for this discrepancy are 
either the lack of accurate data for the parameters used in the calculations or the omission of kinetics 
effects in the models. We believe that a third cause is the elastic interaction between precipitates, which 
clearly results in a delay of the splitting phenomenon. Experimental studies are generally concerned with 
nickel-based model alloys which still present a relatively high amount of y’phase at the aging temperature 
(typically higher than 10 %). We showed that the interaction cannot be neglected in this case. The elastic 
interaction effects can also explain the coexistence of compact and splitted precipitates often observed in 
a given aged sample. Indeed, since the critical size for shape transition depends on the neighbourhood of 
a precipitate, it is therefore different from one precipitate to another.

These conclusions are qualitatively in agreement with our experience of y-> y+ y’ precipitation in 
AM1 and CMSX-2 single crystals (5,6,13). We observed the microstructure of samples quenched during 
continuous toolings at various cooling rates ranging from 200 “C/s to 0.002 “C/s, which allowed to point 
out the occurence of octet transition as far as the cooling rate was “sufficiently” low (typically less than 
5 “Us). Since :nucleus density is directly related to the rate of cooling, this means that it exists a critical 
nucleus density for the splitting to occur. We also observed that the cube -> octet transition is very rapid 
and takes place in the early stages of the precipitation process, for “local” volume fractions less than 15 
%. Although the critical value of the nucleus density for splitting calculated by F.E. modelling seems to 
be of a reasonable order of magnitude, a deeper quantitative comparison between modelling and experi- 
ence is hazardous, for at least two reasons. Firstly, the early stage growth of a free precipitate is extremely 
fast, thus the high temperature microstructure is difficult to freeze and analyse with a good accuracy. 
Secondly, the iassumption of an instantaneously complete precipitate nucleation actually is more or less 
valid, depending on the heat treatment parameters. Indeed, it appears to be not so far from reality in the 
case of high cooling rates (high undercooling, high nucleus density), while nucleation is much more 
heterogeneous in the case of low cooling rates (low undercooling, low nucleus density).

Conclusions 
Finite element micromechanical modelling was shown to be an appropriate tool for analysing the morpho- 
logical changes encountered by coherent precipitates under the influence of mismatch stresses. It allows 
to calculate the levels of internal energy associated with various morphologies and different volume frac- 
tions of precipitates. Thus, on the basis of an energetical criterion, it becomes possible to determine the 
successive precipitate shapes expected to be observed during a near-equilibrium heat treatment, and to 
study the influence of material properties (mismatch, interfacial tension, elastic anisotropy ) and of heat 
treatment parameters (temperature, cooling or heating rate . ..) on this microstructural evolution. In the 
present case, this approach allowed to point out the extend to which elastic interaction influences the y’ 
precipitate splitting process during either growth or coarsening process. Its major limitations are found in 
the lack of accurate data for the parameters required for the calculation, but above all in the fact that it 
does take into account neither the kinetics aspect of the evolution process nor the possible coupling be- 
tween the internal stress/strain fields and the diffusion mechanisms involved by the shape evolution.
