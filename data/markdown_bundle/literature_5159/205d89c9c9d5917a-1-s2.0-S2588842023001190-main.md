ABSTRACT 
The effects of Mo content on the creep property of a 4th generation Ni-based single crystal superalloy were 
investigated systematically at high temperature and low stress. Under the condition of 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa, the 
performance of SC containing 2 wt% Mo and 3 wt% Mo was close, revealing that low Mo addition could meet the 
requirements of creep properties at 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa. However, the massive precipitation of TCP phases 
significantly degraded the creep performance when Mo content was more than 3 wt%. Mo addition also 
accelerated the broadening of the γ channel, decreasing the resistance of the dislocation slip. With regard to the 
positive impacts, Mo addition increased the magnitude of lattice misfit, leading to dense interfacial dislocation 
networks with high creep resistance. Mo also retarded the diffusion of alloying elements, thereby suppressing the 
formation of creep pores and the elemental inhomogeneity during creep. The comprehensive Mo-effect was 
summarized. Moreover, the optimum Mo content in the 4th generation single crystal superalloys was determined 
for the guidance of alloy design based on the creep results and comparison with other alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys (SCs) possess excellent mechani -
cal properties at extremely high temperatures, which have been widely 
applied to aero engines and industrial gas turbines [ 1 – 4 ]. The main 
strengthening mechanisms are solid solution strengthening in the γ 
matrix and the precipitation strengthening mechanism of the coherent γ ′ 
phase [ 5 , 6 ]. During service, the mechanical properties strongly depend 
on the microstructural characteristics, including the size and volume of 
γ ′ phase and the lattice misfit [ 1 , 7 ]. The microstructure and strength -
ening effects are controlled by the chemical compositions of SCs. Over 
the decades, Ni-based SCs have developed into the 5th and 6th gener -
ations [ 8 ]. With the increase of generations, the content of refractory 
elements, such as Re and Ru, significantly increases. However, the 
massive application of Re and Ru significantly raises the cost and density 
of Ni-based SCs [ 9 , 10 ]. The research on low-cost and low-density SCs 
has been carried out widely [ 10 – 12 ]. It has been proposed that Mo is a 
crucial strengthener with a lower price and density than Re, which could 
be a potential substitution for Re [ 13 ]. 
The creep property at 1100 
◦
C is essential to the Ni-based SCs and the 
creep deformation of SCs has been studied and summarized [ 14 ]. Apart 
from the solid solution strengthening effects controlled by the compo -
sition, the mechanical properties at high temperatures are related to the 
γ / γ ′ microstructure, the lattice misfit and the dislocation structure. The 
microstructural change is controlled by chemical composition essen -
tially. Mo mainly partitions in the γ matrix to provide the solid solution 
strengthening mechanism, and Mo enhances the partitioning behavior 
of other alloying elements, such as Cr and Re [ 11 ]. Mo addition also 
increases the magnitude of lattice misfit of γ / γ ′ , which leads to dense 
interfacial dislocation networks and high creep resistance at high tem -
peratures [ 7 , 15 , 16 ]. High lattice misfit caused by Mo addition also ac -
celerates the rafting formation [ 17 ], increasing the creep resistance. 
However, Mo addition promotes the precipitation of topologically 
close-packed (TCP) phases during service at high temperatures, causing

Researchers have developed low-density Ni-based SCs by increasing 
the Mo content [ 12 , 19 ]. However, in some new-generation SCs, Mo 
content is relatively low and has no suitable reference range reported for 
the alloy design [ 8 , 20 ]. Moreover, the literature on the effects of Mo

which might be inconsistent with those in new-generation SCs due to 
complicated interactions between elements [ 11 , 16 ]. In this paper, we 
investigated the effects of Mo content on the creep property of a 4th 
generation Ni-based SC. Microstructural evolution was analyzed and the 
rough range of the optimized Mo content was listed based on the creep 
performance and comparison with different SCs to provide a reference 
for the alloy design.

3. Results 
3.1. Creep data 
Fig. 2 showed the strain-time curve of the three investigated alloys. 
The 2Mo, 3Mo and 4Mo alloys exhibited the creep life of 492.6 h, 511.7 
h and 283.2 h, respectively. The creep life of 4Mo alloy was significantly 
shorter than 2Mo and 3Mo alloy, while 2Mo and 3Mo alloy had a similar 
performance at 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa. The whole creep process could be 
divided into three stages. The creep strain gradually increased at the first 
stage, during which the rafting structure and dislocation networks 
formed [ 1 , 21 ]. At the secondary creep stage (steady creep stage), the 
creep strain increased slowly and the strain rate was maintained at a low 
level. The length of the secondary stage was the longest and reflected the 
strengthening effects of the alloy. During the tertiary stage, the creep 
strain increased rapidly until the final rupture. From the inlet in Fig. 2 , 
the length of the first stage was apparently shorter in 4Mo alloy, which 
was attributed to the fast-rafting rate led by high lattice misfit [ 22 , 23 ]. 
The length of the steady creep stage between 2Mo and 3Mo alloy was 
close, while the steady stage of 4Mo alloy was shorter. 
Fig. 3 showed the ragged morphology with cavities (as shown in the 
insets) of the fracture surface, indicating the typical plastic rupture 
during the creep deformation. The rupture mechanism of the investi -
gated alloy might be dominated by the extension and interlink of 
microcracks induced by micropores [ 24 ]. However, due to the massive 
oxidation at high temperatures, the details of the fracture surface could 
not be interpreted.

the easier γ ′ raft cutting in the interdendritic regions [ 27 ]. The 
faster-coarsening behavior in the interdendritic regions also led to the 
early topological inversion during the creep process. A related previous 
study pointed out that the interdendritic regions possessed a lower 
magnitude of lattice misfit than the dendrite cores, leading to a larger 
interfacial dislocation spacing in the interdendritic regions [ 18 ]. It has 
been accepted that interfacial dislocation networks with narrow dislo -
cation spacing could prevent dislocations shearing into γ ′ phase [ 28 ]. 
Therefore, the shearing events in γ ′ phase were more likely to occur in 
the interdendritic regions. Due to the pipe diffusion led by dislocations 
[ 29 ], the shearing events in γ ′ phase promoted the dissolution of γ ′ phase 
and the coarsening of rafted structure [ 18 ]. Moreover, the shearing 
event in γ ′ phase near the fracture surface was severe due to the stress 
concentration, which explained the reason why the γ channel width was 
larger near the fracture surface. It was worth noting that the width of γ 
channel was larger in 2Mo and 3Mo alloy than in 4Mo alloy. 4Mo alloy 
had the shortest creep life, leading to insufficient coarsening for the γ 
channel. 
3.4. Evolution of dislocations 
The rafting structure could prevent dislocation slip and force the 
dislocations to climb along the longitudinal direction. The different 
dislocations deposited on the γ / γ ′ interface reacted with each other to 
form dislocation networks [ 30 ], forming a source of creep resistance. 
Fig. 6 displayed the morphology of dislocation networks in the samples 
T1 and T2 with different distances from the fracture. In the T1 samples, 
the integrality of dislocation networks was destroyed due to the stress 
concentration near the fracture [ 31 ]. Massive superdislocations cutting 
into γ ′ phase were discovered (marked by blue arrows in Fig. 6 a, b and 
c), which was also attributed to the severe stress concentration. The 
dislocations were denser in 2Mo alloy and relatively fewer in 4Mo alloy. 
T2 samples were sliced away from the fracture, little affected by the 
stress concentration. Therefore, the dislocation networks were less 
degraded and superdislocations were sparsely distributed in γ ′ phase. 
Fig. 7 further analyzed the morphology of superdislocations in 
different two-beam conditions, and some typical dislocations were 
marked by arrows. Generally, dislocations with long straight 
(1,2,6,7,9,10) and short straight (3,4,5,7,8) morphology existed in γ ′ 
phase. Some dislocations consisted of two parallel partials (marked by 
white arrows, dislocation 5 and 6 were enlarged), which could be 
deduced as anti-phase boundaries (APB). Other dislocations exhibited a 
single contrast, of which extended straight dislocations were marked by 
red arrows and short straight arrows were marked by blue arrows. The 
directions of Burgers vectors of the marked dislocations were identified 
and listed in Table 3 . All dislocations were < 011 > type dislocations 
except dislocation 9. It was suggested that such < 001 > type dislocation 
formed via the reaction between two different dislocations in the γ phase 
[ 32 ]. Short dislocation 4, 5, 7, 8 had relatively zigzag contrast, which 
might be further identified as screw or mixed dislocations [ 33 , 34 ]. 
Dislocation 5 coupled with APB probably resulted from the reaction of 
two dislocations with the same Burgers vector [ 35 ]. The projection 
length of dislocation 1, 2, 6 and 10 was approximately 300~400 nm, 
apparently longer than that of dislocation 4, 5, 7 and 8, indicating that 
these long dislocations seemed to lie on the (001) plane [ 36 ]. Such 
dislocations might dissociate and cross slip from {111} planes to {001} 
planes, forming the Kear-Wilsdorf (K – W) locks to further restrain 
dislocation movements [ 37 ]. Combined with the dislocation configu -
rations in Figs. 7 and 6 , it could be concluded that more short straight 
dislocations appeared in the 2Mo alloy near the fracture than in 3Mo and 
4Mo alloy. 
4. Discussion 
The creep properties of different SCs and the microstructure after 
rupture were stated above. Interestingly, the creep property at 1100 
◦
C 
of 2Mo and 3Mo was quite similar, which was inconsistent with the 
conclusion that properly adding Mo improves the mechanical property 
[ 16 , 38 ]. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the influence mechanism 
of different Mo addition on the creep performance to optimize Mo 
content. The second creep stage had the lowest creep rate and the largest 
proportion of creep life. In the tertiary stage, the creep rate increases 
rapidly and the alloy eventually cracks. It is important to study the 
factors that trigger the transition from the second to the tertiary stage. 
The samples were interrupted at 400 h to study the influence of Mo 
content on the transition from the second stage to the tertiary stage in 
2Mo and 3Mo alloy. The short creep life of 4Mo alloy was also further 
discussed. The results were discussed from the perspective of micro -
pores, the width of γ channel, elemental inhomogeneity, dislocation 
morphology and TCP phases. 
4.1. Creep pores 
Fig. 8 displayed macroscopic images of the microstructure of 2Mo 
and 3Mo alloy after being interrupted at 400h. In the rupture samples, it 
was difficult to determine what type of pore caused the crack initiation 
at the beginning of the tertiary stage. The distribution of pores in the 
interrupted samples was exhibited in Fig. 8 a and b. The micropore 
density in the interdendritic region of 2Mo alloy was obviously higher, 
which was consistent with the phenomenon that microcracks were 
mainly distributed in the interdendritic regions in 2Mo alloy in Fig. 4 . In 
3Mo alloy, the micropore density in the interdendritic region was lower 
than 2Mo. The morphology of micropores in the interdendritic regions 
was similar in both samples and had a regular polygon shape, indicating 
that the pores were creep cavities. Creep cavities were formed via the 
condensed vacancies generated by dislocation climbing [ 39 ]. 
The creep pores were massive in the interdendritic regions of 2Mo 
alloy, which led to the final creep rupture. The high creep pore density 
hinted that the dislocation climbing process was enhanced in 2Mo alloy 
compared to 3Mo alloy. The dislocation climb was closely related to 
nonconservative elemental diffusion [ 40 ], which could produce va -
cancies [ 13 ]. It has been suggested that the diffusion rate of Mo was slow 
[ 41 ]. A high concentration of Mo in the γ phase might retard the 
elemental diffusion process by the interaction between Mo and other 
elements [ 38 ], leading to the suppression of dislocation climbing during 
the creep. Therefore, the creep pores were denser in 2Mo alloy than in 
3Mo alloy. It was noteworthy that the creep pores were concentrated in 
the interdendritic regions. It was because the dendrite cores contained 
more elements with slow diffusion rates, such as Re and W. Therefore, it 
was hard for dislocations to climb in the dendrite cores, inhibiting the 
condensing of vacancy and formation of creep pores. Zhu et al. [ 42 ]

4.3. Elemental inhomogeneity 
During the creep process, the long-range movements of dislocations 
between the interdendritic regions and dendrite cores could lead to the 
redistribution of elements [ 50 , 51 ], since the dislocations could act as 
fast diffusion paths for refractory elements [ 29 , 52 ] (pipe diffusion). The 
comparison between the segregation behavior before and after creeping 
for 400 h was displayed in Fig. 10 . The heat-treated specimens were 
marked by ‘HT ’ , and the interrupted samples were marked by ‘CR ’ . After 
long-term heat treatments, the elemental segregation was homogenized 
sufficiently. However, the segregation behavior became more severe 
after 400 h creep, which could be explained by the pipe diffusion 
mentioned above. Apparently, the severity of segregation behavior in 
2Mo alloy was heavier than in 3Mo alloy. It has been reported that Mo 
interacts with other alloying elements via d-d orbital hybridization [ 11 ]. 
Moreover, the diffusion rate of the Mo atom was slow, indicating that 
the diffusion rates of other elements decreased by the interaction with 
Mo atoms. Therefore, Mo addition delayed the inhomogenization of the 
elemental segregation. However, few researchers have focused on the 
elemental inhomogenization during the creep process. Whether the in -
crease of segregation depends on the movements of dislocations remains 
controversial. The exact mechanism of the effect of Mo addition on the 
inhomogenization still requires further investigation. Such inhomoge -
nization would lead to the further weakening of the interdendritic 
strength and final crack initiation. The interdendritic strength weak -
ening of 2Mo alloy was more severe than that of 3Mo alloy, leading to 
the interdendritic fracture of 2Mo alloy more easily.

3Mo and 4Mo alloy ( Fig. 11 ) could impede dislocations from entering γ ′ 
phase better, which could be supported by the fewer superdislocations in 
3Mo alloy in Figs. 6 and 7 . However, the narrower dislocation spacings 
did not significantly increase the creep life of SCs. The creep pores and 
TCP phases were the main reasons caused the crack initiation, leading to 
the insufficient strengthening effect of dislocation networks. And the 
dislocation types did not change dramatically with Mo addition. The 
results revealed that the effect of Mo addition on the dislocation struc -
ture was not the main factor affecting the creep performance. 
4.5. TCP phases 
As a TCP-forming element, Mo addition promoted the formation of 
TCP phases. In Fig. 4 , the content of needle-like TCP phases at the 
dendrite cores increased significantly with Mo addition, which might 
cause adverse effects on the creep life. Typically, the precipitation of 
TCP phases consumes amounts of strengthening elements and causes 
severe local stress concentration. Therefore, the TCP precipitation is 
generally considered detrimental to the creep properties at high tem -
peratures. Though the content of TCP-forming elements such as Re and 
W was relatively low in the interdendritic regions [ 22 ], the high Mo 
content in 4Mo alloy still caused TCP precipitation in the interdendritic 
region. 
Generally speaking, TCP phases lead to crack initiation mainly in two 
ways: stress concentration and interface decohesion. The stress con -
centration near the TCP phases leads to a higher dislocation density, 
which could emit vacancies and then condense to form pores [ 54 ], 
acting as sites for crack initiation. The interface decohesion occurs when 
the interface strength between the TCP phase and the matrix is weak. 
And the crack propagates along the weak interface [ 55 ]. In all three 
investigated alloys, no crack along the interface between the TCP phases 
and the matrix was discovered. Hence, it was reasonable to conclude 
that cracks near the interface were caused by stress concentration. 
Fig. 12 a showed the STEM image near the TCP phase in 4Mo alloy, 
revealing that dislocations were piled up at the interface, especially at 
the intersection of TCP phases. Fig. 12 b exhibited the pore discovered 
near TCP phases in the interrupted 3Mo alloy. The dislocations near the 
interface eventually led to the formation of micropores and cracks 
(illustrated in Fig. 12 c and d). It accounted for the microcracks near the 
TCP phases in 3Mo and 4Mo alloy in Fig. 4 . Moreover, the strengthening 
elements, such as Re and W were lacking in the interdendritic regions, 
indicating that the strength in the interdendritic regions might be weak.

Typically, the transition from the steady creep stage to the tertiary 
creep stage is related to the topological inversion of γ ′ / γ microstructure

transition from the steady creep stage to the tertiary creep stage by

Mo addition did not significantly affect the topological inversion 
( Table 5 ). Therefore, Mo influenced the transition of creep stages mainly 
by different factors causing crack initiation. In 2Mo alloy, the massive 
creep pores induced the crack initiation and the end of the steady creep 
stage. Despite that 3Mo alloy contained fewer creep pores than 2Mo, the 
content of TCP phases was higher in 3Mo alloy, which also induced 
crack initiation and led to the steady creep stage of 2Mo and 3Mo ending 
at a similar time. 
4.6. Role of Mo content in the SCs 
The creep performance at 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa is considered as one 
of the critical indicators of Ni-based SCs. New-generation SCs possess 
significantly longer creep life than low-generation SCs, mainly due to 
the change of composition. In the above sections, the effects of Mo 
content on the creep property were summarized and discussed in the 
investigated 4th generation SCs, which indicated that 2 wt% Mo should 
be enough to reach the standard of 4th generation SCs. And more than 3 
wt% Mo could lead to the emergence of massive TCP phases, weakening 
the mechanical properties. To further investigate the suitable Mo con -
tent range in the SCs, the Mo content, the total content of Mo, W and Re, 
and the concentration ratio between Mo and W, Mo and Re in different 
SCs were listed in Fig. 13 . In different generations of SCs, the Mo content 
fluctuates obviously, and the Mo content in most SCs from the 3rd to 5th 
generations roughly lies between 1.75 wt% and 3.25 wt%. Among the 
alloying elements, Mo, W and Re have similar characteristics as 
strengthening and TCP forming elements. Thus, the content of W and Re 
should be considered when exploring the optimum Mo content [ 13 , 59 ]. 
It is reasonable that the total content of Mo, W and Re increases with the 
development of new generation SCs ( Fig. 13 b) to gain better mechanical 
performance, even though the high content of these elements may cause 
the deterioration of microstructural stability [ 60 ]. However, the ratio of 
Mo/W and Mo/Re in different SCs mainly distribute in the range of 
0.3 – 0.55 and 0.3 – 0.65 ( Fig. 13 c and d), respectively, which is probably 
the optimized ratio in different generations of SCs. Therefore, the opti -
mized range of Mo content in the investigated 4th generation SCs could 
be further estimated to be between 1.7 wt%~3.3 wt%. The Mo content 
of 2Mo and 3Mo alloy in our work lies in the optimized range, while 4Mo 
alloy contains more Mo than the upper limit. 
5. Conclusions 
The influence of Mo content on the creep properties of a 4th gener -
ation Ni-based single crystal superalloy at 1100 
◦
C was systematically 
investigated. Furthermore, the mechanism of Mo addition was analyzed 
in detail. Factors that influenced the creep properties were summarized 
in Fig. 14 . The addition of Mo affected the creep properties both posi -
tively and negatively at 1100 
◦
C. The details were listed below: 
The positive ways: 
(1) The suppression of diffusion and dislocation climb by Mo. Mo 
addition retarded the dislocation climb by delaying the elemental 
diffusion, thereby suppressing the formation of creep pores. The 
reduction of creep pores by Mo addition reduced the crack 
initiation. 
(2) Delay of elemental segregation inhomogeneity. Mo addition 
could retard the phenomenon of enhanced elemental segregation

(3) Dense dislocation networks derived by high lattice misfit. Mo

The negative ways: 
(1) TCP phase precipitation. The content of TCP phases significantly 
increased with Mo addition. Stress concentration near the TCP 
phase led to the formation of pores and cracks easily. 
(2) γ channel widening. Mo addition promoted the γ channel 
widening during the rafting process. Wide γ channel caused the 
decrease of Orowan stress and the dislocations were easier to slip 
in γ channel. 
It could be concluded that the outcome of the creep properties was 
affected by the competition of the above two types of factors. The 
comparable performance of 2Mo and 3Mo alloy was due to the balance 
of both positive and negative effects of Mo addition. Moreover, it was 
reasonable to deduce that low Mo addition in the 4th generation Ni- 
based single crystal superalloys could satisfy the requirements of creep 
property at 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa. According to the creep property and 
composition of other typical SCs, the suitable range of Mo content 
should be within 1.7 wt%~3.3 wt%. The high content of Mo led to a 
large amount of TCP phase precipitation, significantly reducing the 
creep life. Low Mo content might cause problems such as dense creep 
pores, inducing crack initiation.
