Abstract
We studied mechanical properties and deformation behavior of Ti–43.5Al–3V–2Nb alloy at high-
temperatures. Stress–strain curves demonstrated standard work-hardening andﬂow-softening
characteristics,and the data obtained from these curves were used to obtain a corresponding formula
to calculate activation energy(which was qual to 565 kJ mol
−1). Dynamic materials modeling was used
to develop a processing map with a true strain equal to 0.6. Ti–43.5Al–3V–2Nb alloy with an equiaxed
structure has a wide processing window. Instability zones were in the 1000°C–1050 °C and >0.5 s−1.
Flow-soft mechanism in the unstable region is related to stress-relaxation due to the incompatible
deformation of each phase. Flow-soft mechanism in the safe region is related to the dynamic
recrystallization and dynamic recovery ofγ, α and β polymorphic modiﬁcations. Heat treatment at
1235 °C for 60 min/AC+900 °C/6h /FC improved room-temperature tensile properties of the
forged alloy signiﬁcantly: tensile strength was 920MPa and tensile ductility was 2.5%. Thus, the alloy
demonstrated excellent combination of high strength and ductility after this heat-treatment. Heat-
treated Ti–43.5Al–3V–2Nb alloy also maintained tensile strength higher>820 MPa up to 750°C.
1. Introduction
Materials based onγ-TiAl alloys provide excellent high-temperature structural components for aeronautics
because of their low density, high speciﬁc stiffness, resistance to titaniumﬁre, high strength and excellent high-
temperature performance. Additionally, they demonstrate long-term service under operating temperatures up
to 650 °C–800 °C [1, 2]. This material is substituted in rotating blades in high-pressure compressors and low-
pressure turbines of aero engines[3, 4]. However, TiAl alloys lack adequate room-temperature ductility and
workability at high temperatures, both of which reduce their practical usage.
In the as-cast condition, the TiAl alloy is quite brittle, but by proper thermo-mechanical treatment, ductility
greater than 2% can be achieved. Thus, to establish a suitable schedule for the thermo-mechanical process, one
needs to understand deformation behavior of such alloys. Recent efforts to study deformation of fully or nearly
fully lamellar TiAl alloys include research on Ti–43Al–4Nb–1Mo–0.1B [5],T i–43Al–4Nb–1.4W [6],T i–45Al–
5.4V–3.6Nb–0.3Y [7] and Ti-43Al-9V [8] alloys. Dynamic recrystallization of the lamellar structure is widely
accepted as a major mechanism of softening mechanism during TiAl hot deformation. It can also assist in
reﬁning alloy microstructure and improve its deformability at high temperatures.
However, deformation behavior of TiAl alloys during secondary processing is reported very seldomly.
Dynamic recrystallization during primary deformation and subsequent heat treatment generatesﬁne equiaxed
grain microstructures. For example, Liuet al investigated the TiAl alloy and gained a large bar with aﬁne
equiaxed structure after hot extrusion and heat treatment[9]. For TiAl alloys, primary deformation is used to
reﬁne the coarse lamellar colony to obtain a more deformable microstructure, but additional deformation is
required in part forming. Therefore, it is important to focus on deformation processes occurring with TiAl alloys
with an equiaxed structure during secondary high-temperature processing.

Therefore, this paper reports deformation behavior of Ti–43.5Al–3V–2Nb alloy with an equiaxed structure
as well as its mechanical properties at high temperature. We systematically analyzed mechanisms ofﬂow
softening control and hot-deformation behavior. Pancake-like shape of this alloy was fabricated using
isothermal forging process. Nearly fully lamellar(NFL) microstructure was obtained after heat treatments and
the tensile properties were investigated.
2. Experimental material and procedure
The chemical composition of experimental alloy was Ti–43.5Al–3V–2Nb (at%). Cylindrical TiAl ingot, 180 mm
in diameter and 350 mm long, was fabricated in a vacuum induction furnace. The ingot were treated by hot
isostatic pressing(HIP) technique at 1260 °C for 300 min at 170 MPa. Cylindrical metal bars, 165 mm in
diameter and 210 mm long, were cut from these ingots(by electric-discharge) followed by their canning in 304
stainless steel. These canned metal rods were then extruded at 1200°C at 4.5:1 extrusion ratio. After 6 h at
1140 °C, TiAl bars with an equiaxed structure were obtained. Isothermal forging process was performed at
1100 °C and 0.01 s
−1.
Hot isothermal uniaxial compression tests were performed in the 1000°C–1200 °C range using Gleeble-
3800 simulator. Flow curves were obtained for 0.01–10 1 s−1 strain rates. Samples were 12 mm long and 8 mm in
diameter. Each sample was heated at 5°Cs −1 to the desired temperature, held at this temperature for 300 s,
compressed to 50% engineering strain and then quenched immediately. These samples were then dissected in
the direction parallel to the sample surface and along the compression axis. Tensile tests were conducted using
MTS machine with 5×10−4 s−1 strain rate at room temperature and at 650, 700 and 750°C.
Phase compositions were obtained by x-ray diffraction(XRD) using Cu Kα radiation. Microstructure
morphologies of deformed microstructures were inspected using scanning electron microscopy(SEM) coupled
with electron backscatter diffraction(EBSD) as well as by transmission electron microscopy(TEM, using Tecnai
G2 F20). Samples for SEM/EBSD analyses were polished mechanically. For TEM, samples wereﬁrst ground and
then thinned by a twin-jet.

3.4. Microstructural evolution
Cracks at the phase boundaries in samples deformed at 1050°C/10 s−1 (see ﬁgure 6(a)). High-density
dislocation collision and agglomeration occur at phase or grain boundaries at high strain rates, causing stress
concentration and cracking[15] because of difference in deformability ofγ, α2 and B2 phases. Stress at the grain
boundaries caused sample fracture. Stress relaxation because of incompatible deformation of each phase was the
main ﬂow-soft mechanism of the instability regions. Flow instability should be avoided during hot-processing
of TiAl alloys. At 1100 °C/0.01 s
−1 deformation temperature and strain rate, no cracks were observed in the
alloy (see ﬁgure 6(b)), which indicates that concentrated stress was released as strain rate decreased and
temperature increased.
To analyze how microstructure evolved during alloy deformation at 1100°C/0.01 s−1, EBSD analysis of the
alloy microstructure was performed with 0.2-μm step. Deformed microstructure contained 71% ofﬁne γ-phase
grains, 19% of B2-phase grains and 10% ofα2-phase grains (see ﬁgure 7(a)). Volume fraction of high-angle
boundaries increased as the number of recrystallized grains increased[19]. Misorientation angle map(shown in
ﬁgure 7(b)) demonstrates 15°–180°,5 °–15° and 2°–5° angle boundaries marked with blue, green and red lines,
respectively. Angle boundary 2°–15° is a low angle boundary and angle boundary 15°–180° is a high angle
boundary. Volume fractions of high- and low-angle boundaries were 88.9% and 11.1%, respectively. Such large

3.5. Mechanical properties after forging and heat treatment
We used hot-processing map and data on microstructure evolution mechanism to isothermally(at 1100 °C)
forge a pancake at 0.3 mm s
−1 speed and ∼0.005–0.01 s−1 strain rate. As-forged alloy, similarly to the
isothermally compressed alloys, demonstrated uniform equiaxed microstructures(see ﬁgure 8(a)). Average
grain size was∼10.5 μm. Equiaxed microstructure has good deformability, but it is not suitable for serving,
mainly because of its serious softening at high temperature. Numerous studies have shown that the NFL
microstructure has excellent mechanical properties. Figure8(b) shows the SEM image of as-forged Ti-43.5Al-
3V-2Nb alloy after heat treatment at 1235°C/60 min/AC+900 °C/6h /FC. Microstructure contained∼85%
of lamellar colonies and mixture of grains ofγ- and α
2-phases. Average lamellar colony size is 27μm.
Figures 8(c) and (d) show the TEM images of the heat-treated alloy, the inter-lamellar spacing was 92 nm by
statistical analysis.
