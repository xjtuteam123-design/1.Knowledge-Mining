ABSTRACT
Niobium-silicon-based alloys are lightweight ultra-high-temperature composite materials with great application 
potential, capable of withstanding temperatures as high as 1200 – 1400 
◦
C. This study investigates the effects of 
various process conditions in electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) additive manufacturing on the 
compressive strengths of niobium-silicon-based alloys at room and high temperatures. The study explores how 
residual stress, silicide fraction, and grain size contribute to the compressive strength of a niobium-silicon alloy 
across the temperature range of room temperature to 1250 
◦
C. For the first time, experiments identified 1030 
◦
C 
as a balance temperature for mechanical performance, and based on this finding, here proposes process measures 
to enhance compressive strength at different target operating temperatures. This provides a reference for opti -
mizing the compressive strength of intermetallic composites, such as niobium-silicon-based alloys fabricated 
through additive manufacturing to meet specific target temperature requirements.
1. Introduction
Niobium-silicon-based alloys are in situ composites formed by Nb-Si 
intermetallic compounds and a niobium-based solid solution (Nb
SS
) 
[1 – 3] . These alloys exhibit low density (6.6 – 7.2 g/cm³), an exception -
ally high melting point ( ≥ 1750 
◦
C), excellent high-temperature 
strength, and creep resistance, enabling them to withstand tempera -
tures up to 1200 – 1400 
◦
C [4 – 6] . As lightweight ultra-high-temperature 
composite materials, they are promising candidates for application in 
advanced aerospace equipment [7 – 9] .
Over the past decades, research on niobium-silicon alloys has pri -
marily focused on optimizing their composition, with more than 20 
alloying elements introduced into the system [6,10,11] . For example, 
elements such as Zr, Ti, Al, and Cr have been added to improve oxidation 
resistance [12 – 17] , while Mo and C have been employed to further 
enhance high-temperature mechanical properties [18 – 21] . Regarding 
their fabrication, traditional casting processes often lead to the forma -
tion of coarse silicides [22 – 24] , which can compromise material per -
formance [25,26] . In contrast, additive manufacturing (AM) techniques, 
particularly powder bed fusion, can produce fine silicides with sizes in 
the nanometer range, as reported in the literatures [27 – 29] . For 
example, Gao et al. [30] reported that additively manufactured 
Nb-26Ti-16Si-2.2Al-2Cr (at%) samples consist of Nb
3
Si and Nb
SS 
with 
grain witdths of approximately 240 nm and 130 nm, respectively. 
Moreover, these samples exhibit no cracks, as the intrinsic brittleness of 
silicides is effectively mitigated through grain refinement, consistent 
with the findings of Liu et al. [6] . To date, studies on niobium-silicon 
alloys fabricated by powder bed fusion AM have demonstrated finer 
microstructures, improved toughness, higher hardness, and better 
oxidation resistance, highlighting the potential of AM for producing 
crack-free niobium-silicon alloys with improved mechanical properties.
However, research on powder bed fusion AM of niobium-silicon al -
loys remains limited, and studies investigating the influence of process 
parameters on mechanical properties are even scarcer. Specifically, the 
adjustment of processing parameters to improve tensile or compressive 
strength has yet to be thoroughly explored. For electron beam powder 
bed fusion (EB-PBF), the resulting niobium-silicon samples exhibit fine 
grains [30 – 35] , which contribute to grain refinement strengthening at 
room temperature [36 – 38] . Nevertheless, this benefit may diminish at 
high temperatures [39] , necessitating process optimization, such as

This study focuses on the EB-PBF process and investigates the

2. Materials and methods
The experimental material used in this study was spherical niobium- 
silicon alloy powder with a nominal composition of Nb-16Si-10Zr-10Ti- 
3Al-3Hf-3Cr (at%). The powder had an average particle size of 
approximately 109 μ m. Three types of samples were prepared: as-built 
samples fabricated using an EB-PBF additive manufacturing system 
(QBeamLab 200; Quick-Beam Tech. Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), remelted 
samples obtained by repetitively melting the surface of as-built samples 
50 times with an electron beam, and heat-treated (HT) samples sub -
jected to vacuum heat treatment at 1500 
◦
C for 100 hours, followed by 
furnace cooling to room temperature. The EB-PBF processing parame -
ters are detailed in Table 1 .
SEM specimens were prepared by mechanical polishing. EBSD 
specimens were prepared by mechanical polishing and Ar-ion polishing 
(IM4000II, Hitachi). TEM specimens were mechanically polished to 
50 – 60 μ m and ion milling to 50 – 100 nm.
Compression test samples were cylindrical with a diameter of 4 mm 
and a height of 7 mm. These samples were extracted from the three types 
of materials using electrical discharge machining (EDM), with the 
sampling direction perpendicular to the build direction. Room temper -
ature tests followed the GB/T 7314 – 2017 standard at a strain rate of 
10

High-temperature tests were conducted at 700 
◦

◦

1250

C under the same standard and strain rate of 10

LE5105 universal testing machine. For each temperature condition, 
three tests were conducted on each type of sample. The compressive 
strength from each test was recorded by the testing machine, and the 
results were used to generate compressive strength-temperature curves.
3. Results and discussion
As shown in Fig. 1 , the microstructures of niobium-silicon alloys 
under three processing conditions were characterized. The as-built 
samples ( Fig. 1 a – g) consisted of Nb
SS
, Nb ₃ Si, and γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ , with Nb
SS 
exhibiting a clear columnar grain tendency. The remelted samples 
( Fig. 1 h, i) retained the same phase composition, but the additional 
energy input from the remelting process disrupted the preferential 
growth direction of the columnar grains, resulting in more equiaxed 
grains.
In the HT samples ( Fig. 1 j, k), the phase composition was Nb
SS 
and 
γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ , with equiaxed grains. It is because during the high-temperature 
heat treatment, the high-temperature metastable phase Nb ₃ Si formed in 
the rapid solidification underwent a eutectoid decomposition reaction. 
Due to the addition of 10 at% Ti and Zr in the alloy, the formation of 
α -Nb ₅ Si ₃ (tetragonal D8
l 
structure) was suppressed, while the formation 
of γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ (hexagonal D8
8 
structure) was promoted, as reported by Li 
et al. [40] . Therefore, the decomposition products were Nb
SS 
and 
γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ . After 100 hours of heat treatment, Nb ₃ Si completely decom -
posed, resulting in a dual-phase microstructure of Nb
SS
/ γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ . Addi -
tionally, silicides precipitated and grew from the columnar Nb
SS
, 
eventually leading to an equiaxed grain structure.
In niobium-silicon alloys, compressive strength is primarily 
contributed by silicides, with both the fraction and average grain size of 
silicides influencing the compressive strength. Therefore, Fig. 2 presents 
EBSD characterization of the grain structures in the three types of 
samples. The grain orientation maps shown in Fig. 2 a – c indicate that the 
columnar grain tendency gradually weakens from the as-built state to 
the remelted state and further to the HT state. Combined with the 
microstructure observations in Fig. 1 , it can be confirmed that the Nb
SS 
transitions from columnar to equiaxed grains, while the changes in sil -
icides are mainly reflected in their average grain size and fraction. 
Fig. 2 d – f provide statistical data on the grain size of silicides in the three 
types of samples. Meanwhile, Fig. 3 presents the silicide fractions for the 
different processing states, with all statistics summarized in Table 2 .
Compared to the silicide grain size in the as-built state ( Fig. 2 e), the 
average grain size in the remelted state ( Fig. 2 d) is similar, with 
approximately 85 % of the silicide grains being smaller than 0.5 μ m. 
However, more than 70 % of the grains in the remelted state are smaller 
than 0.25 μ m. As a result, the average grain size of silicides in the 
remelted state remains at the nanometer scale, slightly smaller than that 
of the as-built state due to the relatively larger silicide grain size in the 
heat-affected zones of the as-built samples. For the HT state ( Fig. 2 f), the 
silicides exhibit significant grain growth, with their average grain size 
increasing by approximately 25 times compared to the as-built and 
remelted states. This substantial difference arises from the prolonged 
grain growth of silicides during the extended heat treatment process.
Additionally, the fraction of silicides changed significantly before 
and after heat treatment. In the as-built state ( Fig. 3 a, b) and the 
remelted state ( Fig. 3 c, d), the silicide fractions were similar, particu -
larly in terms of distribution and fraction, which closely resembled the 
silicide characteristics in the heat-affected zones of the as-built samples. 
In contrast, for the HT state, the silicide fraction increased from 16.02 % 
to 50.78 %, a 217 % increase. This was due to the nucleation and growth 
of a large amount of Nb ₃ Si during heat treatment, which simultaneously 
decomposed into Nb
SS 
and γ -Nb ₅ Si ₃ , resulting in a substantial increase in 
the silicide fraction, as shown in Figs. 3 e and 3 f. In summary, while the 
silicide fraction and average grain size showed little change between the 
as-built and remelted states, the HT samples exhibited significant in -
creases in both silicide fraction and average grain size.
The compressive strength of the three sample states was tested 
within a temperature range of room temperature to 1250 ℃ , as shown in 
Fig. 4 . It can be observed that the compressive strength of all three 
samples decreases with increasing temperature. At room temperature, 
the order of compressive strength is: remelted > as-built > HT. As the 
temperature rises, the compressive strength of the remelted sample re -
mains the highest. However, at 1030 ℃ , the compressive strengths of the 
as-built and HT samples become similar. Beyond this temperature, the 
HT sample exhibits higher compressive strength than the as-built sam -
ple. At the maximum tested temperature of 1250 ℃ , the compressive 
strength order changes to: remelted > HT > as-built.
The difference in compressive strength variation with temperature 
between the HT and as-built samples is primarily attributed to the 
varying contributions of silicide fraction and grain size to the strength at 
different temperatures. At lower temperatures (e.g., below 1030 ℃ ), the 
fine-grain strengthening effect from smaller silicide grains contributes 
more significantly to compressive strength than the silicide fraction. 
This is consistent with the well-established Hall-Petch effect, exhibiting

Therefore, the as-built sample, with fewer but finer silicide grains, ex -
hibits greater strength than the HT sample. However, as the temperature 
increases (e.g., above 1030 ℃ ), high temperature grain boundary sliding 
mainly influences the deformation [43] . It can be speculated that for the 
finer as-built samples, due to the high density of grain boundary, a large 
number of cracks initiate from the grain boundaries and spread in the 
Nbss and at the grain boundaries during the compressive testing. At the 
same time, the fraction of silicides becomes a more critical factor in 
determining compressive strength. This leads to higher strength in the 
HT sample, which has a significantly increased silicide fraction after 
treatment.
For the remelted sample, its silicide fraction and grain size are 
similar to those of the as-built sample. However, the increase in 
compressive strength compared to the as-built sample arises from the 
tensile residual stress introduced by the remelting process [44,45] . The 
faster solidification during remelting results in the accumulation of re -
sidual stress, which contributes approximately 200 MPa to the 
compressive strength. Even at 1250 ℃ , the remelted sample maintains 
higher compressive strength than the HT sample, indicating that the 
strengthening effect from residual stress surpasses that of increased 
silicide fraction.
This mechanism is summarized in Fig. 5 . As the testing temperature 
rises, the contribution of residual stress to compressive strength remains 
nearly constant at around 200 MPa. In contrast, the contribution of 
grain refinement to compressive strength decreases, while the contri -
bution of increased silicide fraction grows. At 1030 ℃ , these contribu -
tions become balanced, beyond which the influence of silicide fraction 
dominates the strength improvement.
Based on this understanding, the following recommendations can be 
made. If target operating temperatures are below 1030 ℃ , the 
compressive strength of electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) Nb- 
Si alloys can be enhanced by employing remelting techniques or 
adjusting process parameters to refine silicide grains, whereas for target 
operating temperatures above 1030 ℃ , remelting or heat treatment to 
increase the silicide fraction and grain size should be considered to 
improve compressive strength.

Si alloys fabricated by EB-PBF under three different processing condi -
tions. It examined the changes in the contributions of residual stress, 
silicide fraction, and grain size to the compressive strength of the Nb-Si 
alloy with a nominal composition of Nb-16Si-10Zr-10Ti-3Al-3Hf-3Cr (at

balance temperature of 1030 ℃ was identified. At this temperature, 
considering high temperature deformation mechanism, the contribu -

strength tend to be the same, so as called becoming balanced. Based on 
these findings, process strategies for enhancing the compressive strength

• For temperatures below the critical balance temperature (1030 ℃ ): 
Compressive strength can be improved through remelting process or 
refining silicide grains.
• For temperatures above the critical balance temperature (1030 ℃ ): 
Compressive strength can be enhanced through remelting process or 
increasing the silicide fraction via heat treatment.
These recommendations provide valuable guidance for tailoring the 
properties of Nb-Si alloys and other intermetallic composites to meet 
specific high temperature application requirements.
