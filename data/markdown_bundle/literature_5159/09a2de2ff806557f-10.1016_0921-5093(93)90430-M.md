Abstract 
The d-electron concept was applied to the design of nickel-based single-crystal superalloys. This concept was devised on 
the basis of molecular orbital calculations for transition-metal-based alloys. Two calculated parameters were mainly 
utilized in this alloy design. One is the d-orbital energy level (Md) of alloying transition elements and the other is the bond 
order (Bo), which is a measure of the covalent bond strength between atoms. Using these parameters, accurate prediction 
is possible for the appearance of the 7 + 7' eutectic phase, the topologically close-packed phases and the a-W phase in the 
alloys. Also, the strengthening of both the y and y' phases in the alloys can be treated with these parameters. On the basis 
of this concept, single-crystal superalloys were designed successfully. Six kinds of designed alloys lay in the compositional 
range Ni- 10mol.%Cr- 12mol,%Al- 1.5mol.%Ti-( 1.8-2.7)mol.%Ta-( 1.8- 2.6)mol.%W-(0.6-0.9)mol.%Mo-0.25mol.%Re. 
It was confirmed experimentally that these designed alloys had excellent creep rupture properties, high hot corrosion 
resistance, low density and low material cost. In this respect the designed alloys are much superior to any single-crystal 
superalloys published in the literature to date. 
1. Introduction 
A number of single-crystal superalloys have been 
developed in several countries during the past decade 
[1 ]. For example, the first single-crystal turbine aerofoil 
alloy, PWA 1480, entered service in 1982 and since 
then has successfully accumulated a service life of more 
than five million flight hours in commercial and mili- 
tary engines. In order to further improve the alloy 
capabilities that are needed to meet the turbine aerofoil 
and military engine requirements for the 1990s, a 
second-generation single-crystal alloy, PWA1484, has 
been developed by Pratt and Whitney [2, 3]. Also, 
CMSX-4/-4G and SRR99 have been developed by the 
Cannon-Muskegon Corporation [4] and Rolls Royce 
Ltd. [5] respectively. 
However, most superalloys have been developed 
more or less relying on a number of trial-and-error 
experiments and therefore both the money and time 
spent have been extraordinarily high for alloy develop- 
ment. A major breakthrough has been strongly desired

In response to such a great demand, several methods

multicomponent alloys. 
On the other hand, recently a molecular orbital 
calculation (the discrete variational (DV)-Xa cluster 
method) has been applied to the characterization of the 
properties of transition-metal-based alloys. It has been 
shown that alloying effects of elements on the alloy 
properties can be predicted using two calculated 
parameters, Md and Bo [17], where Md is the d-orbital 
energy level of alloying transition elements and Bo is 
the bond order indicating the covalent bond strength 
between atoms. By using these two parameters, for 
example, the phase stability can be treated quantita-

new method to the design of nickel-based single-crystal

2. DV-Xa cluster calculations and new alloying 
parameters 
The principal alloying elements of superaUoys are 
transition elements with unpaired d-electrons. The 
high cohesive energy of transition metals and their 
alloys can be attributed mainly to the covalent bond 
strength of the d-electrons in them. Therefore it is 
important to understand the nature of the d-electron 
bond between the mother element and alloying ele- 
ments in superalloys. 
There are several theoretical approaches to the 
simulation of the d-electron state in metals and alloys. 
For instance, the band theory is a very common 
approach. However, this approach seems less con- 
venient for examining the alloying behaviour of indi- 
vidual elements, because it treats the collective mode of 
electrons in a three-dimensional periodic potential 
field. The cluster theory is more suitable for the study 
of the local electronic state around each alloying ele- 
ment in the mother metal. 
We have calculated the electronic structure of nickel 
alloys by the DV-Xa cluster method [17]. The cluster 
model used was [MNiI2A6], where M represents the 
alloying elements. Both the d-orbital energy level 
(hereafter called Md) of alloying transition metal M 
and the bond order between M and Ni atoms (called 
Bo) were determined for various alloying elements [17, 
23]. These parameters were calculated with the alloy 
cluster [MNi~2A6] consisting of an alloying element M 
and the surrounding mother elements. Therefore the 
alloying effect is naturally included in the parameters. 
The Md values for the non-transition metals AI and Si 
were determined empirically by extrapolating the data 
for transition metals and the validity of this procedure 
has been confirmed by examining a variety of ternary 
phase diagrams [17, 23, 24]. 
The Bo parameter describes the covalent bond 
strength between atoms and correlates with several 
physical properties of alloys [17]. In particular, Bo is 
related in some way to the strength of alloys and the 
high Bo elements are known to be the principal alloy- 
ing elements in most structural alloys [17, 18].

taking the compositional average. Mdt and Bo t are

3. Fabrication conditions and target properties 
relevant to single-crystal superalloys 
There are several conditions and properties to be 
optimized in the design of single-crystal superalloys. 
Firstly, the condition for single-crystal growth can be 
expressed as G/R >--A T/D [25], where G is the temper- 
ature gradient, R is the crystal growth rate, A T is the 
melting range and D is the diffusion coefficient. The 
melting range AT is defined as Liquidus-Tsolidus [26], 
where Ttiquidus and T~olidos are the liquidus and solidus 
temperatures of the alloy respectively. If G/R and D 
are fixed, a lower A T is more suitable for growing 
single crystals [26-28]. In addition, a proper solution 
heat treatment is needed to homogenize the grown 
crystal. Since this heat treatment is performed in the 7 
single-phase region, the 7 single phase has to exist 
stably in a certain temperature range expressed as 
Tsolidus-T~,,solvus [26, 29], where Ty,~oTvu~ is the 7' solvus 
temperature of the alloy. This temperature range is 
called the heat treatment window (HTW). 
Furthermore, for single-crystal superalloys used for 
turbine blades and other high temperature applica- 
tions, it is important to have an excellent combination 
of creep rupture life, creep rupture elongation and hot 
corrosion resistance. Both the creep rupture life and 
the hot corrosion resistance are closely related to the 
microstructural homogeneity and also to the phase 
stability of alloys [19, 30]. In other words, alloy com- 
positions should be controlled so as to suppress the 
formation of the 7 + 7' eutectic phase (hereafter called 
the eutectic 7' phase), the topologically close-packed 
(TCP) phases (e.g. the p and o phases) and the a-W 
phase in alloys [31]. Needless to say, low density and 
low material cost are also desirable for these alloys. 
These conditions and target values of the properties 
employed in this study can be summarized as follows. 
(1) The melting range AT should be less than 50 K 
for single-crystal growth. 
(2) The HTW should be more than 20 K for heat 
treatment. 
(3) The creep rupture life and creep rupture elonga- 
tion should be more than 3.6 Ms and 10% respectively 
under a constant stress of 137.2 MPa at 1313 K [32].

strength of superalloys can be explained by the lattice 
misfit between the 7 and 7' phases. However, less 
attention has been directed towards the strengthening 
by the individual 7 and 7' phases existing in single- 
crystal superalloys [19]. Thus in the present alloy 
design this strengthening was considered to be of 
primary importance in improving the creep strength.

6. Design of promising new alloys 
6. I. Alloy design 
The alloy design was performed with the aid of the 
criteria obtained in Section 5. These criteria are sum- 
marized again as follows. 
( 1 ) Strengthening of both the 7 and 7' phases: Ta, W 
and Mo should be added as much as possible. 
(2) Microstructural homogeneity: Mdt should be 
less than about 0.99 in order to suppress the formation

account the scatter of the data shown in Fig. 3, it is 
apparent that the critical Md t value ranges from 0.985

6.2. Microstructure 
The results on the microstructural homogeneity, the
