Atom Probe Microscopy of Strengthening Effects in Alloy 718
Felix Theska 1, Simon Peter Ringer 2 and Sophie Primig 1*
1School of Materials Science and Engineering, University of New South Wales, Sydney, New South Wales 2052, Australia and 2Australian Centre for Microscopy &

Abstract
Polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys for aerospace and power generation applications are often precipitation hardened to achieve strength-
ening at elevated temperatures. Here, atom probe microscopy has become an essential tool to study the complex morphology of nanoscale
precipitates. This study focuses on Alloy 718, which is hardened by semi-coherent, ordered γ
′ (Ni3(Al, Ti)) and γ′′ (Ni3(Nb)) particles.
According to previous research, these particles often occur as duplets or triplets with a stacking sequence dependent on prior processing.
This creates various interfaces with a strong impact on the mechanical properties, highlighting the importance of quantitative studies which
are challenging with electron microscopy. We present atom probe data reconstruction and analysis approaches particularly suited for
precipitation hardened superalloys. While voltage atom probe allows for an accurate reconstruction, the acquired data volume is often lim-
ited. Laser-assisted atom probe provides statistically significant data, but the loss of crystallographic information requires correlation with
voltage-mode datasets. We further describe an advanced iso-surface method where initially arbitrarily chosen concentration thresholds of
Al + Ti for γ
′ and Nb for γ′′ particles are optimized. Recognizing the importance of the precipitate stacking order, the different types of
precipitate interfaces are quantified, and these methods may be applicable to other engineering alloys.
Key words: atom probe crystallography, atom probe microscopy, Ni-based superalloy, precipitation, thermo-mechanical processing

Introduction
Polycrystalline Ni-based superalloy parts are industrially manu-
factured by thermo-mechanical processing, including forging
and heat treatment. These high-performance materials contain
large alloying contents of up to 40 wt.% of elements such as Fe,
Cr, Mo, Al, Ti, and Nb for applications in high-temperature
parts of jet engines or gas turbines (Pollock & Tin, 2006; Bagot
et al., 2017). High-temperature strength, at temperatures of some-
times up to ∼80% of the homologous melting point under high
mechanical workloads, is often achieved via the controlled precip-
itation of high-temperature stable L1
2-ordered γ′ (Ni3(Al, Ti))
particles, metastable DO 22-ordered γ′′ (Ni3(Nb)) particles or a
combination of both (Cozar & Pineau, 1973; Cao & Kennedy,
2004; Reed, 2006). The design of stronger materials for
fuel-efficient next-generation aircraft engines requires a quantita-
tive understanding of the processing –structure–property relation-
ship, entailing advanced microscopy methods due to their
complex nanoscale morphology. However, studies of γ
′/γ′′
co-precipitates which are commonly found in Alloy 718 are chal-
lenging using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and
energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Blavette et al., 2000;
Zickler et al., 2009; Whitmore et al., 2012; Detor et al., 2018).
Here, atom probe microscopy (APM) represents a powerful
tool for the detailed study of the complex nanoscale morphology
of superalloys, even beyond the standard three-dimensional char-
acterization with quasi-atomic resolution (Gault et al., 2012a). For
example, cluster search algorithms provide quantitative insights
into clustering of solute atoms by their Pearson coefficients
(Moody et al., 2008; Devaraj et al., 2014). Precipitates have been
studied revealing their morphology and chemical composition
in materials including Al-, Fe-, and Ni-alloys (Blavette et al.,
2000; Jiang et al., 2017). In these cases, proximity histogram
and iso-surface methods have been widely used (Miller, 2001;
Hornbuckle et al., 2015; Drexler et al., 2018). Utilizing the crystal-
lographic information present in APM datasets, increasingly
referred to as atom probe crystallography (APC), enables
advanced reconstruction approaches to the atom probe data,
improving the accuracy of subsequent analyses (Gault et al.,
2012b; Cairney et al., 2015; Breen et al., 2017).
In the current study, γ
′ and γ′′ particles in Alloy 718 were stud-
ied after two common industrial processing routes using APM:
“conventional processing” (SA) relates to forging followed by sol-
ution annealing and aging. However, significant yield strength
improvements have been reported after an alternative process
called “direct aging ” (DA) (forging and aging only), but the
detailed microstructural mechanisms remained unknown for
decades (Krueger, 1989; Oberwinkler et al., 2016; Drexler et al.,
2018). Nb, Al, and Ti in Alloy 718 promote the precipitation of
both γ
′ and γ′′ particles, commonly occurring as duplets and trip-
lets (Miller et al., 1999; Geng et al., 2007; Detor et al., 2018;

particular interest, as the corresponding interfaces created provide

precipitate/matrix and precipitate/precipitate interfaces. Here,
accurate APM data reconstruction is required, which can be facil-

datasets and APC routines (Gault et al., 2012b; Larson et al.,
2013). However, the acquired volumes are often limited due to
premature sample fracture. Thermal evaporation via laser-assisted
atom probe enables data acquisition of larger volumes required
for obtaining statistically significant data (Gault et al., 2010;
Viskari & Stiller, 2011). Reconstruction in this situation is chal-
lenging, due to thermally activated surface migration and the cre-
ation of additional charge states, which tend to eliminate the
presence of discernible crystallographic information in the field
evaporation images (Gault et al., 2010; Wallace et al., 2018).
Therefore, alternative approaches to accurately reconstruct laser-
assisted APM data from superalloys remain of major interest.
The current authors recently developed useful APM routines
for Alloy 718 in the course of a program of research on the process-
ing–structure–property relationships during SA versus DA. Our
comprehensive microstructural model of the multiscale micro-
structural evolution and strengthening mechanisms has been pub-
lished recently (Theska et al., 2018). The current contribution is a
companion paper that sets out to address the issues raised above
for APM studies of the nanoscale precipitates in Alloy 718.
Materials and Methods
Triple-remelted, as-forged, and water quenched Alloy 718 mate-
rial was provided by voestalpine Böhler Aerospace GmbH &
Co. KG, Austria. The chemical composition is provided in
Table 1 . Cylindrical billets ( l = 180 mm and r = 127 mm) were
industrially forged at 1,000°C via three sub-steps to pancake-
shaped disks ( l = 72 mm and r = 200 mm). Samples were cut to
7 × 7 × 20 mm from identical radial positions of ∼142 mm from
the center of a selected pancake for aging under an
Ar-atmosphere in a laboratory tube furnace. The detailed thermal
treatments are provided in Table 2 . All samples were water-
quenched following aging. To study the nanoscale precipitate
morphology with APM, the two fully aged conditions SA and
DA are compared. Blanks of 0.5 × 0.5 × 15 mm were cut for
APM characterization with an Accutom-50 (Struers Australia,
Milton, Queensland, Australia) and a SiC cutting wheel. Sharp
tips were prepared via the standard two-step electropolishing
technique and cleaned with methanol (Gault et al., 2012a). A
CAMECA straight flight-path local electrode atom probe LEAP
4000X (CAMECA Instruments Inc., Madison, WI, USA) Si was
used in voltage- and laser-assisted mode. Temperature, pulse
rate, and target evaporation rate were kept constant at 50 K,
200 kHz, and 1.0%, respectively.
In voltage-mode, the pulse fraction was 20%, providing data-
sets of up to 16.3 million atoms for the SA condition and 5.1 mil-
lion atoms for the DA condition. The laser energy was calibrated
based on datasets containing up to 1.0 million atoms for laser
energies between 150 and 15 pJ. Uninterrupted experiments at a
laser energy of 50 pJ produced datasets containing 57.1 million
atoms for the SA condition and 60.1 million atoms for the DA
condition. Four cross-sectional slices of 5 × 45 × 500 nm per data-
set were extracted from this data for subsequent evaluation.
Data analysis was carried out via IV AS 3.6.6 software by
CAMECA. Voltage-mode atom probe data reconstruction was
based on the crystallographic information, such as poles and zone
lines, as observed in field evaporation images. Self-consistent values
for the image compression factor (ICF) and field reduction factor
(kF) were obtained, following routines provided elsewhere
(Moody et al., 2009; Breen et al., 2015).
The iso-surface method was based on routines introduced by
Miller et al. (1999) and Drexler et al. (2018). Via enhancements dis-
cussed in the section “An Enhanced Iso-Surface Method for
Optimized Precipitate Description”, the iso-surface concentration
thresholds for theγ
′ and γ′′ particles were derived. The spatial extent
in local x-, y-, and z-coordinates was extracted from at least ten par-
ticles in voltage-mode APM data to calculate the relative dimensions
x/y, y/z,a n d z/x. The interface characterization was achieved with
dataset cross-sections using FIJI ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012).
Interfaces betweenγ
′/γ-matrix, γ′′/γ-matrix, and γ′/γ′′ were counted
for triplet particles, simplifying the calculation of interfaces per triplet.
“Mixed-mode” APM contained data from the very same sample
tip exposed to voltage-mode acquisition, followed by laser-assisted
acquisition. Therefore, roughly 10 million atoms were collected in
voltage-mode if no premature tip fracture occurred. After complete
acquisition, operation was switched to laser-assisted mode, keeping
x-, y-, and z-positions of the sample tip constant. The following
data acquisition was carried out until tip fracture occurred.

Laser-Assisted Atom Probe Microscopy
Laser Energy Calibration
It is well-known that laser-assisted APM causes spatial or chemical
distortions via thermally induced surface migration or peak over-
laps (Viskari & Stiller, 2011; Araullo-Peters et al., 2015). The ini-
tial, arbitrarily chosen, laser energy of 180 pJ in this study
resulted in spontaneous evaporation events and a heavily distorted
precipitate morphology. Calibrating the laser energy between 15
and 150 pJ for optimal mass resolving power and charge state
ratio revealed two opposing trends, as shown in Figure 2 . This
plot of the laser energy, Ni
2+/Ni+ charge state ratio, and mass res-
olution at full width at half maximum (FWHM) shows that low
laser energies promote optimal charge states close to voltage-mode
APM. However, high laser energies appear to provide a more ben-
eficial mass resolution at the FWHM. For improved visualization,
the values for the Ni
2+/Ni+ charge state ratio and FWHM were nor-
malized using the measured maximum value as a reference and
equal 1. The summary function “FWHM + (Ni
2+/Ni+)” indicates
an optimal laser energy between 50 and 60 pJ for this particular
atom probe design and alloy. However, in this energy range, no
crystallographic poles could be identified in the field evaporation
images, highlighting the need for an alternative reconstruction
approach besides the above APC-enabled method.

Description
In the following enhanced iso-surface method for optimized pre-
cipitate description, cylindrical regions of interest (ROI), as
shown in Figure 3a , were positioned perpendicularly to the γ
′/γ′′
interface through at least ten duplet particles to achieve sharp
concentration profiles. This allows the subsequent characteriza-
tion of non-centrosymmetric γ′/γ′′ co-precipitate structures.
Figures 3b –3d provide the concentration profiles as individual
charts in green (Ni), turquoise (Al + Ti), and brown (Nb) for
one exemplary γ

tion profiles are represented as corresponding black curves.
Global maxima and minima provide the point of the largest con-
centration gradient and thus, the iso-surface concentration
threshold. Averaging the upper and lower thresholds as in
Figures 3b –3d provided the iso-surface concentration thresholds
presented in Table 4 . The γ-matrix is characterized by a Ni con-
centration less than 59.0 ± 1.6 at.%, while γ
′ and γ′′ particles are
represented by Al + Ti and Nb exceeding 10.5 ± 0.7 and 13.0 ±
0.4 at.%, respectively. These error margins correspond to the stan-
dard deviation obtained by averaging at least ten duplet particles.
The following laser-assisted APM reconstructions and corre-
sponding iso-surfaces in Figures 4a and 4b were devised applying
these values from Table 4. Here, uncropped reconstructions of the
SA and DA conditions present the γ
′ and γ′′ particles as turquoise
and brown iso-surfaces. Different length, shank angle, and diam-
eter correlate with the voltage sequence representing the actual tip
shape. Nanoscale precipitates of γ

sandwich-like γ′′/γ′/γ′′ or γ′/γ′′/γ′ triplets, and occasionally qua-

condition, but this requires further analysis for reliable quantifica-
tion. The tip volume was approximated as a truncated cone
defined by the initial and final tip radius at the point of fracture.
The average volume fractions for the γ

surfaces. Therefore, γ

as 17.64 ± 1.13 and 11.28 ± 0.42 vol.% for the SA condition, and
18.61 ± 1.18 and 14.02 ± 0.47 vol.% for the DA condition,

average γ

ferences in the precipitate properties depending on the process

“Precipitate Morphology ”.

Interface Method
Inspection of Figure 4 leads to the intriguing suggestion that the
triplet precipitate stacking sequence is dependent on the process
condition, SA or DA. Quantifying the triplet precipitate stacking
sequence in Figures 4a and 4b (see the section “An Enhanced
Iso-Surface Method for Optimized Precipitate Description ”)
required the development of a method to record the relative frac-
tion of interfaces between the precipitated γ
′ and γ′′ particles, and
the γ-matrix. Figure 6 presents both types of triplet stacking
sequence variants in the form of γ′′/γ′/γ′′ and γ′/γ′′/γ′′ triplets,
which were present under both conditions, SA and DA. The
stacking sequence can be quantified via counting the interfaces
between γ
′/γ-matrix, γ′′/γ-matrix, and γ′/γ′′ per individual triplet.
Therefore, this 3D problem was simplified by a transformation
into two-dimensions via cross-sectioning and manual image
segmentation with ImageJ, as described in Figure 7a showing a
tomographic dataset in side view versus Figure 7b showing a
cross-sectional view of this dataset. In the segmentation process,
cross-sections representing γ
′ or γ′′ precipitates in their iso-
surfaces were first treated independently. This required the binar-
ization of the cross-sectional image into either γ
′ precipitates
(black) and matrix (white) or γ′′ precipitates (black) and matrix
(white). The enclosed precipitate area was filled with the “fill
holes” operation or, in case of edge particles, carried out manu-
ally. The individual γ′ or γ′′ binary images were then merged as
color channels (e.g., γ′ “magenta” and γ′′ “cyan’) to identify the
γ′/γ′′ interfaces as overlapping regions. Interfaces between precip-
itate and matrix were identified as transition to the white area sur-
rounding the colored particles. Therefore, the iso-surfaces were
extracted from separate cross-sections and colored individually
as shown in Figure 7b. Only triplets were considered for counting
the interfaces. Interface ratios per triplet of 1.30 ± 0.05 and 1.73 ±
0.09 γ
′/γ-matrix, and 1.70 ± 0.05 and 1.27 ± 0.09 γ′′/γ-matrix were
recorded. This dependency of the thermo-mechanical processing
and triplet stacking sequence is summarized in the section
“Precipitate Morphology ”.

Discussion
Correlation Between Voltage-Mode and Laser-Assisted Atom
Probe
Based on the point projection model describing ion flight paths
from the tip toward the detector screen, crystallographic poles
such as observed in Figure 1b can be correlated with a
pseudo-stereographic FCC (face centered cubic) lattice projection
(Miller & Forbes, 2014; De Geuser & Gault, 2016). Typically, low-
index crystallographic orientations result in more pronounced tra-
jectory aberrations causing more significant intensity deviations
in the field evaporation image (Miller, 2000). This supports that
the (111), (110), (011), and (020) indices in the present work
are the best fit. Methods using the presence of such crystallo-
graphic poles have been developed in recent years and are now
widely accepted for various crystalline materials (Miller, 2000;
Marquis & Hyde, 2010; Gault et al., 2012a; Marceau et al., 2015).
Mass spectrum ranging as detailed in Figure 1c resulted in an
assessment of the alloy bulk chemical composition as given in
Table 3 . Minor variations between these values and those in
Table 1 concerning the Nb and Al content most likely originate
from the presence of larger, Nb-rich δ-phase precipitates
(Campbell, 2006) not included in these analyses. Similar
mass-to-charge ratios as those reported here were identified in
previous APM studies of Alloy 718 (Blavette et al., 2000;
Strondl et al., 2008). Laser pulsing introduces additional charge
states such as Ni
+ and Nb +, potentially altering the acquired
bulk chemical composition. Peak broadening and overlapping
may be the cause, as demonstrated for an Al-based alloy (Sha &
Ringer, 2009). Therefore, careful laser energy selection is required.
While laser calibration was carried out in previous studies by
similar means, differences in the absolute laser energy may arise
from varying instrument generations and details in the laser
pulsing design [compare the 50 pJ used in this study with the
200–700 pJ used in the study by Viskari & Stiller ( 2011)].
Most contemporary APM data calibration approaches rely on
the presence of crystallographic features, or some knowledge of
the tip geometry obtained via high-resolution scanning or TEM
(Arslan et al., 2008; Gault et al., 2012b; Diercks et al., 2017).
Alternatively, the precipitate morphology-based calibration has
been successfully demonstrated for large plate-shaped precipitates
in an Al –Mg–Li alloy in the case where known precipitate/matrix
crystallographic orientation relationships are available (Gault
et al., 2012b). Similarly, the calibration approach presented here
relies on morphological features in the absence of crystallographic
information in field evaporation images. A comparison of the
APM-based particle morphology observed here to previously
published TEM-based observations is provided in Table 5 . The
results of the current study correlate well, although variations
are evident, and these are likely caused by different alloy chemis-
tries and aging treatments, as well as the intrinsic differences
between these microscopy techniques. Specifically, it may be
expected that local magnification effects will deteriorate the accu-
racy of the precipitate morphology measurements available in
APM (Gault et al., 2012a).

the interface are often absent. Thus “smearing” of these interfaces
is a common atom probe artifact and can reach as far as ±1 nm
from the centerline of the interface. In this case, the proximity
histogram method is an alternative technique (Hellman et al.,
2000, 2002). For reference, the zero-crossing has been used as
the interface concentration. However, studies on low-partitioning
precipitates have demonstrated that this may not correlate with
the actual interface. Further, this requires an iterative, multi-step
approach (Hornbuckle et al., 2015). Also, non-centrosymmetric
precipitates such as the observed duplets and triplets in Figures
4a and 4b limit the applicability of this method.

Precipitate Morphology
The observations based on the aforementioned techniques are
summarized in Figure 9 , although details concerning the metal-
lurgical interpretation are discussed elsewhere (Theska et al.,
2018). Figure 9a provides the overall volume fraction, indicating
the dependency between the thermo-mechanical processing and
the resultant γ
′ and γ′′ volume fraction. The average particle
size and number density are presented in Figures 9b and 9c as
average radius and particles per nm 3, respectively. While a
dependency between the particle size and process is evident as
increased average γ
′′ radius, no clear relationship exists for the
particle number density. As the precipitation strengthening effects
strongly depend on the particle size, two sample t-tests were car-
ried out to verify changes in the average γ
′ and γ′′ particle size
(Snedecor & Cochran, 1989). In the comparison of SA and DA,
412 γ′ and 327 γ′′ precipitates were observed. Including the aver-
ages and standard deviations, the 95% CI for the γ′ precipitates
was CI = { −0.73 nm; 1.25 nm}, with average particle sizes of
dγ′ (SA) = 7.04 nm to dγ′ (DA) = 6.78 nm. For the γ′′ precipitates
CI = {−2.28 nm; 0.56 nm}, with average particle sizes of dγ′′ (SA)
= 6.11 nm to dγ′′(DA) = 6.79 nm, indicating a rather significant
shift toward larger average γ′′ particles. Finally, Figure 9d shows
the ratio of γ′/γ-matrix, γ′′/γ-matrix, and γ′/γ′′ interfaces. In
Alloy 718, the inverted stacking sequence appears predominant
via the increased fraction of γ
′/γ-matrix interfaces and decreased
amount of γ′′/γ-matrix interfaces following DA. In general, the
impact of thermo-mechanical processing on the γ′ and γ′′ volume
fraction is well-established for Alloy 718, although only high-
resolution studies provide quantitative insight (Oblak et al.,
1974; Alam et al., 2010). We assumed spherical γ
′′ particles in
the current study, whereas a disk-shaped morphology may be
more accurate as shown by visual inspection of Figure 4 and pre-
vious studies (Oblak et al., 1974; Han et al., 1982; Fayman, 1987;
Slama & Abdellaoui, 2000). This simplification must be regarded
as the origin of the large standard deviation as no aspect ratio is
introduced for the γ
′′ particles. No significant changes in the
number density are expected as the aging treatment occurred in
similar time –temperature regimes.
Conclusions
We present several enhancements for the quantitative APM based
analysis of the complex nanoscale γ
′ and γ′′ precipitation in Alloy
718. Voltage-mode APM, although commonly limited by prema-
ture sample fracture, provided a basis for the determination of a
self-consistent precipitate morphology. Statistically more signifi-
cant large-volume datasets were acquired via laser-assisted
APM. The following improvements were used to obtain an accu-
rate reconstruction and self-consistent precipitate morphology in
the absence of crystallographic information in the corresponding
field evaporation images:
