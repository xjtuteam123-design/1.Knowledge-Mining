ABSTRACT 
Mg alloys with superior strength-ductility synergy is highly desired for applications. In this study, 
the as-extruded Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr (JDBM) alloy rod was subjected to single-pass drawing over a range 
of temperature 200 ~ 600 
◦
C to enhance the properties. After drawing, a more homogeneous and 
refined microstructure developed because of dynamic recrystallization (DRX) and dynamic pre -
cipitation (DP). With the increase of drawing temperature, grain sizes increased first and then 
decreased due to the competition of grain nucleation and growth, while the sizes of the secondary 
phase particles varied in the same way. And a nearly basal texture evolved from a rare earth 
texture of the as-extruded sample. The yield strength of the as-drawn samples increased by ~2.2 
times with a sacrifice of elongation to fracture at different level. The high yield strength mainly 
originats from grain boundary and dislocation strengthening. An optimal combination of high 
yield strength (~301 MPa) and good ductility (elongation to fracture of ~19 % and improved 
strain hardening capacity) was obtained after drawing at 500 
◦
C. The yield strength enhancement 
is mainly derived from texture and dislocation strengthening. Grain and secondary phase particle 
refinement, large volume fraction of low angle grain boundaries and reduced geometrically 
necessary dislocations are considered to be beneficial to the good ductility. In addition, a novel 
method has been proposed to fabricate materials with superior strength-ductility synergy by 
deformation with large strain at high temperatures to activate severe DP. 
1. Introduction 
Magnesium (Mg), as the lightest structural metallic materials, has been used in the automobile, aerospace and biomedical in -
dustries, due to its high specific strength, low density, biodegradability and good biocompatibility ( Du et al., 2022 ; Fan et al., 2023 ; 
Huang et al., 2023 ; Xie et al., 2021 ). However, there is always a tradeoff between strength and ductility for Mg alloys, which creates a 
concomitant decrease in ductility during strengthening or vice versa ( Han et al., 2023 a; Ma et al., 2022 ). This imperfection poses a 
bottleneck to their wider applications. Therefore, the preparation of high strength Mg alloys without significant loss in ductility is of

Hitherto, two popular strategies have been applied to achieve Mg alloys with high performance, i.e. grain refinement or secondary 
phase precipitation. As for the latter, precipitates could hinder dislocation motion and promote recrystallization, enhancing the 
mechanical properties significantly ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Shuai et al., 2023 ). However, the introduction of the secondary phase usually 
depends on the addition of alloying elements, which drives up production costs ( Pan et al., 2020 ). And coarse secondary phase particles 
would be introduced via excessive alloying addition, causing severe stress concentration against plasticity ( Zhu et al., 2022 ). 
Comparatively, grain refinement seems to be more favorable, and its advantages can be elaborated into the following several aspects. 
Most directly, the k

implies more pronounced fine-grain strengthening. Moreover, fine grains are conductive to alleviate the mechanical anisotropy of Mg 
alloys by suppressing twinning ( Paramatmuni and Dunne, 2020 ; Tang et al., 2020 ; Zhang and Jiang, 2024 ). Additionally, no or low 
sacrifice of ductility would be yielded for Mg alloys strengthened by grain refinement ( Ma et al., 2022 ), and even an improved ductility 
can be obtained ( Han et al., 2023 b; Luo et al., 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2023 c). The fundamental reason for the ductility improvement 
through grain refinement is the sharp increase in grain boundaries (GBs), which could emit new dislocations and release stress ( Zhang 
et al., 2021 ). With grain refinement to a certain extent, the mobility of GBs (rotation or sliding) also increases significantly, facilitating 
the grain adjustment to a soft orientation for further deformation ( Zheng et al., 2020 ). Therefore, the combination of grain refinement 
and secondary phase tailoring for a given alloy would be more effective for developing high-performance Mg alloy samples. 
Many attempts have been carried out to prepare Mg alloys with fine microstructures and thus high performance, e.g. hot extrusion 
( Zhang et al., 2022 ) and severe plastic deformation (SPD) ( Kasaeian-Naeini et al., 2022 ; Kumar et al., 2015 ; Padhy et al., 2018 ; Zhu 
et al., 2024 ). However, despite effectiveness ( Zhang et al., 2022 ), these methods have their own limitations. Hot extrusion with large 
extrusion ratio is a primary means of mass production. In addition, with a decrease in extrusion temperature, the strength of the 
material increases and the plasticity decreases ( Huang et al., 2018 ). However, extrusion with large extrusion ratio and relatively low 
extrusion temperatures may fail due to the reduced extrudability of alloys and large friction. Conventional SPD techniques, including 
equal channel angular pressing (ECAP), high-pressure torsion (HPT), multi-directional forging (MDF), accumulative rolling bond 
(ARB) and so on, are well known methods to prepare samples with fine or ultrafine grains ( Kasaeian-Naeini et al., 2022 ). But the size of 
the samples processed by SPD are usually limited ( Zhu et al., 2024 ). As a new method to introduce SPD, friction stir processing (FSP) 
can be used on large-size bulk materials ( Kumar et al., 2015 ; Zhu et al., 2024 ). And yet, this technique strongly depends on the special 
tools and fixtures ( Padhy et al., 2018 ). Accordingly, the development of an easy technical route for preparation of fine-grained Mg alloy 
profiles with high performance on an industrial scale remains a great challenge. 
In this work, a patented Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr (JDBM) alloy was used, which exhibits moderate mechanical properties and excellent 
biocompatibility and has been used as bone screws applied to the human body ( Xie et al., 2021 ). In order to achieve large-scale 
transformation of this material, further optimized properties and fewer procedures in industrial production have been desired. 
Hence, a method for fabricating JDBM alloy samples with excellent strength and ductility due to refined microstructure via single-pass 
drawing at elevated temperatures after extrusion, has been developed and described here. The microstructure and tensile properties of 
the studied alloy samples were characterized, and the effect of drawing temperatures has been investigated. Furthermore, the ratio -
nales for high performance of the JDBM alloy after drawing at 500 
◦
C were analyzed and discussed in detail.

2.3. Tensile properties tests 
The uniaxial tensile tests were done on a Zwick/Roell 20 kN machine at ambient temperature and displacement controlled with a 
constant strain rate of 0.001 s 
 1
. Dog-bone shaped specimens with circular cross-sections were cut parallel to the extrusion / drawing 
direction for tensile testing. The gauge length and diameter of the tensile samples were 15 mm and 3 mm, respectively. An exten -

3.1. Microstructure 
3.1.1. Grain size 
Representative SEM images at higher magnification are shown in Fig. 1 , which correspond to the regions marked in Fig. S3. Note 
that the prevalent holes were traces of the secondary phase particles (hereafter called particles) after etching, which can also be 
observed in other works ( Huang et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2022 b). Due to the apparently different microstructure in the transverse and 
longitudinal sections, it is necessary to fully consider both sections to reveal the microstructure evolution. The average grain size was 
measured along the two directions of both sections and the results are listed in Table 1 . 
For the as-extruded sample ( Fig. 1 (a, e)), the grain sizes along transverse and normal direction were nearly identical while the 
grains along extrusion direction were of a subtle elongation. As to the as-drawn samples presented in Fig. 1 (b-d) and (f-h), distinctly 
different microstructure produced in the two sections. In the transverse sections , the grains remained equiaxed with an aspect ratio of 
~1 ( Fig. 1 (b-d) and Table 1 ). Besides, the grain size was reduced to varying degrees. The average size of the grains increased with the 
drawing temperature rising from 200 
◦
C to 350 
◦
C, but severe grain refinement occurred at a higher temperature of 500 
◦
C ( Table 1 ). In 
the longitudinal sections, obvious grain elongation along drawing direction was observed. The elongation was intensified and then 
weakened with the drawing temperature. 
As briefly outlined above, equiaxed grains with reduced sizes were obtained in the transverse sections after drawing while grains 
were elongated in the longitudinal sections. Moreover, the grain sizes along all sample coordinates (transverse, normal and drawing 
directions) increased and then decreased with the increase in drawing temperature. The grains were approximately in cylindrical 
shapes, with different diameters and heights. The true change in grain sizes would be discussed later. 
3.1.2. Dynamic recrystallization 
Grains in sample are categorized as recyrstallized, substructured and deformed grains in terms of internal stress, which was 
examined by EBSD analysis and the results are shown in Fig. 2 (a-d). The fraction content of grains of different types in each sample was 
obtained, as displayed in Fig. 2 (e). The as-extruded samples were mainly composed of recrystallized grains, whereas a large number of 
substructured and deformed grains appeared after drawing. With drawing temperature increasing from 200 
◦
C to 500 
◦

of recrystallized grains increased gradually, while the fraction of deformed grains changed inversely.

diagrams. A small area covered by the black dots in GB maps represented unindexed portion, which is mainly caused by the surface

different twin types in terms of corresponding theory misorientation angles / axes ( Li et al., 2022 b). 
The grains contained in the as-extruded sample were fully DRXed, as displayed in Fig. 2 (a) and Fig. 3 (a). After hot drawing, 
deformed microstructure consisting of elongated grains, and in-grain twin lamellae and substructures was observed ( Fig. 3 (b-d)). 
Typical cases were given in Fig. 3 (f-h). The number of LAGBs increased significantly, and a great deal of LAGBs formed in the grain 
interior, further subdividing the grains. As shown in Fig. 3 (e-h), the fraction of LAGBs increased by about 11 times after drawing and 
was 66.1 %, 63.6 % and 58.9 % for the T200, T350 and T500 samples, respectively. The decrease in LAGBs with drawing temperature 
implies a reduction of “ GB dislocations ” ( Fomin and Mayer, 2020 ; Zhu et al., 2020 ). Twins of different types were statistically analyzed 
and the results are listed in Table S1. The total twin volume fraction of the as-drawn samples was 10.1 %, 11.2 % and 6.9 %, 
respectively, which indicates that the twinning activity increased and subsequently decreased with drawing temperature. The reasons 
behind this have been briefly discussed in the supplementary materials. 
In short, both the fraction of LAGBs and twinning boundaries increased sharply after hot drawing. Moreover, the fractions of LAGBs 
decreased gradually with increasing temperature while the quantity of twins increased and then decreased.

Firstly, it is generally accepted that refined grains can efficiently improve the ductility of Mg and its alloys ( Zhang et al., 2021 ; 
Zhang et al., 2023 d). The T500 sample underwent pronounced grain refinement ( Fig. 10 ), which contributes to uniform 
strain-coordination and good crack resistance with less stress concentration of GBs. Moreover, twins have been found to be potential 
sites for crack nucleation, the reduced twinning propensity in fine grains would be unfavorable for crack formation ( Kumar et al., 
2015 ). Indeed, more twins appeared in the T200 and T350 samples than the fine-grained T500 sample (Table S1). 
Secondly, the massive LAGBs should be noted ( Fig. 3 ). The GBs with small misorientation angles would migrate slowly, which 
restricts grain growth ( Yang et al., 2023 b). Moreover, transmission for dislocation with similar orientations to the constitutive dis -
locations of LAGBs is allowed due to the “ soft barrier ” role of LAGBs ( Chen and Yu, 2019 ). And the internal strain can be relieved by the 
emission of new mobile dislocations from LAGBs ( Pan et al., 2020 ). Additionally, the stress concentration caused by pile-ups of 
dislocation can be evaluated by GNDs. The reduced GNDs after drawing at 500 
◦
C implies the release of internal stress ( H ¨artel et al., 
2022 ). Under the aforementioned circumstances, the extensive LAGBs and reduced GNDs facilitate the subsequent plastic deformation 
in the tensile test to a certain extent. 
Thirdly, influence of particles on ductility is often overlooked in the studies concerning mechanical properties, due to their 
outstanding strengthening effect. The benefits of the fine and finer particles to ductility are mainly manifested in the following three 
aspects. (1) Tiny particles provide limited sites for cavity nucleation, thus avoiding transgranular fracture to some extent ( Mohri et al., 
1998 ); (2) Small spherical particles can serve as effective pinning obstacles to grain growth ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Ma et al., 2019 ; Shuai 
et al., 2023 ); (3) Dislocation multiplication would be aroused by dislocation shearing particles ( Fan et al., 2023 ). Therefore, it is 
reasonable that the T500 sample with a prominent character of high density of fine and finer particles in a uniform distribution ( Fig. 5 ) 
possessed a high EL. 
And finally, the weak texture ( Fig. 8 ) helps for homogeneous deformation ( Pan et al., 2020 ). As shown in Fig. S6(e), the T500 
sample had a moderate SF for basal slip (0.26) in spite of a nearly basal texture feature of it. Together with the highest proportion of 
soft-orientation grains (~10 %, as shown in Fig. S6(a)), basal slip should be easier to activate in the T500 sample than other two 
as-drawn samples. Furthermore, a fine-grained microstructure is conducive to the activation of potential non-basal slip systems ( Zhang 
et al., 2021 ). From Fig. S6(b-e), average SF values of non-basal slip for the as-drawn samples were basically the same, and much higher 
than that for the as-extruded sample. Due to the finest microstructure, non-basal slip should also be easier to activate in the T500 
sample. Therefore, both basal and non-basal slip should be more easily activated in the subsequent tensile test for the T500 sample than 
that for the other two as-drawn samples. 
Comprehensively, the T500 sample exhibited a significantly higher EL than the other two as-drawn samples, mainly due to the 
combined effect of the finer grains, extensive LAGBs and reduced GNDs, finer particles, weak texture and more easily activated slip. As 
for the T200 and T350 samples, by contrast, substantial GNDs, and relatively coarse grains and particles should be responsible for their 
limited elongations. Due to similar SF values, texture should not be the main reason for the difference in tensile elongation of the as- 
drawn samples, which is similar to the conclusion of Zeng et al. ( Zeng et al., 2016 ). 
4.2.3. Rationale for improved strain hardening capacity 
The strain hardening capability depends on the dislocation accumulation and storage within grains ( Deng et al., 2022 ). The 
improvement in strain hardening capacity of the T500 sample is mainly due to the increased dislocation storage capacity. Plenty of 
GNDs were introduced by drawing ( Fig. 4 ), leaving limited space to contain newly formed dislocations during further tensile tests. 
Notably, a vigorous dynamic recovery (DRV) and DRX occurred during drawing at 500 
◦
C ( Fig. 2 ), resulting in a heavy consumption of 
dislocations and new DRXed grains with few dislocations ( Wang et al., 2023 b). Thus, the dislocation storage capacity was increased, 
which is conducive to strain hardening. By contrast, the weak DRV and DRX during drawing at low or moderate temperatures would 
lead to a poor dislocation storage capacity. Accordingly, the strain softening of the T200 and T350 samples can be explained. Addi -
tionally, high density of particles within the T500 sample could further promote the work hardening due to the increased accumulation 
of dislocations via interplaying with dislocations ( Wang et al., 2020 ). 
4.3. A novel method for achieving superior strength-ductility synergy 
In this study, JDBM alloy rod with superior strength-ductility synergy was achieved. The refined microstructure of the rod after 
drawing at 500 
◦
C is supposed to be the underlying reason for its outstanding performance. Different from this work, however, 
continuous grain coarsening with increasing temperature was observed in many previous references ( Sadi et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 
2022 a; Yang et al., 2021 ), which may be due to the fact that the deformation temperatures were inadequate to obtain a high su -

5. Conclusions 
In this work, the JDBM alloy rod with superior strength-ductility synergy was prepared by single-pass drawing at an elevated 
temperature of 500 
◦
C after extrusion. Focusing on the temperature effects, the microstructure and mechanical properties of the as- 
extruded and as-drawn samples were investigated. The following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) After hot drawing at 200, 350 or 500 
◦
C respectively, the average sizes of grain and particles reduced, LAGBs increased by about 
11 times, twins increased sharply, GNDs density increased by 5~6 times, and a nearly basal texture was formed. 
(2) As the drawing temperature increased from 200 to 500 
◦
C, the volume fraction of twins and the average sizes of grains and 
Mg
41
Nd
5 
particles increased and then decreased, while the LAGBs and average GNDs density decreased continuously. Among all 
studied samples, the T500 sample achieved the most refined microstructure due to the severest DRX and DP. 
(3) Single-pass drawing at elevated temperatures with a pass reduction ratio of 29 % could significantly enhance the mechanical 
properties of the as-extruded JDBM alloy rods. In comparison with the as-extruded sample, the strength of as-drawn T200, T350 
and T500 samples increased by ~2.2 times. Despite a certain sacrifice of EL, a superior strength-ductility synergy could be achieved 
by drawing at 500 
◦
C, in particular: a high YS of ~301 MPa, a relatively high elongation of 19 % and a positive strain hardening 
exponent n value of 0.06. 
(4) The high YS of the as-drawn T200, T350 and T500 samples mainly originates from GB and dislocation strengthening. The 
increment in YS is principally due to the significant strengthening effect of texture and dislocation, which are enhanced by 54.9 and 
75.3 MPa, respectively. In addition, the T500 sample had a better ductility including the remarkable EL and improved strain 
hardening capacity than other two as-drawn samples. The refined grains, high density fine and finer particles, reduced GNDs and 
large number of LAGBs are considered to be responsible for the relatively high EL. The improved work hardening capacity is 
attributed to the intensive DRV, DRX and DP. 
(5) A novel approach to achieve superior strength-ductility synergy of Mg alloys has been proposed, i.e. activating severe DP by 
deformation with large strain at high temperatures slightly below the transition temperature of the secondary phase.
