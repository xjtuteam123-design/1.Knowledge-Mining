ABSTRACT 
The mechanical properties of an Fe-28 at.% A1 alloy have been examined in 
detail from room temperature up to 700°C. The solute additions contained in this 
alloy lead to higher strength at all temperatures than is shown by the binary alloy. 
These high strengths are associated with dissociated imperfect superdislocations 
from room temperature up to a stress peak. The anomalous stress peak is found at 
500°C, somewhat below the temperature range where disordering of DO, order to 
the B2 ordered state occurs and the Burgers vector of the dislocations present 
changes from (1 11) to (100). The reason for the anomalous peak, the locking of 
the (1 1 1) superdislocations and the eventual change to (100) dislocations is a 
local climb-locking process operating on the (1 11) superdislocations. 
Q 1. INTRODUCTION 
Fe-A1 alloys containing about 25-32 at.% A1 are ordered to the DO, state below 
about 550°C and maintain the B2 ordered state from this temperature up to about 
800-900°C when they disorder completely (Swann, Duff and Fisher 1972). Early 
studies of the deformation behaviour of DO, ordered Fe,A1 alloys by Leamy, Kayser 
and Marcinkowski (1969 a and 1969 b) and by Leamy and Kayser (1969) showed that 
the stress deformation curve could be separated into several stages, the first few being 
linear and the final stage being parabolic. The transition from one stage to another 
depended in a systematic way on the test temperature and the A1 content (over the 
approximate range 22-32 at.% A1 studied) and was explained in terms of the 
nucleation, easy glide and then high work hardening of perfect superdislocations in 
the DO, lattice, followed by the separation of the perfect superdislocations into 
imperfect pairs of dislocations which also gave rise to an easy glide and a high work- 
hardening stage, and finally the dissociation into single dislocations with an 
associated parabolic hardening stage. This model was confirmed by these workers 
using transmission electron microscopy, showing perfect superdislocations initially, 
followed by imperfect paired dislocations later, followed finally by single dislocations. 
In the DO, lattice, the perfect superdislocation normally has the total Burgers 
vector (1 11) (all values are given throughout this report in relation to the DO, lattice, 
even for high-temperature testing, where B2 order may be present), and is split into 
two pairs of dislocations, each of Burgers vector a( 11 1 ) (Leamy ef al. 1969 a). The 
antiphase-boundary (APB) fault between the first and second and between the third 
and fourth dislocations is that maintained in the B2 lattice, where nearest-neighbour 
(NN) bonding is disrupted; it is called here a NN APB. The APB fault between the 
second and third dislocation is that present in the DO, state only and is charaoterized

strengthening peak was initially explained by Brown (1959) as due to relaxation of the 
structure of the APB linking the dislocation components of the superdislocations. 
Subsequently, Umakoshi, Yamaguchi, Namba and Murakami (1976) proposed that 
the stress peak was caused by the cross-slip and locking of (1 11) superdislocations 
from { llO} planes to {I 12) planes. Saka and Kawase (1984) first proposed that the 
stress peak was caused by a change in Burgers vector of the dislocations, from (1 1 1)

more recently observed also by Dirras, Beauchamp and Veyssiere (1989). These 
studies seem particularly relevant to the understanding of deformation in the Fe,Al 
alloys in view of the fact that similar changes in dislocation configurations, notably 
changes in slip plane and in Burgers vector (from (1 11) to (loo)), have also been 
reported for Fe-A1 alloys (Umakoshi and Yamaguchi 1980, Mendiratta, Kim and 
Lipsitt 1984, Baker and Gaydosh 1987). 
The present study sets out to examine the temperature dependence of the flow 
stress in a modified alloy of Fe,Al of higher strength than the simple binary alloy, and 
to determine the factors that are responsible for the high strength and its variation 
with test temperature.

Q 4. DISCUSSION 
Deformation of the Fe,A1 material can be separated into three regimes, namely 
from room temperature to about 400°C where the yield stress is slowly falling, from 
about 400°C to about 500-550°C where the yield stress rises, and above this 
temperature where the stress falls rapidly with increasing temperature. These stages 
are shown schematically in fig. 15, where the controlling dislocation mechanisms are 
also indicated. 
In the low-temperature stage, deformation occurs by the movement of dissociated 
superdislocations of Burgers vector (1 11 ) on (1 10) planes. Fine cusps are visible on 
the mobile dislocations and dipoles and other fine debris (perhaps small loops) are 
produced. At higher temperatures in this regime the fine debris disappears and 
relaxation processes lead to a fall in work-hardening rate. The reason for the higher 
stress levels over those reported, for example by Leamy and Kayser (1969), is not 
clear. As explained by these workers, once the applied stress is sufficiently high, the 
fourfold dissociated (1 11) superdislocation (four a( 11 1 ) partials) can be split into 
separate pairs of +( 11 1) partials. Such separated pairs of partials are observed in the 
present work, but the reason why the initial stress is sufficiently high to cause such 
separation is not known. Modifications of the extent of order in the present material 
or solute-dislocation interactions may be origin of such hardening. 
At temperatures slightly above ambient, cross-slip becomes more and more 
evident, as illustrated by changes in the projected separations of dislocation partials 
(e.g. visible at 450°C (fig. 5) and prevalent at 500°C (fig. 7)), but with dislocations

$5. CONCLUSIONS 
The variation in yield stress with test temperature has been examined for an alloy 
based on Fe-28at.OA A1 which has the DO, structure in equilibrium below about 
590°C and the B2 structure above that temperature. The alloying additions included 
in this alloy (Cr, Nb, B and most importantly Si) lead to stress increases over reported 
values for binary Fe-28 at.% A1 and also for ternary Fe-28 at.% AI-5 at.% Cr alloys; 
the precise reasons for this stress increase are not evident and seem to be associated 
with Si in solid solution (since Nb and B are removed from solution as coarse 
dispersoids that play only a minor role in strengthening). The alloy shows a stress 
peak near 5OO0C, a temperature significantly below the disordering temperature. In 
the lower-temperature range, deformation occurs by the movement of imperfect (for 
the DO, lattice) +( 11 1 ) dislocations which are dissociated into two :( 11 1 ) partials 
separated by an APR and gliding on { 110) planes. In the temperature range where the 
yield stress increases, these dislocations become progressively locked by a local-climb 
process whereby the dislocation partials climb out of their glide plane, normal to their 
Burgers vector. At higher temperatures, dislocations with < 100) Burgers vectors are 
generated and it is their easier movement at higher temperatures that leads to thc 
stress drop. According to the model of superdislocation locking by local climb which 
is considered here, the extent of disordering at the stress peak plays no role other than 
to enhance atomic mobility and to facilitate the climb-locking process but is not a 
necessary condition for dislocation locking, changes in Burgers vector and the stress 
peak. Cross-slip processes, for example of (1 1 I) dislocations between { 110) planes 
and onto (1 12) planes, do not play a significant role in affecting mechanical 
properties.
