ABSTRACT
The face-centered cubic (FCC) high entropy alloy (HEA) exhibits exceptional potential for low-temperature 
applications due to its remarkable phase stability, excellent strength-toughness trade-off, and impact resis -
tance. The challenge of balancing strength and ductility can be effectively addressed through interstitial doping, 
further enhancing the alloy ’ s mechanical properties at cryogenic temperatures. In this study, we compared the 
effects of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) atoms on the mechanical properties at cryogenic temperature and cor -
responding deformation mechanisms of non-equiatomic HEAs. Specifically, 1 at.% C or 1 at.% N was added to

1. Introduction
Designing high-performance metallic materials is of utmost signifi -
cance, especially for applications in extreme environments (e.g., cryo -
genic temperature, neutron irradiation, stress-corrosion conditions, etc.) 
[ 1 – 3 ]. These applications often demand materials with enhanced 
strength, superior plasticity, and excellent toughness properties [ 4 , 5 ]. 
The innovative design concept of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) provides a 
vast composition space for developing solid solution alloys, which are 
potential candidates for the applications mentioned above [ 6 ]. 
Numerous studies have demonstrated the significant potential of HEAs 
for low-temperature applications HYPER[ 7 – 14 ]. For instance, equia -
tomic CrMnFeCoNi alloy (also known as Cantor alloy) exhibits an 
approximate increase of 85 % and 70 % in yield strength and ultimate 
tensile strength, respectively, when subjected to deformation at tem -
peratures ranging from 293 K to 77 K with a grain size in the micron 
scale (~6 μ m). Additionally, there is a notable 25 % enhancement of 
plasticity [ 14 ]. Extensive studies have been conducted to enhance the 
mechanical properties of HEAs through novel composition designs and 
incorporating precipitation hardening, interstitial solid solution

The stronger grain refinement effect of N than C is attributed to several 
factors. First, N has a stronger interaction with grain boundaries 
compared to C [ 30 ]. This stronger interaction helps to stabilize the grain 
boundaries, preventing excessive grain growth and promoting the for -
mation of finer grains. Second, C has a greater tendency to form car -
bides, which can act as nucleation sites for grain growth, hindering grain 
refinement, while N has a lower tendency to form stable nitrides, 
reducing this negative effect. Third, N can influence the diffusion rates 
of other elements in austenite, which can indirectly affect grain growth 
kinetics [ 38 , 39 ].
3.2. Mechanical properties
Fig. 3 compares the engineering stress-strain curves of the N1-HEA 
and C1-HEA alloys at ambient and low temperatures, respectively. 
Fig. 3 (a) shows that C1-HEA has a yield strength of ~321.3 MPa and an 
ultimate tensile strength of ~708 MPa at the ambient temperature. 
When the testing temperature decreases to 200 K, the yield strength and 
ultimate tensile strength present a simultaneous enhancement to 379.6 
MPa (increased by 15 %) and 852.3 MPa (increased by 17 %), respec -
tively. However, the total elongation decreases from 78 % to 56 %. The 
engineering stress-strain curves of the base-HEA at 293K and 200K are 
shown in Supplementary Fig. S3 . With decreasing temperature, yield 
strength increases from 237 to 301 MPa, ultimate tensile strength in -
creases from 608 to 823 MPa, and total elongation decreases from 56.8 
% to ~45 %.
N1-HEA, as shown in Fig. 3 (b), possesses a yield strength of 353.7 
MPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 692 MPa at the ambient tem -
perature. At 200 K, yield and ultimate tensile strength increase to 395.5 
MPa (increased by 11 %) and 1065 MPa (increased by 35 %), respec -
tively. Similar to the C1-HEA, the elongation of the N1-HEA shows a 
descending trend with the decreasing temperature, reducing from 68 % 
to 58 %. The detailed mechanical performance of the two alloys at both 
ambient and cryogenic temperatures is presented in Table 2 . The work- 
hardening curves of C1-HEA and N1-HEA are shown in Fig. 3 (c – d), and 
the work-hardening ability is evaluated by considering the difference 
between the reciprocal of the yield ratio and 1. Based on the results in 
Fig. 3 and Table 2 , the N1 alloy exhibits a more significant work- 
hardening ability than the C1 alloy.

The increase in lattice friction stress is the main cause of the solution

alloy, small interstitial atoms usually occupy lattice positions, causing 
lattice distortion and increasing the resistance to dislocation migration. 
The smaller atomic size of N atoms leads to a stronger lattice distortion

research presented in Refs. [ 42 , 43 ], which indicates that N atoms have a 
stronger low-temperature strengthening impact than C atoms.
We further compare the low-temperature strain hardening ability of 
various HEAs, including C1-HEA, N1-HEA in the present work, and other 
types of HEAs, as shown in Fig. 7 . Notably, N1-HEA exhibits significantly 
higher low-temperature strain hardening ability compared to C1-HEA 
and most current HEAs such as cantor alloy [ 12 – 14 , 44 ], 
non-equiatomic HEAs [ 7 , 45 – 49 ], interstitial HEAs [ 29 , 32 , 42 , 43 , 50 , 51 ], 
and substitutional HEA [ 9 , 52 – 62 ]. In addition, the N1-HEA exhibits 
superior low-temperature strain hardening ability compared to the 
N-doped alloy reported in Ref. [ 32 ], where the TRIP effect serves as the 
dominant deformation mechanism during low-temperature 
deformation. In contrast to C1-HEA, the TWIP effect acts as the main 
deformation mechanism for N1-HEA at cryogenic temperature. With 
increasing strain, deformation twins continuously introduce a signifi -
cant number of deformed twin boundaries that form dense subgrain 
boundaries in the N1-HEA, leading to a pronounced dynamic Hall-Petch 
effect. This effectively impedes dislocation movement while enhancing 
dislocation storage capacity, thereby achieving higher tensile strength. 
In later stages of deformation, the TRIP effect is activated, which facil -
itates efficient co-deformation and load-sharing between FCC and HCP 
phases [ 29 ]. These combined effects contribute to continuous work 
hardening in N1-HEA.
4.2. The difference in strengthening effects between the two interstitials
The addition of interstitial atoms results in an elevation of lattice 
friction stress, thereby enhancing the yield strength of the alloy. Typi -
cally, small-size interstitials reside within the interstices of the matrix 
alloy, leading to lattice distortion and consequently enhancing the stress 
field for dislocation motion [ 43 ]. By comparing the yield strengths of 
both interstitial-doped alloys investigated in this study, N1-HEA exhibits 
higher yield strength than C1-HEA at both ambient and cryogenic 
temperatures, indicating a higher lattice friction stress induced by N 
atoms. The dissimilarity in lattice friction stress is primarily attributed to 
variances in lattice distortion induced by C and N atoms, as depicted in 
Fig. 1 (b), where N1-HEA possesses a larger lattice constant compared to 
C1-HEA. These distinctions can be attributed to two main aspects: 
Firstly, there exists a disparity in atomic size between these two ele -
ments. The atomic radius for N atoms is smaller compared to the C atoms 
(i.e., 0.155 vs. 0.170 nm). The smaller atomic size yields stronger lattice 
distortion, which enhances the Peierls-Nabarro stress, the critical stress 
for dislocation movement, enhancing solid-solution strengthening [ 16 ,
42 ]. Secondly, the electron density surrounding the N atom is consid -
erably high when contrasted with the C atom, enabling the formation of 
an enlarged effective size for the N atom and resulting in greater influ -
ence on lattice distortion [ 63 ].

4.3. Overview of the deformation mechanism
As shown in Fig. 9 , we first summarize the microstructural evolution 
of the two alloys in the present work with the change of strain. Previous 
microstructural analysis ( Fig. 2 (a)) indicates that C1-HEA, in the un -
deformed state, exhibits a single FCC phase structure with a significant 
number of annealing twins and a small number of dislocations. As the 
strain increases, numerous deformation twins are formed, and SFs 
emerge in FCC grains. The continuously generated SFs propagate and 
overlap, providing effective nucleation embryos for the HCP phase 
( Figs. 4 and 5 ) [ 69 ]. The increase of stress and deformation strain pro -
motes further displacive transformation from FCC to the HCP phase, and 
more variants of the HCP phase are activated. In the C1-HEA, the TRIP 
effect becomes the primary deformation mechanism. Different variants 
of HCP phases continuously refine grains, and the FCC → HCP interface 
acts as a strong obstacle to dislocation movement, resulting in the dy -
namic Hall-Petch effect. Additionally, dislocation multiplication was 
observed near grain boundaries within the HCP phase indicating dy -
namic strain partitioning between two phases.
Similar to the C1-HEA, the N1-HEA exhibits an FCC single-phase 
structure in the initial state, as depicted in Fig. 2 (b). With the tensile 
deformation proceeds, a significant number of SFs and deformation 
twins are generated, while no HCP phase is observed. In the later stages 
of deformation, a small amount of HCP phase is detected primarily at 
grain boundaries. This indicates that the stress threshold in the N1-HEA 
is significantly higher than that of the C1-HEA. The TWIP effect serves as 
the dominant mechanism for deformation, as shown in Figs. 4 and 6 .
5. Conclusions
In this work, the mechanical properties of C1-HEA and N1-HEA were 
compared at 293 K and 200 K. A detailed microscopic analysis revealed 
the different deformation mechanisms at different deformation strains. 
The main findings are as follows: 
(1) Both C1-HEA and N1-HEA exhibit a single FCC phase structure 
with the doping of C/N atoms. N1-HEA shows a smaller grain size 
than C1-HEA, and the presence of N atoms causes greater lattice 
distortion in the alloy.
(2) With the decrease in deformation temperature, N1-HEA showed 
better low-temperature performance. The yield strength of C1- 
HEA increases from 321.3 MPa to 379.6 MPa, and the ultimate 
tensile strength rises from 708 to 852.3 MPa. The uniform elon -
gation has been reduced from 78.5 % to 56.6 %. By contrast, the 
yield strength of the N1-HEA increases from 353.7 MPa to 395.5 
MPa, and the ultimate tensile strength rises from 692 MPa to 
1065 MPa. The corresponding uniform elongation has been 
reduced from 68.8 % to 58 %. Specifically, at 200 K, the work- 
hardening values for C1-HEA and N1-HEA are 472.7 MPa and 
669.5 MPa, respectively, and the cryogenic work-hardening 
ability of N1- HEAs outperforms most current HEAs.
(3) Both C1-HEA and N1-HEA display a single FCC phase structure, 
suggesting that doping with C or N improves the SFE of these 
alloys. During cryogenic deformation processes, the TRIP effect is 
identified as the primary deformation mechanism for C1-HEA, 
while the TWIP effect dominates in the case of N1-HEA. This 
change in the deformation mechanism indicates that N raises the 
SFE more significantly compared to the C atoms.
(4) With an increasing degree of deformation, the TWIP and TRIP 
effects are activated sequentially in N1-HEA, leading to the 
generation of numerous twin boundaries through a twinning 
mechanism. This significantly refines alloy grains, thereby pro -
ducing a significant dynamic Hall-Petch effect, enabling contin -
uous work hardening ability.
