abstract
Shear punch test (SPT) was employed to study the high temperature mechanical properties of an Mg –
4Zn–0.5Ca (wt%) alloy in the as-cast and aged conditions in the temperature range 150 –250 °C. The
microstructure of the as-cast alloy consisted of α-Mg matrix with some Ca 2Mg6Zn3 and MgZn second
phase particles distributed in the interdendritic regions. Aging treatment resulted in the formation of
equiaxed α-Mg grains together with coarse Ca 2Mg6Zn3 phase in the form of a semi-continuous network
at the grain boundaries and triple points, while nano-sized Mg 4Zn7, MgZn 2, and Ca 2Mg6Zn3 precipitates
formed in the grain interiors. These phases have high thermal stabilities that could improve the high-
temperature mechanical properties of the tested alloy after aging treatment.

1. Introduction
In recent years, considerable research has been focused on the
development of new high-strength magnesium alloys with en-
hanced high temperature properties. Low-cost Mg –Zn alloys have
great potential for such a purpose, due to their age hardening
response. Zinc can form several intermetallics with Mg in both as-
cast and aged conditions [1–3]. It is well established that the ad-
dition of trace amounts of another element to some age-hardening
alloys can have a marked effect on the precipitation response. This
is achieved by promoting nucleation rate and/or altering the
morphology of the precipitating phase, which results in a modi ﬁed
alloy having superior mechanical properties, as compared with the
base alloy.
It has been reported that addition of even small amounts of Ca
to Mg –Zn alloys can signi ﬁcantly enhance the age-hardening re-
sponse [4]. Furthermore, Ca behaves as a grain re ﬁner [5] that can
form stable Mg
2Ca and Ca 2Mg6Zn3 intermetallic compounds [6,7],
all of which result in enhanced mechanical properties. Studies on
the Mg –Zn–Ca alloys have mostly been carried out on the age-
hardening response of these alloys having various chemical com-
positions [4,8–10]. It has been shown that the addition of 0.35 wt%
Ca to Mg –4Zn alloy could have a substantial positive in ﬂuence on
the precipitation process during arti ﬁcial aging. The micro-
structure of the modi ﬁed Mg –4 wt% Zn –0.35 wt% Ca alloy was
more complex and had much re ﬁned precipitates which were very
stable for prolonged times at elevated temperatures [4]. According
to thermodynamic calculations and TEM observations, precipita-
tion of Ca
2Mg6Zn3 phase contributed to the age-hardening beha-
vior of the ternary Mg –Zn–Ca alloy [6,11 ]. In another study, the
age-hardening response of Mg –0.3Ca–xZn (at%) ( x¼0.0, 0.1, 0.3,
0.6, 1.0 and 1.6) alloys has been investigated. TEM observations
showed that at higher Zn contents, the formation of Ca 2Mg6Zn3
precipitates had the most effect on the age-hardening response of
these alloys [10].
Although many studies have been performed on age-hardening
response of Mg–Zn–Ca alloys, and despite the fact that these alloys
are potentially suitable candidates for high temperature applica-
tions, there are only few studies conducted on their high tem-
perature mechanical properties, both in the as-cast and aged
conditions. Mechanical properties of the aged Mg –4Zn (wt%) with
and without 0.1 wt% Ca were derived from tensile tests at room
temperature, 423 and 450 K [4]. In another study, a comparison
was made on two different solution treatment regimes and their
effects on the age-hardening response and mechanical properties
of the Mg –4Zn–0.3Ca (wt%) alloy [12]. However, there is a lack of
information about the high-temperature mechanical behavior of
as-cast and aged Mg –Zn–Ca alloys in the literature and a com-
prehensive investigation on their mechanical properties in these
conditions are missing. The aged Mg –Zn–Ca alloys which bene ﬁt
from the age-hardening, are supposed to retain their strength at
high temperatures and thus study of the high-temperature me-
chanical properties of the aged Mg –Zn–Ca alloys is of much im-
portance. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the
high-temperature mechanical properties of Mg –4Zn–0.5Ca (wt%)
alloy in the as-cast and aged conditions using the miniature shear

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials and processing
The Mg –4Zn–0.5Ca (wt%) alloy was prepared from pure Mg
(99.8 wt%), Zn (99.9 wt%) and an Mg –10 wt% Ca master alloy. The
alloy was melted in a graphite crucible placed in an electrical
furnace at 750 °C, using the Foesco Magrex 36 covering ﬂux. The
details of the casting process have been described elsewhere [13].
The cast slabs had the dimensions of 12 mm /C2 30 mm /C2 180 mm,
from which 3 mm /C2 12 mm /C2 12 mm and 1 mm /C2 12 mm /C2 12 mm
samples were cut using an electrodischarge wire-cut machine for
heat treatment, microstructural characterization, and mechanical
behavior testing. Solution treatment was carried out at 500 °C for
2 h in a resistance furnace followed by water quenching. Solution-
treated samples were then aged at 210 °C for 24 h.
2.2. Microstructural characterization
The microstructures of both as-cast and aged samples were
studied by optical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The
specimens were polished by 0.3 μm alumina paste, followed by
polishing on an abrasive-free polishing microcloth. Etching was
carried out using an 8 vol% nitric acid in ethanol solution at room
temperature. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was em-
ployed to study the precipitation behavior in the aged material.
Samples for TEM examinations were prepared by the focused ion
beam (FIB) method. Image analysis software was used to de-
termine the dimensional characteristics and volume fractions of
the precipitates.

scribed here. In SPT, the sample is located between a punch and a
receiving-hole in the die. By applying the load, the deforming part

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural observations
Figs. 1 and 2 show the optical and SEM micrographs of the alloy
in the as-cast and aged conditions, respectively. As can be seen in
Fig. 1a, the as-cast microstructure is composed of a dendritic phase
and some second-phase particles distributed in the interdendritic
regions. In a previous study, the authors showed that inter-
dendritic second phase particles of the same alloy had the com-
positions of the MgZn and Ca
2Mg6Zn3 phases [13]. After aging
treatment ( Figs. 1 b and 2b), the microstructure is changed to an
equiaxed grain structure with an average grain size of about
70 μm. Furthermore, there exist some second phase particles lo-
cated mostly in the triple junctions and grain boundaries. There
are also some spherical second phase particles in the grain inter-
iors in the aged condition. These second phase particles together
with the elemental maps of Mg, Zn and Ca are demonstrated in
Fig. 3, where Zn and Ca elements tend to segregate into the second
phase particles. Results of EDS analysis taken from matrix and
grain boundary and intergranular phases are reported in Table 1.I t
is shown that the grain boundary and intergranular phases have
similar chemical compositions. These phases are identi ﬁed as
Ca2Mg6Zn3 that form during the solution treatment stage and
remain in the microstructure after aging treatment [12]. Con-
cerning the chemical composition of the second phase particles, it
should be noted that the quantitative EDS results overestimate the
Mg percentage in all cases. This can be a result of a large inter-
action volume of electron beam with the Mg matrix which leads to
the emission of X-ray from material beneath the particles. This is
why the chemical compositions of the phases obtained by EDS are
somewhat different from the stoichiometric chemical composition
of the Ca 2Mg6Zn3 phase.

cles occurs. STEM images taken from the aged material, exhibited
in Fig. 4, show such precipitates. Different types of precipitates can

cipitates can enhance the high-temperature mechanical properties
of the as-cast alloy.
3.2. High temperature mechanical properties
Typical shear punch test curves are presented as the variations
of shear stress with punch normalized displacement. An example
is presented in Fig. 7 for the aged material tested at 150 °C. Two
important data which can be derived from SPT curves are shear
yield stress (SYS) and ultimate shear strength (USS), both of which
have good correlations with the tensile yield stress and ultimate
tensile strength values obtained in conventional tensile tests. SYS
corresponds to the shear stress level in which the shear stress –
normalized displacement curve deviates from linear elastic beha-
vior. In materials with no distinct deviation point, 1% proof stress is
taken as SYS. After deviation from the linear behavior, shear stress
increases and reaches a maximum point which corresponds to the
USS. To reduce the experimental error, three different samples
were tested for each condition. As shown in Fig. 7 , the results
indicated that SPT data have a high level of reproducibility. This
ﬁnding con ﬁrmed that the casting method used in this study had
resulted in a nearly defect-free casting product.
Fig. 8 shows the shear punch test curves of the as-cast and aged
samples at different temperatures. It can be seen that SYS and USS
values of both conditions gradually decrease with increasing the
test temperature from 25 °C to 250 °C. The obtained SYS and USS
values have been plotted against temperature in Fig. 9 , indicating
that SYS and USS values of the aged material are higher than those
of the as-cast condition in the whole tested temperature range.
The relative retention of the strength at high temperatures could
be stemmed from the modi ﬁed microstructure of the precipitates
with homogeneous distribution in the as-cast and aged conditions.
This is consistent with the view that the formed precipitates are
very stable and show extensive resistance to coarsening for pro-
longed periods [4].

4. Conclusions
The microstructure and high-temperature mechanical proper-
ties of an Mg –4Zn–0.5Ca alloy were investigated in the as-cast and
aged conditions and the following conclusions were drawn.
1. The microstructure of the as-cast material consists of α-Mg
dendrites, Ca 2Mg6Zn3, and MgZn second phase particles dis-
tributed in the interdendritic regions.
2. In the aged condition, the microstructure was composed of
equiaxed α-Mg grains and Ca 2Mg6Zn3 second phase particles
located at triple junctions, grain boundaries and inside the
grains. TEM examination revealed that Ca 2Mg6Zn3,M g4Zn7, and
MgZn2 nano-sized particles were also precipitated during the
aging treatment. Ca 2Mg6Zn3 precipitates, having the highest
volume fraction among the nano-sized particles formed during
the aging treatment, were considered as the main agents af-
fecting the age-hardening response of the studied material.
3. The aged Mg –4Zn–0.5Ca alloy showed improved high-tem-
perature mechanical properties, as compared to the as-cast al-
loy. The presence of Ca 2Mg6Zn3 network at grain boundaries
and the formation of nano-scale precipitates during aging
treatment are responsible for such an improvement.
