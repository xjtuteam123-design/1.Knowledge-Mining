abstract
CoCrFeNi is a face-centered cubic (FCC) high entropy alloy (HEA) that exhibits high ductility both at room 
temperature and at cryogenic temperatures but has limited strength. The present study is focused on 
addition of TiB
2 to the CoCrFeNi HEA with the goal of developing materials with better combinations of 
strength and ductility. HEA composites with varying amounts of TiB 2 were prepared by arc melting under 
an argon atmosphere. The resulting alloys were subjected to chemical, microstructural, and mechanical 
characterization. Interaction of TiB
2 with chromium in the HEA matrix during melting resulted in its 
decomposition, leading to in-situ formation of chromium borides with plate morphology. A eutectic 
mixture, consisting of a hard phase (chromium boride) and a tough phase (the FCC matrix) was also 
developed with the amount of the eutectic mixture increasing with increasing amount of TiB
2 added to 
the HEA. Mechanical characterization of the developed composites revealed a very wide range of me -
chanical properties, including yield strengths ranging from 180 MPa to 1282 MPa, hardness values ranging 
from 200 to 750 HV, and elongation ranging from 3.4% to more than 60%. Outstanding combinations of 
mechanical properties could also be obtained, for example the HEA composite developed by addition of 
6 wt% TiB
2, exhibited very high hardness (400 ± 10 HV) and very high yield strength (918 MPa), along 
with very good values of elongation before fracture (30%). The results in this study demonstrate that 
development of ceramic-reinforced HEA composites can help in obtaining alloys with excellent combi -
nations of strength and ductility.

1. Introduction
The limitations of the conventional alloy design approach have 
led to the development of the high entropy alloy concept [1,2]. High 
entropy alloys (HEAs) have been shown to exhibit a remarkable set 
of desirable physical and mechanical properties, including excellent 
mechanical properties even at cryogenic temperatures [3–6], high 
fracture toughness, high hardness, good corrosion resistance and 
wear resistance, as well as excellent creep resistance, and fatigue 
resistance [2,4,7–16]. The excellent mechanical properties of HEAs 
are commonly attributed to the presence of four characteristic core 
effects, namely high configurational entropy [17], severe lattice 
distortion [18], sluggish diffusion [19], and the cocktail effect [3]
. 
HEAs are generally categorized according to crystal structure as ei -
ther face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC), or hex -
agonal close-packed (HCP) HEAs [20]. Combinations of transition 
metals such as Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, and Ni, result in the development of 
FCC HEAs. The five-element HEA CoCrFeMnNi (known also as the 
Cantor alloy) is the most common and well-studied FCC HEA [18]. 
FCC HEAs are characterized by outstanding ductility (even at cryo -
genic conditions), though this comes at the expense of low values of 
strength 
[5]. BCC HEAs are generally formed by the combination of 
refractory metals (typically W, V, Nb, Mo, Ta, and Zr), and exhibit 
high strength, but have limited ductility. In addition, the typical 
constituent elements have a relatively high density, resulting in low 
specific strength.
Demanding structural applications require a combination of both 
high strength and high ductility. Different approaches for micro -
structural modifications, such as mechanical working, thermo -
mechanical processing, and compositional variations, have been

grain refinement of the CoCrFeNi HEA through cold-drawing has 
been reported to increase the alloy strength from 300 MPa to 
1200 MPa, but at the cost of a severe loss in ductility from 60% to 
12.6% [6]. Addition of Al to the CoCrFeNi HEA was found to increase 
the yield strength to 786 MPa while retaining 22% elongation, due to 
the formation of a multi-phase microstructure [21]. The addition of 
both Ti and Al to the CoCrFeNi HEA increased its yield strength to 
645 MPa with an elongation of 39%, where this was attributed to the 
formation of precipitates [22]. The shot peening technique has also 
been used to develop hierarchical-type microstructures in the 
CoCrFeMnNi HEA [23]. The combination of a thin deformed layer 
with an un-deformed core was shown to give an increase in strength 
from 323 MPa to 610 MPa. Alloy design to promote transformation- 
induced plasticity (TRIP) and twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) 
resulted in an increased tensile strength to 841 MPa with 76% 
elongation to fracture [24]. Non-equiatomic high entropy alloys and 
eutectic high entropy alloys have also been explored in the search for 
materials possessing both high strength and high ductility [25–31].
Ceramics are known for their high strength, hardness, and sta -
bility at elevated temperatures. These properties, however, come at 
the expense of exceptionally low fracture toughness. It is believed 
that addition of ceramics to FCC HEAs can help in obtaining a better 
combination of strength and ductility. One reason for this is that ex- 
situ addition of ceramics can also cause grain size refinement, by the 
provision of nucleation sites due to their high melting point. 
Additionally, in-situ development of a ceramic phase can result in 
the formation of a uniformly dispersed hard phase in the ductile HEA 
matrix. Various studies have been reported in which a ceramic phase 
has been introduced, either ex-situ or in-situ, leading to improved 
combinations of strength and ductility. The addition of WC 
[32–34], 
Al2O3 [35], SiC [36,37], TiC [38–44], TiN [45], and Y2O3 [46] have all 
been reported to improve the properties of FCC-type HEAs. These 
alloy have developed through a variety of routes, including vacuum 
arc melting [32,37,41,47,48], mechanical alloying [34,36,49], spark 
plasma sintering [49–51], vacuum hot pressing [34], hot iso-static 
pressing [35] and additive manufacturing [38,45]. Addition of 0.25% 
Y2O3 nanoparticles was reported to result in an increased strength to 
1000 MPa, but the ductility as low at just 10% [46]. Moreover, TiC and 
TiN have also been utilized to strengthen the CoCrMnFeNi HEA, re -
sulting in a strength increases to 723Mpa and 1100 MPa, respectively 
[38,45]. In another study, the CoCrFeMnNi HEA was reinforced with 
5% nano α
-Al2O3 additions [35]. The finely distributed nanoparticles 
refined the microstructure and leading to a very high yield strength 
of 1600 MPa, again though at the cost of a very limited ductility of 
just 6.8%. The addition of WC to the CoCrFeNi HEA resulted in a 
remarkably enhanced yield strength to 1098 MPa, while still re
-
taining ∼
20% ductility [32].
In the present study, ceramic-reinforced HEA composites have 
been developed using an arc melting route by adding varying 
amounts of TiB2 ceramic particles to base the CoCrFeNi FCC HEA. The 
CoCrFeNi system is preferred over the more widely studied Cantor 
alloy (CoCrFeNiMn) [52,53] due to its higher toughness, attributed to 
rapid strain-hardening. The vulnerability of Mn in the Cantor alloy to 
oxidation also makes CoCrFeNi a more suitable system. The CoCrFeNi 
HEA is very ductile even at cryogenic temperatures and has low 
stacking fault energy of 31.7 mJ m
−2 [54–57]. In contrast, TiB 2 be-
longs to a category of ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs) 
[58,59] that possess very high melting temperatures of ∼
3000 °C, 
high moduli of ∼ 400 GPa, and high hardness of 20–23 GPa, but lack 
fracture toughness [59]. In this work we explore the combination of 
highly ductile FCC with a UHTC with a view to developing compo -
sites with an outstanding combination of mechanical properties. 
The interaction of TiB 2 particles with the matrix elements and their 
effect on the microstructure and mechanical properties are also 
discussed.
2. Materials and methods
High-purity (> 99.95%) pellets of cobalt, chromium, iron, nickel, 
and titanium diboride powders (purity 99%, size −400 mesh) were 
used as raw materials for the development of high entropy alloy and 
composites. Pellets corresponding to the equiatomic CoCrFeNi HEA 
composition were placed in a hemispherical water-cooled copper 
mold inside the chamber of an arc melting furnace which was then 
evacuated to 10
−5 millibar. The furnace was purged with high-purity 
argon on attaining the desired level of vacuum, and melting was 
carried out by using the arc created by a non-consumable W elec
-
trode. Melting was repeated five times to ensure chemical homo -
geneity, with the prepared buttons flipped over after each melting 
procedure. Composites were then produced by adding varying 
amounts of TiB
2 to the base CoCrFeNi HEA, with melting carried out 
following the same procedure. The as-cast buttons of the composites 
(about 20 mm in diameter and 8 mm in height) were sectioned using 
an electrical discharge machine wire cutter for further testing and 
characterization. Microstructural studies were initially carried out 
with the help of an optical microscope (Leica DM6M Microscope), 
equipped with the Leica image analysis software (LAS Phase Expert) 
for quantitative phase analysis. Detailed microstructural studies and 
elemental distribution analysis were performed in a scanning elec
-
tron microscope (SEM - JEOL JSM-6490LA) equipped with an energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis system. Crystal structure 
characterization of the developed composites was carried out using 
X-ray diffraction (Bruker D2 phaser, Cu K
α radiation) measurements. 
Mechanical testing of the developed composites was carried out 
with via hardness and compression testing. A minimum of 10 
Vickers micro-hardness indents were made on polished composite 
specimens at a load of 500 N held for 15 s. Room temperature 
compression testing was carried out on rectangular-shaped speci
-
mens (7 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm) using a universal testing machine 
(UTM, SHIMADZU 50KN) at an initial strain rate of 0.001 s −1.
3. Results and discussion
High entropy composites were prepared by the addition of 
varying amounts of TiB 2 to the CoCrFeNi matrix HEA by arc melting 
under an argon atmosphere. A reference sample without TiB 2 addi-
tion (named S 0 in this work) was also prepared, together with 
samples containing 3 wt% TiB 2, 6 wt% TiB 2, 9 wt% TiB 2, and 12 wt% 
TiB2 (named as S1, S2, S3 and S4 respectively). Optical micrographs of 
the high-entropy composites S 1 to S4 are shown in Fig. 1.
It is evident from Fig. 1 that addition of TiB 2 changes the single- 
phase microstructure of the CoCrFeNi HEA to a multiphase micro -
structure. The number and amount of each phase formed in each 
composite was determined through image analysis using the LAS 
Phase Expert software. Example results of this image analysis are 
shown as an inset image in the upper right corner of each micro -
graph. The presence of some coarse-scale precipitates was observed 
in the S1 composite. The content of this phase (represented in white 
in the Fig. 1 phase insets; the matrix phase is shown in green) was 
estimated to be 2.5%. The S 2, S3, and S4 samples (Figs. 1(b), 1(c), 1(d) 
respectively) showed contrast indicating the presence of at least 
three phases. The amount of the matrix phase was found to decrease 
with increasing amount of TiB
2 addition, and was estimated to be 
60.0%, 49.9%, and 39.9% in the S2, S3, and S4 high entropy composites, 
respectively. In contrast the amount of the phase represented in 
white showed very little variation increasing amount of TiB 2 at ap-
prox. 21.0% for S2, 22.1% for S 3, and 21.3% for S 4. The amount of the 
phase represented in red was found to increase with increasing 
amount of TiB2 addition, with fractions of 19%, 28.1%, and 38.8% for 
S2, S3, and S 4, respectively. Based on these optical observations fur -
ther detailed microstructural investigations of the composites were 
carried out in an SEM equipped with an EDS detector. SEM images

XRD analysis of the base HEA (CoCrFeNi) showed only the pre -
sence of peaks corresponding to the single-phase FCC solid solution. 
Peaks corresponding to the TiB 2 phase were not observed in any of 
the developed composites. The XRD patterns of the developed 
composites revealed peaks that corresponded to the formation of 
chromium boride, as it is thermodynamically favorable 
[61]. Inter-
action of chromium with TiB 2 at high temperatures leading to the 
formation of chromium boride has also been recently reported [62]. 
On the basis of the SEM, EDS, and XRD results, it is concluded that 
the chromium from the matrix interacts with added TiB
2 during 
melting causing decomposition of the hard ceramic. Free boron then 
interacts with the chromium to formed chromium boride during 
solidification. Boron has been reported to possess restricted solid 
solubility in the constituents of the HEA used in this study. Addition 
of even 1 at% B has been reported to result in the eutectic solidifi
-
cation [63,64]. It is therefore believed that boron released as a result 
of the decomposition of TiB 2 during melting has resulted in the 
formation of a eutectic mixture of fcc and chromium boride. The 
increase in the amount of eutectic mixture (shown in Fig. 2) with 
increasing amount of TiB
2 addition to the base HEA can be attributed 
to the increased availability of free boron. The aforementioned 
conclusions were also verified by calculating the pseudo-binary 
phase diagram for CoCrFeNi-boron using the THERMOCALC software 
(TCHEA4 database) (Fig. 4).
The mechanical behavior of the developed composites was stu -
died with the help of compression testing and hardness testing. 
Stress-strain curves obtained from the compression testing of the 
composites are shown in Fig. 5 a. A scatter plot showing hardness, 
specific yield strength, yield strength, and elongation of the devel -
oped composites is shown in Fig. 5b.
The yield strength of the base HEA (136 MPa) increased with TiB2 
additions in the base HEA of up to 9% with values of 918 MPa, 
1282 MPa, and 1012 MPa for the S
1, S 2, S 3, and S 4 composites, re -
spectively. The stress-strain curves of the S 0 and S1 composites both 
show a convex appearance, pointing toward the possibility of acti -
vation of either twinning or TRIP effects [65,66]. The stress-strain 
curve of the S 2 composite, in contrast, showed a concave shape in -
dicating conventional dislocation glide as the primary deformation 
mode 
[65,66]. Both the S 3 and S4 composites exhibited very limited 
plasticity. The hardness of the samples was found to increase con -
tinuously with increasing amount of TiB 2 in the base HEA. The base 
CoCrFeNi HEA showed a hardness of 130 ± 5 HV, while hardness 
values of samples S
1, S 2, S 3, and S 4 composites were found to be 
200 ± 10 HV, 400 ± 10 HV, 600 ± 20 HV and 750 ± 20 HV, respec-
tively. The variation in hardness agrees well with the observed var -
iation in yield strength and specific yield strength (except for S 4 
composite which failed in a brittle manner) obtained from com -
pression testing.
The introduction of hard phases (borides) is considered to the be 
primary reason for the high values of hardness, yield strength, and 
specific yield strength in the developed composites. In particular the 
very fine scale of the eutectic microstructure, consisting of alter -
nating soft (HEA matrix) and hard phases (chromium boride) is 
expected to provide significant amount of resistance to dislocation 
glide resulting in the high yield stress values, as observed in other 
eutectic-based systems consisting of a soft-phase and a ceramic 
phase. It cannot also be ruled out that additional strengthening is 
provided in the form of nanoscale precipitation of borides within the 
HEA matrix as observed by Zhang et al.
[67], though additional 
transmission electron observations will be required to investigate 
this possibility. Similar investigations will be needed also to estab
-
lish whether very fine scale twinning, as seen in Wang et al. [68] also 
contributes to the significant work-hardening rate in the S 2 com-
posite, leading to a maximum supported stress of more than 
1900 MPa. The decrease in the elongation before fracture with in
-
creasing amount of TiB2 in the base HEA can also be attributed to the 
increasing amount of the hard boride phase in the matrix. It is in -
teresting to note that the S 2 composite showed an excellent com -
bination of both strength (918 MPa), ductility (30%), and hardness 
(400 ± 10 HV). It is believed that the FCC matrix in the S
2 composite 
contributes to the ductility, while the presence of the finely dis -
tributed hard boride phase provides strengthening without pre -
mature failure due to fracture of the hard ceramic phase.

4. Conclusions
Ceramic-reinforced HEA-matrix composites have been success -
fully prepared by the addition of TiB 2 to a CoCrFeNi matrix through 
the vacuum arc melting route. The evolution of microstructure and 
mechanical properties of the TiB 2-HEA composites have been in -
vestigated. The following are the conclusions of the present study;. 
• TiB2 particles interact with the chromium in the HEA and de -
compose during solidification leading to the formation of chro -
mium borides and a eutectic mixture of FCC HEA matrix and 
chromium borides.
• The fraction of the eutectic component increases at the expense 
of the FCC matrix with increasing amount of added TiB 2 in the 
composite.
• The developed HEA-composites exhibit a very wide range of 
mechanical properties, including yield strengths in the range 
from 180 MPa to 1282 MPa, hardness values from 200 to 750 HV, 
and elongation from 3.4% to more than 60%.
• The presence of a eutectic mixture of hard (borides) and soft 
phases (FCC HEA) in the developed composites results in ob -
taining an outstanding combination of mechanical properties. 
The S2 composite in particular showed high hardness (400 ± 10 
HV) and yield strength (918 MPa) along with very good values of 
elongation before fracture (30%).
• The results of the study illustrate the potential for the develop -
ment of ceramic-reinforced high entropy matrix composites and 
highlight that such alloys may help in achieving outstanding 
combinations of mechanical properties for demanding structural 
applications.
