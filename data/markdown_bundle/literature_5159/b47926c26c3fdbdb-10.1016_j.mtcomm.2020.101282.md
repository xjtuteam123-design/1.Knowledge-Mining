Abstract 
 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 3

Despite the great potential of Friction Stir Welding (FSW) process, there is a lack of 
technical information on Co-based alloys designed for FSW tools. Therefore, this work 
evaluated two multicomponent (Co-Ni)-based superalloys tools of high aluminum 
contents for better wear and oxidation resistance. The two alloys differed on the 
presence of either Nb or Ta in their chemical composition. The alloys were produced 
via vacuum induction melting / investment casting and then heat treated at 1000 °C for 
16 hours. The as-cast and heat-treated microstructures of both alloys presented γ, γ′, 
Co7W6, CoAl, Laves and MC phases. Yield strength near 550 MPa at 900 °C and 
hardness values about 500 HV0.5 have been measured for both heat-treated alloys. 
Friction stir welding (FSW) tools of the two alloys were successfully used to weld (bead 
on plate) a plate of ASTM A1008 steel. However, superior results have been obtained 
from the Ta-alloy tool. 
 
Keywords: Co,Ni-superalloys; Friction Stir Welding (FSW); FSW tool; characterization 
 
1. Introduction 
 
Friction stir welding (FSW) is a solid-state joining process able to eliminate 
common welding defects from fusion welding processes [1,2]. The FSW technique uses 
a non-consumable rotating tool, which contains a shoulder and pin, that heats up and 
stirs the workpiece material through severe plastic deformation, resulting in reliable and 
improved joining interface [3,4]. Considering several features of the FSW tools and 
their constitutive materials, the metal flow around the pin can be quite complex[4] and 
tool wear becomes an important issue [2]. 
Usually, FSW tools are based on W-Re alloys, which are used to weld steel 
plates and titanium alloys [5,6]. These tools often show good fracture toughness; 
however, they tend to wear rapidly [5]. Polycrystalline cubic boron nitride (PCBN) 
tools show better wear resistance when compared to refractory W-Re tools [7], although 
they present high cost and low fracture toughness [5]. Co-Ni based superalloys with γ/γ′ 
microstructure have been considered for these demanding applications, because they 
exhibit high mechanical strength and toughness, as well as high oxidation resistance. In 
addition, these tools can be obtained by low cost investment casting process, suitable for 
large scale production [8]. 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 4

With respect to Co-Ni based superalloys for FSW tools, the desired 
microstructure consists of embedded hard particles in a γ/γ′ matrix [9]. The γ′-
Co3(Al,W) phase with L12 structure, coexisting with γ (Coss - FCC), plays the same role 
as in nickel-based superalloys [10–12]. Park et al. [8] evaluated a Co-based superalloy 
tool with γ/γ′ microstructure that successfully welded 45.7 m length of a mild steel, 
without major geometrical variation before 10 m of welding. However, significant wear 
of the tip and shoulder were reported at 45.7 m. Little deformation of the pin was 
observed after welding 200 mm length of Ti-6Al-4V, with some wear at the shoulder. In 
addition, 1.4 m of Zircaloy 4 was welded without any important macroscopic changes 
of the tool geometry. Sugimoto et al. [13] tested several tool geometries of Co-based 
alloys produced by investment casting, aiming the weld of low carbon steel. Reasonable 
weld surfaces were obtained for travel speeds lower than 250 mm/min and pin shorter 
than 9 mm. According to the authors, the pin geometry is highly affected by the output 
power and promising results were obtained with a 6 mm pin and rotation speed of 250 
revolutions per minute (RPM). 
Despite the great potential of FSW process, there is a lack of technical 
information on Co-based alloys designed for FSW tools. Therefore, this work evaluated 
two multicomponent Co-Ni superalloys tools of much higher aluminum contents than 
those previously presented in the literature [6,14–17] for better wear and oxidation 
resistance in terms of phase transformation temperatures, microstructural 
characterization of as-cast and heat-treated materials, Vickers hardness and hot 
compression tests. In addition, the produced tools were used for FSW experiments of an 
ASTM A1008 plate steel and both presented promising results. 
 
2. Materials and methods 
 
2.1 Alloys production and heat treatments 
 
Table 1 shows the chemical composition determined by X-ray fluorescence 
spectroscopy (XRF) of the two (Co,Ni)-based alloys investigated in this work. B and C 
contents are from the nominal compositions, based on the weighted amount of each 
material. The alloys are identified as Nb-alloy and Ta-alloy since the main difference 
between them corresponds to the presence or not of each of these elements in the alloy 
composition. Note that in both alloys the amount of either Nb or Ta was maintained 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 5

equivalent (2.7 at. %) for the sake of comparison. The alloys were produced by 
investment casting using a high frequency induction furnace under vacuum (VIM) to 
produce multiple bars with dimensions of Ø 12 mm x 70 mm. Considering that during 
FSW process the tool can achieve temperatures around 1000 °C, the bars were heat 
treated (HT) at 1000 °C for 16 hours followed by air cooling to produce stable 
microstructures. 
 
Phase transformation temperatures for both materials were calculated with 
Thermo-Calc® using the TTNI8 database. 
 
 
2.2 Mechanical properties 
 
Vickers microhardness tests were performed from as-cast and heat-treated (HT) 
samples using a load of 500 gf, dwell time of 15 s. A total of 10 randomly taken 
indentations were performed for each material and condition. 
Isothermal compression tests of the HT alloys were performed using a thermo-
mechanical simulator Gleeble 3800® to determine the 0.2 % yield stress. The specimens 
presented diameter and length of 7 mm and 14 mm, respectively. The hot compression 
tests were carried out in triplicate samples at 900 °C and 1000 °C, applying a strain rate 
of 4.5×10−4 s−1. For each test the specimens were heated at 15 °C/s up to the test 
temperature, followed by 60 s of soaking time to ensure isothermal homogeneity before 
loading. The temperature of the specimens was recorded by a type-K thermocouple 
welded onto their central part. All specimens were deformed up to 0.1 of true strain. 
 
2.3 FSW tests 
 
Bars of Nb- and Ta-alloys, heat treated at 1000 °C / 16 hours, were used to 
fabricate the FSW tools by electrical discharge machining (EDM) according to the 
geometry shown in Fig. 1 (noting a shoulder diameter of 11.0 mm and an unthreaded 
pin of 1.6 mm). FSW experiments (bead on a plate) were conducted in a 12 mm thick 
plate of ASTM A1008 steel. To minimize surface oxidation, the tool was shielded by 
argon gas during FSW test. FSW parameters were spindle speed of 180 RPM, the travel 
speed of 60 mm/min, no tilt, and penetration depth near 2.0 mm. The geometrical 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 6

profile of the tool was examined by perfilometry before and after the FSW tests. 
Moreover, cross sections of the tools and the welded steel were also investigated via 
microscopy. 
 
2.4 Microstructural characterizations 
 
Microstructural characterization of the as-cast (AC) and heat-treated (HT) 
alloys, as well as tool materials and steel after welding, were carried out using a 
scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi TM 3000) in backscattered electron mode 
(BSE) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The superalloy specimens were 
ground, polished and then electrochemically etched applying a voltage of 5 V for 10 s 
using a sulfochromic acid solution consisted by K2Cr2O7 + H2O + H2SO4. The 
crystalline phases in the AC and HT materials were determined by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD, Empyrean Panalytical) using a Cu Kα (λ = 0.1541 nm) radiation source. The 
patterns used for phase identification were taken from the Inorganic Crystal Structure 
Database (ICSD). 
 
3. Results and discussion 
 
3.1 Microstructural characterization of the as-cast alloys 
 
Fig. 2 (a) presents the X-ray diffractogram of as-cast Nb-alloy, in which 
reflections of major phases γ, Co7W6 and CoAl were indexed. Peaks from γ′ have not 
been indicated in the diffractogram due to superposition with those from γ phase. 
SEM/BSE micrographs of as-cast Nb-alloy are shown in low and high magnification in 
Figs. 2 (b) and (c), respectively, depicting γ/γ′ dendrites, interdendritic γ′, Co7W6 and 
CoAl. Small solid state formed γ′ particles were observed throughout the γ dendrites. 
Small precipitates are observed in the interior of the CoAl grains, likely associated to 
formation in solid state during cooling. In addition, Fig. 2 (c) also shows minor presence 
of NbC carbides as well as a Laves phase, which were characterized based on calculated 
thermodynamic data. The as-cast Nb-alloy presented hardness values near 470 ± 19 
HV0.5.

As in the case of the Nb-alloy, the X-ray diffractogram of as-cast Ta-alloy, 
shown in Fig. 3 (a), also indicates reflections of major γ, Co7W6 and CoAl phases. 
Again, peaks from γ′ have not been indicated in the diffractogram due to superposition 
with those from the γ phase. Fig. 3 (b) and (c) depict low and high magnification 
SEM/BSE micrographs of as-cast Ta-alloy, exhibiting a γ/γ′ matrix, Co7W6, CoAl, 
interdendritic γ′ and Laves phase, a microstructure very similar to that of as-cast Nb-
alloy. Solid state formed γ′ intermetallic particles are now clearly shown along the 
whole γ dendrites, as shown in detail in Fig. 3 (c). As in the case of the Nb-alloy, TaC 
carbides should be present in this case. However, due to its low volume fraction and low 
image contrast with respect to other phases, it could not be pointed out in Fig. 3 (b,c). 
The as-cast Ta-alloy presented hardness values of 482 ± 11 HV0.5, similar to that of the 
as-cast Nb-alloy. 
Because of the higher aluminum content of these alloys, around 16 at.%, a large 
amount of CoAl phase precipitated in the microstructure of both alloys, differently from 
a previous work [18], where the CoAl phase has not been formed for a similar alloy 
with 10 (at. %) of Al. 
 
3.2 Microstructural characterization of heat-treated alloys 
 
Fig. 4 (a) shows the X-rays diffraction pattern of the Nb-alloy after heat 
treatment at 1000 °C for 16 hours. The same phases present in the as-cast counterpart γ, 
Co7W6, and CoAl were indexed, and peaks from γ′ were superposed with those from γ. 
Fig. 4 (b) and (c) show SEM/BSE micrographs of this heat-treated alloy, indicating the 
same phases present in the corresponding as-cast alloy, i.e. γ, γ′, Co7W6, CoAl, Laves 
and NbC. The main observed microstructural modification due to the heat-treatment 
was the precipitation of Co7W6 needle-like particles in the γ/γ′ matrix, which are also 
surrounded by γ′ phase in the microstructure. The presence of γ′ particles smaller than 1 
µm inside former γ dendrites is clearly seen in Fig. 4 (b). Hardness values of the HT 
Nb-alloy were in the 495 ± 27 HV0.5 range, which are considered equivalent to those of 
the as-cast Nb-alloy taking into account the standard deviation interval. 
 
For Ta-alloy a similar microstructure constituted of γ, γ′, Co7W6, CoAl and 
Laves phases was determined based on X-ray diffraction data [Fig. 5 (a)], as well as 
SEM/BSE micrographs [Fig. 5 (b,c)]. Also, in this case, Co7W6 needle-like precipitates, 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 8

formed during heat-treatment, are surrounded by γ′ phase. TaC phase, even though not 
detected in the microstructure, should be present based on thermodynamic calculations 
data, similar to the presence of NbC in the Nb-alloy. The HT Ta-alloy presented 
hardness values of 524 ± 21 HV0.5, which are slightly higher than those of the 
corresponding as-cast material considering standard deviation intervals. 
 
 
Table 2 shows EDS data from γ/γ′ constituent and phases present in HT Nb- and 
Ta- alloys which presented suitable dimensions for accurate measurements. Note that 
Nb/Ta are mainly concentrated in the Co7W6 and Laves phases, especially considering 
the very small amount of MC (M = Nb,Ta) in both alloys. 
 
The calculated phase fractions in equilibrium conditions at 1000 °C for both 
alloys can be determined from data in Fig. 6 (a,b). It is worth nothing from these 
calculations that both alloys are to be constituted by γ/γ′, CoAl, Co7W6 and MC phases, 
which is in good agreement with the microstructural data previously presented, except 
for the absence of Laves phase. It should be pointed out that this Laves phase is present 
in Scheil solidification simulations for both alloys, which corroborates its presence in 
the as-cast alloys as previously shown. Thus, its absence in the calculated equilibrium 
data might mean either its metastability in the microstructure of the HT alloys or 
inaccuracy of the thermodynamic database. Fig. 6 (a,b) also shows the calculated γ′- 
solvus temperatures for the Nb- and Ta- alloys, which are 1202 °C and 1184 °C, 
respectively. A higher amount of γ′ phase is expected for the Nb-alloy and similar 
amounts of Co7W6 phase in both alloys under equilibrium conditions at 1000 oC. A 
large quantity of discrete γ′ particles at service temperature is highly desirable to 
strengthen the (Co,Ni)-alloy matrix, as well as a large amount of hard Co7W6 particles 
for wear resistance. Larger Al contents are beneficial in terms of γ′ stabilization, as well 
as enhancement of oxidation resistance. From evaluation of four Co-based superalloys 
with different amount of hard phases (M6C and µ-Co7W6), Sato et al. [5] concluded that 
around 9 % volume fraction of these phases produced a Co-based tool that could 
successfully weld several materials (e. g. titanium; mild steel).

Table 3 shows the 0.2 % yield stress obtained from hot compression tests of HT 
Nb- and Ta- alloys. As expected, lower yield stresses were obtained at higher 
temperatures for both alloys. 
 
Lower values of 0.2 % yield stress were obtained when compared to alloys 
investigated by Costa et al. [18] and Lapin et al. [19]. Some of the several factors that 
could be responsible for this behavior are related hereafter. In the present work, the 
tested specimens were heat treated at 1000 °C for 16 hours, which promoted γ′ phase 
coalescence. The negative effects of γ′ coalescence have been shown by Joseph et al. 
[20] who found coarse γ′ in a Ni-based superalloy Haynes 282, with an average size of 
100 nm. This coarse γ′ led to a significant reduction in the strength level. According to 
Lapin et al. [19], the 0.2 % tensile yield strength of single crystal Ni-based superalloy 
CMSX-4 at 950 °C decreased with increasing ageing time as a consequence of γ′ 
coalescence. A reduction of 15.7 % on the 0.2 % tensile yield strength has been found 
when ageing time increased from 100 hours to 2000 hours. Finally, the lower 0.2 % 
tensile yield strength might have a contribution of the CoAl-phase, which was not 
present in the alloys of other investigations [16]. 
 
3.4 FSW using Nb- and Ta- alloys tools 
 
Fig. 7 shows the tool appearances before (a) and after (b,c) FSW tests. After one 
meter of welding, the Ta-alloy tool exhibited a better visual aspect [Fig. 7 (c)], while the 
Nb alloy tool [Fig. 7 (b)] presented few spalling, as it can be observed on the SEM 
micrographs shown in Figs. 7 (d1,e1). For both alloys, no important microstructural 
changes have been detected due to the welding process, as indicated in the micrographs 
shown in Figs. 7 (d2,e2), both very similar to their HT counterparts (Figs. 4 and 5). 
 
Fig. 8 (a1, a2) shows defect-free beads on plate welds with smooth surfaces from 
the ASTM 1008 steel, produced from tools of Nb- and Ta-alloys, respectively. Ta-alloy 
tool exhibited a better tool exit [Fig. 8 (b2)], without excessive roughness when 
compared to that of the Nb-alloy tool [Fig. 8 (b1)]. EDS measurements data have not 
indicated the presence of alloying elements from the tools in the steel, suggesting no 
reaction between them during welding. A refined grain microstructure was acquired in 
the steel after the FSW process [Fig. 8 (c1,c2)]. Similar behavior of grain refinement 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 10

has been observed for FSW of steel, and Ni and Al-based alloys, as reported in the 
literature [17–19]. For both welds, an important hardness value increase is observed 
from the base metal to the weld region, from 140 HV to approximately 220 HV [Fig. 8 
(d1,d2)]. 
 
 
Despite the inferior 0.2 % yield stress of the Nb- and Ta- alloys from this work, 
compared to alloys of previous investigations, both FSW tools manufactured in this 
work were able to weld one meter of steel with minimum wear, and pin height variation 
lower than 3 %, which appears reasonable in view of their high hardness. In addition, 
the microstructure of the FSW tools for both alloys have not changed due to the welding 
process, maintaining a γ/γ’-matrix with hard phases, proving the reliability level of the 
FSW tools fabricated in this work to weld low carbon steels. The weld exhibited good 
surface finish with a minimum of surface oxidation as shown in Figs. 8 (a1,a2). 
The cross-section of the steel exhibiting the tool exit showed a smooth surface 
and larger weld metal region when the Ta tool was used [Fig. 8 (b1)]. Differently, 
important surface roughness with small amount of material flow on the edges could be 
observed in the steel welded using the Nb-tool [Fig. 8 (b2)]. This defect could not be 
related with the amount of hard phases in the alloys, because both present near 10 vol.% 
of MC + Co7W6 according to the thermodynamic simulations at 1000 °C. Further 
detailed investigations are still needed to understand this phenomenon. Even though the 
high roughness presented by the steel welded with the Nb-tool was observed, no 
element contaminations of the FSW tools were found from EDS data. Based on the 
previous results, the next steps are to carry out a robust test of qualification for these 
tools, in order to identify their possible field of application. 
 
4. Summary 
 
In this work two alloys, one containing Nb and the other Ta, of composition 
close to Co (balance)- 42 Ni -15 Al - 9 Cr- 5 W - 3 (Nb/Ta) - 0.06B - 0.6C (at. %) were 
produced in the form of bars via vacuum induction melting / investment casting and 
heat-treated at 1000 °C for 16 hours. Both alloys presented γ, γ′, Co7W6, CoAl, Laves 
and MC phases in the as-cast as well as in the heat-treated samples. The 0.2 % yield 
strength of the alloys is about 550 MPa at 900 oC and their hardness near 500 HV0.5 at

room temperature. Friction stir welding (FSW) tools of the two alloys were successfully 
used to weld (bead on plate) a plate of ASTM A1008 steel. Even though both alloys 
exhibited good performances, it is important to emphasize the superior results obtained 
by the Ta-alloy tool.
