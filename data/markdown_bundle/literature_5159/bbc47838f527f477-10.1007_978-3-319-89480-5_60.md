Abstract Fabrication and welding of structural components for the hot section of
aero-engines continues to be of high importance to the manufacturing industry of
aero-engines. This paper discusses and reviews the literature on hot cracking and
strain age cracking, cracking phenomena that can occur during welding or subse-
quent heat treatment of precipitation hardened Ni- and Fe-Ni-based superalloys.
The in ﬂuence of chemical composition in terms of i.e. hardening elements and
impurities, microstructure of base material and weld zone, together with welding
processes and corresponding parameters and heat input are discussed and related to
the cracking susceptibility of different nickel based superalloys.
Keywords Hot cracking
⋅ Strain age cracking ⋅ Superalloys
Introduction
The aero-engine and speci ﬁcally the hot section is one of the most demanding
application for materials. It is therefore of utmost importance that the welds used in
the design are of suitable quality to account for the demanding environment.
Despite these challenges, fabrication in which welding is commonly utilized is
being more and more employed to tailor structural fabrications in an improved
manner to reduce weight as well as to increase performance of aero-engines. The
trend of increased share of fabrication emphasizes the importance of understanding
welding and the associated metallurgy. The importance of weld associated concerns
can be furthered coupled with a tremendous boost in interest of additive manu-
facturing (AM) which is very linked to welding and where the lessons learned in
welding can and should be used for AM. Looking at the number of publications that
has developed during the years it can be clearly seen that both welding as well as
AM of superalloys are still increasing [ 1, 2], Fig. 1.

Producing the Weld
The weld geometry is crucial to produce a defect-free weld, however, even if the
weld is properly made, residual stresses from the welding operation can still cause
signiﬁcant problems i.e. cracking or distortion. The in ﬂuence of welding on the
material is also of signi ﬁcance, not at least with the precipitation hardened Ni- and
Fe-Ni-based superalloys being used in aero engines as well as gas turbines. Various
post weld heat treatments (PWHT) are normally carried out to account for some of
the aforementioned issues, however, the PWHT can cause additional problems on
its own (i.e. distortion, SAC).
The welding process and associated control have improved quite signi ﬁcantly in
the last decades and now allow for good control and ability to use very low heat
input during welding, being necessary when joining sophisticated materials i.e.
superalloys. More recent techniques such as cold metal transfer (CMT), pulsed
mode of gas metal arc welding (GMAW) and various modes of gas tungsten arc
welding (GTAW) such as Force GTAW and high frequency (HF) GTAW.

However, the GTAW, laser beam welding (LBW), plasma arc welding (PAW), and
electron beam welding (EBW) still seem to be the most frequently applied methods
in welding of structural components for aerospace. The methods of LBW and EBM
are quite attractive when it comes to their abilities to make narrow and
deep-penetration welds while also maintaining high production rates. These
methods also provide very low heat input per unit length for a given depth of
penetration. However, there is still a signi ﬁcant amount of challenges not at least
with respect to micro ﬁssuring in coarse grain materials, despite the low heat input
by using EBW or LBW. Since the abilities to make narrow and deep-penetration
welds increases when using EBW and LBW it should also be stressed that the
restraint factor increases as well, which goes hand in hand with increased sensitivity
towards cracking.
The shape of the weld pool is dependent on the welding process and techniques
and is apart from the thickness and geometry of the part being welded also of
signiﬁcant importance when it comes to cracking. High heat input welds are more
prone to hot cracking than low heat input welds but the weld pool shape should not
be neglected. A tear-drop shaped weld pool, which is usually obtained in automated
and robotic welding, is more susceptible to hot cracking due to the orientation of the
solidifying grains than the more desirable elliptical shaped weld pool [ 7]. One
reason for the tear-drop shaped weld pool being more susceptible lies in the fact that
it results in centerline segregation and as such accumulating high amounts of
melting point depressant elements which reduces the hot ductility of the material.
The more elliptical shaped weld pool will on the other hand have a larger distri-
bution of these melting point depressant elements and as such a better hot ductility
in comparison. As mentioned above, the weld pool shape is dependent on the
welding process and conditions, where the welding speed stands out as a very
inﬂuential parameter in controlling the shape of the weld pool. A high welding
speed together with high heat input promote a tear-drop shape weld pool whereas a
reduced travel speed and as such a higher heat input results in a more elliptical
shaped weld pool. In addition to the discussed weld pool shape it has also been seen
that the contour of the weld cross-section can be of importance to account for hot
cracking. A convex shaped contour is generally seen to be more bene ﬁcial in
comparison to a concave contour which can easily crack despite having an elliptical
shaped weld pool. So, to provide the most suitable conditions for a successful weld
it seem like the trailing edge of the weld pool should be elliptical together with a
convex weld contour.
Hot Cracking
Superalloys and some other austenitic materials are susceptible to the so-called “hot
cracking” phenomena, that is, cracking that takes place during welding and involves
liquid phase. It occurs due to complex reactions in the microstructure in

combination with stresses/strains developed by the thermal gradients during
welding and by any additional external restraint from i.e. ﬁxturing. Hot cracking
can be divided into different categories depending on how they form, solidi ﬁcation
or heat affected zone (HAZ) liquation cracking. Solidi ﬁcation cracking can be the
worse type since it can basically grow until the weld stops. It forms in the last
remaining liquid in between growing dendrites or cells during solidi ﬁcation. The
solidiﬁcation range or brittle temperature range (BTR) are usually pointed out to be
closely connected to the susceptibility towards cracking. The second group of hot
cracks is the HAZ liquation cracks where secondary phases close to the fusion
boundary aid liquation of the grain boundaries.
Theories with regard to explaining the cause for these cracks originate back to
dedicated research on hot tearing during casting in the 1950s. Theories such as the
“Strain theory of hot tears ” [8] and the “Shrinkage-Brittleness theory” [9] ﬁrst came
along and were later combined into Borland ’s combined theory known as the
“Generalized theory ” [10]. Many other theories exist as well [ 11–14] where seg-
regation phenomena often is a key to the understanding. Elements such as S, O, B,
P, C, Ti, N, H and most importantly Nb partitions into the interdendritic regions
during solidiﬁcation and as a consequence suppress the melting point. The presence
of these elements is also important in eutectic phase reactions both during heating
and cooling [ 15]. As a result of low melting impurities or constituents, so-called
wetting ﬁlms are created which signi ﬁcantly reduces grain boundary ductility [ 16].
Solidiﬁcation Cracking
The “Shrinkage Brittleness Theory ” was developed by Pumphrey et al. [ 9]t o
explain hot cracking in aluminium casting as well as welding. It was from this
theory where the BTR was postulated and in which a material possesses an
increased risk of cracking due to an intrinsically low ductility. The BTR range is
primarily in ﬂuenced by the composition of the alloy, however, it was postulated
that the BTR had an upper limit called the coherent temperature, the temperature at
which the growing dendrites come into mutual contact by forming a solid-coherent
network. The lower end of BTR was de ﬁned by the solidus temperature.
Pellini, on the other hand, formulated the “Strain theory ” [8] based on the
research performed to investigate hot tearing in castings and adapted the same
theory to welding. The strain theory relies on the presence of liquid ﬁlms close to
the solidus temperature. The liquid ﬁlm at the grain boundaries is the main cause of
reduced ductility due to the low stress required for ﬁlm separation. According to the
strain theory, hot cracks cannot form in the so-called mushy zone, because of the
ability of the liquid to ﬂow and heal the cracks.
Borland developed the “Generalized theory ” [10], which combines the
“Shrinkage Brittleness Theory ” and the “Strain theory ” but with some modi ﬁca-
tions. The solidi ﬁcation range was divided into four stages:

Stage 1, the primary dendrites form as the temperature drops below the liquidus.
Stage 2, the dendrites interlock by forming a solid network and it is only the liquid
which is capable of movement between the dendrites.
Stage 3, grain development takes place and the liquid is not capable of free
movement due to the continuous network.
Stage 4, complete solidi ﬁcation.
Borland postulated that cracking can only occur during stage 2 as soon as an
“almost continuous ” network of dendrites has formed. Crack healing is possible at
this stage since there is suf ﬁcient amount of liquid to back ﬁll the incipient cracks.
Borland also argued for the so called “critical solidi ﬁcation range ” (CSR) being
associated with stage 3. Stage 3 is where signi ﬁcant amount of cracking can occur,
as the liquid is con ﬁned the interdendritic areas inhibiting the free liquid movement
and as such crack healing. The wider CSR, the larger temperature range over which
liquid is present, and hence an increased susceptibility towards cracking. In stage 4,
cracking is no longer possible as the material is fully solidi ﬁed.
There have been a number of modi ﬁed concepts with regard to the previously
mentioned theories whereas Matsuda et al. [ 17] suggested a modi ﬁed concept of
solidiﬁcation cracking to the “Generalized theory ”. The theory was developed by
studying and analyzing the solidi ﬁcation front using a high speed camera mounted
on a microscope placed in the proximity of a weld torch. The set-up and equipment
allowed for movement of the plate being welded, while the weld torch and the
microscope were ﬁxed. Based on their observations, it was concluded that stage 1
(where primary dendrites form as the temperature drops below the liquidus) and
stage 2 (where the dendrites interlock by forming a solid network) should be shifted
towards the liquidus temperature. Furthermore, stage 3 (where the grain develop-
ment occurs) was divided into several stages (the liquid mass stage, liquid ﬁlm stage
and liquid droplet stage). In the modi ﬁed concept, the liquid mass stage forms
during the earliest phase of the solidi ﬁcation process. The critical temperature
where CSR starts as de ﬁned by Borland, is at the transition between the liquid mass
and the liquid ﬁlm stage. Solidi ﬁcation cracks form in the liquid mass stage and can
propagate both towards the liquid mass stage and liquid droplet stage. The prop-
agation of cracks in the liquid mass stage would be slower because of the high
amount of liquid that heals them, in comparison to the droplet stage. However, new
cracks cannot form in the liquid droplet stage, which is characterized by presence of
liquid droplets, making crack initiation dif ﬁcult to occur. In Alloy 718 and
ATI
®718PlusTM it is known that Nb, which is present in both these alloys, pro-
motes the formation of NbC and Laves eutectic products. The latter is more
detrimental since it solidi ﬁes at a lower temperature and thus extends the solidi ﬁ-
cation interval [ 18–26]. The chemical composition in general and the presence of
Nb and C in particular is important in this context [ 18, 27, 28]. However, it has
been concluded that a eutectic liquid is not always detrimental for the hot cracking
since in some cases it has been shown to heal cracks by back ﬁlling [29–32]. Some
researchers have monitored the grain structure of the weld deposit through variation

Heat Affected Zone Liquation Cracking
HAZ liquation cracks forms mainly due to two reasons, constitutional liquation of
secondary phases and grain boundary liquation caused by segregated melting point
depressant elements. Rapid heating is required in both cases to cause liquation
based on large temperature and compositional gradients leading to crack formation
if the stresses involved are suf ﬁciently high. Slow heating, on the other hand, lead to
solid state dissolution of secondary phase constituents by diffusion into the matrix
wherein the material will be more homogenized. As a general assumption, the HAZ
does not experience temperatures above the matrix solidus, liquid can only form if
the melting point is locally suppressed by solute segregation or by the presence of
second phase particles. The formation of liquid ﬁlms at grain boundaries lowers the
ductility, thus, increases the susceptibility towards cracking when a critical strain is
exceeded. The phases or segregating elements responsible for crack formation vary
from alloy to alloy. The formation of the grain boundary liquation can be due to
different mechanisms which have been reviewed by i.e. Robinson and Scott [ 35] for
austenitic stainless steels and nickel alloys. In general, liquation can occur in dif-
ferent ways, i.e. preferential grain boundary melting, segregation of impurities and
by the so-called “Rehbinder-Medovar” effect which involves migration of surface
active elements to the HAZ and constitutional liquation.
Constitutional liquation was originally proposed for maraging steel weldments
by Pepe and Savage [ 36]. It involves melting of a secondary phase particle present
in the HAZ of the material being welded. The melting can occur at any temperature
between the eutectic and the equilibrium solidus of the alloy, see the pseudo binary
phase diagram of ATI
®718PlusTM in Fig. 4 [37].
The liquid formed through constitutional liquation is a result of the interaction of
the intermetallic compound with the surrounding matrix to form a low melting
phase, i.e. Laves phase in ATI
®718PlusTM and Alloy 718. The heating rate need to
be rapid to achieve liquid phase below the equilibrium solidus of the alloy.
Assuming a spherical precipitate together with fast a heating rate to avoid equi-
librium conditions, the phenomenon can be explained by considering Fig. 4. Below
temperature TEUT, the precipitate does not experience any phase transformation. As
the temperature reaches T EUT, a eutectic liquid ﬁlm, γ-Laves, forms at the interface
between the particle and the matrix. As the temperature is increased above the
eutectic point, the amount of melting will increase. Apart from constitutional
liquation, Pepe and Savage [ 37] also proposed the “grain boundary mechanism ”,
explaining how the grain boundary gets covered by liquid which has been formed
around a particle. During welding and due to the very high heating rate, grain

Strain Age Cracking
SAC is a type of cracking that occurs in the solid state due to hardening in the
material. The phenomenon was initially recognized and investigated in the early
1960s and led to the development of Alloy 718 [ 58], which is resistant to SAC due
to its sluggish hardening response. Cracking takes place when weld stresses are
high at the same time as hardening occurs in precipitation hardened superalloys and
in γ′ hardened alloys in particular. SAC generally occurs during the post weld heat
treatment (PWHT) and is consequently sometimes referred to as “PWHT cracking”
or “Reheat cracking”. The crack formation during PWHT is related to low ductility
in the HAZ combined inadequate stress relaxation. The localization of stress to the
HAZ is related to a lower strength as compared to the base material. It is thus
reasonable that a softer base material lowers the susceptibility to SAC which has
been conﬁrmed by several investigations [ 59–63]. Fully age hardened base material
leds to most severe cracking in the HAZ. Over-aged material on the other hand has
been found to have a bene ﬁcial in ﬂuence, as the lower strength of such a mi-
crostructure results in stress relaxation in the base material [ 61]. An important
feature in the context of what type of cracking takes place during welding is that
SAC may actually also occur during welding, e.g. during multipass repair welding
[64]. SAC is more or less only a problem for γ′ hardened superalloys and is one of
the major reasons why Alloy 718 had such a success since there was a large demand
for a high temperature capable alloy which was readily weldable. The reason is that
Alloy 718 is more or less immune to SAC due to its sluggish hardening response of
γ″ phase. The so called PWHT is carried out to restore strength of the fusion zone
(FZ) as well as the heat affected zone (HAZ) through a full solution and hardening
processing cycle that at the same time relieves stresses that are always introduced
by the welding. This stress relief is unfortunately concomitant with uncontrolled
hardening during the heating cycle of the heat treatment which imposes high strain
on the grain boundaries. In addition, hardening of the alloy generally leads to a
reduced overall ductility [ 59]. This loss of ductility may on its own lead to

Chemical Composition and Microstructure
A relation between Al and Ti was suggested by Prager and Shira, who proposed a
strong in ﬂuence on the precipitation characteristics of the γ′ phase exists [ 59]. The
higher the content of these hardening elements, the more susceptible towards SAC
since a higher volume fraction of the γ′ phase is present [ 65]. The elements forming
the hardening phase have the largest in ﬂuence on the cracking susceptibility. In
general, a combined Al + Ti content above 6 at.% leads to severe problems with
regard to SAC. This is evident when comparing the highly susceptibility René 41
[66] with that of Alloy 718. The latter was developed with a low amount of γ′
forming elements to achieve a better weldability [ 58]. The more sluggish precipi-
tation of its main hardening phase ( γ″) enables stress relaxation to occur in advance
of any hardening reactions [ 67]. Elements such as Nb and its role on SAC has not
been speci ﬁcally investigated, most likely due to the high SAC resistance of Alloy
718. Alloy 718 contains a quite high amount of up to 5.5 wt% Nb. Hughes and
Berry reported a detrimental effect of C, with a sparse carbide distribution for low-C
heats as the major bene ﬁt[ 66]. Other studies suggest a bene ﬁcial effect of C, since it
limits grain growth and inhibits grain boundary sliding [ 68, 69]. Thamburaj et al.
[70] also found a positive in ﬂuence of C by analyzing data from Carlton and Prager
[71] using regression analysis, but, as pointed out by Prager and Sines [ 69], this
effect is mainly attributed to grain size control and high C content has to be
considered as well for overall susceptibility towards hot cracking [ 51, 72–76].
Another element of high importance is B which also has been reported to have a
positive effect on creep and stress rupture life and as such often intentionally added
as an alloying element [ 77]. However, an increased creep resistance could also lead
to a lower susceptibility towards SAC. The in ﬂuence of other minor and trace
elements like Fe, Mn and S on SAC has been reported not to be signi ﬁcant within
the usual composition range whereas Si has been found to have a negative effect
[70]. The effect of furnace atmosphere during PWHT has been studied by Carlton
and Prager who reported a strong in ﬂuence of O, as they found that SAC could be
completely eliminated for René 41 heat treated in Ar, 0.5 vol.% O and vacuum
atmospheres [ 71]. Udimet 500, on the other hand, did show cracks in all tested
atmospheres, indicating that the O content is only a contributing factor and not
necessary the primary cause for SAC (note that Udimet 500 has a nominal Al + Ti
content larger than 10 at.%, whereas René 41 is in the range of 7 at.%). This can be
further supported by the ﬁndings of d’Annessa and Owens, where a bene ﬁcial effect
of low-O atmospheres could be seen, but no complete elimination of cracking [ 78].
The generally adverse effect of O has also been con ﬁrmed by the ﬁndings of Nakao,

co-existence of oxide ﬁlms at the grain boundaries and the presence of ﬁnely
dispersed γ′ particles in the grain interior [ 80, 81].
In terms of microstructure, it can be divided into grain size and homogeneity
where the former has been found to have a positive effect if kept small [ 61, 71]. The
rationale here is the distribution of the overall strain to a larger grain boundary area
and hence lower stresses acting on the grain boundaries which basically has the
same validity also for hot cracking (Fig. 5)[ 71]. The drop in ductility in areas
where SAC takes place is presumably associated with severe strain concentrations
at grain boundary triple points due to grain boundary sliding. Bene ﬁcial effects have
been pointed out for carbide precipitation (e.g. M
23C6 and MC) in resisting grain
boundary sliding and decohesion at elevated temperatures. MC type carbides are
believed to have a bene ﬁcial in ﬂuence in restricting grain boundary migration, by
creating tortuous grain boundaries by pinning [ 82]. Microstructural inhomo-
geneities are mainly a concern when it comes to hot cracking [ 83] but the occur-
rence of hot cracking during welding can facilitate further cracking in a subsequent
PWHT.
Precipitation-Induced Stress Development
An effect of mismatch on the development of contraction stresses, that is, the
susceptibility towards SAC has been proposed in several studies [ 59, 84, 85] and
more recent material modelling by Andersson indicate a positive correlation of
negative lattice mismatch on the cracking resistance [ 65]. The change of lattice
parameter of the γ phase as a function of δ, γ′ and γ″ has been investigated for
Alloy 718 by Liu et al. [ 86] who reported a decrease for higher precipitate contents.
However, no information is available about the mis ﬁt created by the precipitation
reactions. Tiley et al. investigated René 88DT by means of synchrotron X-ray and
neutron diffraction and reported increasing lattice mismatch with aging time while
initially decreasing as a function of temperature [ 87]. Whitmore et al. studied
ATI
®718PlusTM and were able to disclose a small positive lattice mismatch
between γ and γ′ after aging following on a HAZ simulation with deformation [ 88].
Dirand et al. measured the lattice mismatch during creep in a single crystal nickel
based superalloy and were able to conclude that the mis ﬁt is strongly dependent on
temperature, stress and previous plastic deformation [ 89]. Available data indicates a
strong relationship between lattice parameter mis ﬁt and SAC, however, quanti ﬁable
data from the time-temperature range where SAC occurs is yet to be obtained.

Conclusions
Hot cracking and SAC is a major concern when welding precipitation hardening Ni-
and Ni-Fe-based superalloys. There are different cracking theories providing a
general explanation of the mechanisms involved, however, there is a large gap
between the theoretical explanations and the “practical” aspects. The general beliefs
with regard to hot cracking can be summarized as follows:
• Cracking occurs when a liquid ﬁlm is present in combination with a critical
strain from the thermal gradient in the weld or from mechanical restraint.
• The wider the solidi ﬁcation range, the wider the temperature at which liquid
ﬁlms exist and therefore, a greater susceptibility towards cracking of the weld
metal.
• The level of impurities is important and should be kept as low possible.
• It is evident that the segregation plays an important role, as it is one of the
prerequisites for liquation to occur since it extends the BTR. Elements such as
Nb contribute to it.
• Fine grain size seem to have a bene ﬁcial impact.
• Increasing heat input results in increased hot cracking.
With regard to SAC, obvious challenges becomes more vital when welding high
temperature resistant nickel based superalloys. The mechanism and its main
inﬂuencing factors can be summarized as follows:
• Increasing strain in terms of weld restraint or precipitation-induced stress have
an impact on SAC as well as the ability of the material to relax residual stresses.
• The kinetics of the hardening phase is in ﬂuenced by the chemical composition
as well as the dislocation density which in turn in ﬂuence the susceptibility
towards SAC.
• Fine grain size together with grain boundary precipitates seem to be bene ﬁcial
since it inhibits grain boundary sliding.
