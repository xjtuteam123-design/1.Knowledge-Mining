TOPICAL COLLECTION: HIGH ENTROPY ALLOYS
Microstructural, Oxidation, and Mechanical Behavior
of NbTi-Based Refractory Alloys with 5 to 10 Pct Co,
Cr, and Ni Additions
U.P. OCHIEZE , L. SUN, N.A. SIMPSON , S. JOSYULA , T.K.J. KIM ,
J.L.W. CARTER , M.A. STEINER , and E.J. PAYTON
NbTi-based refractory alloys with additions of Co, Cr, and Ni represent an interesting
medium-entropy alloy system with potential for protective oxide ﬁlm formation, high strength,
and ductility. This study investigates the microstructural evolution, oxidation behavior, and
mechanical properties of NbTi-based alloys containing 5 to 10 at. pct Co, Cr, and Ni. CALPHAD
predictions suggest that this composition range can be heat treated to obtain a predominantly
body-centered cubic matrix phase. Mechanical properties, including microhardness, yield
strength, maximum strength, and speciﬁc strength are evaluated through isothermal compression
tests conducted between room temperature and 800 /C176 C. The oxidation kinetics of these alloys are
assessed through discontinuous oxidation tests. Parabolic oxidation kinetics were observed for
NbTi–10Ni and NbTi–5Co, while linear oxidation kinetics were found for NbTi–10Cr and
NbTi–10(CoCrNi). Microstructures and oxide layers are characterized using X-ray diﬀraction,
electron backscatter diﬀraction, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and scanning electron
microscopy. All alloys exhibit signiﬁcant mechanical softening between room temperature and
800 /C176 C, with elastic-perfectly plastic ﬂow observed at 800 /C176 C. The addition of 10 pct Cr to NbTi
resulted in two BCC phases up to 1050 /C176 C, conﬂicting with CALPHAD predictions of a
single-phase solid solution at this temperature, and resulting in higher ﬂow stress at 800 /C176 C.
NbTi–10(CoCrNi) exhibited the lowest ﬂow stress at 800 /C176 C despite having more ‘cocktail eﬀect’
potential and insuﬃcient molar fractions of Co, Cr, or Ni to form a desirable protective oxide ﬁlm.

I. INTRODUCTION
IN recent years, there has been a renewed interest in
refractory alloys for extreme environmental applications,
such as liquid propellant rocketengines and fusion reactors.
While it has been shown that re latively high strengths at
elevated temperatures can be retained by focusing on
solid-solution strengthening mechanisms in alloys with high
melting points, alloys of this family (which have been
referred to by multiple terms, including refractory complex
concentrated alloys, RCCAs, refractory multi-principal
element alloys, RMPEAs, or refractory high-entropy alloys,
RHEAs), frequently exhibit poor native oxidation resis-
tance, poor processability, high densities, and low ductility
at room temperature. These features limit their potential
utility.
[1–4] We will subsequently refer to this family of alloys
as RCCAs in the present work. Many of these properties
exhibited by RCCAs are linked to the elasticity tensor and
the local variability of its co mponents, including strain
energy gradients that occur during diﬀusion in the disloca-
tions,[5–8] secondary phases, and interfaces,[9–13] as well as
variations of the preferred slip planes along the dislocation
line. It is the latter eﬀect that makes RCCAs unique relative
to conventional alloys with body-centered cubic (BCC)
structures.
[14–17]
The CoCrNi equimolar system (which has a face-cen-
tered cubic structure) has been shown to exhibit a high
yield strength at elevated temperatures, high ductility,
and excellent low-temperature fracture toughness, [18]
and has garnered signiﬁcant interest both scientiﬁcally
and practically for elevated temperature and cryogenic
applications.[19,20] Senkov et al. noted that many
RCCAs contain equiatomic concentrations of NbTiZr,
NbTaTi, NbTaZr, and/or MoNbTi. [4] It is noted that

Most research on RCCAs has focused on yield
strengths at high temperatures, ignoring the fact that
these alloys pass through lower temperatures where they
are multi-phase en route to the higher operating temper-
atures. The behavior of individual phases at moderate
temperatures can play an important role in the oxidation
behavior and mechanical properties of an alloy (e.g.,
References 25, 26). The higher strengths produced by the
interfaces in the multi-phase conditions as well as the high
melting temperatures make many RCCAs diﬃcult to
process. In the presented work, BCC alloys of the
Nb–Ti–Cr, Nb–Ti–Co, Nb–Ti–Ni, and Nb–Ti–-
Co–Cr–Ni systems were studied to assess their ﬂow
behavior at elevated temperature, density, and oxidation
resistance. We hypothesize that alloys in these systems
could exhibit better processability, better room-temper-
ature ductility, lower density, and improved oxidation
resistance relative to many RCCAs previously studied in
the literature, where the primary focus has been on
strength at very high temperatures. It is well known now
that the eﬀect of equimolar composition on the actual
conﬁgurational entropy is complicated by chemical
short-range ordering and phase separation, and that
enthalpy contributions are typically larger than entropic
contributions to the Gibbs energy. We have focused on
compositions for which a predominantly BCC structure
can be obtained under standard heat treatment condi-
tions. Thus, this paper presents observations of the
microstructures, mechanical properties, and oxidation
behavior of NbTi–10(CoCrNi), NbTi–5Co, NbTi–10Cr,
and NbTi–10Ni refractory alloys, by comparing the
contribution of the individual elements Co, Cr, and Ni,
respectively, on the phase, microstructure, and strength of
the NbTi-parent alloy. The choice of these alloys was
based on the maximum solubility of Co, Cr, Ni, and
(Co–Cr–Ni) BCC single phases expected for the individ-
ual alloys at temperatures below 0.6 T
m. According to
Zhou et al.,[27] the eﬀects of high-entropy alloys, RCCAs
being a form of these, can be determined by their valence
electron concentration (VEC) and conﬁgurational
entropy. This was taken into consideration in the alloy
design and selection. The observations are made via
analytical microscopic techniques, compression strength
measurements, and discontinuous oxidation tests.

The XPS analysis was initially performed as a

IV. DISCUSSION
According to Ye et al. and Zhou et al. ,[27,31] based on
Hume–Rothery’s rules, in the absence of an observable
atomic size eﬀect, the VEC of an alloy inﬂuences the
crystallinity of the solid solution. Therefore, VEC is a
key component in determining the phase structure of
high-entropy alloys (HEAs). The face-centered cubic
(FCC) phase is present when the VEC is greater than
7.5, while the BCC phase forms at VEC values below
7.5. It was found that both solid solutions and
intermetallic phases tend to form when the VEC is
relatively low. Guo et al.
[32] established the correlation
between VEC and phase composition in high-entropy
alloys. Their ﬁndings indicate that VEC below 6.87
favors the formation of a BCC phase, while an FCC
phase becomes more stable when VEC is greater than or
equal to 8. Furthermore, both FCC and BCC phases are
observed to coexist when the VEC falls between 6.87
and 8.
[32] Based on this information, the atomic sizes of
the individual elements that constitute the alloys in
study are quite close and therefore the obtained VEC is
less than 7.5. Hence, the presence of BCC phase is
anticipated in these alloys. Entropy on the other hand
depends on the overall composition and conﬁguration of
the alloy. The conﬁgurational entropy is expressed in
terms of R (gas constant) such that certain HEAs are
deﬁned to form above 1.61 R, medium-entropy alloys
form between 0.69 to 1.61 R, and below 0.69, we have
low-entropy alloys. However, there is no ﬁxed number
for conventional alloys such that no clear distinction is
made between low-entropy and conventional alloys.
[33]

C. Oxidation
At both temperature testing regimes (800 /C176 C and
1200 /C176 C), NbTi–5Co and NbTi–10Ni exhibit parabolic
oxidation kinetics, with higher speciﬁc mass change and
increased oxidation rate at 1200 /C176 C. NbTi–10Cr exhibits
linear oxidation throughout the experiment, initially
being the least oxidation-resistant alloy, when compared
to NbTi–5Co and NbTi–10Ni at lower temperatures.
However, a switch occurs at elevated temperatures. This
behavior can be attributed to NbTi–5Co and
NbTi–10Ni approaching their liquidus temperatures,
which leads to an increase in their oxidation kinetics,
surpassing that of NbTi–10Cr. NbTi–10(CoCrNi) on
the other hand also had an improved oxidation resis-

performing alloy overall at 800 /C176 C. Cr is dispersed in
the parent phase with Nb, and this could also be the
reason why we are having improved oxidation resistance
of the NbTi–10Cr alloy with a combination of Ti that
diﬀuses fast to the surface layer. Ni-containing alloys
have shown improved resistance to corrosion and

V. CONCLUSIONS
From the observations in the present work, the
following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) Additions of 5 at. pct Co, 10 at. pct Ni, and
CoCrNi can be made to the NbTi system to
produce single-phase BCC solid solutions
approaching the lower bound of the medium-en-
tropy alloy range (near 1.0 R). The addition of 10
pct Cr to NbTi resulted in two BCC phases,
conflicting with CALPHAD predictions of a
single-phase solid solution at 1050 /C176 C.
(2) NbTi–10Cr exhibits superior oxidation perfor-
mance, high-temperature strength, and density to
NbTi–10(CoCrNi); however, it still exhibits linear
oxidation kinetics below 1200 /C176 C. NbTi–5Co and
NbTi–10Ni exhibit parabolic kinetics that
degrade rapidly between 800 /C176 C and 1200 /C176 C.
(3) All alloys exhibited a large drop in strength between
400 /C176 C and 800 /C176 C and elastic-perfectly plastic flow
at 800 /C176 C. This flow behavior is not unexpected for
the 10 at. pct Co, Ni, and CoCrNi alloys, which all
are predicted to have relatively low solidus temper-
atures. The NbTi–10Cr alloy had the highest flow
stress at 800 /C176 C, followed by NbTi–5Co,
NbTi–10Ni, then NbTi–10(CoCrNi). The higher
flow stresses of the NbTi–10Cr and NbTi–5Co
compositions could be rationalized based on a
higher solidus and retaining a two-phase
microstructure at higher temperatures, respectively.
Further investigation is required to explain the low
flow stress of NbTi–10(CoCrNi) at 800 /C176 C.
