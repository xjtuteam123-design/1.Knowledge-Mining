Here, novel medium-entropy alloys with chemical compositions of Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo 4 C 1 and 
Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo 3 C 2 (at%) exhibiting excellent tensile properties at both room and liquid nitrogen 
temperatures have been developed. Precipitation of carbides changes the chemical composition and phase 
stability of the matrix, resulting in the controlled deformation-induced martensitic transformation from 
face-centered cubic to body-centered cubic of the alloys. The carbide precipitation, lattice distortion, and 
martensitic transformation led the alloy to have ultra-high yield strength of ~1 GPa and ultimate tensile 
strength of ~2 GPa with extra work hardening at liquid nitrogen temperature.

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) 
are new classes of multi-component solid solution alloys that have 
been widely investigated over the past decades [ 1 , 2 ]. Notably, 
face-centered cubic (FCC) structured HEAs and MEAs have been re- 
ported with excellent ductility, strength, and fracture toughness at 
cryogenic temperatures [ 3 –6 ], and the alloys have become new at- 
tractive candidates for next-generation structural materials. How- 
ever, FCC HEAs and MEAs have a signiﬁcant drawback for industri- 
alization due to their relatively low yield strength at room temper- 
ature [ 5 , 7 ]. To overcome this drawback, strategies for strengthen- 
ing the alloys such as precipitation strengthening [ 8 , 9 ], grain re- 
ﬁnement [10] , and heterogeneous structuring [7] have been intro- 
duced. Recently, the non-equiatomic Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo 5 , and 
Co 18 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 7 Mo 7.5 (hereinafter referred to as Mo5, Mo7.5, at%) 
MEAs developed by Bae et al. [11] exhibited a great combination 
of strength and ductility at room temperature. The alloys exhibited 
FCC matrix and μ-phase precipitates, and the precipitates played 
an essential role in their strengthening mechanism. 
From the fact that Mo can act as a strong carbide former ele- 
ment in various steels and other alloys [ 12 –14 ], we expect that C 
doping of the non-equiatomic CoCrFeNiMo MEAs would vary the 
microstructure of the alloy with pertinent heat treatment. We ad-

The phase evolution of the Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo x C (5-x) alloys 
is shown in Fig. 1 a. The Mo5 without C is comprised of dual phases 
of the FCC matrix and μ-phase precipitates when annealed at 900 
°C, and this microstructure is expected to change with C addition. 
When C is added more than 0.2 at%, carbides start to form instead 
of μ-phase. Alloys containing 1~2 at% C consist of FCC matrix, Cr- 
rich M23C6 carbides, and (Fe, Mo)-rich M6C carbides. X-ray diffrac- 
tion (XRD) patterns of the alloys containing 1 and 2 at% C in Fig. 1 b 
indicate that the alloys consist of FCC structured matrix, Cr 23 C 6 
carbides, and Fe 3 Mo 3 C carbides, which is in good agreement with 
the calculated phase diagram.

Based on the computational thermodynamics approach, 
Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo 4 C 1 , Co 17.5 Cr 12.5 Fe 55 Ni 10 Mo 3 C 2 (hereinafter 
referred to as Mo4C1, Mo3C2, at%) MEAs were designed. This 
study demonstrates how doping C affects the microstructure and 
phase stability of FCC matrix of non-equiatomic CoCrFeNiMo 
MEA and successfully improves mechanical properties. Tensile 
properties and microstructural evolution of the designed MEAs 
were examined to this purpose, and the strengthening mechanism 
was investigated with the focus on precipitation and metastability 
engineering. 
For this experimental work, ingots of Mo4C1 and Mo3C2 were 
fabricated using vacuum induction melting with elements of purity 
above 99.9 wt%. The alloy ingots were homogenized for 6 hours at 
1100 °C, and then cold-rolled by 78.6% thickness reduction (from 7 
to 1.5 mm). The cold-rolled plates were annealed for 1 hour at 900 
°C. 
Tensile testing of the annealed alloys was conducted using In- 
stron 1361 at a strain rate of 10 −3 s −1 . The tests were performed 
at room temperature (298 K) and liquid nitrogen temperature (77 
K). Dog-bone shape tensile samples with a gauge geometry of 
6.4 × 2.5 × 1 mm 3 were cut along the rolling direction. For the 
tensile tests at 77 K, the tensile test was carried out in a liquid 
nitrogen chamber. 
Microstructures of the annealed and deformed samples were 
analyzed using XRD, scanning electron microscope (SEM), and 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Crystal structures were 
characterized with an incident beam of Cu K α radiation using a 
Rigaku D/MAX-2500 X-ray diffractometer, with a step scan speed 
of 2 °/min. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analyses were 
conducted using a ﬁeld-emission SEM with a TSL OIM EBSD soft- 
ware. Samples for XRD and SEM analyses were mechanically pol- 
ished down to 1200 SiC grit papers and electro-polished using a 
92% CH 3 COOH + 8% HClO 4 solution at 20 V. Samples for TEM anal- 
ysis were prepared with dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB, FEI He- 
lios, Pegasus) and examined with JEOL JEM-2200FS.

Initial microstructures of the alloys are shown in Fig. 2 . On 
back-scattered electron (BSE) images, (Fe, Mo)-rich M6C-type car- 
bides are identiﬁed as white precipitates in the FCC matrix located 
both in the grain boundaries and in the grain interiors, while the 
Cr-rich M23C6-type carbides appear as round-shaped grey precipi- 
tates ( Fig. 2 (b, d), Supplementary Fig. S1, and S2). The average grain 
sizes of Mo4C1 and Mo3C2 are ~5.87 μm and ~3.75 μm on EBSD 
inverse pole ﬁgure maps ( Fig. 2 (e, f)), respectively. The decrease 
in average grain size with an increase in C content is due to more 
precipitation of carbides, which hinder grain growth [20] . However, 
the decrement is not remarkable because the carbide fractions of 
the alloys are not much different, and the annealing temperature is 
not high enough to promote grain growth [ 11 , 21 ]. Bright-ﬁeld (BF) 
TEM images sharply show carbide distribution tendency within the 
matrix. The carbides are spotted both in grain interiors and bound- 
aries, and overlapping of the carbides in multiple areas suggests 
that the precipitates exist as mixtures of several carbides [22] . The 
overall volume fractions of the carbides are ~5.41% and ~6.61% for 
Mo4C1 and Mo3C2 alloys, respectively, and the mean precipitate 
diameters of the carbides are ~83.16 nm and ~93.47 nm for Mo4C1 
and Mo3C2 alloys, respectively.

Work hardening rate (WHR) curves of the alloys depicted in 
Fig. 3 b show a difference in work hardening behavior at room 
temperature and cryogenic temperature. The WHRs of the alloys 
show a monotonous decrease with strain at room temperature. 
However, at cryogenic temperature, the curves can be divided into 
three stages in the hardening behavior. For example, Mo3C2 ex- 
hibits a rapid decrease in WHR in the early plastic deformation re- 
gion (stage I), then an increase with the onset of martensitic phase 
transformation (stage II), as in other reported HEAs [23-25] , and 
a decrease in stage III. It is also clear that the increment of WHR 
becomes higher in the order of Mo3C2 > Mo4C1 > Mo5 alloys. 
Comparison of the tensile properties at 77 K with other existing al- 
loys such as HEAs/MEAs and steels noted for cryogenic applications 
(stainless steels, high-Mn steels, and high-Ni steels) on Fig. 3 (c, d) 
highlights the strikingly enhanced cryogenic performance of the 
present alloys [ 3 , 4 , 23 –33 ]. (For the comparison of tensile prop- 
erties with other alloys at room temperature, see Supplementary 
Fig. S3.) 
Many HEAs show that the deformation-induced martensitic 
transformation from metastable FCC to BCC largely contributes 
to the enhancement of strength and ductility at cryogenic tem- 
peratures [ 23 –25 ]. In addition to strengthening effects from hard 
BCC martensite itself, the increase in work hardening in Fig. 3 b 
that accompanies the phase transformation leads to this improve- 
ment in strength. Microstructural characterization of the alloys af- 
ter tensile deformation in Fig. 4 helps further understanding of 
the transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) behavior at cryogenic 
temperature. XRD patterns of the alloys after fracture at 298 K 
and 77 K are plotted in Fig. 4 a. While the XRD patterns of the 
specimens deformed at 298 K exhibit major diffraction peaks of 
FCC, carbides, and minor BCC peaks, clearer BCC peaks with higher 
intensity are detected in the specimens deformed at 77 K. This 
proves that deformation-induced phase transformation from FCC to 
BCC was activated mainly at 77 K by lower FCC stability at cryo- 
genic temperature.

the presence of hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phase along with

The WHR curve of Mo3C2 increases faster with strain and is sat- 
urated at earlier strain than that of Mo4C1. Increased C doping 
in Mo3C2 leads to reduced stability of the FCC matrix, as seen 
from the decrease in /Delta1G FCC → BCC ( Fig. 1 b), and this accelerates the 
martensitic transformation into BCC. The martensitic transforma- 
tion leads to a signiﬁcant boost in the work hardening rate, which 
greatly enhances both strength and ductility by the TRIP effect at 
cryogenic temperature. The interfaces between the FCC phase and 
the newly formed intermediate HCP, BCC phases act as additional 
hindrance to dislocation motion and increases work hardening [ 23 , 
26 , 34 ]. Strain partitioning between the FCC phase and the HCP, 
BCC phases contributes to the formation of geometrically neces- 
sary dislocations (GNDs) near the phase interfaces to accommodate 
strain gradient induced by strain partitioning, and the pile-up of 
the GNDs leads to increased hetero-deformation induced strength- 
ening which improves both strength and ductility [ 35 –38 ]. Note 
that the deformation-induced phase transformation of the C-doped 
alloys is observed at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. S4). 
However, the fraction of the transformed phase is negligible (be- 
low ~20%) even at a high applied strain ( εT = 30%) and cannot be 
considered to affect strain hardening of the alloys suﬃciently at 
room temperature. 
A salient feature of the present alloys is their superior yield 
strengths. There have been other HEAs or MEAs of high ultimate 
tensile strength or ductility at cryogenic temperature by using TRIP 
effects from metastability [ 23 –25 ] as in the present work. Never- 
theless, attaining yield strength over 600 MPa at room temper- 
ature and over 1 GPa at cryogenic temperature is a remarkable 
achievement. High yield strengths of the present alloys are mostly 
attributed to solid solution strengthening, precipitation strengthen- 
ing, and grain boundary strengthening effects. The yield strengths 
can be calculated as the sum of each strengthening contribution as 
follows: 
σY S = σ0 + /Delta1σss + /Delta1σpp t + /Delta1σgb , (1)

Alloying of Mo and C in the matrix enhances solid solution 
strengthening. C atoms ﬁll interstitial sites of the matrix and in- 
duce interstitial strengthening [ 39 , 40 ]. Mo has a larger atomic size 
than the other alloying elements and intensiﬁes lattice distortion 
by ﬁlling substitutional sites, leading to additional solid solution 
strengthening as well [41] . In Eq. (1) , σ0 + /Delta1σss can be thought 
of as the sum of the interstitial strengthening by C and the lat- 
tice friction stress enhanced by Mo. By taking the lattice friction 
stress from CoCrFeMnNi HEA (~125 MPa) [42] and the interstitial 
strengthening by C from CoCrFeMnNi HEA containing 1 at% C (~78 
MPa/at%) [22] , σ0 + /Delta1σss can be estimated as ~146 MPa and ~156 
MPa for the Mo4C1 and Mo3C2 alloys, respectively, because C con- 
tents dissolved in the matrix of the Mo4C1 and Mo3C2 alloys are 
estimated to be ~0.27 at% and ~0.4 at% ( Table 1 ). 
The carbides also play an important role in strengthening the 
alloys. M6C and nano M23C6 carbides formed at the annealing 
temperature of 900 °C exist in grain boundaries and the interior of 
grains. (Fe, Mo)-rich M6C carbides are reported to have a high es- 
timated Vickers hardness of 23.3 GPa [43] , which renders the car- 
bides non-shearable in plastic deformation. Hard M6C particles in 
grain interiors impede the movement of dislocations, and therefore 
induce precipitation strengthening. M23C6 carbides are also known 
to induce bowing of dislocations and Orowan strengthening, as the 
carbides are incoherent with the FCC HEA matrix [ 39 , 44 ]. The pre-

In summary, doping C in non-equiatomic CoCrFeNiMo MEA 
led to metastability of the matrix at cryogenic temperature, by 
alteration in the composition of the matrix during heat treat- 
ment due to the precipitation of carbides, and eventually activat- 
ing phase transformation during deformation. The resulting Mo4C1 
and Mo3C2 alloys initially consist of metastable FCC matrix and 
two types of carbides, M6C- and M23C6-types, and the metastable 
FCC transforms into BCC during deformation at cryogenic temper- 
ature. The alloys performed excellently under tensile tests both at 
room temperature and liquid nitrogen temperature, showing supe- 
rior levels of yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and ductility. 
Especially, at liquid nitrogen temperature, the strength and ductil- 
ity of the alloys are on an unprecedented level. Formation of the 
carbides in the interiors and boundaries of grains induces precipi- 
tation strengthening and grain boundary strengthening effects, re- 
sulting in high yield strength of the present alloys, along with solid 
solution strengthening effect enhanced by alloying of Mo and C. 
The high tensile strength and ductility at 77 K are attributed to 
work hardening by TRIP effects, achieved by decreasing phase sta- 
bility of FCC matrix with increasing C doping and decreasing tem- 
perature. This study offers wider possibilities in alloy design with 
enhanced mechanical properties of HEAs and MEAs, with the aid 
of multiple strengthening mechanisms.
