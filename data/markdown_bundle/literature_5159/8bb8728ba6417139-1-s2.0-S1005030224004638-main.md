The long-lasting expectation “the hotter the engine, the better ” calls for the development of high- 
temperature metallic alloys. Although the high speciﬁc strengths of titanium alloys are compelling for 
such applications, their deleterious softening beyond 600 °C imposes serious limitations. Much has been 
known for decades regarding the phase metallurgy for precipitation strengthening design in titanium al- 
loys, however, the other facile strength promotion mechanism, dispersion strengthening, remains compar- 
atively less-explored and unutilized. The present research concerns the multi-scale dispersion strength- 
ening in titanium alloys, with mechanistic emphases on the critical plasticity micro-events that affect 
strength preservation. Due to the simultaneous introduction of intragranular dispersoids and intergran- 
ular reinforcers, the current titanium alloys present superior engineering tensile strength of 519 MPa at 
700 °C. Throughout the examined 25–800 °C temperature range, noticeable softening induced by the ther- 
mal activation occurs above 600 °C, accompanied by evident strength loss. The temperature-dependence 
transition of dominated softening mechanisms from dynamic recovery to dynamic recrystallization has 
been clariﬁed by theoretical calculations. Furthermore, the strengthening effect of multi-scale architec- 
tures is underpinned as the enhanced dislocation strengthening owing to the introduction of thermally- 
stable heterointerfaces, which could generically guide the design of similar heat-resistant titanium alloys.

1. Introduction 
The loss of strength is ubiquitous in metallic alloys upon tem- 
perature increase, which mechanistically originates from the en- 
hanced atomic mobility [1] . Because of thermal activation, screw 
dislocation glide and cross-slip become easier and the potency of 
edge dislocation climb also enhances [2] . As a result, the rearrange- 
ment and annihilation of dislocations reduce the work hardening 
rate, which is well-documented as the plasticity origin of high- 
temperature softening [ 3 , 4 ]. At the poly-crystal length scale, grain 
boundary sliding, migration, and intergranular fracture were also 
reported when the testing temperatures exceeded 0.5 Tm [ 5 , 6 ], im- 
posing further challenges to strength preservation at elevated tem- 
peratures. 
The promotion of high-temperature mechanical properties of 
metallic alloys thus fundamentally relies on the hinderance of 
dislocation motion and the suppression of grain/phase boundary-

mediated plasticity micro-events. It is well-known that nanosized 
precipitates can act as barriers against dislocation motion through 
the compelling dislocation/precipitate interactions [ 7 , 8 ]. Provided 
the established theoretical guidance [9] , pursuing effective pre- 
cipitation strengthening requires considerations of thermodynamic 
and crystallographic factors including the lattice misﬁt between 
precipitate/matrix, precipitate size, distribution, volume fraction 
and especially the thermal stability [10] . But for titanium alloys, 
one of the most promising materials in aero-industry due to the 
higher speciﬁc strength and superior heat resistance [11] , it is 
diﬃcult to obtain thermally stable precipitates through composi- 
tion optimization. Thus, dispersing second phases with better ther- 
mal stability is hence the preferred strategy of high-temperature 
strengthening for titanium alloys. 
Over the past three decades, several kinds of reinforcements 
such as TiB, TiC, Ti5 Si3 , Al2 O3 and rare earth oxides (REO) 
have been applied to dispersion-strengthened titanium alloys [12–
14] . Owing to the introduction of reinforcements with high- 
temperature endurability, the high-temperature strength, creep 
and oxidation resistance are signiﬁcantly enhanced. Classical the- 
ories of dispersion strengthening always suggest the maximization 
of strength promotion by achieving a spatially uniform distribu-

tion [15] . It is however unfortunate that these sorts of consider- 
ations overlook the origin of grain/phase boundary-induced soft- 
ening, which is spatially inhomogeneous. Even at ambient tem- 
perature, numerous in-situ deformation experiments have under- 
pinned the plastic strain localized at grain/phase boundary can 
be orders of magnitude higher than grain interior [16–18] . Such 
a trend is exacerbated with thermal activation, as discussed ear- 
lier, leading to detrimental grain/phase boundary sliding and hence 
early-stage damage nucleation [19] . In this regard, achieving site- 
speciﬁc strengthening mechanisms is of fundamental importance. 
Thereby, tailoring the nanoscale REO uniformly distributed into α- 
Ti and microscale TiB and TiC at grain boundaries, the dislocation 
motion and the grain boundary sliding can be simultaneously im- 
peded. 
However, with the further increase of temperature, all the 
reported dispersion-strengthened titanium alloys suffer from a 
precipitous reduction in strength, indicating an obvious high- 
temperature softening [ 20 , 21 ]. It is widely recognized that the 
degradation of strength is mainly attributed to the intrinsic limi- 
tation of titanium. Obvious dynamic recovery (DRV) and dynamic 
recrystallization (DRX) often take place in titanium alloys when the 
deformation temperatures exceed 0.5 Tm [22] . Numerous studies 
have investigated the softening behaviors of titanium alloys within 
a wide temperature range [23–25] . However, to our best knowl- 
edge, the researches on the softening behaviors of dispersion- 
strengthened titanium alloys are mainly carried out above 800 °C 
[26–28] . There is a lack of detailed discussions on the intrinsic 
softening mechanisms from a theoretical perspective, and the ef- 
fects of reinforcements on the softening of matrix alloys are still 
ambiguous. Thus, a systematic investigation of the deformation 
mechanisms within a wide temperature range is necessary, and the 
corresponding softening mechanisms are needed to be clariﬁed. 
The present research proposes a multi-scale dispersion 
strengthening strategy and successfully constructs multi-scale 
architectures in the high-temperature titanium alloys by powder 
metallurgy process. Within the multi-scale architectures, there 
exist intragranular dispersoids and intergranular reinforcers with 
sizes ranging from micro-scale to submicron-scale. Analyzing the 
strengthening effects of different types, sizes and distributions 
of reinforcements could guide the design of more heat-resistant 
titanium alloys in the future. With the assistance of multi-scale 
electron microscopy and diffractometry characterizations, two 
fundamental questions are examined: (1) to what extent can the 
grain and phase boundary-induced softening be improved in the 
25–800 °C temperature range? (2) What are the critical disloca- 
tion plasticity micro-events that are responsible for softening? 
Answering these questions would also provide a new perspective 
for revealing the high-temperature deformation mechanisms of 
dispersion-strengthened titanium alloys. 
2. Material and methods 
2.1. Materials processing 
Ti55 alloy powders (with the chemical composition of Ti-5.5Al- 
3.7Sn-1.9Zr-1.0Mo-0.27Si-0.83Nd) obtained through the atomiza- 
tion powdering process are selected as the matrix alloy powders. 
The high cooling rate in the atomization powdering process can ef- 
fectively control the size and distribution of submicron rare earth 
compounds, which is challenging for the traditional casting pro- 
cess. The average particle size of Ti55 alloy powders was about 
120 μm. High-purity TiB2 powders and Si powders with particle 
sizes less than 5 μm were selected as the B and Si sources. Ac- 
cording to the speciﬁc chemical composition of the composites, the 
raw powders were weighted and mixed using low-energy milling 
(LEM) under the protection of Ar atmosphere. Then the mixture 
of powders was hot pressed (HP) in a vacuum (10−2 Pa). During 
the in-situ reaction process, multi-scale architectures were synthe- 
sized according to the following chemical reactions as shown in 
Eqs. (1) and (2) . 
Ti +Ti B2 → 2TiB (1) 
xTi + ( 5 − x ) Zr + 3Si → (TiZr ) 5 Si3 (2) 
The detailed process parameters can be found in Fig. S1 in Sup- 
plementary Information. For clarity, dispersion-strengthened tita- 
nium alloys with multi-scale architectures were named as compos- 
ites in this paper, for instance, (TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 composites. 
As illustrated in Figs. S2 and S3, the content of reinforcements in 
the composite was designed as 3 vol.% TiBw + 2 vol.% (TiZr)5 Si3 , 
and the rest was Ti55 matrix alloy (95 vol.%), namely (3 vol.% 
TiBw + 2 vol.% (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 composite. For comparative analysis, 
Ti55 alloy, 3 vol.% TiBw/Ti55 composite and 2 vol.% (TiZr)5 Si3 /Ti55 
composite were also fabricated via the same manufacturing pro- 
cess, and the corresponding microstructural information can be 
seen in Fig. S2. 
2.2. Mechanical testing 
Tensile tests were conducted over a wide temperature range 
from 25 °C to 800 °C with a strain rate of 10−3 s−1 . Specimens 
used in the tests had a 3 mm wide and 15 mm long gauge section, 
which was ground and mechanically polished before deformation. 
Specimens were heated to the test temperature and held for 5 min 
for homogenizing the temperature of the gauge section. To in- 
vestigate the evolution of microstructures during straining, tensile 
tests at 650, 700 and 800 °C were interrupted at peak strains. The 
Young’s modulus and shear modulus were measured from 25 °C 
to 800 °C by the high-temperature dynamic elastic modulus and 
damping analyzer (RFDA-HTVP-1750C). The specimens were cut 
into 80 mm × 20 mm × 2 mm, which were ground and mechani- 
cally polished before measurement. 
2.3. Microstructural characterization 
Microstructural characterizations of the as-sintered and the 
stretched materials were examined by employing scanning elec- 
tron microscopy (SEM, Zesis Supra 55 Sapphire) equipped with 
an electron backscattering diffractometer (EBSD). The SEM sam- 
ples were mechanically polished and then etched in Kroll’s reagent. 
EBSD measurements were performed on the normal direction 
plane of the deformed specimens and the data were processed by 
the Channel 5 software. The EBSD specimens were prepared by 
mechanical grinding and electropolishing using a solution of 60 % 
carbinol, 35 % N-butanol and 5 % perchloric acid (vol.%) at 45 V. To 
examine the evolution of dislocation densities and the activation of 
slip systems, X-ray diffraction (XRD, Panalytical X’PERT) with Cu- 
Kα radiation was carried out with the scanning speed of 1 °/min. 
For the XRD specimens, the preparation processes were consis- 
tent with those of EBSD specimens. The dislocation conﬁguration 
was characterized by scanning transmission electron microscopy 
(STEM, FEI Talos 200X) equipped with a high-angle annular dark 
ﬁeld (HAADF) detector. The TEM specimens were ﬁrst mechanically 
ground to about 50 μm in thickness, followed by ion milling using 
a precision ion polishing system (GATAN).

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructures of the as-sintered materials 
Fig. 1 shows the initial microstructures of the as-sintered 
(TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 composites. It can be seen from Fig. 1 (a) 
and (d) that the micron-TiB whiskers were distributed around the 
matrix alloys forming the ﬁrst-scale network-structure. The aver- 
age aspect ratio of TiBw was about 11.1 μm (statistical analysis 
shown in Fig. S4). The size distribution of ﬁrst-scale networks is 
shown in Fig. 1 (g). The average size of the network cell was about 
110.7 μm, which was comparable to the average particle size of 
alloy powders. Due to the large difference in solid solubility of Si 
in α-Ti and β-Ti, the saturated Si would precipitate at the α/ β
interface during the phase transition, generating the second-scale 
network-structure ( Fig. 1 (b)). Based on the EDS mapping results 
in Fig. 1 (e), the silicides were rich in Si and Zr. Such silicides 
have been reported in our previous works and were identiﬁed as 
(TiZr)5 Si3 according to the SAD patterns [29] . The average particle 
size of (TiZr)5 Si3 was about 0.54 μm (Fig. S4). The average thick- 
ness of α lamellar microstructures was about 6.7 μm, which could 
be considered as the size of second-scale networks, as depicted in 
Fig. 1 (h). Besides, a large number of submicron rare earth com- 
pounds were dispersed in the interior of α-Ti, which could serve as 
the intragranular strengthening structures ( Fig. 1 (c)). The average 
particle size of rare earth compounds was about 0.29 μm (Fig. S4). 
Combined with EDS mapping results and XRD results, the nominal 
composition of the rare earth compounds was Nd5 Sn3 , as shown 
in Fig. 1 (f) and (i). To further conﬁrm the phase composition of 
Nd5 Sn3 , the corresponding electron diffraction results can be seen 
in Fig. S5.

3.4. Microstructure characterization 
3.4.1. Evolution of LAGB misorientations 
According to the analysis of σ−1/ T relationships, the tempera- 
ture range of 25–800 °C can be subdivided into three stages: ( Ⅰ ) the 
hardly DRV stage below 600 °C, ( Ⅱ ) the obvious DRV stage above 
600 °C, and ( Ⅲ ) the DRX stage above 700 °C. To investigate the mi- 
crostructure evolution during high-temperature softening, the mi- 
crostructures of Ti55 alloys after tensile fracture at three character- 
istic temperatures (650, 700 and 800 °C) are presented in Fig. 5 (a–
c). The α colony exhibited an elongation along the tensile direction 
with the increase of temperature and plastic strain. At the temper- 
ature of 800 °C, a large number of ﬁne grains were observed at the 
α colony boundaries and formed a “necklace structure”, which was 
deemed as an important indicator of DRX [24] . While compared 
with the alloys, the microstructures of the (TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 
composites after tensile fracture at the three temperatures were 
much similar, as shown in Fig. 5 (d–f). 
The evolution of grain (and subgrain) boundary structures is 
also an important aspect of investigating the softening mecha- 
nisms [ 3 , 35 ]. To systematically investigate the evolution with in- 
creasing plastic strains, the microstructures of the samples with

work. The distributions of low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) 
and high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) for Ti55 alloys and 
(TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 composites with different strains at 650, 
70 0 and 80 0 °C are shown in Fig. 6 . When the temperature was 
low, the frequency of LAGBs for the alloys was increased with the 
increase of strains ( Fig. 6 (a)). When the temperature increased to 
700 °C, the frequency of LAGBs increased to the maximum at the

4.2. On the origin of high-temperature softening 
As mentioned in the previous sections, the (TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/ 
Ti55 composites would experience a transformation from DRV to 
DRX with the increasing temperature. Although there were many 
differences between the two softening behaviors (such as the 
stress-strain curves, strain-hardening rate curves, and microstruc- 
tures), the essence of them was to consume the stored energy 
through the rearrangement and annihilation of dislocations. Ac- 
cording to the kinetic equation [52] , the peak stress σp is de- 
pendent on the peak strain, strain rate, testing temperature and 
structure parameter. It is well recognized that the dislocation den- 
sity is the most signiﬁcant structural parameter in determining the 
strength of metallic materials. Thus, utilizing Bailey-Hirsch rela- 
tionship, the contribution of the effect of dislocation density on 
the peak stress can be described as Eq. (16) [ 34 , 53 ], 
σdis = αMT μb√ 
ρ (16) 
where α is the dislocation interaction constant (typically taken as 
0.5 [53] ), MT is the Taylor factor (typically taken as 2.0–3.2 for the 
α-Ti [54] ), μ is the shear modulus (taken as 33, 32 and 30 GPa 
for 650, 700 and 800 °C, respectively, which are measured from 
the modulus tests), b is the modiﬁed Burgers vector and ρ is the 
measured dislocation density. It should be noted that the results 
obtained here did not indicate the exact values of the dislocation 
strengthening. Nevertheless, it was found that in the temperature 
range of 650–700 °C, the decline extent of the dislocation strength- 
ening /Delta1σdis ( ∼109 MPa) was equivalent to the reduction of the 
peak-stress /Delta1σpeak ( ∼98 MPa). While in the temperature range of 
70 0–80 0 °C, the /Delta1σpeak ( ∼226 MPa) was much higher than the 
/Delta1σdis ( ∼174 MPa). Such phenomena suggested that the reduction 
of dislocation densities was the dominant softening mechanism 
when the temperature was relatively low, while there existed an- 
other softening mechanism above 700 °C. 
Besides the reduction of dislocation densities, the fractions of 
⟨ a ⟩ and ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocations also changed with the rising temper- 
ature. It can be seen in Table 1 that the fraction of ⟨ a ⟩ disloca- 
tions and the ratio of the fraction for ⟨ a ⟩ to ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocations 
increased ﬁrstly and then tended to be stable. Compared with the 
other slip systems, the fraction of ⟨ a ⟩ type was the highest, sug- 
gesting that the deformation of composites was dominated by ⟨ a ⟩ 
slip, which was consistent with the previous studies [55] . With the 
increase of temperature, the CRSS for all slip systems would de- 
crease slightly [46] . While the < a > / < c + a > ratios increased ob- 
viously, indicating that ⟨ a ⟩ slip became easier with the rising tem- 
perature. Thus, the signiﬁcant decrease in CRSS for ⟨ a ⟩ slip would 
also contribute to the reduction of peak-stress. 
GBS and PBS are another important high-temperature deforma- 
tion mechanisms for the polycrystalline and multiphase materials, 
respectively. Utilizing the internal-variable theory of inelastic de- 
formation, Kim et al. pointed out that a signiﬁcant amount of GBS 
occurred during the deformation of Ti-7Al-1.5 V and Ti-6Al-4 V al- 
loys at 815 °C [56] . Considering the effects of dislocation slip and 
GBS, the predicted curves showed good agreement with the exper- 
imental data. Besides, Alabort et al. investigated the superplastic 
mechanisms for the Ti-6Al-4 V alloys through in-situ SEM obser- 
vations [57] . A certain amount of slip bands and cavitations were 
found to accommodate the GBS when tension at intermediate tem- 
peratures (800–850 °C), while no GBS was observed below 700 °C.

4.3. On the effectiveness of multi-scale dispersion strengthening 
It is widely recognized that the enhancement of the yield 
strength for dispersion-strengthened alloys was attributed to the 
following aspects: ( Ⅰ ) the ﬁne-grain strengthening, ( Ⅱ ) the disloca- 
tion strengthening, and ( Ⅲ ) the load-bearing strengthening, which 
could well explain the strengthening effects during the elastic- 
plastic deformation [60] . In this work, the softening mechanisms of 
alloys and composites have been discussed in detail in the previ- 
ous sections. Based on this, the corresponding strengthening mech- 
anisms for dispersion-strengthened titanium alloys during the in- 
elastic deformation can be revealed. Owing to the large difference 
in mechanical properties, the incompatible deformation would in- 
evitably cause a large stress concentration between the matrix and 
reinforcements. The presence of stress concentration promoted the 
activation of ⟨ c + a ⟩ slip, which was hard to be activated in alloys 
[61] . Therefore, the multiplication of dislocations in composites can 
be accelerated. Although the dislocation densities decreased with 
the rising temperature, the dislocation density of composites was 
higher than that of alloys, exhibiting a more effective dislocation 
strengthening. To clarify the synergistic strengthening mechanisms 
of the multi-scale architectures, the concept of strength increment 
/Delta1σb is introduced to describe the strengthening effects of different 
strengthening architectures on the ultimate strength. The strength 
increment can be expressed as Eq. (17) , 
/Delta1σb = σcomposite − σalloy

Ti55 alloy. Based on the strength of Ti55 alloy at different tem- 
peratures, the strengthening effects of different reinforcements ex- 
hibited distinct trends with the increase of temperature, as shown 
in Fig. 14 . When the temperature was below 600 °C, the strength- 
ening effects of TiBw, (TiZr)5 Si3 and TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 were inde-

of (TiZr)5 Si3 and TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 increased while the strengthen-

TiBw was mainly attributed to the single dislocation strengthening 
effect due to the introduction of Ti/TiBw heterointerfaces. As the 
temperature increased, obvious DRV occurred when the tempera- 
ture was above 600 °C and the sliding tendency of interfaces was 
also enhanced. Since the micron-TiBw (ﬁrst-scale network struc-

were hardly able to inhibit the gliding of α-Ti/ β-Ti interfaces. Due 
to the relatively higher proportion of α-Ti/ β-Ti interfaces in Ti55 
alloys, failure to suppress the α-Ti/ β-Ti interface sliding would lead 
to the reduction of the strengthening effect. However, com pared 
with ﬁrst-scale network structures, second-network structures and 
multi-scale architectures could not only impede the dislocation 
motion, but also play a signiﬁcant role in suppressing the α-Ti/ β-Ti 
interface sliding, which would guide for the design of more heat- 
resistant advancing titanium alloys.

5. Conclusions 
In this work, we have fabricated novel heat-resistant titanium 
alloys with multi-scale structures. The corresponding softening 
mechanisms in the temperature range of 25–800 °C were system- 
atically investigated based on the evolutions of tensile properties, 
LAGB misorientations and dislocation conﬁgurations with the ris- 
ing temperature. Furthermore, the essence of strengthening mech- 
anisms for such dispersion-strengthened titanium alloys was dis- 
cussed. Several conclusions are drawn as follows: 
(1) The introduction of multi-scale architectures could signiﬁ- 
cantly enhance the strength of (TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 com- 
posites from 25 °C to 800 °C. The heat-resistant tempera- 
ture has been increased by nearly 50 °C. However, the trend 
of tensile properties, such as stress-strain curves and strain- 
hardening rate curves, with the increase of temperature dis- 
played a similar softening behavior, which was regardless of 
the reinforcement type and distribution. 
(2) Combined with the analysis of tensile properties and the mi- 
crostructure characterizations, the temperature range of 25–
800 °C could be subdivided into three stages. Below 600 °C, 
the stress increased with strains until the facture, represent- 
ing the hard DRV stage. With the increase of temperature, an 
inﬂection point near 600 °C was detected in the Arrhenius 
plot and the rearrangement of dislocations was observed 
above 600 °C, representing the obvious DRV stage. Above 
700 °C, the deformation activation energy was equivalent to 
that for dislocation creep and a typical “necklace structure”
was found in alloys, representing the DRX stage. 
(3) The reduction of dislocation densities and decrease in CRSS 
for ⟨ a ⟩ slip systems were the major softening mechanisms 
below 700 °C, which could be summarized as DRV domi- 
nated. Above 700 °C, PBS behaviors were observed at the α- 
Ti/ β-Ti and α-Ti/TiBw interfaces, which was another impor- 
tant softening mechanism. Furthermore, the fractions of ⟨ a ⟩ , 
⟨ c + a ⟩ and ⟨ c ⟩ dislocations were used to modify the average 
Burgers vector. Applying the averaged Burgers vector to the 
Estrin-Mecking model, the transformation from DRV to DRX 
was veriﬁed in theory. 
(4) Although the introduction of multi-scale architectures could 
not avoid the occurrence of softening, the dislocation den- 
sity of (TiBw + (TiZr)5 Si3 )/Ti55 composites was higher than 
that of Ti55 alloys. Dislocation strengthening was the main 
strengthening mechanism of dispersion-strengthened tita-
