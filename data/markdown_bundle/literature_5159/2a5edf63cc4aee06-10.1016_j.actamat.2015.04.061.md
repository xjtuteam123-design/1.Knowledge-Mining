abstract
Nickel superalloys with cubic L1 2 structured c0 (Ni3(Al, Ti)) precipitates exhibit high strength at high tem-
peratures and excellent corrosion resistance when exposed to water. Unlike prior studies on irradiation
damage of other Ni-based superalloys, our study on Rene N4 involves much larger c0 precipitates,
/C24 450 nm in size, a size regime where the irradiation-induced disordering and dissolution kinetics and
the corresponding mechanical property evolution are unknown. We report that under heavy ion irradi-
ation at room temperature, the submicron-sized c0 precipitates were fully disordered at /C24 0.3 dpa and
only later partially dissolved after 75 dpa irradiation. Nanoindentation experiments indicate that the
mechanical properties of the alloy change signiﬁcantly, with a dramatic decrease in hardness, with irra-
diation dose. Three contributions to the change in hardness were examined: defect clusters, disordering
and dissolution. The generation of defect clusters in the matrix and precipitates slightly increased the
indentation hardness, while disordering of the submicron-sized
c0 precipitates resulted in a dramatic
decrease in the total hardness, which decreased further during the early stages of the intermixing
between c0 precipitates and matrix (<18 dpa). Controlling the long-range-ordering and chemical inter-
mixing can be used to tailor the mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys under irradiation.

1. Introduction
Ni-based alloys have been proposed as structural materials in
next generation nuclear reactors due to their superior
high-temperature mechanical properties and corrosion resistance
[1–4]. For instance, solid solution strengthened Inconel 617 is con-
sidered as a candidate material for the components in high temper-
ature gas-cooled fast reactors (GFR), such as in the heat exchangers
and turbine blades [1]. The precipitation-hardened Nimonic PE 16
alloy has been tested as a cladding material in fast breeder reactors
(FBR) [5]. In addition, Ni-based superalloys are often used for spal-
lation target and window applications, where the materials have to
withstand low-temperature irradiation with high-energy protons
and spallation neutrons [6–8].
The high-temperature strength of Ni-based superalloys mainly
originates from the long-range ordered (LRO) c0 (Ni3(Al, Ti)) precip-
itates, possessing the cubic L1 2 structure, and c00 (Ni3Nb)
precipitates, possessing the hexagonal D0 22 structure [9,10].
However, these precipitates are susceptible to the phase instability
under irradiation environments. Several radiation damage studies
have focused on the behavior of these precipitates under irradia-
tion. Examples include a study using neutron and proton irradia-
tion separately at low temperatures (32–55 /C176C), where it was
shown that the precipitate phases, c0 and c00 with a size of 10–
20 nm, in Inconel 718 became disordered by 0.6 dpa [11].I n
another study on Nimonic PE16 with /C24 20 nm c0 (Ni3Al and Ni 3Ti)
precipitates, it was shown that the precipitates were fully dis-
solved after Ni ion irradiation to a dose of 10 dpa at room temper-
ature [12,13].
Irradiation-induced disordering of LRO precipitates is attributed
to the creation of vacancies, interstitials and anti-site defects,
which can disrupt the chemical and structural ordering of the pre-
cipitates [14–18]. However, as diffusion is enhanced, for example,
by irradiation or thermal annealing, the disordered atoms tend to
return to the equilibrium sites and the annihilation of defects can
be promoted [12,19,20]. Müller et al. [21] reported that the disor-
dering kinetics below room temperature are mainly based on the

In addition to disordering, the displacement of atoms induced
by irradiation can cause the atoms in precipitates to migrate into
the surrounding matrix, resulting in the dissolution of precipitates
[14,23,24]. Nelson et al. [25] pointed out that the occurrence of dis-
ordering can destabilize the precipitates and promote dissolution.
The chemical dissolution can be controlled by cascade-induced
ballistic displacements and thermal spikes. Sigmund et al. [26]
concluded that ballistic displacements have a dominating inﬂu-
ence on the mixing behaviors during the low-energy ion bombard-
ment. Rossum et al. [27] explained the ion mixing rate of metallic
bilayers at temperature of liquid nitrogen by using a model based
on thermal spikes. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on
radiation-induced mixing of Cu/Nb interface showed that the
atoms undergo ballistic displacement within ﬁrst 0.3 ps. After
0.3 ps, thermal spikes start to shrink and last /C24 10 ps [28].
Ordered intermetallic compounds normally have high strength
and poor ductility at ambient temperature, whereas their disor-
dered counterparts tend to be softer and more ductile. For exam-
ple, disordered fcc Ni 3Al exhibited almost twice the critical strain
to fracture, more than three times the fracture toughness and
/C24 20% lower hardness than ordered L1 2 structured Ni 3Al [29].
Ni-based superalloys with c and c0 phases generally possess a good
combination of strength and ductility, while the buildup of inter-
stitials and vacancy clusters, radiation-induced disordering and
dissolution of c0 precipitates can cause a signiﬁcant degradation
of mechanical properties. Maloy et al. [30] reported that, for
Alloy 718 strengthened by c0 and c00 precipitates /C24 20 nm in diam-
eter, the uniform elongation decreased to less than 2% after 0.5 dpa
of neutron exposure, and the yield strength increased slightly at
low doses up to 1 dpa due to the generation of dislocation loops
and then decreased at higher doses because of the disordering
and dissolution of the precipitates.
Ni-based superalloy Rene N4 is the ﬁrst generation single crys-
tal turbine airfoil alloy with enhanced oxidation resistance and
superior long-time rupture strength [31,32]. Compared to the
other precipitation hardening Ni-based superalloy, like Inconel
718 and Nimonic PE 16, the size of the c0 precipitates of Rene N4
in this study is much larger, /C24 450 nm. Irradiation-induced disor-
dering, dissolution and the corresponding mechanical response of
submicron-sized
c0 precipitates are still unknown. Here we per-
formed in situ ion irradiation experiments to directly observe the
order–disorder transformation of c0 precipitates and ex situ irradi-
ation experiments to explore the kinetics of chemical intermixing
between c0 precipitates and matrix under high-dose irradiation.
Nanoindentation testing was employed to examine the evolution
of mechanical properties of irradiated Rene N4 with respect to
the disordering and dissolution of c0 precipitates.
2. Methodologies
2.1. Materials and ion irradiations
The chemical composition of the single crystal Ni-based super-
alloy Rene N4 is Ni–9.8Cr–7.5Co–6W–4.8Ta–4.2Al–3.5Ti–1.5Mo–0.
15Hf–0.05C (wt.%), and the details of the heat treatment have been
reported [33]. In situ Kr2+ ion irradiations with an energy of 1 MeV
at room temperature were performed under IVEM at the Argonne
National Laboratory [34] to study the microstructure evolution at
a low ﬂuence, 1 /C2 1014 ions/cm2. The average ﬂux of Kr ions was
2 /C2 1011/cm2 s. Ni 3+ ion irradiations with an energy of 3 MeV at
room temperature were performed at different ﬂuences. Ex situ
Ni3+ ion irradiation experiments were performed in the Ion Beam
Materials Laboratory in Los Alamos National Laboratory on a
Tandem accelerator using ﬂuences of 1 /C2 1015, 2.4 /C2 1016 and
1 /C2 1017/cm2 with a ﬂux of 6 /C2 1012/cm2 s, and in situ irradiation
facility at Sandia National Laboratories [35] was used to examine
the order-disordering transition under Ni 3+ ion irradiation. SRIM
simulations were performed to predict the proﬁle of damage and
Kr and Ni ion concentration distribution by using Kinchin–Peace
method [36]. The displacement energy, 25 eV, was used in the cal-
culations. The results in Fig. 1 show that the Kr ions with an energy
of 1 MeV and Ni 3+ ions with an energy of 3 MeV penetrate deep
into the material, leaving behind a relatively uniform damage
region over the ﬁrst 100 nm of the sample. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) sample foils were used in irradiation experi-
ments for microstructure characterization, and disks with 3 mm
diameter were irradiated for mechanical testing.
2.2. Material characterization
Electron microscopy analysis was performed to study the evolu-
tion of long-range ordering, defect clusters and chemical stoi-
chiometry before and after irradiation. Field emission scanning
electron microscopy (FESEM), using an FEI Inspect, was used to
observe the overall microstructure of Rene N4. TEM measurements
were performed to examine the irradiation-induced microstruc-
ture using both a FEI Tecnai F30 and JEOL 3000F operating at
300 keV. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)
equipped with energy dispersion spectrum (EDS) was used to iden-
tify the chemical dissolution of precipitates under irradiation. TEM
sample foils were prepared by twin-jet polishing in 5% perchloric
acid ethanol solution at temperature of /C0 30 /C176C.

To investigate the effect of irradiation-induced disordering, dis-
solution and defect clusters on the mechanical strength, nanoin-
dentation hardness measurements were performed on the
samples with different radiation damage levels. A Nano Indenter

4. Discussion
4.1. Disordering of c0 precipitates
The c0 precipitates in Rene N4 start to disorder immediately
after irradiation and become completely disordered by a very
low dose of 0.3 dpa at room temperature. The order-disordering
transformation occurs over three different timescales under irra-
diation. First, in the initial collision stage, disordering can be
induced by mixing within displacement cascades with local mol-
ten cores. Second, the population of the cascade-induced point
defect increases rapidly and defect clusters are created, as shown
in Fig. 3 e. The generation and migration of point defects, defect
clusters and anti-site defects disrupt the ordered sites and induce
disorder. Third, the dissolution of the precipitates results in the
instability of the stoichiometry and thus destabilizes the ordering
of the precipitates. Destruction of LRO in precipitates and the
chemical intermixing between precipitates and matrix occur
simultaneously [12], while for our study at room temperature,
disordering of the c0 precipitates in Rene N4 occurs at relatively
low doses (<0.3 dpa), which do not allow for the full dissolution
of submicron-sized c0 precipitates, as shown in Fig. 8 . MD simu-
lations [38] also show that L1 2 crystalline LRO of Ni 3Al was lost

4.2. Dissolution of c0 precipitates
The chemical concentration proﬁles of phase boundaries in irra-
diated Rene N4 show that after irradiation at 75 dpa, the precipi-
tates are only partially dissolved and the chemical aggregate
domains can still be clearly observed in the sample, as shown in
Fig. 8 . In Camus’s work [12], the c0 precipitates in Nimonic PE16

kinetic model and suggested that the ordered precipitates could
survive with longer life time before complete dissolution through
increasing their volume fraction. The chemical intermixing associ-

thermal spike processes. The mixing parameter ( k)o f c/c0 interface

4.3. Evolution of nanoindentation hardness
Precipitates can impede the motion of mobile dislocations
through a variety of interaction mechanisms, including ordering
strengthening, coherency strengthening, modulus hardening, etc.
[43]. Here the evolution of the nanoindentation hardness of irradi-
ated Rene N4 is determined by the combined effects of the forma-
tion of defect clusters, disordering and dissolution. Each has a
different effect on the strength of the alloy: (1)
Radiation-induced defect clusters, such as dislocation loops, pro-
duce resistance forces against dislocation glide and thus result in
obstacle hardening [44–46]. (2) During the interaction of disloca-
tions with ordered precipitates, antiphase boundaries (APBs) can
be created. The creation of APBs opposes the motion of dislocations
that move into the ordered precipitates and thus enhance the
strength of the alloy. Radiation-induced disordering can result in
the loss of the ordering strengthening. (3) The misﬁt of lattice
parameters between precipitates and matrix gives rise to strain
ﬁelds which interact with dislocations and can also impede the
movement of dislocations. Chemical decomposition under irradia-
tion mitigates the hardening effects of misﬁt strain and leads to a
decrease in hardness. In our study, the order–disorder transforma-
tion occurs at low-dose region and signiﬁcant chemical intermix-
ing arises at high-dose region, the change of the total strength of

tion at elevated temperatures in this alloy.
5. Conclusions
We performed in situ and ex situ heavy ion irradiations at
room temperature on Ni-based superalloy Rene N4 with
submicron-sized c0 precipitates. Irradiation-induced order-
disorder transition, dissolution and the corresponding mechanical
property evolution were studied. The c0 precipitates become fully
disordered at a dose of 0.3 dpa, and after irradiation up to
75 dpa, the c0 precipitates are only partially dissolved mainly due
to the thermal spike effects. The combined effects of defect
clusters, order-disordering transition and dissolution determine
the evolution of indentation hardness under irradiation.
Irradiation-induced defect clusters slightly harden the alloy, while
disordering of
c0 precipitates results in a dramatic drop in the total
hardness due to the loss of ordering strengthening. Chemical
intermixing between c0 precipitates and matrix softens the alloy
only in the early stages of dissolution (<18 dpa). This study sheds
light on the degradation mechanisms of Ni-based superalloys
under extreme irradiation environments.
