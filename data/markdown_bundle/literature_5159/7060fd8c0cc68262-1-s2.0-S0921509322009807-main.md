ABSTRACT 
The influence of refractory additions on tensile deformation behavior has been studied at an intermediate 
temperature (760
◦
C) using scanning and transmission electron microscopy. Tensile experiments revealed that 
elemental additions of tantalum (Ta), tungsten (W), and rhenium (Re) could improve the yield strength (YS) of 
the model alloys with an order of YS(Ni – Al – W) > YS(Ni – Al – Ta) > YS(Ni – Al-Re) > YS(Ni – Al). During the plastic 
deformation of model alloys at the intermediate temperature, the deformation via Orowan bypassing and shear 
could be activated synergistically. The changes of anti-phase boundary (APB) energies on {001} and {111} 
planes are related to the cross-slip driving force, critical resolved shear stress and stage II work hardening rate in 
γ’ precipitates. The effects of γ’ size and refractory additions (Ta, W, and Re) on the yield strength of model alloys 
at intermediate temperature are also discussed, which can provide qualitative reference for alloy design. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys have been used extensively to 
manufacture turbine blades in high-temperature jet engines and land- 
based gas turbines. The mechanical behavior of superalloys at high 
temperatures is determined by γ’ -Ni
3
Al precipitate strengthening [ 1 ]. 
Because of an anomalous yield effect, the yield stress increases with an 
increasing temperature within a certain range, and the peak stress is 
reached at ~760
◦
C [ 2 – 4 ]. The temperature resistance of Ni-based sin -
gle-crystal superalloys, particularly the yield stress, is controlled by the 
chemical compositions, volume fraction of γ’ phase, morphology, and γ’ 
particles distribution [ 5 – 7 ]. To effectively enhance the creep resistance 
at high temperatures, refractory elements, such as tantalum (Ta), 
tungsten (W), and rhenium (Re), are often added [ 8 ]. Tensile tests at an 
intermediate temperature can be used as a reference for the primary 
creep regime. This is of practical significance because the temperature 
range of turbine blading during operation is ~750 to 1100
◦
C in the 
Ref. [ 9 ], and the yield strength of the single-crystal superalloys is quite 
critical to the successful service of the turbine engine. The addition of 
alloying elements can improve the tensile properties of Ni-based sin -
gle-crystal superalloys, but what is the difference between the elements? 
Therefore, it is crucial to know the quantitative effect of additional solid 
solution strengthening elements (such as Ta, W, and Re) in the alloy 
design to replace. 
The primary approach to figure out above problems is to study the 
effect of individual alloying elements on the hardening mechanism of 
ternary model alloys due to complexity of elemental interactions in 
superalloys that contain more than ten elements. Traditional dislocation 
theory combined with density-functional theory (DFT) indicated that 
alloying elements can decrease cross-slip activation enthalpy in γ’ -Ni
3
Al 
[ 10 ] and increase the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) in γ -Ni [ 11 ], 
respectively. Although extensive work has done focusing on the tensile 
deformation behavior of multicomponent and polycrystalline alloys, a 
few studies have continuously delivered on the topics of strengthening 
effects of refractory additions in ternary model single-crystal alloys. To 
exclude the interference of other alloying elements and grain bound -
aries, and to clearly understand the effects of individual alloying ele -
ments on the physical phenomenon of hardening at operating 
temperature, the influence of critical elements Ta, W, and Re on the 
tensile behavior of a ternary model single-crystal alloy was studied. 
Tensile tests were performed at 760
◦
C on four model superalloys: M0 
(Ni – Al), M2(Ni – Al – Re), M7(Ni – Al – W), and M8(Ni – Al – Ta). These

M8 alloys.

the competition between different planar defects in energy level, such as 
complex stacking faults (CSF), SISF, APB, etc. Defects with a low energy

can be easily introduced from the {111} plane towards the cross-slip 
plane {001} by thermal activation to form a Kear-Wilsdorf (KW) lock, 
thus the superdislocations are immobile to resist deformation [ 25 ].

which is associated with so-called Re effect.
