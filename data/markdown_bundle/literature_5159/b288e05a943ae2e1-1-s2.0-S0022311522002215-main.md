Two CrFeMnNi face-centered cubic complex concentrated solid-solution alloys (CSA) have been evaluated 
for phase stability, mechanical properties, and radiation damage effects from heavy ions. Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 
and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 were predicted by thermodynamic calculations to phase separate and maintain a 
single phase at 700 °C, respectively. Aging experiments at this temperature conﬁrmed varying degrees of 
precipitation of a body-centered cubic phase in both Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . The alloys 
showed promising strength in tensile deformation at room temperature, with yield strengths of 155 MPa 
and 151 MPa for Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 , respectively. At 500 °C, the yield strength 
of Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 fell to 93 MPa, and to 100 MPa in Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . Unlike Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 gained some ductility at 500 °C compared to room temperature. The two CSAs were 
irradiated to 75 dpa at 500 °C in the plateau region of the displacement curve using 3.7 MeV Ni 2 + ions, 
alongside model alloy 709 as a reference. Irradiation results produced similar densities and sizes of dislo- 
cations loops in the two CSAs compared to the reference. However, while large voids form in the plateau 
region of Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , small voids form just beyond the displacement peak of Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . 
Atom probe tomography and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy-equipped scanning transmission elec- 
tron microscopes were used to characterize the alloys for changes in chemical distribution.

1. Introduction 
The advent of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) presents new possi- 
bilities for alloy design, particularly for highly specialized applica- 
tions such as nuclear fuel claddings and ducts. HEAs represent a 
radical departure from traditional alloy design, which has histor- 
ically involved a single-principal alloying element with small ad- 
ditions (usually < 20 at% of alloying elements) to mitigate corro- 
sion, resist radiation damage and/or improve mechanical proper- 
ties [1] . HEAs typically consist of four or more elements in dis-

[2] . Due to their attractive properties such as potential high duc- 
tility, strength, wear resistance, and creep resistance, single-phase 
HEAs, called compositionally complex solid solution alloys (CSAs), 
represent a potential opportunity for further improvement of opti- 
mized austenitic steels such as D9, ferritic-martensitic steels such 
as HT9 or G92, and oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) ferritic- 
martensitic steels, currently being considered for advanced sodium 
fast reactors [3–10] . CSAs present a new alloy design parameter 
in their compositional complexity, which could be used as a ma- 
trix for high-temperature alloys. CSAs based on 3d transition met- 
als exhibit comparable strength to SS316 and acceptable ductility 
under tension, as do those based on light refractory metals when 
compared to Inconel 718 under compression [ 2 , 11 ].

Atomistic modeling and experiments suggest that certain CSA 
compositions possess inherent resistance to radiation damage, such 
that CSAs could show potential for replacement of conventional 
alloys, or at least their base matrices, in radiation environments 
[12–17] . It is well understood that microstructural changes un- 
der irradiation are driven by point defect accumulation and in- 
ﬂuenced by the distribution of individual point defects and de- 
fect clusters after the end of the damage cascade created by 
energetic particles. CSAs were ﬁrst believed to possess intrinsic 
resistance to radiation damage because a highly disordered solid 
solution would experience sluggish mass diffusion due to an in- 
herently strained lattice, which would prevent signiﬁcant growth 
of induced extended defects [ 2 , 18 ]. While some CSAs have shown 
high resistance to irradiation-induced void swelling and hardening, 
these properties are not simply dependent on the number of alloy- 
ing elements and the resulting disorder, but rather on the interac- 
tions which take place between cascades, defect clusters, isolated 
defects, and sinks [ 1 , 19-22 ]. A clear outcome of recent research on 
CSA design for irradiation damage resistance is the new design pa- 
rameter that is offered by compositional complexity beyond the 
conventional design that relies on microstructure and sink densi- 
ties. Kumar et al. were able to show that the face-centered cu- 
bic (FCC) CSA Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 experienced no swelling or radi- 
ation induced segregation after 10 displacements per atom (dpa) 
Ni 2+ ion irradiation at 700 °C [11] . Chen et al. showed that harden- 
ing effects and microstructural evolution as a function of dose up 
to 1 dpa at 300 °C were similar for CoCrFeMnNi and Al 0.3 CrFeNi 
compared to SS316H [23] . Li et al. also showed similar changes in 
Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 compared to conventional austenitic FeNiCr al- 
loys [24] . This would suggest that compositional complexity inﬂu- 
ences radiation damage tolerance to a similar degree as alloying 
SS316 with Ti, Si, P, and native precipitates [25] . Several model- 
ing studies aim at understanding the underlying physical princi- 
ples and have proposed mechanisms for increased point defect re- 
combination within the displacement cascade or by reduced de- 
fect or defect cluster mobility in a compositionally complex base 
matrix [12–17] . We recently demonstrated, using in situ irradi- 
ation under TEM, that cascade-surviving point defect production 
was reduced in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 compared 
to less compositionally complex materials and that the two com- 
positions experienced signiﬁcantly different kinetics for nucleation 
of interstitial loops at 500 °C [26] . Experimental studies are crit- 
ically needed to better understand the irradiation damage evolu- 
tion mechanism inherent to CSAs. It is worth noting that CSAs 
are unlikely to outperform radiation resistant austenitic and fer- 
ritic/martensitic steels, but a complex base matrix may be another 
design path for slowing the irradiation induced degradation of 
components. Before compositional complexity can be included in 
alloy design considerations, CSAs must be compared against sim- 
pler materials using bulk heavy ion irradiations to high ﬂuences 
and temperatures to simulate what beneﬁts CSAs could exhibit in 
extreme environments 
This study focuses on the characterization of selected CSA 
compositions in terms of properties relevant to nuclear reactor 
environments. High-temperature phase stability, tensile properties, 
and irradiation effects are all evaluated. Candidate CSA composi- 
tions from the CrFeMnNi system were irradiated at the Wisconsin 
Ion Beam Laboratory with Ni 2 + ions, up to a damage level of 75 
dpa at 500 °C. Irradiation results are compared to structurally sim- 
ilar but less compositionally complex model alloy 709. Transmis- 
sion electron microscopy (TEM) and atom-probe tomography (APT) 
characterizations were performed for irradiation-induced voids, 
dislocation loops, and chemical segregation/precipitation. 
2. Experimental procedures 
2.1. Alloy fabrication and mechanical properties 
Two CSA compositions were tested and given compositions are 
in atomic% (at%) unless otherwise speciﬁed. Fe bal Cr 20 Ni 25 (wt%) 
model alloy 709 was used for reference against two face-centered 
cubic (FCC) CSA: Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . The cri- 
teria for alloy selection are detailed in [26] but are based upon Ku- 
mar et al. and further exploration of CrFeMnNi CSAs predicted by 
CALPHAD to maintain a single phase at reactor-relevant tempera- 
tures.

material used in these experiments. The chemistry of the ﬁnal 
ingots was characterized using inert gas fusion (IGF), combus-

plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The results are

1.5 at% for alloying elements. Samples were fabricated by both vac-

unit. The material was sectioned into 1–2 mm thick circular discs,

samples, vacuum induction melting was used to produce sheets of

2.3. Irradiation experiment 
Arc-melted (Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 ) and vacuum-induction melted 
(Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 ) samples were used for the irradiation portion 
of this study. Model alloy 709 (M709) was also fabricated by vac- 
uum arc melting before hot rolling and annealing [27] . M709, used 
as a comparison to the CSAs, is an austenitic FeNiCr alloy with 
nominal composition Fe bal Cr 21 Ni 24 at% and a recrystallized mi- 
crostructure. For the irradiation experiments, samples of each alloy 
were polished to a mirror ﬁnish with a surface roughness of less 
than 20 nanometers as veriﬁed using a ZYGO NewView 60 0 0 op- 
tical proﬁlometer and in accordance with ASTM E521–16 [28] . The 
ﬁnishing polish was a 0.05 μm diamond paste. 
Samples were irradiated at 500 °C at the Wisconsin Ion Beam 
Laboratory (WIBL) using a defocused beam of 3.7 MeV Ni 2 + ions 
up to 75 dpa in the midrange of the plateau region of the damage 
proﬁle ( ∼130 dpa at damage peak) as computed by SRIM and Irad- 
ina [ 29 , 30 ]. Threshold displacement energy was set at 40 eV for all 
elements in FCC materials [28] . A density of 7.2 g/cm 3 was used. 
The full cascade calculation in SRIM was used and the number of 
displacements was calculated from the damage energy dissipated 
by recoil atoms as phonons, as outlined in [29] . The binding ener- 
gies were set to the sum of the formation energies of one inter- 
stitial and one vacancy, as advised by Agarwal et al. [31] . Values 
of 7.8 eV, 5.8 eV, 5.3 eV, and 5.9 eV were used for Cr, Fe, Mn, 
and Ni, respectively while surface energies were set to 0 eV for 
all elements [32] . An example damage and implanted ion proﬁle 
for Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 is shown in Fig. 2 . All materials were irra- 
diated to a ﬂuence of 1.25 × 10 17 ions/cm 2 . For austenitic mate- 
rials with such similar composition, these proﬁles are also sim- 
ilar, mainly because the input displacement threshold energy for 
all four constituents is the same. High vacuum was maintained be- 
tween 1.3 × 10 –5 and 1.3 × 10 –6 Pa. 
Lamellae for TEM were produced from the irradiated region of 
each alloy using a FEI Quanta 3D focused-ion beam (FIB) at the 
Microscopy and Characterization Suite (MaCS) facility at the Cen- 
ter for Advanced Energy Studies (CAES) and a Zeiss Auriga FIB at 
the University of Wisconsin-Madison Nanoscale Imaging and Anal- 
ysis Center (NIAC). TEM lamellae were examined using a FEI Tecnai 
TF-30 STwin scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM)

3. Results 
3.1. Aging experiment 
The microstructure of the arc-melted Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 sam- 
ple after homogenization is displayed in Fig. 1 . The grain size for 
this material is on the order of hundreds of micrometers and EDS 
reveals that the material is fully chemically homogeneous. Simi- 
lar observations were made for the Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 (not shown 
here). The laboratory X-ray diffraction conﬁrmed both alloys to be 
single-phase with polycrystalline FCC crystal structures, as shown 
in Fig. 1 for Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 . The long-term thermal stability of 
each alloy was tested at 700 ºC for 40 days. Experimental results 
are compared to CALPHAD predictions, and phase evolutions for 
Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 are given in Fig. 3a and 
d alongside the lattice parameters calculated by predicted molar 
volume. The elemental mapping for Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 shows that 
new phases precipitate out after the annealing process. A newly 
formed phase is distributed evenly throughout the sample and is 
Cr-rich, Mn-containing, and Fe/Ni-deﬁcient as shown in Fig. 3b . 
XRD data given in Fig. 3c conﬁrmed the formation of this new BCC 
phase, reinforcing CALPHAD predictions for this composition. In- 
deed, CALPHAD predicts that the Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 starts to phase 
separate below 780 °C. At 700 ºC, the alloy is expected to have 
both FCC and Cr 72 Fe 10 Mn 18 BCC ( ∼4% volume fraction) phases. Al- 
though the Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 composition was predicted to main- 
tain a single-phase structure in the temperature range down to 
600 ºC, post-aging EDS shows a relatively low volume fraction of 
Cr-rich clusters, precipitating most favorably at grain boundaries, 
as shown in Fig. 3e . XRD in Fig. 3f data indicates a small vol- 
ume fraction of a secondary phase. Both sets of XRD include shifts 
in peak intensity which indicate a change of texture as the BCC 
phase forms and annealing time increases. The lattice parameters 
determined by Nelson-Riley method are included in the plots [33] . 
The lattice parameter predictions for both FCC and BCC phases 
are quite close to those calculated from experimental data, over- 
predicting by a maximum of 0.12 ˚A in the FCC phase found in 
Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 . Low intensity peaks from oxides phases induced 
by the aging are present below 2 θ= 5 º and between 2 θ= 6 and 11 º, 
respectively. In summary, a BCC precipitating phase, rich in Cr, is 
conﬁrmed in both CSAs after aging. EDS shows this phase is dis- 
tributed throughout the bulk of the aged Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , but 
primarily only near the grain boundaries in aged Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . 
These ﬁndings highlight the importance of CALPHAD calculations, 
and validated databases, in predicting the long-term stability of 
CSAs.

3.3. Irradiation experiment 
3.3.1. TEM / stem / eds characterization 
Pre-irradiated Microstructures 
Dark and bright-ﬁeld micrographs showing the pre-irradiation 
microstructure for Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 are 
shown in Fig. 1d . The microstructure with large grains, no pre- 
cipitates, and few dislocations is observed. M709 has been stud- 
ied elsewhere and features a microstructure with large grains and 
no precipitates [34] . Although Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 experienced phase 
separation after aging, as detailed above, the duration of the irra- 
diation experiment (a few hours) did not result in the formation of 
new phases, such that the results can be considered to represent 
the irradiation of a single phase. A large volume fraction of pre- 
cipitation would hinder the applicability of the results to the base 
matrix compositions of interest, while a high density of nanoscale 
precipitates may act as a sink for point defects, hindering the for- 
mation of extended defects. This could be a design feature similar 
to ODS steels and nanostructured ferritic alloys, but is undesirable 
in experiments seeking to investigate the isolated effects of a com- 
positionally complex base matrix [3] . 
Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 irradiated at 500 ºC 
Fig. 5b shows an EDS line scan of the depth proﬁle of the sam- 
ple. The high Pt content at the sample surface is due to the pro- 
tective layer of Pt deposited during the FIB process. Mn is enriched 
just before the displacement peak and slightly depleted on either 
side, with Fe and Ni showing the opposite behavior and Cr left rel- 
atively unaffected. This is likely due to vacancies migrating down 
the vacancy concentration gradient by exchanging primarily with 
Mn atoms. A population of voids with average size of ∼100 ±32 nm 
and a number density of 1.2 × 10 20 m –3 has formed in the plateau 
region, corresponding to a swelling level of 8.6%. The microstruc-

alloy and size distribution is also given as a box-and-whisker plot

Fig. 7 . This contrast condition resolved ¼ of the faulted loops pop- 
ulation, so the densities have been multiplied by 4. The loop den-

The oxygen level in Fig. 5c is overpredicted by EDS and was found

materials.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Thermal stability and mechanical properties 
Nuclear materials must maintain acceptable mechanical prop- 
erties despite an extreme set of environmental conditions includ- 
ing high operating temperature, corrosive coolant, and radiation ef- 
fects. Evaluation of new alloy candidates or design techniques must 
then include experimental quantiﬁcation of mechanical properties, 
thermal stability, radiation response, and the relationship between 
the three. The Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 alloys were 
found to be single-phase FCC after the casting and homogeniza- 
tion processes. The alloys show higher ductility and lower strength 
compared to the values reported by Kumar et al. [11] . Preparation 
of samples in [11] included a cold rolling step to a total thickness 
reduction of 86% in the last step. The cold work likely increased 
the alloy strength and reduced ductility dramatically compared to 
this study, as shown in Fig. 4 . A more in-depth study on process- 
ing parameters, such as grain size and cold work, will be necessary 
to optimize strength and ductility for given operational conditions. 
It is worth noting that Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 also gained some ductil- 
ity at the higher temperature of 500 ºC, unlike Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 . 
This is supported by the aging experiment, because although pre- 
cipitation occurred in both materials at 700 ºC, the driving force 
for precipitation is stronger in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , which may have 
slightly hardened the material compared to Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 .

may play an important role in various engineering applications, 
but it remains crucial to investigate their long-term stability to 
ensure materials maintain their structure and properties. Mean- 
while, CALPHAD calculations can be employed to predict their 
stability, but comparison to experimental aging studies is vital 
to perfecting techniques on both fronts and designing alloys sys-

stability predictions in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 at 700 ºC and highlight 
an opportunity for improvement of the database’s predictions for 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . Development of high-throughput experimen- 
tal tools is needed to generate large amounts of microstructure 
and microchemistry data, which can be used to rapidly improve 
CALPHAD models and optimize composition, fabrication, and 
processing [41–44] . 
4.2. Irradiation experiment 
The dominant radiation effect which degrades structural mate- 
rials such as fast reactor fuel cladding is void swelling [37] . In the 
context of fundamental research which informs alloy design, tun- 
ing the compositional complexity parameter may reduce the on- 
set of void swelling to enable the development of fast reactors 
which can achieve high burnups. Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 CSA was al- 
ready shown to resist void swelling and evolve similarly to conven- 
tional austenitic Fe-Ni-Cr alloys under neutron and heavy ion irra- 
diation but, according to the present study, swells by 8.6% after 75 
average plateau dpa irradiation by heavy ions at 500 °C [ 11 , 23 , 24 ]. 
The formation of voids in this work compared to [11] is likely due 
to the increased dpa. It can be concluded that the nucleation of 
voids in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 occurs between 10 and 75 dpa. The ob- 
served swelling is higher than A709 and 316 stainless steel irradi- 
ated with 3.5 MeV Fe ++ heavy ions at 575 ºC to a peak dose of 100 
dpa, but is in reasonable agreement with swelling experiments in 
which 316 stainless steel was irradiated at 500 and 700 ºC using 
5.0 MeV Ni 2 + ions [45–47] . The lack of voids in the plateau re- 
gion of Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 is also a promising result. Several stud- 
ies have shown that although austenitic alloys swell at a rate of 
1%/dpa at steady state, void nucleation and overall swelling is fur- 
ther promoted at 575 ºC compared to 500 ºC [ 45 , 47 , 48 ]. The ion 
species differ in our study but are similar sizes and energies. The 
peak dose in Fig. 2 is approximately 130 dpa, meaning the plateau 
doses between this study and the reported irradiation experiments 
may be reasonably compared. The presence of a dense disloca- 
tion network and faulted loops population in the damage plateau 
region and a population of small voids in Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 just 
beyond the damage peak supports the notion that these plateau 
dislocation loops are less mobile, and so remain at a shallower 
depth than in materials with a less complex base matrix. Such 
sessile dislocation loops would then act as a sink for vacancies 
generated at this depth, and limit void swelling due to the lack 
of vacancy supersaturation. Compared to A709 irradiated with 3.5 
MeV Fe ++ heavy ions at 500 ºC to a peak dose of 150 dpa, the

at low dpa become unstable as dpa and vacancy population in- 
crease. Denser populations of faulted loop populations are present 
in M709 and Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 , and a few loops have managed to

ation of faulted interstitial loops in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 would allow 
longer dislocation lines to form a network alongside larger, more 
disparate faulted interstitial loops, which are then a less effective 
sink for vacancies as voids nucleate. 
The Mn content may be an important factor to explain such 
differences in microstructural evolution between CSAs of differ- 
ent compositions [26] . Mn increases the vacancy mobility and the 
stacking-fault energy (SFE) for Mn composition beyond ∼15 wt%, 
and may increase the local SFE variation [50–53] . Vacancies born 
near the displacement damage peak may begin to migrate deeper 
into the material down the steep vacancy concentration gradi- 
ent, where the sink strength drops off quickly with depth. This 
can explain the nucleation of the deeper population of voids in 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 . Since vacancies preferentially diffuse via Mn, 
as shown in [54] , there will be less diffusion down the vacancy 
concentration gradient for the lower Mn-content Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 
relative to the Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 alloy. In both cases, Mn is some- 
what depleted beyond the displacement peak, indicating vacan- 
cies have diffused deeper into the material from the peak. Nei- 
ther composition experienced signiﬁcant swelling near the dis- 
placement damage peak, likely because of the implanted ions [55] . 
In Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 , with a lower Mn-content, these vacancies will 
ultimately remain more localized and experience less annihilation 
with interstitials, implanted ions and at sinks which form at low- 
dpa, and may form an early supersaturation and nucleate voids, 
especially at the back end of the displacement curve where the 
gradient is sharp. These voids experience suppressed growth as im- 
planted ion population rises, and doesn’t contribute signiﬁcantly to 
void swelling compared to voids which nucleate in the plateau re- 
gion. In conventional alloys, the implanted ion species may have a 
stronger effect on void nucleation suppression if it does not match 
the base matrix, but in a multicomponent base matrix the effect 
should be comparable between ion species already abundant in the 
solid solution. In the plateau region, with negligible ion implanta- 
tion and a smoother gradient in point defects, the void nucleation 
is primarily inﬂuenced by the sink strength, with the high den- 
sity of small, sessile, faulted loops in Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 working to 
shift void nucleation to higher dpa compared to Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 . 
These loops remain small for the most part, but because they re- 
duce the overall ﬂux of vacancies per interstitial loops, some of 
them may grow to the large sizes seen in Fig. 7 . Further investiga- 
tions of the Cr-Fe-Mn-Ni composition space under more favorable 
void swelling conditions (higher temperature, higher energy heavy 
ions, and higher dpa) are needed to explore the effect of compo- 
sition on void swelling behavior, and further characterization and 
comparison of the proportion of dislocations in loops and in net- 
works may support the theory developed above. 
It is also vital to ensure such a design of alloy base matrix does 
not exacerbate other forms of radiation degradation, namely RIS 
precipitation, or embrittlement. APT results and EDS performed on 
dislocation lines indicate no signiﬁcant elemental ordering or seg- 
regation under these irradiation conditions, except for the case of 
Ni enrichment near voids in both CSAs, which has been observed 
previously in more conventional austenitic FeNiCr alloys [56] . A 
network of dislocations and irregularly shaped perfect loops have 
begun to interact with each other, which nearly overpowers other 
populations of dislocation loops in all alloys. The distributions of 
loops sizes were similar in the three FCC alloys. Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 
and Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 had similar loop densities, with 2.43 × 10 21 
m –3 and 1.32 × 10 21 m –3 , respectively while the less complex 
M709 had a slightly higher density with 6.49 × 10 21 m –3 . These 
densities are much lower than those of faulted dislocation loops 
which were found to grow with increasing dpa in the same CSAs

5. Conclusions 
Two high entropy alloys, Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 , 
were investigated for applications in the nuclear ﬁeld. CAL- 
PHAD calculations were performed to select and predict CSA 
compositions that are stable in SFR operational conditions. 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 was predicted to maintain a single-phase struc- 
ture at 700 ºC, while a BCC phase was expected to precipitate 
in Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 . The aging experiment showed that both al- 
loys experience phase separation at 700 ºC, which limits their 
applications in advanced reactor concepts pending further opti- 
mization. The alloys exhibited good mechanical properties, with 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 slightly less ductile than Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 with 
similar strength. Interestingly, Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 gained ductility at 
lower temperature. Along with model alloy 709 as a reference, 
these CSAs were irradiated at 500 °C with 3.7 MeV Ni 2 + ions to 
75 dpa at the WIBL. The irradiations of FCC materials resulted 
in the formation of perfect dislocation loops against a dislocation 
network. The average sizes of all loops are quite similar, ranging 
from approx. 13 to 20 nm in the longest dimension. The CSAs 
demonstrate less void swelling resistance than M709, which did 
not experience swelling by 75 dpa. Voids corresponding to 8.6% 
swelling were observed in the plateau region of Cr 18 Fe 27 Mn 27 Ni 28 , 
while voids corresponding to 0.40% swelling were also found in 
Cr 15 Fe 35 Mn 15 Ni 35 near a depth of ∼1.5 μm, which is beyond the 
damage peak predicted by SRIM, but not at more shallow depths. 
This supports the notion that the matrix itself can affect the void 
nucleation via the mobility of point defects and extended de- 
fects. Interstitial loops are slowed by inherent lattice strain and 
begin to unfault and interact with the dislocation network. In an 
isotropic, neutron-irradiation environment, the formation of dislo- 
cation loops with limited mobility may increase the sink strength 
and increase the time needed to form a supersaturation of vacan- 
cies and for voids to grow to large sizes. APT data does not indi- 
cate that dislocation segregation or precipitation has occurred or 
that the bulk composition of the alloys change signiﬁcantly during 
irradiation, while EDS characterization of the voids showed some 
enrichment of Ni by about 10 at% near voids and some enrichment 
of Mn just before the displacement peak. Irradiation experiments 
at higher temperature and ﬂuences beyond the nucleation dose, as 
well as a deeper understanding of the stacking fault energy of CSAs 
will help to further explain the void evolution in compositionally 
complex base matrices.
