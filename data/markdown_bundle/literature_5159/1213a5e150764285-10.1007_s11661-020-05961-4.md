Inhomogeneous Strains and Dislocation Evolution
in Surface-Treated Ni-Base Superalloys
SHASHWAT SHUKLA, SUO HON LIM, and DONG ZHILI
Surface treatment processes such as shot-peening create near-surface residual stresses in the
component, which retard the initiation and growth of fatigue cracks. These processes, however,
also induce considerable amount of strain hardening and alter the sub-surface dislocation
structure of the material, which can impact its mechanical and electrochemical properties.
Although these eﬀects are critical for part performance, they have not so far been studied for
surface-treated Ni-base superalloys, which are widely used for load-bearing applications. To
address this, we have developed an X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) framework based on Convolutional
Multiple Whole Proﬁle (CMWP) ﬁtting approach that can be used to quantify dislocation
densities in shot-peened Inconel 718 components. The method exploits physics-based mecha-
nisms for size and strain broadening to model these eﬀects and uses dislocation contrast factors
to account for deformation-induced crystallographic anisotropy in the diﬀraction proﬁle. Our
results suggest that shot-peening leads to one order of magnitude increase in the dislocation
density of Inconel 718 alloys, which is supported by direct evidence from transmission electron
micrographs. The approach developed in this work can be extended to other types of Ni-base
superalloys, enabling a simple non-destructive method to calculate dislocation densities in this
commercially important class of materials.

I. INTRODUCTION
NI-BASE superalloys show exceptional strength at
high temperatures and are often the preferred material
when resistance to creep, fatigue, and oxidation is
required. Most of the rotating turbine parts in aircraft
industry and turbocharger discs for large diesel or racing
car engines are made using high-performance Ni-base
superalloys. These alloys also ﬁnd applications in
chemical industry where they withstand both elevated
temperatures and strong corrosives, such as brines,
carbonates, phosphates, chlorides, nitrates, as well as
seawater.[1–4]
The essential solute elements in Ni-base superalloys
are Al and/or Ti and their total concentration is
generally less than 10 at. pct. This gives rise to a
two-phase equilibrium microstructure, consisting of a
matrix of gamma ( c) phase in which the minor
gamma-prime ( c¢) phase is homogeneously precipitated.
The c-phase is a solid solution with a face-centered cubic
(FCC) structure and a random distribution of Al/Ti/Ni
atoms in the lattice. In contrast, c¢-phase has a primitive
cubic lattice with a deﬁnite chemical ordering, which is
characterized by the presence of Ni atoms at face-centers
and Al/Ti atoms at the corners of the cube. [5,6] The
exceptional creep resistance and high-temperature
strength of Ni-base superalloys arise from precipitation
hardening imparted by this minor c¢ phase.[7]
There are several applications, e.g., in aircraft turbine
discs, where the strength required is higher than that of
c¢-alloys, but the operating temperatures are relatively
lower. In such cases, Ni-base superalloys are strength-
ened using another ordered phase known as
gamma-double prime ( c¢¢). Signiﬁcant amount of Nb
or V needs to be added to achieve the chemical ordering
required to form the c¢¢-phase. The crystal structure of
this phase is body-centered tetragonal (BCT) with an
ordered arrangement of Ni and Nb/V atoms and it
strengthens the alloy primarily by coherency harden-
ing.[6] One of the best known examples of this type of
superalloy is Inconel 718 which is widely used in jet
engine turbine discs in aircraft industry. [8]
It is a common practice to apply surface treatment
processes, such as shot-peening [9,10] and laser shock
peening,[11,12] to enhance the fatigue life of Inconel 718
components. As fatigue cracks initiate from surface,

Although strain hardening and residual stresses are

most of the research in previous literature has been
focused solely on residual stress. There are several
studies on how stress distribution in Ni-base superalloys

current literature and dislocation evolution during

Strain hardening is generally measured by change in
cross-sectional area of the specimen that undergoes
plastic deformation. This method is widely used for
processes such as rolling, but its application is not
feasible in shot-peening or laser shock peening, where
the bulk of the work-piece remains undeformed and just
the surface layer that is only a few hundred lm thick
experiences strain hardening. Due to this practical
limitation, the alternative approach in literature has
been to calculate the Full Width Half Maximum
(FWHM) of the X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) peaks of
Inconel 718 alloys and assume it to be a measure of
plastic deformation. [27]
This approach, however, is not fully consistent with
the theory of XRD peak broadening. It is known that
increase in dislocation density can increase the XRD
peak width, so FWHM does depend on plastic defor-
mation, but it is inﬂuenced by other factors as well, such
as beam optics and crystallite size of the material. [28] As
a result, ascribing the measured increase in FWHM
solely to plastic deformation and ignoring the size eﬀects
seems questionable and may lead to erroneous
conclusions.
Several methods of XRD analysis have been devel-
oped to estimate the extent of plastic deformation in
terms of dislocation density, which can be broadly
classiﬁed into two categories: (i) breadth methods that
consider only a single reﬂection and (ii) full proﬁle/pat-
tern ﬁtting methods that make use of the whole
diﬀraction data to extract microstructural information.
A brief review of these methods is presented below.

In this work, we report detailed XRD analysis based
on Williamson–Hall (WH) as well as Convolutional
Multiple Whole Proﬁle (CMWP) ﬁtting methods that
enable the quantiﬁcation of strain hardening eﬀects in
Inconel 718 alloys in terms of their dislocation densities.
Our results demonstrate that shot-peening does not just
induce residual stresses, but it also leads to one order of
magnitude increase in the dislocation density of Inconel
718 alloys, which is strongly supported by direct
experimental evidence from transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) data. The theoretical framework
developed in this work can be readily extended to other
Ni-base superalloys, enabling a simple non-destructive
method for dislocation density analysis in this important
class of materials.

with the microstructure of Inconel 718 system, one peak

not use two individual peak phases to account for two
diﬀerent precipitates in the matrix.
While doing so can improve the quality of ﬁts, this
approach would not be physically meaningful, as the
two precipitate phases in Inconel 718 system are nearly
isostructural. On the other hand, using just one peak
phase function for both matrix and the two precipitate
phases does not yield acceptable ﬁts (not shown here)
either, as it does not realistically reﬂect the heteroge-
neous constitution of this multi-phase alloy.

Figure 7 also suggests signiﬁcant changes in the
density and structure of dislocations in Inconel 718
alloy, induced by shot-peening. Peening intensity of 0.12
mmA led to one order of magnitude increase in
dislocation density, which reached a value of 54(3) 9
1014 m/C0 2 due to severe plastic deformation caused by
strain hardening of the surface and sub-surface regions
of the alloy coupon. However, interestingly, when the
peening intensity was further increased and almost
doubled to 0.25 mmA, no corresponding increase in
dislocation density was observed, instead, its value
slightly decreased to 38(4) 9 1014 m/C0 2.
These results suggest that with increasing intensity of
shot-peening, dislocation density also increases as new
dislocations generate and arrange themselves in diﬀerent
conﬁgurations. This includes creation of dislocation
tangles within the grains as well as formation of dense
dislocation walls along the slip planes. [46] However,
further increase in plastic deformation leads to some
degree of atomic rearrangement and annihilation of
dislocations as dislocation walls transform into low-an-
gle sub-boundaries, in order to minimize the total energy
of the system. [47] With even further increase in peening
intensity, sub-grain boundaries evolve into high-angle
grain boundaries, which results in the partitioning of the
original grains into smaller crystallites and this phe-
nomenon continues until dislocation density reaches a
steady-state value. [48]
Thus, the dislocation evolution during shot-peening
of Inconel 718 alloy can be explained to occur in two
stages: the ﬁrst stage involves the generation of new
dislocations, driven primarily by peening-induced defor-
mation. This, then is followed by dislocation recombi-
nation/annihilation and atomic rearrangements that
lead to signiﬁcant grain reﬁnement in the second stage.
The above mechanism is in good agreement with the
experimental data on crystallite size obtained from
CMWP ﬁtting. While the crystallite size corresponding
to lower peening intensity (0.12 mmA) was 34 nm, its
value decreased noticeably to just about 12 nm when the
shot-peening intensity was performed at 0.25 mmA. This
clearly indicates the formation of smaller crystallites
resulting from the division of initially larger grains
induced by severe plastic deformation.
Apart from dislocation density, the value of the
parameter q was also aﬀected by shot-peening—it
increased from 1.5 for the un-peened coupon to 2.1 for
the shot-peened coupons. This data provides strong
evidence to suggest that the surface and sub-surface
strain hardening induced by shot-peening changes the
character of dislocations from a mixed (screw + edge)
type to pure screw type.
Comparing the two methods of XRD analysis
employed in this work, we observe that the results of
the breadth method, i.e., mWH do not fully agree with
the results of the full pattern ﬁtting method, i.e.,
CMWP. More speciﬁcally, for the sample shot-peened
at the intensity of 0.12 mmA, CMWP ﬁtting procedure
predicted a dislocation density value of 54(3) 9 1014
m/C0 2, which is one order of magnitude higher than 4.4(7)
9 1014 m/C0 2, the value calculated from the mWH
method.
Scardi et al. [49] have examined the limitations of the
breadth methods and determined few key reasons why
the quantitative data from such methods may not
always be reliable: (i) typical linear combination
approaches, as given in Eqs. [ 9] and [ 10], that are used
to integrate the diﬀerent peak broadening contributions
are not based on any sound physical foundation; as a
result, their description of XRD peak widths is not quite
accurate; (ii) apparent size/strain parameters that model
the size/strain components in breadth methods do not
fully account for all relevant microstructural eﬀects that
lead to anisotropic peak broadening. As a result, the
dislocation arrangement parameter M has either to be
assumed or estimated, which may not always be
accurate and lead to uncertainty in dislocation density
data. In contrast, in CMWP procedure, determination
of M is more accurate as it results directly from the
ﬁtting of full XRD proﬁle on the basis of physics-based
models and there is no requirement to make any
arbitrary assumptions or ad hoc estimates in this regard.
These points explain why the dislocation density data
obtained from the mWH methods above is not consis-
tent with the more accurate CMWP method. Thus, in
agreement with previous literature, [49] our results sug-
gest that while the breadth methods provide a useful
means for qualitative comparison of plastically
deformed alloys, care should be taken in extending this
kind of analysis for quantitative evaluations as it may
lead to incorrect conclusions.

IV. CONCLUSIONS
Inconel 718 alloys show excellent mechanical proper-
ties and corrosion resistance at high temperatures.
However, Inconel 718 components need to be surface
treated to enhance their fatigue life. Two most signif-
icant eﬀects of surface treatment are residual stress and
strain hardening which alter the arrangement and
structure of dislocations in the sub-surface region of
the part. While residual stresses in surface-treated
Inconel 718 alloys have been extensively investigated,
there are no studies on dislocation evolution and strain
hardening. To address this, we have developed an XRD
framework based on Convolutional Multiple Whole
Proﬁle (CMWP) ﬁtting approach that can be used to
quantify strain hardening in terms of dislocation den-
sities during surface treatment of Ni-base superalloys.
The important ﬁndings of our work are as follows:
1. Classical WH approach which relies on different
dependencies of crystallite size and plastic defor-
mation on diffraction angle cannot deconvolute the
size and strain parameters in shot-peened Ni-base
superalloys.
2. Modified WH approach that includes dislocation
contrast factors to model crystallographic aniso-
tropy is useful for a qualitative analysis, but it is not
reliable for quantitative evaluation of dislocation
densities and its results do not fully agree with the
more accurate whole-profile-based methods, e.g.,
CMWP fitting.
3. Results of CMWP fitting showed that shot-peening
leads to one order of magnitude increase in the
dislocation density of Inconel 718 alloys. Disloca-
tion density values from XRD and TEM were
found to be comparable.
4. CMWP fitting also indicated a relatively homoge-
neous and quasi-regular distribution of dislocations
in the matrix, which was supported by direct
observation from TEM micrographs. Further
increase in shot-peening intensity induced
