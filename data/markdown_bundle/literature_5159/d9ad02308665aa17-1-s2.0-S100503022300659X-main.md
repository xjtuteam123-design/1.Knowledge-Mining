Designing microstructurally stable FeCrAl alloys with excellent strength-ductility synergy is highly desir- 
able for their engineering applications. However, due to the preference nucleation of precipitates at grain 
boundaries (GBs), the improved precipitation strengthening of these alloys is usually accompanied by in- 
tergranular embrittlement. Here, we propose a novel thermomechanical processing route coupled with 
the Si alloying strategy via precipitation of coherent deformable Laves precipitates inside equiaxed ﬁne- 
grains to achieve FeCrAl alloys with ultrahigh yield strength over ∼992 MPa, excellent uniform elongation 
of ∼7.6% at room temperature, and superior thermal stability at temperature ∼1200 °C. The Si alloying not 
only decreases the stacking fault energy of Laves precipitates favorable for their stacking-fault-mediated 
deformation but also hinders grain coarsening at 1200 °C due to the Si-enrichment favorable for GB pin- 
ning effects. Our results prove the possibility of achieving the collaborative enhancement of mutually 
exclusive properties in alloys, such as strength-ductility-thermal stability via nanoprecipitation engineer- 
ing, and offer a promising route to prepare dispersion-strengthened materials.

1. Introduction 
Iron-chromium-aluminum (FeCrAl) alloy is one of the promis- 
ing accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding materials for the next- 
generation nuclear reactors as they can exhibit superior oxida- 
tion resistance in high-temperature steam environments compared 
with zirconium (Zr) alloys [1–4] . However, the neutron absorption 
cross-section of FeCrAl alloys ( ∼2.5b) is ten times larger than that 
of Zr alloys ( ∼0.18b) [5] , rendering that the wall thickness of Fe- 
CrAl alloy cladding tubes needs to be thinned below 0.5 mm [6] to 
compensate for the extra neutron loss. To ensure the safety of nu- 
clear reactors in service and/or accident condition, high mechani- 
cal performance and excellent microstructure stability are both re- 
quired for FeCrAl cladding tubes. 
The mechanical properties of polycrystalline alloys can often 
be tuned through interfaces such as grain boundaries (GBs) and 
phase boundaries (PBs) [7–13] . However, the high concentration of 
Al in FeCrAl alloys makes the BCC ferrite structure stable until the 
melting temperature, eliminating the chance of increasing GB den- 
sity via phase transformation [ 1 , 2 , 14 , 15 ]. Fortunately, second-phase

more coarse-grained Laves precipitates form at GBs with increased 
precipitate contents, leading to severe intergranular embrittlement

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials preparation 
Four alloys with a nominal composition of Fe-13Cr-4.5Al-1Nb- 
2Mo- x Si ( x = 0, 0.5, 1, and 2 wt.%) were prepared by arc-melting 
and cast in the copper moulds with a size of about 55 mm × 22 
mm × 15 mm. For simplicity, these alloys are hereafter referred 
to as 0Si-, 0.5Si-, 1Si-, and 2Si-alloys. All cast ingots were ﬁrst ho- 
mogenized at 1300 °C for 1 h, followed by air cooling (AC) to room 
temperature. For the conventional processing route, the alloy in- 
gots were heated to 1300 °C at a heating rate of ∼30 °C/s and 
rolled at this high temperature to avoid the formation of coarse 
Laves precipitates. Then, the plates after hot rolling (HR) were fur- 
ther subjected to 300 °C warm rolling (WR) and recrystallization 
annealing (RX) [16] , as shown in Fig. 1 (a). To introduce the nano- 
precipitates into the alloy matrix, a novel processing route was de- 
signed in Fig. 1 (b). The effect of each thermomechanical process on 
microstructural evolution was summarized as follows. 
(i) The 15-mm thick alloy plates were ﬁrst heated to 1100 °C at 
a heating rate of ∼30 °C/s and hot rolled to 10 mm, and then 
thinned to 5 mm at 800 °C to reﬁne the coarse grain of the 
homogenized alloys and produce Laves submicron precipitates, 
followed by AC to room temperature (RT). 
(ii) The 5 mm-thick alloy plates underwent 300 °C WR with 70% 
thickness reduction to further reﬁne the layered grain obtained 
by HR but also introduce more substructures such as disloca- 
tion cells. Such severe plastic deformation is beneﬁcial for the 
formation of reﬁned recrystallization grains during subsequent 
high-temperature fast annealing (FA) [31] . 
(iii) The WR samples experienced FA to 1150 °C at the heating 
rate ∼100 °C/s for ∼3 s to promote RX and regression of 
Laves precipitates obtained in the HR process, followed by wa- 
ter quenching (WQ) to RT. The premature precipitation of the 
Laves phase can alleviate the competition between precipita- 
tion and RX during FA, and thus promote RX. In this regard, 
the maximal nucleation rate of RX grains and minimal RX grain 
growth can be achieved [ 32 , 33 ]. On the other hand, annealing 
at 1150 °C also would cause the dissolution of Laves submicron- 
precipitates and their re-precipitation at the nanoscale during 
subsequent aging processes. 
(iv) To enhance the density of nucleation sites of Laves nanopre- 
cipitates and promote their uniform distribution in grain inte- 
riors during aging, 15% pre-rolling at RT was applied to the al- 
loys to introduce more nucleation sites (e.g. dislocations) be- 
fore aging with the thickness reduction to ∼1.25 mm. The ag- 
ing treatment at 600 °C for 1 h was ﬁnally performed, followed 
by AC to RT, rendering a high density of Laves nanoprecipitates 
prepared uniformly in grain interiors. Considering the retained 
Laves submicron-precipitates after FA, heterogeneous Laves pre- 
cipitates were actually obtained in the ﬁnal Si-doped FeCrAl al- 
loys. 
2.2. Microstructure characterization 
The crystal structure and grain sizes of Si-doped FeCrAl alloys 
were examined on a ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, Zeiss GeminiSEM 500) equipped with electron backscatter- 
ing diffraction (EBSD). The SEM and EBSD specimens were pre- 
pared by mechanical grinding and subsequent electro-polishing. 
During the EBSD testing, data were collected at a step size of 1 
μm. Transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F) ob- 
servations were performed on a foil sample at 200 kV to investi- 
gate the characteristics of Laves precipitates. The grain sizes and 
volume fraction of Laves precipitates were measured using Im- 
age J software. To characterize the chemical composition and el- 
ement distribution of Laves nanoprecipitates, atom probe tomog- 
raphy (APT) technology was employed via an Imago Scientiﬁc In- 
struments 30 0 0HR local electrode atom probe (LEAP). Integrated 
Visualization & Analysis Software (IVAS) version 3.6.8 was used for 
the 3D reconstructions and compositional analyses of the APT data 
of the specimens.

2.3. Mechanical testing 
Dog-bone-shaped specimens were machined by an electrodis- 
charge machine along the rolling direction, and the gauge sizes 
were 10 mm × 4 mm × 1.2 mm [ 34 , 35 ]. The static tensile tests 
were performed at room temperature using a SANS CMT4204 test- 
ing machine at a strain rate of 1 × 10 –3 s –1 . To ensure experimen- 
tal reliability, at least three samples were tested in each specimen. 
2.4. Calculations of ﬁrst-principles 
To obtain the parameters such as the formation enthalpy, bulk 
modulus, and stacking fault energy of the Laves precipitates, calcu- 
lations based on density functional theory (DFT) were conducted 
using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) program 
[ 36 , 37 ]. During calculation, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) 
generalized gradient approximation to the exchange-correlation 
functional, and projector augmented wave (PAW) method pseu- 
dopotentials provided with VASP were selected [38] . More details 
about the calculations can be referred to the Supplementary Mate- 
rials. 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure of C-RR samples 
Fig. 2 exhibits the SEM images of the C-RR Si-doped FeCrAl 
alloys. The average grain sizes of the 0Si-, 0.5Si-, 1Si-, and 2Si- 
alloys reached 167.4, 38.7, 48.2, and 85.5 μm, respectively, which 
were not ideal for the cladding materials with a thickness of 500 
μm or even less. This is because such coarse-grained structure not 
only induces nonuniform reduction of wall thickness due to in- 
homogeneous deformation of individual grains but also compro- 
mises the mechanical properties of the clad tubes [6] . On the other 
hand, the sizes of retained Laves particles in the alloy matrix are 
in the micron scale, rendering weak strengthening effects on al- 
loys. Speciﬁcally, sub-micron-thick Laves ﬁlms precipitated along 
GBs (in the insets in Figs. 2 and S1 in the Supplementary Material) 
and hard/brittle GB intermetallics (e.g. Laves particles) usually act 
as the cracking source, which results in intergranular brittle frac- 
ture of alloys [ 25 , 39 ].

3.2. Microstructure of the RFPA samples 
3.2.1. Grain sizes of the RFPA samples 
Fig. 3 shows EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps of RFPA Si- 
doped FeCrAl alloys with different Si contents. Obviously, ﬁne 
equiaxed ferrite grains bounded by high angle GBs (HAGBs, > 15 °) 
have been prepared successfully as compared with the C-RR sam- 
ples. By measuring more than 100 grains based on the linear in- 
ception method, the average grain sizes of 0Si-, 0.5Si-, 1Si-, and 
2Si-alloys were obtained to be ∼10.3, ∼10.1, ∼8.2, and ∼5.6 μm, 
respectively, which is only 1/15 to 1/4 of those of C-RR samples. 
It appears that the grain sizes of the RFPA samples gradually de- 
crease with increasing Si contents. Fig. 3 also reveals the texture in 
obtained RFPA samples with different Si contents exhibit the char- 
acter of random pole distribution, implying that the deformation 
textures have been greatly weakened during the heat treatment 
[6] .

Si alloying. For example, the average nano-precipitate size of the 
0Si-alloy was measured to be ∼16.8 nm, while the average parti-

3.3. Microstructure evolution upon high-temperature annealing 
To explore the high-temperature resistance of the present RFPA 
FeCrAl alloys with different Si contents, all the RFPA samples 
were reannealed at 700 °C to 1200 °C for 1 h, as shown in 
Fig. 6 . It appears that the average particle size apparently increases 
with increasing annealing temperature, meanwhile, three distinc- 
tive stages can be uncovered in the precipitate coarsening process 
for all four alloys. In the ﬁrst stage, the annealing temperature (e.g. 
70 0 °C and 80 0 °C) is low, the coarsening rate of Laves nanopre- 
cipitates in four alloys is relatively small, and the average sizes 
of initial Laves nanoprecipitates are still in the nanoscale. For in- 
stance, the average size of Laves nano-particles in RFPA 2Si-alloys 
increases from the initial size of ∼12.5 nm to ∼36.9 nm and ∼68.3 
nm after annealing at 700 °C and 800 °C, respectively, as shown 
in Figs. 6 (a2) and 8 (a). In the second stage, when the annealing 
temperature increases to 90 0 °C and 10 0 0 °C, the Laves nanopre- 
cipitates coarsen rapidly to several hundreds of nanometers in all 
four alloys. The size of initial Laves nanoprecipitates in 2Si-alloys 
reached ∼117 nm and ∼310 nm, respectively, see Figs. 6 (a3) and 
8 . In the third stage, when the annealing temperature was further 
increased to ∼110 0 °C and 120 0 °C, the Si effect on the thermal 
stability of Laves precipitates became pronounced, i.e., the high- 
temperature resistance of Laves precipitates increases obviously 
with increasing Si contents. The Laves precipitates in the 1100 °C 
annealed 0Si-alloys have almost completely dissolved into the alloy 
matrix, see Fig. 6 (a4), however, they still account for ∼2.1% in the 
1200 °C annealed 2Si-alloys, see Figs. 6 (d5) and 8 (b). The dissolu- 
tion of Laves precipitates also makes their size no longer mono- 
tonically increase with the elevated annealing temperature, even 
signiﬁcant size reduction was observed in 0Si-and 0.5Si-alloys an- 
nealed at 1100 °C and 1200 °C, see Fig. 8 (a). 
Given the tensile embrittlement of ferrite steels is strongly re- 
lated to the grain size [ 16 , 22 , 25 ], the grain size evolution of RFPA 
samples during high-temperature annealing was investigated. Dif- 
ferent from the three-stage evolution of Laves precipitates, the 
grain sizes in all four alloys increased slowly even though the an- 
nealing temperature is high about 10 0 0 °C, as shown in Figs. 7 and 
8 (c). As a result, the grain of 2Si-alloys increases slightly from the 
initial average size of ∼5.6 μm to ∼6.3 μm after annealing at 10 0 0 
°C. However, corresponding to the complete dissolution tempera- 
ture of Laves precipitates, the grain size in the four alloys increase 
rapidly once the pining effect of the Laves phase on GBs disap- 
peared. Because of the Laves dissolution in 0Si-alloys, the average 
grain size reaches ∼111 μm after 1100 °C annealing, see Fig. 7 (a4). 
In contrast, the grain size of 2Si-alloys only increases to ∼13.8 μm 
under the same condition, see Fig. 7 (d4). Along with the increase 
of the complete Laves dissolution temperature, the instability tem- 
perature of grain sizes in 0.5Si-and 1Si-alloys increases to 1100 °C 
and 1150 °C, respectively, as shown in Fig. 8 (c). In particular, the 
average grain size of the 2Si-alloy is still as small as ∼30.5 μm 
after 1200 °C annealing. The grain evolution of our Si-doped al- 
loys with low coarsening rates is in sharp contrast with previously 
reported FeCrAl alloys [ 1 , 16 , 20 ], see Fig. 8 (c). The grain sizes for 
all alloys increase with increasing annealing temperature, our Si- 
alloyed FeCrAl alloys in this work exhibit excellent thermal stabil- 
ity.

4.1. Grain stabilizing mechanisms under high-temperature annealing 
Based on the above ﬁndings, the synergistic evolution of Laves 
nanoprecipitates (in Fig. 6 ) and the grain sizes (in Fig. 7 ) with an- 
nealing temperature for the Si-doped FeCrAl alloys was schemati- 
cally depicted in Fig. 10 (a). It is found that the coarsening behavior 
of grains is quite sensitive to the annealing temperature, in partic- 
ular for the low Si-doped alloys at high temperatures above 10 0 0 
°C, due to the dissolution of Laves precipitates associated with the 
notably reduced GB pinning effect. 
To uncover the relationship between grain size and Laves pre- 
cipitates, and the underlying mechanism for the thermal stability 
of RFPA 2Si-alloys, we compared the evolution of the Zener pin- 
ning effect ( P z , originating from Laves particles on grain bound- 
aries) and the driving pressure for grain growth ( P g , originating 
from the decrease in total grain boundary energy) as a function 
of annealing temperature. Here, P z can be described as P z = 3 f γ/ d , 
where γ is the interfacial energy per unit area of the boundary 
( ∼1 J/m 2 ) [1] , f is the Laves volume fraction, and d is the average 
particle diameter [47] ; P g can be expressed by the average grain 
diameter D , i.e., P g = 2 γ/ D . Substituting the data shown in Fig. 8 , 
the calculated P z and P g with annealing temperature are shown 
in Fig. 10 (b). Owing to the high content of Laves nano-precipitates 
under low-temperature annealing, P z is far greater than P g , there- 
fore, grain growth at 700 °C is diﬃcult due to the suﬃcient Zener 
pinning effect. When the annealing temperature was enhanced to 
900 °C, the Laves nanoprecipitates progressed into the rapid coars- 
ening stage, leading to a rapid decrease in P z . In this case, P z is

4.2. Si-alloying effects on the thermal stability of Laves precipitates 
The thermal stability of Laves precipitates is closely related 
to the Si enrichment. However, the underlying mechanism of the 
high-temperature resistance of Laves precipitates with Si addition 
is still unclear. In this section, we try to reveal the Si effect on the 
stability of Laves precipitates in terms of the parameters of forma- 
tion enthalpy and Gibbs free energies (GFE) [43] .

compound at 0 K. First-principles calculations were performed to

4.3. Enhancement of ductility via Si-alloying 
Apart from the signiﬁcantly enhanced strength of the present 
Si-doped RFPA FeCrAl alloys (see Supplementary materials), an- 
other outstanding advantage is the good ductility achieved by im- 
proved work hardening capability. It may be ascribed to the follow- 
ing two work-hardening mechanisms. First, the increased GB den- 
sity and uniform nanoprecipitates produce strong interaction with 
dislocations so that homogeneous deformation substructures were 
formed in the alloy matrix, as shown in Fig. S6, which is beneﬁcial 
for promoting strain delocalization and elevating the work hard- 
ening rate for enhanced uniform elongation [ 10 , 11 , 52 ]. Secondly, 
the Laves precipitates exhibit good deformability to accommodate 
plastic strains during tension. Although SFs have been observed 
within some Laves nanoprecipitates before deformation ( Fig. 4 ), 
the diffraction pattern of the deformed samples reveals a more ev- 
ident SFs characteristic in Fig. S6(b), implying partial dislocations 
glide within the Laves nanoprecipitates, thus contributing to the 
plasticity. Speciﬁcally, Fig. 13 clearly shows that (0 0 01) basal plane 
SFs were emitted from the Laves/matrix interface to the precipitate 
interior, resulting in the extended SFs. This also conﬁrms that the 
interfaces can serve as dislocation sources to emit dislocations for 
large ductility, in addition to acting as dislocation barriers for high 
strength. Partial dislocation motion in the interior of Laves parti- 
cles allows to alleviate the stress concentrations at interfaces and 
leads to synergistic deformation between precipitates and the alloy 
matrix [48] , which are beneﬁcial for enhancing the work harden- 
ing ability and preventing the crack nucleation in alloys at high 
strain/stress levels.

5. Conclusions 
(1) Our proposed RFPA strategy is demonstrated to be able to 
prepare ﬁne equiaxed grains strengthened by (semi-)coherent 
Laves nanoprecipitates in Si-doped FeCrAl alloys. Compared 
with the C-RR samples prepared by the conventional process 
route, their yield strength of RFPA Si-doped FeCrAl alloys al- 
most doubled with enhanced ductility in both 1Si- and 2Si- 
alloys. 
(2) The enhanced thermal stability of Si-doped FeCrAl alloys is 
mainly related to the elevated high-temperature resistance of 
Si-enriched Laves precipitates, which renders the reﬁned fer- 
rite grains retained under high-temperature annealing due to 
the strong precipitate pinning on GBs. 
(3) First-principle calculations uncovered that Si enrichment in 
Fe 2 (Nb, Mo) Laves precipitates is energetically favorable, which 
contributes to the reduced SFE of deformable Laves precipitates. 
Moreover, the Nb element plays a more important role than 
Mo in reducing the SFE and enhancing the thermal stability of 
Laves precipitates.
