1. Introduction
To meet further elevated-temperature, high-performance demands 
of efficient aircraft turbines, there is a substantial need of tougher and 
more softening-resistant alloys, capable of withstanding even higher 
temperatures, than nickel-based superalloys [ 1 ]. In the recent decade, 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs [ 2 – 4 ]) and particularly body-centered cubic 
(BCC) refractory HEAs (RHEAs [ 5 , 6 ]) of great attention have shown 
exceptional mechanical properties especially at elevated temperatures 
and demonstrated high potential for application in extreme environ -
ments. Attributable to the local chemical fluctuations, the intersection of 
kink pairs at the energy valley with each other to form immobile cross 
kinks improve the strength of RHEAs [ 7 – 9 ]. And, the activation of dis -
locations on high-order slip planes to reduce the mobility of edge dis -
locations provide RHEAs even more excellent elevated-temperature 
mechanical performance [ 10 – 12 ]. However, due to the segregation of 
impurities to grain boundaries and the reduction of grain boundary 
cohesion to cause detrimental intergranular fracture [ 13 – 19 ], most 
RHEAs containing the VB and VIB groups of elements with a high 
strength and resistance to elevated-temperature softening have the 
drawback of brittleness [ 20 ], e.g. the single-phase WTaMoNb alloy with 
a retained yield stress of 405 MPa even at 1600 
◦
C but a low fracture 
strain less than 2 % under ambient temperature [ 13 ], and HfMoTaTiZr 
and other RHEAs with a high yield stress of 1300 – 1600 MPa but a small 
strain of only 3 – 4 % at room temperature [ 14 , 15 ]. The selection of 
alternative refractory metals such as the IVB and VB groups of elements 
might enhance the ductility but however lose the strength, e.g. 
HfNbTaTiZr with a high fracture strain up to 50 % but a dropped yield 
stress of only 92 MPa at 1200 
◦
C [ 16 , 17 ]. For developing tough RHEAs 
with both a high strength and good ductility, the selection of constituent 
elements needs to be carefully considered, and the week grain bound -
aries need to be strengthened.
A proper addition of small atom-size solutes, such as carbon (C), 
nitrogen (N), oxygen (O) and boron (B) [ 20 – 25 ], in the form of in -
terstitials than carbide or boride precipitates [ 26 ], has been observed to 
effectively improve the mechanical properties of alloys. These intersti -
tial atoms would generate a strain field to hinder dislocation movement 
and hence to improve the strength [ 27 – 29 ]. The interstitials have also 
been found to balance the distribution of charge density between grains 
and boundaries, thus enhancing the cohesion of grain boundaries and 
preventing intergranular fracture [ 29 , 30 ]. In the early study, B doping 
into Ni

2. Experimental details
The selection of constituent metallic elements for developing robust 
RHEAs with a high strength and moderate ductility was based on their 
basic properties in Supplemental Table S1 [ 33 – 35 ] and mixing en -

lattice mismatch and generate high solid-solution strengthening; a high 
modulus (Mo) to increase strength; a high melting point (Nb and Ta, also 
as a BCC stabilizer) to maintain a high strength at elevated temperatures;

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and chemical compositions
Fig. 1 shows the microstructure, grain orientations (and phases), 
crystal structures and chemical compositions of RHEA and RHEB sam -
ples. The EBSD IPF maps in Fig. 1 (a) – (d), with the phase maps inserted 
in Fig. 1 (b) and (d) , first indicate the randomly distributed orientation 
of BCC-phase equiaxed grains without the precipitation of borides. As 
suggested by the literature [ 25 ], attributable to the enhanced cohesive 
energy of grain boundaries and the reduced energy difference between 
grains and boundaries to raise the activation energy of grain growth, the 
addition of B inhibited grain growth: the average grain size decreased 
from about 140 (for RHEA1) and 200 μ m (RHEA2) to about 100 μ m 
(RHEB1 and RHEB2). The XRD patterns in Fig. 1 (e) suggest the for -
mation of single-phase, BCC-structured solid solution for the four sam -
ples, and the elemental mappings in Fig. 1 (f) – (i) reveal the uniform 
distribution of constituent metallic elements. As also summarized in 
Supplemental Table S3 , the average compositions were close to the 
designed ones, and no significant difference in the contents of the 
metallic elements was observed between the RHEA and RHEB samples. 
To more precisely determine the addition and distribution of B element, 
EPMA WDS analyses of the RHEB1 sample were performed, as shown in 
Fig. 1 (j) – (n). The WDS mapping in Fig. 1 (j) – (l) indicated a relatively 
uniform distribution of B element in the sample with a slight segregation 
tendency towards grain boundaries. The WDS line scanning in Fig. 1 (m)
and (n) further confirmed the minor segregation (approximately 5.8 at. 
%) of B element at the grain boundaries while rather low but uniform 
distribution in the grains. It was attributable to the high diffusion rate 
and low segregation energy of B atoms, which thermodynamically and 
kinetically favored the segregation of B solutes to the grain boundaries 
[ 29 ]. On the other hand, no precipitation of borides was noticed, 
possibly owing to the severe lattice distortion of HEAs to allow a massive

3.2. Hardness of grains and grain boundary
Fig. 2 presents the nanoindenting hardness of RHEA and RHEB 
samples and indicates the large difference in the hardness of grain 
boundaries between the two types of samples. As the nanoindenting 
hardness mappings in Fig. 2 (a) – (d) show, the hardness of the grain 
boundary regions was relatively lower than that of the grains particu -
larly for the RHEA samples, whereas the hardness distribution for the 
RHEB samples was much uniform. As more clearly plotted in Fig. 2 (e) 
and (f), as well as listed in Supplementary Table S4 , the originally low 
hardness of the grain boundary regions in the RHEA samples (about 5.0 
GPa) was improved to the level of the grains in the RHEB samples (about 
6.2 and 5.9 GPa for RHEB1 and RHEB2, respectively), suggesting the 
effective strengthening of grain boundaries with the addition of B sol -
utes. It is worth noting that the effect of B interstitials on grain boundary 
strengthening is much obvious especially for RHEA/B1 (from 5.0 to 6.2 
GPa), while the effect on overall solid-solution strengthening is marked 
for RHEA/B2 (from 5.3 to 6.0 GPa), possibly because of a higher content 
of the harder but less boundary-cohesive VIB group of elements in 
RHEA/B1, but a larger fraction of the softer and more ductile IVB group 
of elements in RHEA/B2 [ 39 ]. Because the RHEA1 sample with a higher 
content of VIB group of elements would exhibit a greater sensitivity to 
oxygen, resulting in weaker grain boundaries (grain boundary hardness 
5.0 GPa compared to grain hardness 6.5 GPa) [ 20 ]. The addition of B 
solutes then significantly increased the grain boundary hardness of 
RHEB1 to 6.2 GPa (for 24.0 %). In contrast, the RHEA2 sample with a 
higher content of IVB group of elements had a lower sensitivity to ox -
ygen and intrinsically stronger grain boundaries (grain boundary 
hardness 5.0 GPa compared to grain hardness 5.3 GPa). Although B 
solutes also improved the grain boundary hardness of RHEB2 to 5.9 GPa 
(for 18.0 %), the enhancement was less pronounced. Nevertheless, the 
grain boundary hardness of RHEB2, 5.9 GPa, was much comparable to 
the average grain hardness, 6.0 GPa, still indicating marked grain 
boundary strengthening. Moreover, the average grain hardness of 
RHEA/B1 just increased from 6.5 to 6.6 GPa (for 1.5 %), but that of 
RHEA/B2 increased from 5.3 to 6.0 GPa (for 13.2 %) because the strong 
interaction of B interstitials and dislocations in RHEA/B2 was expected 
to more markedly enhance the work hardening and hardness of the 
originally softer and more ductile alloys [ 39 ]. Additionally, it is noticed 
that the hardness discrepancy between differently oriented grains, i.e. 
the 〈 111 〉 , 〈 110 〉 and 〈 100 〉 grains, representing the typical plastic 
anisotropy, is not as significant as expected, which suggests the much 
uniform plastic deformability of HEAs [ 32 , 40 ].

3.4. Strengthening of grain boundary
Fig. 4 , by conducting the compression of dual-grain micropillars 
(with an inclined grain boundary between the 〈 100 〉 and 〈 111 〉 grains), 
clearly illustrates the weak grain boundaries of the undoped RHEA 
samples (RHEA2P-GB) but the much enhanced boundary cohesion of the 
B-added RHEB samples (RHEB2P-GB). As shown in Fig. 4 (a) , the stress- 
strain curves of several RHEA2P-GB micropillars present early stress 
drops at relatively low stresses (of about 600 – 1000 MPa, probably 
owing to different inclined angles of the grain boundaries) prior to the 
normal yielding of RHEA2 (at about 1280 MPa). The stress drops just 
correspond to the catastrophic cleavage of the dual-grain structure along 
the weak grain boundary in Fig. 4 (b) and (c), and the low failure stresses 
suggest the low cohesion strength of the grain boundary possibly at the 
level of only several hundreds of MPa. By contrast, the stress-strain 
curves of RHEB2P-GB in Fig. 4 (a) are much close to those of single- 
grain RHEB2P given in Fig. 3 (a) , without obvious or deep stress 
drops. The micropillars yielded at the high stress level of about 
1300 – 1400 MPa and were normally strain hardened. As presented in 
Fig. 4 (d) and (e), the dual-grain micropillar deformed via typical slips or 
plastic shear, first on the regular {110} 〈 111 〉 slip systems in the 〈 100 〉 
grain and then on the alternatively activated {123} 〈 111 〉 systems in the 
〈 111 〉 grain [ 40 ], rather than through the intergranular cleavage. That 
means, the cohesive strength of grain boundaries was effectively 
improved with the addition of B solutes, which could be attributable to 
the retained charge density [ 29 ]. In RHEAs, as suggested [ 29 ], the 
inevitable segregation of highly electronegative O contaminants to grain 
boundaries would attract charges from adjacent metallic elements, 
causing a depletion zone of charge density between the O atoms and the 
host elements. The depletion of charges would then cause the weakening 
of the interatomic bonds as well as the grain boundary cohesion. 
Nevertheless, the addition of B solutes with a high diffusivity to replace 
O atoms at the grain boundaries would prevent the segregation of O 
contaminants. Owing to the relatively low electronegativity of B ele -
ments, the original high charge density between the B interstitials and 
the host elements could be retained, accordingly enhancing the cohesive 
strength of grain boundaries.

Prior to plastic deformation and grain boundary decohesion, the 
calculated yield stress of each bulk sample is basically equivalent to the 
experimental data. However, for the micropillar samples of the small 
scale, the measured yield stresses are obviously lower than the calcu -
lated values possibly due to the early nucleation of dislocations from the 
pillar surface [ 41 ]. Among all the strengthening mechanisms, the solid 
solution strengthening looks to be the key factor as it is contributed by 
the severe lattice distortions of the multicomponent alloys with much 
different atom sizes. By contrast, the grain boundary strengthening is 
insignificant as the grain sizes of the samples are all about hundreds of 
μ m. It is noticed that the measured 15 % flow stresses (yield stress +
work-hardening stress) of all the RHEB samples are higher than the 
predicted values, whereas the measured stresses of all the RHEA samples 
are lower than the predictions especially for RHEA1. As verified above, 
the early decohesion of grain boundaries caused the relatively weak 
work hardenability of the RHEA samples. Nevertheless, with the addi -
tion of B solutes and the much improved grain boundary cohesion in the 
RHEB samples, in contrast to the rather small strengthening effect of 
interstitials on pinning of dislocations, an accommodated high density of 
dislocations to entangle with each other dominates the high work 
hardening. The B atoms are expected to trap dislocations, leading to the 
massive multiplication of dislocation loops [ 25 ], and, meanwhile, the 
undecohered grain boundaries act as an effective dislocation source 
[ 56 ], both of which increase the density of dislocations beyond the 
predicted amount and thereafter contribute to the observed high work 
hardenability.
Moreover, the piling-up of dislocations near grain boundaries pri -
marily determines the grain boundary strengthening factor in the Hall- 
Petch equation [ 54 ]. Due to the premature decohesion of grain bound -
aries in the RHEA samples, the accumulation of dislocations at grain 
boundaries was limited, resulting in lower measured stresses than pre -
dicted values. Contrarily, improved grain boundary cohesion in the 
RHEB samples led to more dislocation aggregation and thus a higher 
grain boundary strengthening factor. According to the literature [ 55 ], 
the addition of only 500 ppm B solutes to an Fe-45Al alloy effectively 
hindered the slip transfer of dislocations across grain boundaries. 
Additionally, the work hardenability was also influenced by the 
compositional difference between the RHEA/B1 and RHEA/B2 matrices. 
The higher Mo content in RHEA/B1 yielded a stronger atomic bonding 
[ 57 ] and led to a lower dislocation mobility. As a result, the trapping of 
dislocations by B interstitials and the improvement of work harden -
ability in RHEB1 would be less pronounced. By comparison, in RHEA/B2 
with a lower Mo content, a higher dislocation mobility was expected, so 
the trapping of dislocations by B interstitials and the enhancement of 
work hardening would be more effective in RHEB2 as evidenced by the 
measurement of the micropillar samples.
Furthermore, according to the literature [ 12 , 58 – 60 ], the above 
strengthening mechanisms might possibly alter at elevated temperatures 
as discussed below. 
(1) Solid-solution strengthening: since the mobility of edge disloca -
tions in the highly lattice-distorted RHEAs is much lower than 
that in conventional alloys at high temperatures [ 12 ], 
solid-solution strengthening is still expected to be the dominant 
strengthening mechanism.
(2) Grain-boundary strengthening: as grain boundary cohesion will 
decrease with increasing temperature [ 58 ], the grain-boundary 
strengthening factor is expected to decrease. However, B sol -
utes are still capable of enhancing grain boundary cohesion at 
elevated temperatures.
(3) Interstitial strengthening: the contribution of interstitial 
strengthening would be negligible at elevated temperatures 
because of the rapid increase in the diffusion rates of solutes [ 59 ].
(4) Work hardening: dynamic recovery would be much active at 
elevated temperatures, so the dislocation density and work 
hardening are expected to decrease, ex. the dislocation density in 
BCC iron at 900 K was approximately half that at room temper -
ature [ 60 ].
Given the temperature-dependent behavior of each strengthening 
mechanism, solid-solution strengthening is still expected to be the pri -
mary mechanism at elevated temperatures, followed by work hardening, 
grain-boundary strengthening, and interstitial strengthening.
Most BCC RHEAs (including the RHEA samples here) still have the 
problem of strength-ductility trade-off, as presented in Fig. 7 (b) (all by 
compressive tests [ 13 , 15 , 16 , 61 – 63 ]). Upon grain boundary strength -
ening by B interstitials, nevertheless, both the strength and plastic strain 
of the RHEB samples are significantly improved to a level better than 
that of previous RHEAs. The addition of B solutes effectively toughens 
the alloys to circumvent the strength-ductility trade-off, paving the way 
for the development of high-performance RHEAs.
