abstract
During high temperature loading, the regular microstructure of nickel-base superalloys consisting of a c-
matrix (Ni) containing a large volume fraction of c0 -particles (Ni 3Al) degrades. The cubic precipitates
coarsen and elongate in a direction normal to the applied stress in a process called rafting. In this paper
a phenomenological relation is proposed to describe the kinetics of this degradation process. Also, a rela-
tion is presented for the isotropic coarsening process that acts simultaneously and becomes dominant
when the rafting is complete. The degradation affects the mechanical response of the material. The rel-
evant mechanisms are discussed and formulations are developed to incorporate the degradation effects
in an existing multiscale constitutive framework to describe the mechanical behaviour of nickel-base
superalloys and in a recently postulated damage evolution equation. The capability of the model to sim-
ulate the response of the degraded material is demonstrated by comparing the computational results to
experimental observations.

1. Introduction
Single crystal nickel-base superalloys are widely used as gas
turbine blade materials because of their high resistance against
high thermo-mechanical loads. The superior high temperature
behaviour is attributed to the two-phase composite microstructure
consisting of a c-matrix (Ni) containing a large volume fraction of
c0-particles (Ni 3Al). The strengthening effect of the c0-precipitates
depends largely on their size and morphology.
During high temperature service, the microstructure gradually
degrades by a coarsening process. In the presence of stress, e.g.
caused by the centrifugal load in a gas turbine blade, a severe
directional coarsening, so-called rafting, of the initially cuboidal
c0-particles into a plate-like structure occurs, see Fig. 1. The gradual
adaptation of the microstructure results in a deterioration of the
mechanical properties. Therefore, to perform a reliable life assess-
ment on gas turbine blades, it is highly desirable to include this
effect in the constitutive models for Ni-base superalloys.
In the last decades, a lot of experimental research has been done
on the microstructure evolution of Ni-base superalloys. This
research focused, on the one hand, on the mechanisms and kinetics
of the degradation process itself and on the other hand on the
effect of the microstructural changes on the mechanical response
of the material. The existing literature on these two aspects is
reviewed in the next subsections and the work presented in the
present paper is positioned within that ﬁeld of research.
Finally, throughout the literature, rafting is commonly examined
in uniaxially loaded specimens. This is not only a relatively simple
and convenient load condition, but also a condition that is represen-
tative for many locations in a turbine blade subjected to a radially
directed centrifugal force. The external load is commonly deﬁned
in the vertical direction, resulting in an elongation of the precipitates
in the horizontal direction, see Fig. 2. This geometrical convention is
also used in this paper, since uniaxial stress is the dominant loading
mode to which most discussions refer. Nevertheless, this paper also
deviates from this common convention in the part where extension
of the model to the multiaxial case is concerned.

From these experimental observations it can be concluded that

strain. Matan et al. [2] reported a threshold of 0.1% for CMSX-4
at 950 /C176C, while Henderson et al.[7] found 0.35% at 1000 /C176C.
Whereas a certain amount of plastic deformation is a require-
ment for the onset of rafting, the presence of an external stress sur-
prisingly appears not to be a requirement to sustain the rafting
process. Spontaneous rafting is observed experimentally [2,7,8] at
high temperatures and is attributed to internal stresses (e.g. misﬁt
stress, dislocation induced back stress or dendritic stress [9]) that
act as a driving force for rafting.

during rafting at temperatures below 980 /C176C, as was shown by Ser-
in et al. [10]. Only beyond this temperature, the
c0-volume fraction

decrease can occur without violating the conservation of mass,
since c and c0 are two phases within the same alloy. The position
of the alloying elements (mainly Al) in the crystal lattice
determines whether a certain region is denoted as matrix phase
(Al as a solid solution in Ni) or precipitate phase (ordered Ni
3Al)
The actual precipitate volume fraction is therefore governed by
thermodynamic equilibrium.
The rather constant precipitate volume fraction implies that
elongation of the precipitates in the horizontal direction must be
accompanied by a reduction of the precipitate dimension in the
vertical direction and thus a widening of the associated horizontal
matrix channels (Fig. 2). That process can proceed until the vertical
matrix channel in between two neighbouring precipitates has dis-
appeared completely.
Apart from the rafting process, two other coarsening processes
can be active. Firstly, simultaneously with the rafting, isotropic
coarsening due to Ostwald ripening occurs [11]. In this process,
the larger precipitates increase their size to the expense of the
smaller particles, which disappear. Experiments [12] have shown
that the microstructure periodicity increases up to 20% during raf-
ting, which can be attributed to the ripening process. Nevertheless,
the majority of the rafting models neglects the ripening mecha-
nism. Secondly, when the rafts have fully developed, the micro-
structure continues to coarsen, but at a reduced rate, as shown
by Epishin et al. [12]. The coarsening process appears to be con-
trolled by the migration of
c0-terminations [13], which widens
the remaining matrix channels. The process is explained by using
the model proposed by Graham and Kraft [14] for the thermal
coarsening of lamellar eutectics. Eventually, a topological inversion
occurs: the
c0-phase surrounds islands of the c-phase. This is
caused by the formation of junctions penetrating the c-phase
and connecting neighbouring c0-rafts. The junctions are formed

Two approaches can be pursued to derive numerical relations
for the description of the degradation process: (i) using experimen-
tal results to formulate phenomenological relations at a global
scale and (ii) exploiting knowledge of the driving forces for rafting
and coarsening to develop more fundamental methods. Since the
precipitate size and matrix channel width are geometrically re-
lated through their overall volume invariance, the kinetics of deg-
radation can be described in terms of either channel widening or
precipitate growth.
The phenomenological approach has been followed by Serin
et al. [10] and Kamaraj et al. [15], who present a relation for chan-
nel widening. The rate of channel widening is assumed to be gov-
erned by the applied stress. They attribute this dependence to the
higher equilibrium c0 volume fraction at higher stress. Epishin et al.
[12,16] performed a lot of experimental work on CMSX-4 in the
framework of the European COST538 project. They observed that
the channel widening process shows two stages. Initially the
channels widen relatively fast until the channel width has
increased to three times the original width, and then the widening
rate reduces considerably, yet again constant in time. They also
derived a phenomenological relation for the initial channel widen-
ing rate at a range of temperatures and applied stresses, which will
be discussed in Section 2.1. Finally, Matan et al. [2] described the
kinetics of rafting by deﬁning dimensional parameters L, T and S
that characterize the microstructure, and measuring their values
in interrupted creep tests.
Several researchers have studied the driving force for degrada-
tion and a number of models have been proposed [3,4,6,17–19].
Most of these approaches are based on energy considerations,
where the precipitates evolve towards a morphology with a lower
energy. The earlier models only considered the elastic energy (e.g.
[18,20]), but later the role of plastic deformation was generally rec-
ognized and its effect was included in the models as well [3,4,6].
Véron et al. [3] presented a model for channel widening that is
based on the competition between plasticity induced mass trans-
port and diffusional mass transport. Socrate and Parks [4] used
an energy perturbation approach to derive an interfacial normal
stress
sn that predicts the direction of movement of the c/c0-inter-
face. They performed FE calculations and were able to simulate
correctly a number of experimental cases. Svoboda and Lukas
[6,21] extended this idea and developed an energy-based model
for the growth of precipitates, which is similar to the model by
Véron et al. [3]. The increase in precipitate size is related to the bal-
ance between total energy (consisting of elastic energy, potential
energy of loading and energy of dislocations) and dissipation (dif-
fusion). Bufﬁere and Ignat [17] developed a dislocation based crite-
rion to describe the raft formation, where a local diffusion
mechanism is suggested as the driving force. Fedelich [19] recently
demonstrated that the reduction of the internal stress, caused by
the lattice misﬁt and the dislocation ﬁeld, is the driving force for
rafting. This is the ﬁrst approach that correlates the rafting directly
to the speciﬁc arrangement of the dislocations. Finally, the driving
force based models discussed in the present section are mainly
used to describe the direction of rafting under different load
conditions. Only the models by Svoboda [6] and Fedelich [19] also
attempt to capture the kinetics of the rafting process. For the
coarsening process, the reduction of interfacial energy appears to
be the driving force [19].
1.2. Effect of degradation on the mechanical response
The effect of microstructure degradation on the mechanical
properties of the material has been subject of research the last
decade [12,13,22–26]. Most of the published work focused on the
effects on the creep behaviour, while the effects on the tensile
and low cycle fatigue behaviour remained relatively unexplored.
Reed et al. [22] claim that in CMSX-4, at temperatures higher
than 1000 /C176C, the raft formation and eventual inversion of the
microstructure prevents the passing of
c0-particles by glide and
climb of matrix dislocations, which reduces the creep rate. Kamaraj
[1] states in his review that some authors report enhanced creep
properties in rafted material, while others report the opposite. Shui
et al. [23,24] studied the effect of P-type rafts (parallel to the load
axis) on the creep behaviour. They found different effects at 800 /C176C
and 1000 /C176C. At 800/C176C the creep rate was higher, since the shearing
of the incoherent rafts is easier than shearing of the coherent
cubes. On the other hand, at 1000 /C176C, the performance is better,
since the driving force for climb is larger on the horizontal
c/c0-
interfaces and thus, in a P-type rafted structure, climb is effectively
impeded by the large vertical
c/c0-interfaces. Finally, topological
inversion of the microstructure, as observed by Epishin et al. [13]
in SRR99, is accompanied by a drastic increase of the creep
rate. The zigzag shape of the interfaces in this inversed state en-
hances c0-shearing, while dislocation climb is also still possible.
Ott and Mughrabi [25] studied the effect of rafting on the low
cycle fatigue (LCF) life of CMSX-4 and CMSX-6 at 950 /C176C. It was ob-
served that cracks propagate in c or at the c/c0-interface. Therefore,
rafts in the material parallel to the crack reduce the life time, since
they enhance crack propagation, while rafts perpendicular to the
crack increase the life by blocking the cracks. This is contradicted
by Zhou et al. [26], who report that rafts perpendicular to the load-
ing axis increase the LCF life, since they hinder the dislocation
movement.
In the context of the recent European collaborative project
COST538, several groups performed tensile and low cycle fatigue
tests on degraded CMSX-4 specimens. It was observed that the de-
graded specimens show a signiﬁcantly weaker tensile response
than virgin specimens and that the fatigue life for the degraded
specimens is lower than that of the virgin specimens [12].
1.3. Implications for modelling
From this literature review it can be concluded that the kinetics
of degradation has been studied extensively and that the mecha-
nisms are now at least partially understood. A number of authors
proposed models to describe the kinetics of rafting, but these mod-
els are almost exclusively limited to the uniaxial load case. The
amount of published experimental data on the mechanical proper-
ties (other than creep) of degraded microstructures is rather lim-
ited. Moreover, the mechanisms that are responsible for the
observed effects are not fully clear yet.
The number of numerical models capable of simulating the ef-
fects of microstructure degradation is limited. Some of the devel-
oped ﬁnite element based unit cell models have been applied to
explore the effects of changes in the microstructure morphology
on the tensile response of the material [27,28]. Also the model
developed by Fedelich [29] was applied to study the effects of
changes in the microstructure. Finally, Sakaguchi and Okazaki
[30] developed a numerical model based on Eshelby’s equivalent
inclusion theory, which they used to explain the difference in
low cycle fatigue life for cubic, rafted and needle-type microstruc-
tures. In all these approaches, the microstructure evolution was
not incorporated in the models and hence, a change in morphology
required the creation of a new model. Consequently, only up to
three different microstructures have been analyzed.
In the present paper, two original contributions are forwarded.
Firstly, formulations will be proposed for the kinetics of the
degradation process for a general load condition (not limited to
the commonly treated uniaxial stress state). Secondly, the physical

2. Kinetics of microstructure degradation
The microstructure degradation is governed by three mecha-
nisms: rafting, ripening and coarsening. The relations proposed
by Epishin et al. [12] describe the evolution of the width of the ma-
trix channels normal to the applied load (horizontal channels) in a
uniaxially loaded specimen during the rafting stage. In the present
section, relations are derived to additionally calculate the evolu-
tion of the channel widths in the matrix channels parallel to the
applied load (vertical channels) and of the precipitate dimensions.
Further, the relations will be extended to make them applicable to
a general multiaxial stress state. Also, a formulation for the kinetics
of the ripening and coarsening processes is proposed and it is dem-
onstrated that rafting and ripening occur simultaneously.

3. Effect on the deformation
The changes in matrix channel widths and precipitate dimen-
sions have several effects on the mechanical response of the
material:
/C15 The Orowan threshold
sor is inversely proportional to the chan-

increased matrix slip rate [19].
/C15 The precipitate shearing process becomes easier, since the
coherency between the matrix and precipitate phases reduces
during rafting. Misﬁt dislocations are formed at the
c/c0-inter-

through the coherency loss, which also affects the mechanical

results in a zigzag shape of the rafts. This provides a number
of {1 1 1} oriented interfaces, which also enhance precipitate

/C15 The recovery climb mechanism will experience more resistance,
since the climbing distance increases when the precipitates
become elongated. From the moment that the microstructure
is completely rafted (large numbers of precipitates connected),
the climbing distance is increased signiﬁcantly [38,22].
The ﬁrst two mechanisms yield an increased slip rate in rafted
material at conditions where matrix slip and/or precipitate shear-

stress levels). On the other hand, at creep conditions where the
climb mechanism dominates the deformation rate (low stresses

capacity of the material (reduces the slip rate).

growth occurs preferably along the extended

5.1. Tensile response
To demonstrate the ability of the model to predict the effect of
microstructure degradation on the material tensile response, ten-
sile tests are simulated with both virgin and degraded microstruc-
tures. The effect of microstructure degradation can be incorporated
in the simulations in two ways: modifying the geometry by chang-
ing the values of L and h instantaneously or, more sophisticated, by
simulating a complete degradation test prior to the tensile test
simulation until the required channel width is reached. In the lat-
ter case all internal variables, the plastic strain and the dislocation
densities (SSD and GND), evolve during the degradation period. At
the start of the tensile test, these internal variables will therefore
take different values in these approaches. However, typical degra-
dation conditions (e.g. creep for 2500 h at 1050 /C176C and 150 MPa)
only yield small plastic strains in the order of 0.1–0.5%. Moreover,
the values of the SSD densities do not change signiﬁcantly and the
internal back stresses caused by the GNDs are also relatively small.
Therefore, for short degradation periods, a tensile test simulation
on degraded material can be approximated quite well by perform-
ing a simulation with incorporation of an updated geometry only.

rated in a simulation in advance to obtain the proper values of
the internal variables at the start of the tensile test. The difference

Finally, the practical relevance of the presented model is out-
lined. Under normal operating conditions, the microstructure of
gas turbine parts quickly degrades. Typically, the hot regions of
the components are fully rafted within only a few percent of their
total service life time, which means that the structural integrity is
largely determined by the mechanical properties of the degraded
material. Nevertheless, experiments on virgin material are per-
formed to quantify the mechanical response of the alloy. Moreover,
due to the limited duration of tensile tests and low cycle fatigue
tests, no signiﬁcant degradation occurs during these experiments.
However, the resulting data is used to assess the life time of the
parts in the design process. Obviously, this leads to a discrepancy
between the calculated life times and the life times observed in
practice. The present model assists in solving this discrepancy,
since it enables the quantiﬁcation of the effects of degradation.
The experimental results on the virgin material can be used to cal-
ibrate the constitutive model and the method presented in this pa-
per then enables the incorporation of degradation effects.
6. Summary and conclusions
In the present paper, the kinetics of the microstructure degrada-
tion in Ni-base superalloys has been discussed, as well as the effect
of this degradation on the material mechanical response and dam-
age accumulation. The following original contributions are thereby
achieved:
/C15 a new phenomenological formulation is proposed to describe
the kinetics of rafting for a general load condition (not limited
to the commonly treated uniaxial stress case);
/C15 a formulation for the coarsening process, acting simultaneously
with the rafting process, is incorporated in the kinetics

Although the kinetics of degradation has been studied exten-
sively and the mechanisms are now rather well understood, the
amount of published experimental data on the mechanical proper-
ties of degraded microstructures is remarkably limited. Moreover,
the mechanisms that are responsible for the observed mechanical
effects are not fully clear yet. More experimental work in this ﬁeld
will enable the further development of the numerical methods, to
which this paper contributes.
