Abstract 10 
Strength-ductility synergy of the Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi high-entropy alloy can be tuned largely by cold-rolling 11 
followed by annealing treatments. However, strain-h ardening behaviors and the underlying deformation 12 
mechanisms of the low- to high-temperatures-anneale d microstructures have not been well clarified. In this 13 
paper, the Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi high-entropy alloy was cold-rolled, follow ed by annealing at 673–1,273 K to 14 
investigate the effect of annealing temperatures on the microstructure and deformation behavior. Annea ling 15 
at 673, 873, 1,073, and 1,273 K resulted in no recrystallization, partial recrystallization, full recrystallization, 16 
and grain-coarsening, which yielded a stretched-gra ined, heterostructured, fine-recrystallized, and 17 
coarse-grained microstructure, respectively. The heterostructured microstructure was comprised of stretched, 18 
partially recrystallized, and fine recrystallized g rains. Uniaxial tension results show that the 873 K -annealed 19 
specimens possessed a good combination of strength (a yield strength of ~746 MPa) and ductility (a uniform 20 
elongation of ~28%), compared with the high-strengt h-yet-low-ductility cold-rolled and 673 K-annealed 21 
specimens, and the high-ductility-yet-low-strength 1,073 and 1,273 K-annealed specimens. The relations hip 22 
between the yield strength and average grain sizes of the Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi high-entropy alloy follows the 23 
Hall–Petch equation with a friction stress of ~137 MPa, and a high Hall–Petch coefficient of ~664 24 
MPa· µm1/2 . Five and three different regimes existed in the s train-hardening rate versus true strain curves for 25 
the 873, and 1,073 and 1,273 K-annealed samples, re spectively. The occurrence of the second and fourth 26 
regimes, and the second regime were detected essent ially caused by nanotwinning. The correlation betwe en 27 
microstructure, tension properties, deformation mechanism, and strain-hardening behavior is discussed. 28

1. Introduction 2 
Considerable interest has focused recently on multi -principal-elements alloying [1, 2] , which is a novel 3 
alloy-design paradigm in the field of materials sci ence. The research motivation is to develop new all oys 4 
with solid-solution structures for a high configura tional entropy [1-4] . The newly developed 5 
multi-principal-elements alloys, which are often re ferred to as high-entropy alloys (HEAs) in the lite rature, 6 
have a common definition as an alloy with at least five major elements with individual atomic 7 
concentrations between 5% and 35% [1] . Single-phase solid-solutions with a face-centered cubic (fcc) [5-7] , 8 
body-centered cubic (bcc) [8-10] , or hexagonal close-packed (hcp) [11, 12] structure rather than intermetallic 9 
compounds are formed in HEAs, and these materials h ave shown excellent performances in various service 10 
applications owing to the unique dislocation and twinning mechanisms of their solid-solution structures. Fcc 11 
single-phase Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA, as a typical and one of the most widely studied HEAs, shows outstanding 12 
mechanical performances, such as a high ductility [13] , a high strength [14] , a high impact toughness at ambient 13 
and liquid-nitrogen temperatures [13] , a high ballistic impact resistance [15] , and an good resistance to ion 14 
irradiation [16, 17] and corrosion [18] . 15 
Previous studies on the mechanical behavior of Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEAs focused mainly on the effect of 16 
alloying elements on the microstructure and propert ies [19, 20] , or to report abnormal deformation 17 
behaviors [21-23] and to disclose underlying new mechanisms [24-29] . Limited studies have focused on the effects 18 
of processing procedures. Yu et al. [30] conducted a high-pressure torsion treatment on Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA 19 
disks to refine the grain sizes from several millim eters to ~80 nanometers. In their study, the microh ardness 20 
was increased from ~135 Hv initially to ~482 Hv aft er torsion. Chen et al. [31] introduced a gradient 21 
hierarchical structure with a grain size from ~130 µm at the center, to ~10 µm at the edge of the 22 
Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA bars by using a cyclic dynamic torsion method. The gradient-hierarchical-structured 23 
sample had a superior strength–ductility synergy of a yield strength of ~510 MPa, and a uniform elonga tion 24 
of ~19% compared with the annealed counterpart. 25 
Processing Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA by rolling followed by annealing can t une the microstructure to 26 
enhance the strength–ductility synergy. The rolling-followed-by-annealing method is simpler and easier than 27 
those of high-pressure torsion or cycle dynamic tor sion, thus, it can be used in more laboratory 28 
investigations and real industrial applications. Ou r previous work [32] introduced a heterostructured 29 
microstructure that was composed of three grain typ es with different length scales and a dislocation-d ensity 30 
heterogeneity by cold-rolling, followed by intermed iate-temperature-annealing at 873 K. The 31 
heterostructured-HEA samples had an outperformed st rength–ductility combination of a yield strength of 32

2. Materials and Methods 17 
Master alloys with a nominal composition of Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi (Al 2.4 Cr 24.4 Fe 24.4 Co 24.4 Ni 24.4 , at.%) were 18

Ti-gettered high-purity argon atmosphere. The maste r ingots were flipped and remelted at least four ti mes 20 
and then drop-cast into a water-cooled Cu mold to y ield flats with a length of ~85 mm, a width of ~10 mm, 21

per step) at a rolling speed of ~3.3 mm/s to reduce the thickness from ~2 mm to ~0.5 mm. The cold-roll ed 23

temperature on the microstructure and deformation behavior. 25

3.1 Initial microstructural evolution with increasing annealing temperature 15 
Figure 1 shows the XRD curves of the cold-rolled an d annealed Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA samples. All 16 
curves reveal an fcc structure. The high intensity of the (111) peak relative to others indicates the existence 17 
of a preferred orientation of cold-rolled sample th at was introduced by cold-rolling. Such a preferred 18 
orientation remains almost unchanged after annealin g at 673 K, decays with an increase in annealing 19 
temperature to 873 K, and disappears with annealing at 1,073 and 1,273 K. The microstructural evolutio n 20 
with annealing temperature is shown in Fig. 2. For the cold-rolled sample, the microstructure comprise s 21 
stretched grains along the rolling direction with a n average width (space between the two boundaries o f the 22

4.2 Strain-hardening behavior 9 
Annealing treatment at 673 K did not change the pre vious cold-rolled microstructure that was 10 
composed of stretched grains (Figs. 2a–b). The 673 K-annealed sample, with a higher yield strength 11 
(increment of ~163 ± 58 MPa) than that of the cold- rolled sample (Fig. 7b), shows that the hardening w as 12 
induced by annealing at 673 K, akin to those of oth er cold-rolled fcc HEAs [43, 44] that were subjected to a 13 
low-temperature annealing treatment. Such a hardeni ng is neither due to the thermal stress induced by the 14 
air-quenching out from furnace which was reported to be small as ~20 MPa for the air-quenched low-carbon 15

5. Conclusions 15 
The microstructural evolution, tension property, deformation mechanism, and strain-hardening behavior 16 
of the cold-rolled Al 0.1 CrFrCoNi HEA with an annealing temperature range of 673–1,273 K were 17 
investigated. Based on our studies, the following conclusions can be drawn: 18 
1) The cold-rolled sample showed no recrystallization, full-recrystallization, and grain-coarsening with 19 
annealing temperatures of 673, 1,073, and 1,273 K, respectively, which provides stretched, fine 20 
recrystallized, and coarse recrystallized microstru ctures with average grain sizes of ~20, ~8, and ~88 21 
µm, respectively. Annealing at 873 K causes a partia l recrystallization to form heterostructured 22 
microstructures that comprise stretched, partially- recrystallized, and recrystallized grains with volu me 23 
fractions and sizes of ~10% and ~20 µm, ~37% and ~1 to ~20 µm, and ~53% and ~0.2 to ~5 µm, 24 
respectively. 25

microstructures of the Al 0.1 CrFeCoNi HEA follows the classical Hall–Petch behav ior with a lattice 27

Petch coefficient of ~664 MPa· µm1/2 was higher than that of most fcc metals and alloys , except for 29 
VCoNi and CrFeCoNi alloys. 30
