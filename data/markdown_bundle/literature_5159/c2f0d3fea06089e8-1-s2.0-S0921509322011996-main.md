1. Introduction 
Since high entropy alloys (HEAs) were first proposed by Yeh et al. [ 1 ] 
and Cantor et al. [ 2 ] in 2004, they have attracted widespread attention 
due to their excellent properties such as good mechanical properties [ 3 ], 
good resistance to wear [ 4 ] and corrosion [ 5 ]. HEAs are initially defined 
as being composed of five or more elements, each concentration be -
tween 5 at% and 35 at% [ 6 ], and they tend to form a single-phase solid 
solution due to high-entropy effect [ 1 ]. However, it is challenging to 
achieve high strength-ductility synergy in single-phase HEAs. More 
specifically, single-phase face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs are usually 
ductile but lack sufficient strengths [ 7 ], whereas single-phase body- -
centered cubic (BCC) HEAs typically exhibit high strengths but are often 
brittle [ 8 ]. In order to circumvent the strength-ductility trade-off, 
dual-phase and multi-phase HEAs are increasingly becoming a hot spot 
in alloy design [ 9 , 10 ]. Among them, eutectic high entropy alloys 
(EHEAs) have shown great research and application value since first 
proposed in 2014 [ 11 ]. EHEAs provide an opportunity to circumvent the 
strength-ductility trade-off of single-phase HEAs owing to the coupling 
effect of in-situ composite structures. So far, many high-performance 
EHEA systems have been proposed and developed, such as Ni

Printing parameters play an important role in the microstructure of 
the as-printed Ni
2.1 
sample, a fully nano-lamella structure can be ob -
tained under optimal parameters. The cellular structure could be formed 
for the Ni2.1 EHEA in laser additive manufacturing, even though the 
Ni2.1 EHEA commonly exhibits a typical lamellar structure [ 11 , 16 , 34 ]. 
Guo et al. [ 35 ] studied the effects of the printing parameters on the 
formation of eutectic structure, and they found that the microstructure 
underwent a morphological evolution from lamella to cellular when the 
printing parameters were changed. Solidification mode of the eutectic 
structure in SLM process can be determined by the combination of 
chemical composition and two solidification parameters such as tem -
perature gradient (G) of the liquid and solidification rate (R). The two 
solidification parameters (G and R) can be adjusted by varying the 
printing parameters. In the case of a very high G, the constitutional 
supercooling cannot be formed ahead of the solid-liquid interface 
resulting in a planar solid-liquid interface. When G becomes lower and R 
is higher, small grains form at the solidification interface and continue to 
grow into the supercooled liquid phase due to the unstable condition of 
the planar crystal interface. Hence, the temperature gradient and solute 
concentration gradient in the liquid phase will increase, resulting in 
constitutional overcooling, destroying the growth of the flat interface 
and forming cellular crystals or cellular dendrites. Besides, the forma -
tion of the cellular structure is also related to the compositional fluc -
tuation or the kinetic conditions of the liquid phase in melt pool. Fig. 2 
(b – c) and (e-f) show a typical lamella structure of the SLM-ed Ni
2.1 
sample at higher magnification, where the coarse lamella is FCC phase 
and the fine lamella is B2 phase (will be identified by TEM in the 
following section). During the solidification process of the SLM-ed Ni
2.1 
sample, one of the phases is formed first from the liquid, and its phase 
interface can act as a nucleating site for another phase. Therefore, a 
lamella morphology can be obtained by symbiotic growth of the two 
phases. In addition, SLM has a rapid cooling rate compared to other 
traditional processing methods such as casting, the two phases have 
little time to grow, so that the lamella spacing (~211 nm) of the SLM-ed 
Ni
2.1 
sample in this study is much finer than that of the as-casted Ni
2.1 
EHEA (the eutectic spacing ~3.5 μ m) [ 36 ]. Moreover, the eutectic 
structure in Fig. 2 (e and f) grows towards the inside of the melt pool in a 
direction perpendicular to the melt pool profile. This is because the 
growth direction of the eutectic structure within a melt pool tends to 
align with the maximum heat flux direction during the printing process.

amount of nano-precipitates in the two phases are strong barriers for 
dislocations movement, resulting in a strong precipitation strengthening 
effect, which further leads to the increment in strength of the alloy.
