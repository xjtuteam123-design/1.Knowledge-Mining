1. Introduction 
Ni
3
Al-type high-temperature alloys, a type of intermetallic com -
pounds, exhibit different comprehensive properties based on the addi -
tion of different alloying elements and the control of composition 
content. Notably, they exhibit high melting points, excellent resistance 
to high-temperature oxidation and corrosion, elevated high-temperature 
strength and resistance to creep, as well as superior specific strength. 
Furthermore, below the peak temperature, they can exhibit a positive 
temperature effect on yield strength [ 1 , 2 ]. Due to these exceptional 
characteristics, Ni
3
Al-type high-temperature alloys have garnered sig -
nificant attention as structural materials for high-temperature 
applications. 
However, the early research on intermetallic compounds encoun -
tered various issues such as poor plasticity, brittleness, and insufficient 
intermediate temperature strength, which have limited their practical 
application and widespread adoption. Aoki and Izumi [ 3 ] effectively 
improved the room temperature brittleness of Ni
3

high-temperature alloys by incorporating 0.1% grain boundary 
strengthening element boron (B) into the matrix. The research con -
ducted by Liu et al. [ 4 ] demonstrated that the segregation of element B 
at grain boundaries effectively suppressed intergranular fracture in the 
alloy. Building upon previous studies, Yang et al. [ 5 ] successfully ach -

Fig. 3 c is local magnification images of Fig. 3 b, revealing the pres -
ence of two distinct nano-sized γ ′ phase morphologies within the inter -
dendritic γ + γ ′ region: elongated and cuboidal shapes. The varying γ ′ 
phase morphologies can be explained by the system ’ s overall free en -
ergy, which includes elastic and interfacial energy and always is mini -
mized. During the printing process, where laser scanning occurs 
repeatedly, resembling long-term aging, the γ ′ phase tends to coarsen in 
order to reduce interfacial free energy and satisfy the requirements of 
the energetically favorable configurations. This process is also known as 
Ostwald ripening [ 11 ]. The coarsening of the γ ′ phase leads to the 
transformation of the cuboidal microstructure into the elongated 
microstructure. This transformation may be attributed to the merging of 
adjacent γ ′ phases during the growth process. The coarsened γ ′ phase 
typically aligns along the lowest Young ’ s modulus direction to minimize 
the energy associated with lattice-coherent strains [ 12 ]. 
Chai et al. [ 13 ] explained the solidification mechanism of Ni
3
Al- -
based IC21 alloy by utilizing a pseudo-binary phase diagram with 
varying Al content. It has been found that the L + γ → γ ′ peritectic re -
action occurs in the inter-dendritic region when liquid composition 
reaches peritectic point. Mathieu Terner et al. [ 14 ] investigated the 
solidification path between the dendrites of the nickel-based high- -
temperature alloy CMSX-10 and demonstrated that the nucleation and 
growth of the γ ′ phase follow the encapsulation reaction L + γ → γ ′ . 
Based on the previous reports and combined with the analysis of the 
physical phases as well as microstructures, the microstructural evolution 
of the present alloy during laser deposition can be preliminarily 
deduced, as shown in Fig. 3 d γ dendrites emerge from the liquid phase as 
the temperature drops, and the B accumulates in the residual liquid 
phase. The L + γ → γ ′ peritectic reaction takes place when the liquid 
phase composition reaches the solvus point, forming a γ ′ envelope 
[ 13 – 15 ]. At this time, the B element content exceeds a certain threshold. 
TiB

due to cyclic thermal effects and rapid quenching, coarsening and 
merging of the interdendritic γ ′ phase occur [ 11 , 12 ], and cubic γ ′ phase 
precipitates inside the γ dendrites.

973 K. However, the tensile strength decreases rapidly with increasing 
temperature after 973 K. The elongation also decreases at 973 K, 
consistent with the “ intermediate temperature brittleness ” of Ni

alloys [ 23 ]. There are multiple explanations for this phenomenon, with 
one attributing it to the emergence of a new deformation mechanism 
[ 24 ]. The primary deformation mechanism at intermediate-low tem -
peratures is dislocation glide induced by γ ′ phase precipitation. At high 
temperatures, the primary deformation mechanism is Orowan bypass 
[ 25 ]. At intermediate temperatures, the operation of multiple slip sys -
tems results in dislocation transfer and recombination, creating addi -
tional obstacles for cross-slip. Additionally, the presence of numerous 
shear bands and deformed twins can also serve as barriers to cross-slip, 
leading to highly heterogeneous deformation and the simultaneous 
attainment of maximum local strains [ 25 ]. Another explanation is the 
oxygen absorption at grain boundaries, which reduces the interfacial 
bonding strength. Beyond 973 K, the elongation significantly increases 
with temperature. This can be attributed to the fact that the γ ′ phase is 
the dominant phase on a volumetric basis and appears to govern the 
mechanical properties of the Ni

Fig. 6 d summarizes the yield strength as a function of temperature 
for the current alloy, typical HEAs, high-entropy superalloys, superalloy 
steels, and Co-, Fe-, and Ni-based superalloys [ 11 , 28 – 31 ]. Fig. 6 d 
illustrates that, in contrast to CoCrFeMnNi HEAs [ 28 ], which show a 
constant drop in strength over the whole temperature range, the alloy ’ s 
yield strength rises with increasing temperature prior to 973 K. This 
anomalous phenomenon is related to the decomposition structure of 
< 110 > superlattice antiphase boundaries (APBs) [ 32 ]. The APB energy 
generated by {111} planes in Ni
3

planes, creating an energy difference that acts as a driving force, causing 
partial dislocations to glide across {111} planes towards {001} planes, 
leading to the formation of non-planar dislocation structures (K – W 
locks) and a decrease in dislocation mobility [ 23 , 32 ]. As the tempera -
ture increases, cross-slip becomes more favorable, resulting in an 
increased occurrence of cross-slip. At 1173 K, the yield strength is 
significantly reduced to 593 MPa. The anomalous increase in yield 
strength disappears possibly due to the deformation temperature 
exceeding the peak temperature, resulting in a transition from planar 
slip to cube slip [ 28 ].
