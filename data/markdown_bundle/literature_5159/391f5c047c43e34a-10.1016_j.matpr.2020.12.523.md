abstract
This review paper examined the mechanical, microstructural, corrosion properties, high-temperature
applications (HTAs) and protective measures that have been applied for optimal performance of superal-
loys. The quest for materials with excellent characteristics necessitated the development of superalloys
over the years. Superalloys are class of nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co) and iron (Fe) based alloys used in jet and
marine turbine engines due to their outstanding dimensional stability at a much higher temperature
compared to most structural and high-temperature materials. Superalloys can use a high fraction of their
melting point, and this positioned them in the category of high-temperature application materials. They
are also reported to exhibit reasonable corrosion resistance and good mechanical properties even at ele-
vated temperature, which facilitates their suitability for high-stress manufacturing and advance applica-
tions, such as in the production of turbine engines for the marine and aerospace industries. The surface of
superalloys can be protected using diffusion, overlaying and thermal barrier coatings (TBCs), which act as
a barrier against oxidation, corrosion, depletion in microstructure and thermal deformation.

1. Introduction
Superalloys have been widely used for varieties of industrial
applications [1,2]. They are also referred to as high-performance
alloy due to their outstanding mechanical strength, high corrosion
resistance, good stability of surfaces, and higher resistance to ther-
mal creep deformation compared to most alloys [3,4]. The capabil-
ity of superalloy to be used at a high ratio of its melting point
makes it one of the highly exploited materials especially for HTAs
[5,6]. Superalloys are produced majorly via both chemical and pro-
cedural innovations and are known to exhibit crystal structure that
is typical of face-centred cubic (FCC) austenitic [7,8]. They are usu-
ally cast as a single crystal. Although grain boundaries may offer
some degree of strength at low temperature, however, they reduce
creep resistance. Superalloys high-temperature strength are
developed through age hardening and solid solution reinforcement
from precipitates of secondary phase like gamma prime ( c’) and
carbides [9,10]. They were reported to have been primarily used
in marine turbine engines and aerospace applications since creep
is majorly the lifespan restraining issue in gas turbine blades
[11]. Some other areas of applications are in chemical processing
industries and coal conversion plants. Since these alloys are devel-
oped for HTAs, retaining their shapes at temperatures closer to
their melting is a major experimental design consideration. There-
fore, their oxidation resistance and creep are of immense signiﬁ-
cance [12]. This had resulted in the emergence of Ni based
superalloys (NBSs) as a choice material for these applications
because of their distinctive c’ precipitate [13,14].
A notable attribute of nickel (Ni) based superalloys is in their
applications for load-bearing at temperatures in surplus of 80% of
their initial melting temperature, a percentage that is relatively
higher compared to the use of any other class of engineering alloys
for such application [15]. The characteristics of NBSs can be
modiﬁed to a certain degree by adding other elements of metals,
non-metals and metalloids such as aluminium (Al), tungsten (W),
molybdenum (Mo), vanadium (V), carbon (C), zirconium (Zr),

The processing of superalloys begins with the production of
large ingots via vacuum induction melting (VIM): a standard melt-
ing practice through which superalloys are prepared in bulk. The
charging of raw metallic materials which could include scraps into
a refractory crucible is done ensuring that the crucible is kept
under vacuum during charge melting. However, the scraps could
contain some undesired element but could be removed in the
reﬁning process. Characteristically, more than thirty elements are
reﬁned or separated from the liquefy superalloy during vacuum
induction melting processing. The concluding stage involves
decanting of the liqueﬁed metal from the crucible to a pouring
device, and casting of it into moulds is done under partial argon
pressure. The large ingots produced are eventually used for one
of these three main processing routes: re-melting and successive
investment casting, re-melting followed by processing of wrought
or formation of superalloy powder from re-melting of superalloy
ingots which is further consolidated and exposed to wrought pro-
cessing operations. Although some alloys can be melted potentially
in slag or air environments utilizing electric arc furnaces, however,
vacuum induction melting of superalloys is more efﬁcient in the
elimination of trace contaminants with low melting point
[20,21]. Considering the demanding requirements for the reduc-
tion of defects in most industries like the gas turbine industries
good understanding of the processing procedure of superalloys
and further research on how to improve vacuum induction melting
is required. More so, several applications require secondary melt-
ing process (electro-slag/vacuum arc re-melting) to reduce inclu-
sions level and to improve the chemical homogeneity of alloy
material [22]. This paper will consequently provide more insights
into the processing, characteristics and engineering applications
of the three major categories superalloys.
2. Categorization and development of superalloys
Superalloys are categorized into three, based on the major or
principal metal present in the alloy. They are Ni based superalloys
(NBSs), Fe based superalloys (FBSs) and Co based superalloys
(CBSs) [23–25].
2.1. Ni based superalloys (NBSs)
The NBSs are good corrosion-resistant, high strength, long fati-
gue life and high-temperature alloys mostly used at operational
temperatures higher than 550 /C176C. They often contain some alloying
elements like carbon or boron and weighty refractory elements like
tungsten and tantalum [26]. They are the most intricate of three
superalloys, the extensively used for the hottest sections, and, for
numerous researchers, the most interesting of all the superalloys.
In the 2nd and 3rd decade of the 20th century, austenitic stainless
steels of gamma (
c) phase were produced for gas turbines operat-
ing at high temperatures [27]. In the late 1920 s, 80% Ni-20% Cr
alloy was the standard, with some inclusions of Al and Ti, even
though the early metallurgists were not aware they were
producing small gamma prime ( c’) precipitate in Ni-based superal-
loys. Ni-based alloys represent 45 to 50% of the entire materials
required for the production of aircraft engines as a result of their
exceptional strength and oxidation resistance at high tempera-
tures. They were ﬁrst utilized for the fabrication of the blades of
turbines in the early 1940 s where the creep rupture strength
was enhanced remarkably through the inclusion of small amounts
of Al and Ti [28]. Application of these alloys rapidly surpassed
those of the cobalt and iron-based superalloys, which were rein-
forced by solid solution strengthening and carbides [29,30].
Although chromium was a great defence against the corrosion
and oxidation of the alloys up to 700 /C176C. Subsequently, researchers
began to reduce the percentage weight of chromium in favour of
aluminium. However, corrosion resistance at a much higher tem-
perature was reduced, and this prompted the development of coat-
ings to combat hot corrosion [31].
Presently, above 50% of the sophisticated aircraft engines
weight consists of superalloys [32]. The fundamental properties
of NBSs are the high phase stability of the FCC Ni matrix. It can also
be strengthened through several methods, and with either, alu-
minium or chromium, the surface stability is enhanced [33]. Creep
resistance is partially dependent on the rate of dislocation motion,
therefore, in the present-day NBSs,
c’-Ni3 (Al, Ti) stage operates as
an obstacle to dislocation motion, and as a result of this, the
intermetallic c’ phase, when it is abundantly in large percentage
volume, hugely increases the alloys strength because of its well-
ordered personality and high coherency with the c matrix (phase
composing of the matrix of Ni-based superalloy) [34]. The
inclusion of elements such as titanium and aluminium enhance
the creation of c’ phase, whose size can be speciﬁcally controlled
via careful age-hardening heat treatment [35]. Oxidation resis-
tance is increased through the chemical addition of aluminium,
chromium and boron. The chromium and aluminium form oxide
layer on the alloy, passivate and protect it from further oxidation,
while yttrium and boron enhance the bonding of the oxide scale on
the alloy [36,37]. Niobium and molybdenum are added to over-
come cracking problems during welding due to a crack healing
effect [38,39]. Zirconium and boron tend to separate off the grain
boundaries, reduces the energy of grain boundary and conse-
quently results in better cohesion of grain boundary and improved
ductility [40,41]. Addition of carbon and carbide former such as
tungsten, chromium, niobium, molybdenum, hafnium, tantalum
or titanium also strengthened the grain boundary. The precipitate
of carbides is driven at the grain boundaries and thereafter
decreases grain boundary sliding and increasing microstructural
stability [42,43].

2.2. Cobalt (Co) based superalloys (CBSs)
Despite the proven usefulness of nickel-based superalloy mate-
rials for signiﬁcantly high-temperature applications, CBSs poten-
tially exhibit higher hot corrosion, superior oxidation and better
resistance to wear compared to NBSs [46]. Although, for these sta-
ted reasons, efforts have been made in the development of CBSs by
researchers, and despite the effort, the conventional CBSs have not
been used extensively because they possess lesser strength at ele-
vated temperature, compared to NBSs [47,48]. The gamma prime
(c’) precipitation strengthening, which is responsible for high-
temperature strength appeared to be lacking in cobalt-based
superalloys until recently. It was reported that cobalt-based super-
alloys exhibit metastable c’-Co3 (Al, W) compound with ordered
FCC arrangement, which suggests CBSs as a substitute for NBSs
[49]. As a result of the limitations of NBSs at ultrahigh tempera-
tures, cobalt-based superalloys are employed for any application
in a combustion chamber, where the temperature of about
1100 /C176C could be attained [50].
Although CBSs are not as tough as NBSs, however, they can hold
on to their strength up to higher temperature. The cobalt-based
superalloys draw their strength majorly from the spread of refrac-
tory metal carbides which tends to gather at the grain boundaries,
thereby strengthening the grain boundaries and making the alloy
becoming stable near their melting point. A recent study has
revealed that cobalt-based superalloys exhibit c/c’ microstructure,
where the c’ phase was Co 3 (Al, W) and partition to Ta, V, Ti, Mo
and Ni, while Mn, Cr and Fe partition to c matrix [51]. More so,
cobalt-based superalloys similar to c/c’ microstructure was
discovered to be W free and has c’ phase of c’-Co3 (Al, Nb, Mo).

3. Processing, oxidation and protection of superalloys
3.1. Processing of superalloys
Generally, processing of superalloys involves VIM, investment
casting, secondary melting, conversion (cogging and homogeniza-
tion), powder metallurgy (PM), spray forming/casting, deformation
processing, heat treatment and coating. The raw metallic materials
are melted using the VIM. The vacuum induction melting process-
ing also allows reﬁning and removal of unwanted elements from
the molten metal, followed by ﬁnal decanting of the melt and cast-
ing into a mould. The investment casting, also known as the lost-
wax process is used for the fabrication of complex-shaped superal-
loy components such as turbine blades. The casting’s wax model is
prepared through injection molten wax into a master mould made
of metal, which is arranged in clusters and connected via wax
replicas of risers and runners to enable the production of many
blades in a single casting. The mould is eventually baked to
improve its strength. Then molten metal is then poured into an
already preheated and degreased mould under vacuum. After
solidiﬁcation of the metal, the investment casing is removed, and
the interior ceramic hub leached out through chemical approaches,
using autoclave of high pressure [66–72].
A secondary melting process is required for several applications,
to improve the homogeneity of chemical in the alloy and to reduce
the inclusions level. After the VIM processing, the cast ingot usu-
ally exhibits sizeable solidiﬁcation duct and considerable segrega-
tion. Eliminating the duct lowers productivity. Fissuring and
cracking could take place in subsequent mechanical and thermal
working. Besides, VIM can retain some non-metallic inclusions in
the alloy, which could be detrimental to the fatigue characteristics
of the material. The problems related to these effects can be
reduced through the application of the secondary melting: using
vacuum arc re-melting or electro-slag re-melting. Nevertheless,
the product of superalloy ingots obtained from the re-melting pro-
cess is not ﬁt for mechanical applications. Therefore, they must go
through thermal–mechanical working to breakdown the structure
of as-cast, and to minimize grain size to permissible levels. This
process is referred to as ingot conversion; during which cylindrical
ingot’s diameter is reduced to approximately half its original size.
Generally, the conversion process is accomplished in a series of
phases, or heats. The superalloy ingot is positioned in a furnace
and then cogged or forged upon removal from the furnace. Primar-
ily the ingot deforms many times along its length at various points.
The applied deformation causes signiﬁcant recrystallization to a
structure of ﬁner grain, and the entire process is repeated after
re-heating [73–78].
Powder metallurgy is usually required for the production of
superalloy component like the turbine discs which demands high
integrity. Although the processing technique is expensive, how-
ever, products can be heavily alloyed, with good homogeneity of
the constituents. PM is majorly used for alloys that will be used
in critical fatigue applications. The production of powder metal-
lurgy product begins with the atomization of inert gas from master
molten metal produced through VIM processing. The powder is
usually consolidated via processing by extrusion. The powder is
initially packed in a cleaned container made of steel, and it is evac-
uated to pave way for out-gassing, sealed and afterwards com-
pressed either by closed die forging or hot Isostatic pressing.
Isothermal forging, which involves deformation of the billet at
the same temperature as the dies, with the two being held in a fur-
nace, is the ideal route through which the powder metallurgy
superalloy is produced after powder consolidation [79–83].
Spray forming/casting process starts with a VIM, where the
melt is transported to a tundish and then metered via a regulated
nozzle or oriﬁce. The alloy stream metered is atomized to extre-
mely ﬁne droplets by argon gas impingement of high purity. When
billets are being produced, pre-heated carbon steel mandrel is
spray-deposited, which revolves beneath the spray. Spray-shaped
rings can also be formed by spraying on to a revolving mandrel.
The spray formed or cast alloy may then be subjected to deforma-
tion processing. Deformation processing is carried out to modify
the cross-section, and to reﬁne the microstructure of wrought
superalloys, typically through ring rolling, forging and forging,
which are the major deformation processes. After deformation pro-
cessing of superalloy, it can be subjected to the major heat treat-
ments, which includes solution treatment, in-processing
annealing, precipitation hardening and full annealing. The in-
processing annealing may be employed to relieve stress after a sev-
ere forming operation, while full annealing helps to obtain com-
plete recrystallization and improved structural ductility. Solution
treatment dissolves secondary phases to make extra solute avail-
able for precipitation hardening, while precipitation hardening,
also known as age hardening drives out the strengthening phases,
control carbides and the topological close-packed phases [84–88].
Ultimately, superalloys are protected via coating since they are
susceptible to oxidation and hot corrosion. Three major types of
protective coatings have been developed, which are diffusion (alu-
minide), overlay and TBCs. These coatings are reported to have pro-
vided reasonable protection against degradation of superalloys
used in several industrial applications [89].

sequential oxidation. Most often, elements like chromium and alu-
minium are used for this purpose due to their capability to form
protective thin oxide layers such as chromium (lll) oxide and alu-
minium oxide, respectively. Besides, these layers exhibit low oxy-
gen diffusivities, which can successfully stop further oxidation on
the alloy surface [92]. However, these protective oxide layers can
be damaged via several mechanisms. The continuous protective
performance of these layers can be shattered by mechanical inter-
ference or build-up of stresses. The stability of these oxide layers is
largely inﬂuenced by some minority elements such as silicon,
yttrium and boron, which enhances the adhesion of the oxide lay-
ers, reducing ﬂake formation and sustaining the reliability of the
thin protective layers [93]. Chemical deterioration which results
from oxidation is the most basic degradation that could be experi-
enced by superalloys. Although, the corrosion process could
become more complex when the alloys are been used in saline or
acidic environments, and as such, it becomes necessary to address
the challenges of oxidation and corrosion by thin surface coatings
[94].
3.3. Signiﬁcance of thin surface coating on superalloys
Coatings have been applied on superalloys using several tech-
niques. Some of these methods are physical vapour deposition,
thermal spraying and chemical vapour deposition. These coating
methods are effective for different coating applications. Coatings
are developed on the surfaces of metals for several protection pur-
poses [95]. Coatings could be designed to prevent surface contact
of alloys with ﬂuid or to act as a barrier against extremely high
temperature, which is commonly referred to as thermal barrier
coating (TBC) [96]. Other forms of coating used for the oxidation
and corrosion protection of superalloys are the diffusion coatings,
which has platinum-aluminide or aluminide as the major con-
stituent, and overlay coatings such as Ni-Cr-Si-Al, Co-Cr-Hf-Al
and Co-Ni-Al-Cr-Y coatings [97]. Coatings can also be made of a
single or multi-layer depending on the service condition of the
material to be protected [98,99]. In the modern-day gas turbine,
very high service temperature is required; therefore, with the help
of surface engineering, thermal barrier coatings of about
300
lm thick can essentially provide improvement in working
temperature and preserve components life span. The function of
a TBC system is to reduce the temperature at the superalloy surface
through the help of its constituent. A typical TBC system is made
up of:
/C15A bond coat, which adheres TBC to the superalloy surface. It also
protects against oxidation and operates as a barrier in opposi-
tion to the diffusion of atoms in the superalloys to the environ-
ment. Ni-Cr, Co-Cermets and aluminides are some of the
common types of bond coats.
/C15A thermally grown oxide (TGO) formed through the oxidation of
chromium or aluminium in the bond coat.
/C15A thermally insulated ceramic topcoat with thickness ranging
from 100 to 300 lm. It provides insulation layer for the TBC sys-
tem due to their low thermal conductivity (LTC). A well-known
example of topcoat is the yttria-stabilized with zirconia, which
has a good high thermal stability, relatively high coefﬁcient of
thermal expansion and LTC of about 2.6 W/mK for material that
is fully dense [100–102].
In a TBC system, failure can occur as a result of the temperature
gradient between the room temperature and operational condi-
tions during thermal cycling. It could also result from the differ-
ence in thermal coefﬁcient of expansion of the coating and the
superalloy alloy substrate. Although TBC systems hardly ever fail
completely, some components of the coatings often remain intact.
However, before failure occurs there must have been oxidation at
the TBC interface and the bond coat, weakening of aluminium in
the bond coat, imperfections at the region close to the TGO layer,
a build-up of thermal stresses due to variance in coefﬁcient of ther-
mal expansion and developed stress due to the creation of TGO
layer, and many other intricate factors during the operation pro-
cess of the engine [103–105]. The ceramic TBCs protect superalloys
by providing insulation, which reduces their temperature by
150 /C176C or more. They have been applied in gas turbines and compo-
nents of an aircraft engine as a form of heat exhaust management
[106]. They are made of thermal resistance materials which insu-
late components from prolonged exposure to heat and able to sus-
tain a substantial temperature difference between the coating
surface and the load-bearing material. TBCs allow for a very high
operating temperature while limiting the exposure of component
to a severe thermal attack. They are commonly used for the protec-
tion of NBSs from thermal cycling and melting in turbines. In com-
bination with the circulating cool air, TBC increases the
temperature of allowable gas above the melting point of superal-
loys. These coatings are also becoming increasingly used in auto-
motive applications, where they are designed speciﬁcally to limit
the loss of heat from the components of engine exhaust system
such as casings of turbochargers, exhaust manifolds, tailpipes
and downpipes. Generally, TBCs are required to have: high melting
point and very low thermal conductivity, low rate of sintering for a
permeable microstructure, good substrate adherence potential and
analogous thermal expansion compared to the substrate, no phase
change between ambient temperature and service temperature,
and chemical inertness. These requirements enormously reduce
the number of materials that can be used for TBC, making ceramic
materials the most widely used, since the often satisfy the essential
properties [107–111].
4. Conclusion
This review paper presented a summary of the literature rele-
vant to superalloys. Superalloys were reported to have been pri-
marily used in marine turbine engines and aerospace
applications since creep is majorly the lifespan restraining issue
in gas turbine blades. The properties of superalloys can be modiﬁed
to a certain degree by adding other elements of metals, non-metals
and metalloids such as aluminium (Al), tungsten (W), molybde-
num (Mo), carbon (C) etc. to enhance oxidation, corrosion and
wear resistance. Some of these alloying elements also promote
the formation of
c’ precipitation strengthening, which is responsi-
ble for high-temperature strength and more so, acts as a barrier to
dislocation motion. However, for longevity and optimal perfor-
mance of superalloys, their surfaces can be protected using diffu-
sion, overlaying and thermal barrier (TBC) coatings, which act as
