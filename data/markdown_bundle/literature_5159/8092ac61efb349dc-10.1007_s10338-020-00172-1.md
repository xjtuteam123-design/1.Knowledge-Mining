ABSTRACT In this paper, the morphology and evolution of interfacial dislocation networks of
(100), (110) and (111) interphases of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys are studied by molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations. Three-dimensional cubic-type and sandwich-type models are chosen
to explore the orientation-dependent morphology of dislocation networks, and their respective
advantages and disadvantages are compared. From the simulations, it is observed that various
lattice orientations and model types lead to diﬀerent morphologies of dislocation networks. Based
on the analysis of average atomic energy and dislocation characteristics, the (100) orientation
model has a more regular dislocation network, lower energy and better stability than the (110)
and (111) orientation models after MD relaxation, which are supported by previous experimental
and numerical simulations. Moreover, the cubic-type model has lower energy and better stabil-
ity than the sandwich-type model. This will be helpful for choosing a more appropriate and
reasonable model for simulating the interfacial dislocation networks of Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys.
KEY WORDS Ni-based single-crystal superalloys, Molecular dynamics simulation, Orientation,
Interfacial dislocation networks
1. Introduction
Owing to the excellent high-temperature creep, fatigue and corrosion resistance, Ni-based single-
crystal superalloys are used for the primary materials on the manufacture of advanced aero-engine
turbine blades, which has been the focus of scholars’ attention for a long time [ 1–3]. The material
is strengthened by a high volume fraction of hard cubical γ′ precipitates embedded coherently in
a softer γ matrix, where the volume fraction of γ′ phase may reach 70% or even more [ 4]. Due to
the lattice mismatch between the γ and γ′ phases, the atoms at the γ/γ′ interface will rearrange and
interact with each other and eventually form misﬁt dislocation networks after a relaxation process [5, 6].
Interfacial misﬁt dislocation networks can hinder the movement of dislocations, which are beneﬁcial
for improving the high-temperature mechanical properties of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys [7–9].

Additionally, it can also suppress the γ′ rafting, eﬀectively reduce the interfacial mismatch stress and
greatly improve the creep resistance of alloys [10–12]. In the microstructure of Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys, the γ′phase is uniformly distributed in theγ matrix phase along a certain crystallographic
direction. Related experimental observation has indicated that the morphology of dislocation network
is closely related to lattice orientation [ 13]. Moreover, the mechanical properties (such as creep and
fatigue) of alloys are also sensitive to the lattice orientation [13–17]. Therefore, it is very important to
study the orientation dependence of interfacial dislocation networks of superalloys. Due to the extreme
diﬃculties of in situ experimental observation, the details of formation and evolution of interfacial
dislocation networks at atomic level are still not clear. Considering that the phase interface is in the
micro–nanoscale range, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations play an important role in the study of
dislocation behavior, which have been widely used to study the movement of dislocations and evolution
of dislocation networks at theγ/γ
′phase interface. Xiong et al. [18] and Zhu et al. [19] investigated the
interaction between matrix dislocation and interfacial misﬁt dislocation networks in Ni-based single-
crystal superalloys. Yu et al. [ 20] carried out MD simulation to study the microstructure evolution
and mechanical behavior of the Ni/Ni 3Al interface under thermal–mechanical coupling. Using MD
simulation, Zhu et al. [21] found three types of dislocation network, i.e., the square, rectangular and
equilateral triangle patterns, at the (100), (110) and (111) phase interfaces, respectively. Wu et al.
[10] further studied the evolution and damage processes of these three types of interfacial dislocation
networks under the inﬂuences of temperature and loading by MD simulations. All of the above atomistic
models are based on the simpliﬁed γ/γ
′ phase interface with misﬁt in one direction. However, the
actual structure of the alloy is strengthened by a high volume fraction (about 70%) of the cubic γ′
phase embedded coherently in a γ phase, where interface phases exist in three dimensions. And the
interfacial dislocation networks at all interface phases are connected and interacted with one another.
Therefore, this paper improves the model and establishes cubic-type models with diﬀerent orientations.
The cubic γ′ precipitates with a volume fraction of about 70% embedded in the cubic γ matrix are
taken into account in the present model, which is basically consistent with the actual structure in
Ni-based superalloys. In addition, (100), (110) and (111) orientations of sandwich-type models are also
created, which are convenient to compare and verify the advantages of models with diﬀerent types and
orientations.
In this work, six models including three diﬀerent orientations ((100), (110) and (111)) and two
types of models (cubic and sandwich types) are established to investigate the eﬀect of orientation
on morphologies of interfacial dislocation networks and reveal the formation and evolution processes
of dislocation networks with diﬀerent orientations by using MD simulations. At the atomistic level,
the morphologies and evolutions of interfacial dislocation networks with diﬀerent orientations and
the variations of average atomic energy during MD relaxation are discussed in detail. The present
work also provides important information for understanding the orientation dependence of interface
microstructures and extending the study of mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys from an
atomistic perspective.

3. Simulation Results and Discussion
3.1. Results of the Cubic-Type Model
The dislocation network patterns of six diﬀerent models are shown in Fig. 1. Regardless of cubic-
type or sandwich-type models, the dislocation network patterns are consistent in the same orientation.
Namely, the dislocation network of the (100) orientation model exhibits a square structure at the phase
interface, a rectangular structure exists at the (110) orientation model, and an equilateral triangle
structure exists at the (111) orientation model. This indicates that the morphology of dislocation
network is deﬁned by lattice orientation.
In order to compare the energy changes in diﬀerent models with diﬀerent orientations during MD
relaxation, the average atomic energy (E

that the average atomic energy change rates of all orientation models tend to be zero after about 20
ps, which means that the system reaches an equilibrium state. After relaxation, the (111) orientation
model has the highest energy, followed by the (110) orientation model, and the (100) orientation model
has the lowest energy. This is mainly because the (111) plane is the closest-packed plane of atoms in the
face-centered cubic crystal structure, the atoms on the closest-packed plane are more active, and the
interaction between the atoms is stronger, leading to a relatively high energy. Figure2 shows that the
(100) orientation model reaches an equilibrium state with the lowest energy of− 4.583 eV, the atomic
interaction is weaker, and the system of model is more stable. Thus, the (100) orientation model is the
most stable in the case.

4. Conclusions
MD simulations have been performed to characterize the morphology and evolution of interfacial
dislocation networks of three diﬀerent ((100), (110) and (111)) orientations and two types (cubic and
sandwich) of models during relaxation, and the average atomic energy of six models is also calculated
during relaxation. The major ﬁndings are as follows:
1. The morphology and evolution of dislocation networks depend on the lattice orientation. Diﬀerent
orientation models have diﬀerent patterns of interfacial dislocation networks and diﬀerent average
atomic energies. As a result, the stability of dislocation networks formed after relaxation is also
diﬀerent.
2. Comparing the three diﬀerent orientation models, regardless of the cubic-type or sandwich-type
models, the square dislocation network composed of perfect dislocations at (100) orientation phase
interface has the most regular dislocation network, the lowest average atomic energy and the most
stable model system.
3. A comparison between the cubic-type model and the sandwich-type model indicates that the cubic-
type model has a lower average atomic energy and better stability, which also provides helpful
information on how to choose appropriate models for simulating the interface microstructure of
Ni-based superalloys in the future.
