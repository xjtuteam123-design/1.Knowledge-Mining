1. Introduction
High-conductivity and heat-resistant copper alloys are widely used 
in structural high heat flux fields, such as rocket fuel chamber liners 
[ 1 – 4 ], welding electrodes [ 5 ], and thermal reactor components [ 6 – 8 ]. In 
high heat flux environments, high thermal gradients cause non-free 
thermal expansion of the structural materials and thermal mismatch 
between precipitates and the substrate generates intergranular strains 
[ 9 , 10 ]. These initiate complex and inhomogeneous thermal stress dis -
tributions. Under the influence of thermal stress, progressive plastic 
deformation accumulates within the material. This accumulated per -
manent deformation eventually leads to fracture and failure of the 
component [ 9 – 11 ]. To improve the service life of machine parts at high 
temperatures, it is necessary to develop high conductivity and heat- 
resistant copper alloys.
The primary high-conductivity and heat-resistant copper alloys 
currently in use are Cu-Ag-Zr alloy (NARloy-Z) [ 10 , 12 , 13 ] and Cu-Al
2
O
3 
alloy (GlidCop) [ 6 , 14 – 16 ]. In Cu-Ag-Zr alloys, the second-phase parti -
cles coarsen rapidly at high temperatures and become incoherent 
[ 10 , 17 ]. Cu-Al
2
O
3 
alloys utilize in-situ reactions to generate nanoscale 
second-phase particles without lattice correspondences with the matrix 
[ 18 , 19 ]. These particles remain stable at high temperatures and 
effectively pinning grain boundaries. However, the addition of disper -
sions not only reduces the thermal conductivity but also causes stress 
concentrations, leading to an increase in the brittleness [ 20 – 22 ].
Cu
–
Cr alloys are widely used because of their excellent room tem -
perature properties [ 23 , 24 ]. However, the thermal stability of Cu
–
Cr 
alloys limits their application at high temperatures [ 25 , 26 ]. A single, 
controlled, and high temperature-stable hard-phase particles with Cr 
element would help develop high-temperature materials with high 
conductivity. Nb is feasible due to its low solid solubility and diffusivity 
in the copper matrix and the formation of only Laves-Cr
2

and Cr [ 2 , 27 , 28 ]. Composition calculation and process design indicate 
that the atomic ratio of Cr and Nb should be 2:1. Recently developed Cu-

[ 29 ] and laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) [ 16 , 30 , 31 ]. These alloys have 
been successfully applied to the bushings of the main combustion 
chambers (MCC) of the fourth-generation rocket motors [ 32 ].

low cooling rate methods. This was due to the formation of large Cr

strength. However, the high cost of rapid solidification method limits 
the industrial application of alloys. Yang et al. [ 33 ] concluded that the

precipitates in Cu-Cr-Nb alloys. Some Ti atoms are repelled from the 
precipitates during high-temperature deformation [ 40 ]. These Ti atoms 
accumulate at the precipitate-matrix interface [ 47 ]. The biased elements

reducing the driving force for competitive roughening [ 40 , 47 , 48 ].

5. Conclusions
(1) Trace Ti elements promoted the precipitation of fcc Cr pre -
cipitates. Higher dislocation densities were obtained in the 
CuCrNb-Ti alloy, which resulted from the storage of more dislo -
cations during cold deformation and the lower recovery rate 
during aging. The CuCrNb-Ti alloy has superior room tempera -
ture mechanical properties compared to the CuCrNb alloy.
(2) Trace Ti element reduced the growth rate of nanoscale Cr pre -
cipitates under thermal stress and substantially improved the 
high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy. After high- 
temperature deformation at 550 
◦
C, the average size of nanoscale 
Cr precipitates increased by 98.0 % and 4.3 % for CuCrNb and 
CuCrNb-Ti alloys in the aging state in Process I, respectively.
(3) The addition of Si and Co elements did not significantly improve 
the room and high temperature mechanical properties of the al -
loys. Si and Co elements dissolved into the Cr
2
Nb phase, forming 
(Cr, Si)
2
Nb and (Cr, Co)
2
Nb particles, respectively. Ti elements 
were not enriched in the micron Cr
2
Nb phase. During high- 
temperature deformation, Ti atoms accumulated at the 
precipitate-matrix interface, which not only reduced the elastic 
mismatch energy at the interface, but also reduced the diffusion 
rate of Cr elements by competing to occupy vacancies. These 
findings are meaningful in developing high-conductivity and 
heat-resistant Cu – Cr – Nb alloys.
