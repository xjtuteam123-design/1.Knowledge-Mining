Microstructures and mechanical properties of Nb–Ti–C alloys

Abstract
The microstructures and mechanical properties of a series of ternary Nb–Ti–C alloys have been investigated. The microstructures were charac-
terized using scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction. The alloys contain only two phases: (Nb,Ti)C
carbide and Nb solid solution. The solid solution is a ductile phase while the carbides strengthen the alloy. Compression tests were performed at
room temperature and 1473 K. All of the alloys showed good ductility at room temperature, and the alloy with 20 at.% Ti and 20 at.% C showed a
0.2% offset yield stress of 197 MPa at 1473 K. The room-temperature and high-temperature strengths increased with increasing Ti and C content,
but the room-temperature ductility decreased with increasing C content. The carbides deformed plastically, even at room temperature. Strategies
for the future development of these alloys, aimed at balancing the high-temperature strength and room-temperature ductility, are discussed.

1. Introduction
Niobium-based alloys have been investigated as next gener-
ation high-temperature structure materials in place of Ni-based
superalloys, which have a maximum operating temperature of
about 1373 K. Pure niobium has a relatively low density, a
very high melting point (2741 K) and good room-temperature
ductility. However, at temperatures above 1200 K, the strength
of Nb decreases substantially. In order to improve the high-
temperature strength, various approaches have been utilised,
including solid solution strengthening, precipitation strength-
ening and composite strengthening. Solid solution strengthened
Nb alloys with V , Ta, Mo and W have been reported [1], and
also some two-phase in situ composites containing a bcc Nb
solid solution (Nb
ss) and an intermetallic compound such as
Nb3Al [2] or Nb5Si3 [3]. The results showed that Nb–Mo–W
alloys [4,5] had excellent strength even at temperatures above
1773 K. However, those alloys exhibited very poor ductility
and fracture toughness at room temperature, which is a major
drawback in practical applications. Most of the Nb-based in
situ composites also suffer from the same problem, although

In order to improve the low-temperature mechanical prop-

approaches have been employed. One of the most promis-
ing approaches is the introduction of carbides. For example,
additions of HfC to Nb–5 at.%Mo–15 at.%W alloys gave con-
current increases in both the high-temperature strength and
the room-temperature fracture toughness [5]. Similarly, in
multicomponent Nb–Al–Ti alloys [6–11] minor additions of
TiC increased the high-temperature strength without impair-
ing room-temperature ductility. Moreover, it has been shown
that Nb–20Zr–C alloys exhibit both excellent high-temperature
strength and good room-temperature toughness [12,13].
In research on Nb alloys, Ti is regarded as a toughen-
ing element but no systematic studies of Nb–Ti–C alloys
have been reported. As such, the purpose of this research
is to examine the potential of the Nb–Ti–C system as
new alloys for high-temperature applications. A series of
three Nb-rich alloys was produced, and their microstructures
were characterized using a combination of X-ray diffraction
(XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmis-
sion electron microscopy (TEM). The mechanical behaviour
of the alloys was assessed under ambient conditions and
at elevated temperature, and the deformation microstructures

2. Experimental procedure
The nominal compositions of the alloys in this study are
Nb–15Ti–5C, Nb–20Ti–5C, Nb–20Ti–10C and Nb–20Ti–20C
(all in atomic %), which are indicated on an isothermal section
of the Nb–Ti–C ternary phase diagram in Fig. 1. High purity
Nb, Ti and TiC powders were used as the starting materials.
The alloys were prepared by arc-melting and casting into but-
tons. The buttons were then heat-treated at 2000 K for 4 h in
vacuum, followed by argon gas quenching. The density of the
Nb–20Ti–20C was measured to be 7.4 g/cm
3. TEM specimens
were prepared by twin-jet electropolishing to perforation using
a solution of 10 vol.% sulphuric acid in methanol at 243 K and
25 V . TEM observations were performed using a Philips CM20
operating at 200 kV .
Conventional constant strain-rate deformation tests were per-
formed at a strain rate of about 10
−4 s−1 at room temperature in
an ESH frame with a 200 kN capacity. Specimens for these tests
with dimensions 4.5 mm× 4.5 mm× 9.0 mm were cut using an
electro-discharge machine (EDM). High-temperature compres-
sion was performed in a 1 kN Instron 8501 frame. The limited
load capacity of the machine required smaller specimens with
dimensions of 2 mm × 2m m× 4 mm. High-temperature con-
stant strain-rate testing was carried out under vacuum.

3.2. Room-temperature mechanical tests
No apparent change in hardness was detected between the
as-cast and annealed specimens of each alloy. The values of
Vickers hardness for these alloys in the annealed state are listed
in Table 2.
The compression stress–strain curves obtained from each
alloy at room temperature are shown in Fig. 7. It is evident that
these alloys exhibited excellent compressive ductility. The com-
pression tests were interrupted when the strain reached 30%,
except for Nb–20Ti–20C, which failed when the strain was
around 15%. Cracking was observed on the surface of this speci-
men after the compression test, although Nb–20Ti–20C showed
a higher compressive strength at room temperature than the other
alloys. The alloys with low carbon concentrations all exhibited
very low yield strength values of around 400 MPa. The values for
the Nb–15Ti–5C and Nb–20Ti–5C alloys are very similar and
thus increasing the Ti concentration has no obvious beneﬁcial
effect on the strength. However, a signiﬁcant increase in strength
is obtained by increasing the carbon content from 5 to 20 at.%.
The 0.2% offset strength of Nb–20Ti–20C is about 800 MPa at
room temperature.
3.3. High-temperature compression test
The yield strengths for the four alloys at 1473 K were obtained
by constant strain-rate compression under vacuum. The results
are shown in Table 2. With increasing testing temperature, the
0.2% offset ﬂow stress decreases dramatically. Similarly to the
results at room temperature, on increasing the carbide content
in the alloy, the yield stress increases. The 0.2% ﬂow stress of
Nb–20Ti–20C is 197 MPa at 1473 K compared with 70 MPa for
pure Nb.
3.4. Deformation microstructure
The Nb–Ti–C alloys exhibited substantial room-temperature
ductility in compression. After testing, the deformation
microstructures were examined using TEM. A high density
of dislocations could still be seen in the Nb
ss matrix, which
underwent large-scale plastic deformation. Using the g·b =0
invisibility criterion, the Burgers vector of the dislocations
was determined to be (1/2) ⟨111 ⟩. It is difﬁcult to deﬁne the
difference in dislocation density between the deformed and
undeformed alloys because the density in the undeformed speci-
men is already rather high, but undoubtedly the density does rise.
No deformation twins were detected in the bcc Nb
ss matrix. The
carbides in these alloys also experienced considerable defor-
mation in compression. Fig. 8 shows an image of a deformed
(Nb,Ti)C carbide; the SADP is shown in the inset. A high density
of linear features is observed inside the carbide. In the diffrac-
tion pattern, there are pronounced streaks perpendicular to these
features, implying that they correspond to stacking faults and/or
microtwins.
After deformation at high temperature, an even higher den-
sity of dislocations was present in the Nb
ss as shown in Fig. 9.
Here again, these dislocations have (1/2) ⟨111 ⟩-type Burgers
vectors. Dislocations and a high density of stacking faults were
present in the eutectic (Nb,Ti)C particles ( Fig. 10). Diffraction
contrast analyses revealed that the Burgers vectors of the dis-
locations are (1/2)⟨110 ⟩, and that these lay on {111 } planes.

4. Discussion
In developing new superalloys, several major aspects such as
low-temperature fracture toughness, high-temperature strength,
creep resistance and oxidation resistance should be consid-
ered. Clearly, these properties are controlled by the composition
and microstructure of the alloy. At room temperature, the
compression tests demonstrated pronounced ductility for the
Nb–Ti–C alloys examined in this research. With the exception
of Nb–20Ti–20C, which showed limited plastic deformation
before failure, all of the other alloys were very ductile. In these
alloys, Nb
ss is the ductile phase and (Nb,Ti)C strengthens and
embrittles the alloy. With increasing (Nb,Ti)C content the alloy
becomes more brittle. However, from the compression tests, it
can also be seen that with increasing (Nb,Ti)C content the yield
strength increases, which conﬁrms that (Nb,Ti)C is an effec-
tive strengthening phase for Nb
ss. Comparing Nb–20Ti–5C with
Nb–15Ti–5C, there is no noticeable difference in yield strength,
which suggests that Ti is not an effective solid solution strength-
ening element for Nb. From the EDXS analysis, there is only
25 at.% Ti in (Nb,Ti)C and about 13 at.% Ti in Nb
ss. In most Nb-
based alloys, such as in Nb–silicide and Nb–Cr alloys, Ti acts
as an effective ductilising and toughening element[3]. The frac-
ture toughness of Nb–Ti–Cr solid solution alloys increases with
Ti content. In Nb–Ti–C alloys, the solubility of C in Nb is very
small, about 0.02 at.% at room temperature [14,15]. Therefore,
the effect of C additions to Nb alloys is to form dispersive car-
bides, which strengthen the Nb matrix. In Nb
ss/Nb3Al alloys,
it is found that Ti can increase the strength until 1273 K but
decreases the strength above 1573 K[16]. The yield strengths of

source of strength for the Nb–Ti–C alloys, while as the temper-
ature increases, second-phase particle hardening may make a
more signiﬁcant contribution to the overall strength of the alloy.
More recently, studies of carbide strengthened Nb alloys, such as
Nb–Zr–C [12] and Nb–Ti [6,8] alloys, have been published. For
precipitate hardening Nb-carbide alloys, the ideal microstruc-
ture is a ﬁnely dispersed carbide in a Nb
ss matrix. However, in
as-cast Nb–Ti–C alloys, many (Nb,Ti)C particles are oversized
(the ideal size is less than 100 nm) which is not suitable for
Orowan strengthening (dislocation looping). Therefore, in order
to improve the strength of the alloy, solid solution hardening by
Al, V , Mo, Hf and W, is one choice, the other approach being to
decrease the size of the (Nb,Ti)C particles by using cold and/or
hot working. From our results, it is conﬁrmed that the (Nb,Ti)C

Hf appear to be the most promising [1,16] for solid solution
strengthening and further investigation is underway.
5. Conclusions
Based upon mechanical tests and microstructural analyses of
Nb–(15–20)Ti–(5–20)C alloys, the following conclusions can
be drawn:
(1) In the as-cast state, the alloys consist of Nb solid solution
and (Nb,Ti)C carbide. Two types of (Nb,Ti)C have been
observed, large irregularly shaped eutectic carbides and ﬁner
needle-shaped secondary precipitates. After annealing at
1700
◦C, more secondary carbides precipitated in the Nb ss
matrix. With increasing C content in the alloys, the volume
fraction of carbide increases.
(2) The alloys exhibited excellent ductility in compression at
room temperature. The 0.2% ﬂow stress increased with
increasing volume fraction of carbide in the alloys. Due
to the difference in thermal expansion coefﬁcient between
carbide and Nb
ss, a high density of dislocations was intro-
duced into the Nb ss during casting. After deformation, an
even higher density of dislocations with Burgers vector of
(1/2)⟨111 ⟩was observed in Nb
ss with a high density of
stacking faults and microtwins in the carbides.
(3) The (Nb,Ti)C particles effectively strengthen the Nb ss
phase, but further alloying is needed to increase the high-
temperature strength of Nb–20Ti–20C. Alloying with Mo
and Hf appears to be a promising approach.
