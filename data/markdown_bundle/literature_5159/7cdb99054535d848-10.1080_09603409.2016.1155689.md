Results of high-temperature grain boundary engineering of an experimental, low stacking-fault energy 
(LSF) Ni-base superalloy were compared to a commercially available superalloy RR1000. Deformation 
mechanism maps for thermal-mechanical processing were compared along with the resulting length 
fractions of Σ3 boundaries following sub-solvus and super-solvus annealing. Compared to the hot 
deformation processing characteristics of RR1000, lowering the stacking-fault energy reduces dislocation 
mobility and expands the range of temperatures and strain rates over which dislocation-based plastic 
flow mechanisms were operative in the LSF alloy. For both alloys, processing conditions conducive 
to dislocation-based plasticity allowed for the storage of strain energy within the microstructure that 
was utilised for strain-induced boundary migration (SIBM) and the formation of Σ3 boundaries upon 
annealing. Based on the results of this study, alloying changes that serve to reduce the stacking-fault 
energy of Ni-base superalloys also make the alloys more amenable for grain boundary engineering 
techniques that utilised hot deformation.
Keywords: Grain boundary engineering, Twin, Σ3, CSL, Deformation, RR1000, Stacking-fault energy, Strain energy
Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys are materials that are well suited for 
use in high-temperature structures in advanced gas turbine 
engines due to their excellent creep, fatigue, and corrosion 
resistance at elevated temperatures.1,2 The unique properties 
of Ni-base superalloys can be attributed to their underlying 
microstructure consisting of coherent intermetallic γ′ (Ni
3Al) 
precipitates exhibiting an ordered L12 crystal structure, con-
tained within a disordered γ (Ni) face-centred cubic (FCC) 
matrix. The unique microstructure provides substantial order 
and coherency-type strengthening by restricting dislocation 
motion during plastic deformation.
3 In the design of mod-
ern polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys, additional strength 
can be obtained through the addition of refractory alloying 
elements such as W, Mo, Cr, and Co, which serve as solid 
solution strengtheners of the γ matrix, and Al, Ti, Nb and 
Ta, providing precipitate strengthening through the optimi -
sation of the fraction and size of the γ′ precipitates. As the 
operating temperatures of advanced turbine engines continue 
to increase, design requirements for polycrystalline Ni-base 
superalloy structures are approaching temperatures of up to 
750 °C.4 As a result, innovative classes of Ni-base superalloys 
with improved thermo-mechanical properties are required.5,6 
Although increasing the amount of alloying additions leads 
to incremental improvements in temperature capability, these 
changes also contribute to making the alloy more difficult to 
process.
7,8 Thus, there is a need to develop novel classes of 
alloys that exhibit improved properties and are amenable for 
manufacturing.
Grain boundary engineering (GBE) has been successfully 
employed in various investigations to improve the properties 
of polycrystalline materials such as the creep,9 intragranular 
stress corrosion,10 and fatigue crack growth resistance 11 by 
breaking up the random grain boundary network with special 
grain boundaries. The random grain orientations that exist in 
isotropic polycrystalline materials lead to the formation of 
a random grain boundary network consisting of a statistical 
distribution of grain boundary orientations. Depending on the 
relative orientations, the coherency of the individual grains 
with their neighbours or coincident site lattice (CSL) at the 
boundary may vary significantly. 12 Low CSL boundaries, 
or Σ > 29, both weaken the interface and promote diffusive 
mechanisms at elevated temperatures. Under these condi-
tions, grain boundary diffusion and sliding are accelerated 
along these boundaries. This leads to environmental degra-
dation and poor resistance to creep deformation. On the other 
hand, adjacent or neighbouring grains that exhibit high CSL 
boundaries, or Σ < 29, have relatively coherent interfaces that 
contain few crystalline defects that would serve to weaken 
the boundaries. In this regard, coherent twin boundaries with 
Σ = 3 are highly desirable features in grain boundary-engi-
neered materials, since they possess the lowest grain boundary

In order to induce the formation of sufficiently large frac-

of cold work first needs to be stored in the material. Upon 
subsequent annealing, the supplied thermal energy enables the 
system to effectively utilise the stored strain energy to form

route, however, is not suitable to the forging of large Ni-base 
superalloy components for turbine engines. High-temperature 
deformation is required in order to minimise flow stresses and 
prevent cracking of the alloy or degradation of the forging 
equipment. Therefore, strain energy needs to be stored in the 
microstructure during hot deformation.
14 This process leads 
to the formation of annealing twins13 considered in this inves-

dislocation recovery or grain boundary migration induced by 
the differences in strain energy. Although GBE techniques 
have been evaluated on a variety of polycrystalline metallic 
materials and the potential benefits and improvements in both 
the physical and mechanical properties of GBE alloys have 
been well documented,16 a clear understanding of the underly-
ing physics and mechanisms responsible for the phenomenon 
are still lacking. There is, however, some consensus that the 
stacking-fault energy of the material plays a significant role 
in the ability of the material to form Σ3 and other high CSL 
boundaries that are key to GBE.
13
Compared to the commercial RR1000 Ni-base superalloy, 
the experimental LSF Ni-base superalloy studied in this inves-
tigation has been heavily alloyed with Co in an attempt to 
make it more amenable for GBE and increase the overall pro-
portion of coherent twin boundaries during annealing. Various 
studies have shown that Co additions to Ni-base superalloys 
can effectively lower the material’s stacking-fault energy. 17 
Compared to Ni, Co possesses lower stacking-fault energy and 
preferentially substitutes onto the Ni lattice sites as its concen-
tration is increased in the alloy. Recent studies have also noted 
that increasing the concentration of Co in Ni-base superalloys 
has a direct influence on the recrystallisation characteristics 
of the material as the magnitude of the stacking-fault energy 
affects the ability of dislocations to dissociate into partial 
dislocations.
18 According to dislocation theory, high degrees 
of dislocation dissociation restrict dislocation climb or cross 
slip mechanisms that govern dynamic recovery during defor-
mation. Additionally, grain boundary interactions with the 
disassociated partial dislocations often promote the formation 
of annealing twins during grain growth. The present study was 
aimed at comparing the results from high-temperature grain 
boundary engineering of two alloys, RR1000
14 and the LSF 
alloy,19 and relate the respective fractions of twin boundaries 
to deformation mechanisms and the stacking-fault energy. 
Following a first presentation of the starting conditions of 
both materials, the deformation mechanism maps from Refs. 
[14] and [19] are reconstructed and related to the calculated 
length fractions of Σ3 boundaries. Based on the comparison 
of both alloys, both the influence of the stacking-fault energy 
and the influence of the γ′ precipitates are then discussed.
Materials and experimental details
Materials
RR1000 is a high strength, damage-tolerant Ni-base superal-
loy with a high overall alloying content and the composition 
is given in Table 1. Processing of RR1000 involves powder 
metallurgy techniques that help avoid segregation-induced 
defects. The specimens used in this investigation were cut 
using electrical discharge machining (EDM) from a billet 
slice originally fabricated via gas atomisation of powder, 
consolidation, and hot extrusion to a ratio greater than 5:1. 
This produces a fine-grained, polycrystalline microstructure 
of average grain size ~2.1 μm in the billet that is amenable 
for isothermal forging. The billet microstructure and grain 
size distribution are described in a previous investigation.
14
The experimental LSF alloy was produced by vacuum 
induction melting of high purity elements to form an ingot 
that was then spray atomised in argon. The powder was then 
sieved and consolidated via hot isostatic pressing in a mild 
steel can to form a 10-kg billet. The composition of the LSF 
alloy, Table 1, differs from that of RR1000 as the Mo con-
tent reduced from 5 to 0.17 wt-% and contains additions of 
Nb and W at 3.29 and 3.27 wt-%, respectively. To reduce 
the stacking-fault energy of the LSF alloy, the Co content 
is significantly higher by ~5.5 wt-% compared to RR1000.
Experiments
Cylindrical compression samples with a length-to-diameter 
ratio of 1.5 were prepared from both materials. The RR1000 
samples measured 10 mm in diameter and 15 mm in length, 
while the LSF alloy samples measured 8 mm in diameter 
and 12 mm in length. For both alloys, a preliminary heat 
treatment was performed at 1110 °C for 4 h in an argon atmos-
phere tube furnace in order to normalise the microstructure 
of the as-received materials. In addition to the compression 
cylinders, small disc-shaped specimens were metallograph -
ically prepared to study the starting microstructures of the 
alloys as well as the twin length fraction and density in the 
billet material. The samples were etched using a 33%HCl – 
33%NO3 – 33%CH3COOH – 1%HF γ′ etchant for observation 
under a JEOL JSM 6701-F field emission scanning electron 
microscope (FESEM) in secondary electron (SE) mode with 
an accelerating voltage of 10 kV . Five distinct images of the 
microstructure were taken at 2500× in different regions of 
each sample in order to obtain a good representation of the 
primary γ′ phase distribution. The software ImageJ was used 
to determine the area fraction and ferret size of the primary 
γ′ phase in each alloy.
A series of high-temperature compression tests were 
designed to simulate isothermal forming conditions using 
a Gleeble 3500 system over a broad range of deformation 
parameters. Three deformation temperatures (1020, 1060, and

Results
Starting conditions
Figure 1 shows the microstructures of both alloys, which 
reveal the presence of primary γ′ precipitates located inter -
granularly in RR1000 and both inter and intragranularly in the 
LSF alloy. RR1000 contains coarse γ′ precipitates of diameter 
equal to 1.60 ± 0.09 μm compared to the LSF alloy, which 
has finer precipitates of diameter 0.86 ± 0.03 μm. As the pri-
mary γ′ precipitates in the LSF alloy are located throughout 
the microstructure, their volume fraction of 22.0 ± 1.4% is 
significantly higher than that of RR1000 at 11.8 ± 0.9%. The 
secondary γ′ precipitates are not visible in Fig. 1, as they were 
solutioned during preliminary heat treatment at 1110 °C and 
re-precipitated out at much finer sizes during cooling.
The EBSD maps in Fig. 1 reveal the grain boundary char-
acter of the alloys in the starting condition prior to deforma-
tion. The LSF alloy possesses a larger grain size calculated 
at 5.7 ± 0.2 μm compared to that of RR1000 at 3.9 ± 0.1 μm. 
Interestingly, the morphology of the Σ3 boundaries differs 
from one alloy to the other, where RR1000 exhibits elon-
gated straight twin boundaries located across the grains while 
the LSF alloy contains convoluted twins rarely extending 
throughout the entire grain. This is most likely due to the 
strong presence of intragranular primary (or coarse second-
ary) γ′ precipitates in the LSF alloy interrupting the connec-
tivity of the twin boundaries. Additionally, the length fraction 
and density of Σ3 boundaries of the LSF alloy at 16 ± 1% and 
0.07 ± 0.01 μm−1 was found to be lower than that of RR1000 
at 33 ± 1% and 0.25 ± 0.00 μm−1, respectively.

Deformation mechanism maps
Deformation of the alloys resulted in a broad range of max-
imum stresses and stress–strain curves corresponding to dif-
ferent deformation mechanism. Specific results along with 
grain orientation and intragranular misorientation maps can 
be found in [14] and [19]. The corresponding deformation 
mechanism maps14,19 were reconstructed in Fig. 3 to allow 
for a comparison of the occurrence of the flow mechanisms 
in the two alloys by mapping the values of maximum flow 
stress. Decreasing the deformation temperature and increasing 
the strain rate resulted in an increase in the maximum flow 
stress for both alloys. The values of maximum stress for the 
LSF alloy were significantly higher than those of RR1000 
for nominally identical deformation parameters. As such, the 
maximum stresses in RR1000 ranged from 22 to 252 MPa at 
deformation conditions of 1100 °C – 0.001/s and 1020 °C – 
0.05/s, respectively, compared to 70 and 439 MPa in the LSF 
alloy. The maximum and minimum stresses in the LSF alloy 
were calculated to be 479 MPa when deformed at 1020 °C 
– 0.1/s and 36 MPa when deformed at 1100 °C – 0.0005/s. 
The range of deformation parameters investigated span across 
regimes where superplastic flow and dislocation-based plas-
ticity mechanisms are dominant and are reported in the pro-
cess maps with transition zones represented by dashed lines 
in Fig. 3.
Deformation at higher temperatures (1100 °C) and lower 
strain rates (0.001/s at 1020 °C, and 0.001/s and 0.01/s at 
1060 °C) resulted in predominantly superplastic flow dur -
ing deformation of RR1000, characterised by the absence of 
significant grain refinement, intragranular misorientations, or 
flow stress hardening.14 Additionally, dynamic grain growth 
was also observed following deformation at the highest defor-
mation temperature and lowest strain rate, Fig. 3c. The pro-
cessing space corresponding to superplastic flow in the LSF 
alloy was significantly smaller than that of RR1000 and is rep-
resentative of deformation at 1100 °C – 0.0005/s and 0.001/s, 
and 1060 °C – 0.0005/s. In those regions where the macro -
scopic strains are accommodated via superplastic flow of the 
material, the enhanced grain boundary diffusivity allows for 
grain boundary rotation and sliding.22 For deformation condi-

dislocation recovery suppressed any significant accumulation 
of strain energy within the microstructure.19
The deformation mechanism maps for both RR1000 and 
the LSF alloy show a set of dashed lines at ~110 MPa that 
correspond to the initiation of strain hardening, Fig. 3. This 
transition was estimated from the flow stress curves of the 
two alloys.
14,19 Deformation resulting in maximum stresses

evidence of grain refinement was observed, Fig. 3d, and can 
be associated with grain fragmentation, refinement and/or 
twin reorientation.

Length fraction of Σ3 boundaries
To evaluate the effects of the stacking-fault energy, the calcu-
lated twin length fractions for both alloys in the as-deformed 
conditions and after sub-solvus or super-solvus annealing are 
compiled into surface plots in Fig. 4. Additionally, the grain 
sizes following annealing are reported in Table 2. The vari-
ations in twin length fraction were found to be very similar 
for both alloys.
Quantitative assessment of the as-deformed microstruc -
tures revealed that, for both RR1000 and the LSF alloy, the 
length fraction of Σ3 boundaries decreased following defor-
mation, Fig. 4a. The magnitude of the decrease was found to 
be higher for deformation conditions where dislocation-based 
plasticity mechanisms were dominant as dislocation pile-up 
occurs at the pre-existing Σ3 boundaries due to their high 
coherency that restricts dislocation motion. With increasing 
strain, the dislocation density around the Σ3 boundaries may 
exceed a critical amount and result in twin reorientation that 
decreases the degree of their coherency.24 Thereafter, fractions 
as low as 6 and 8%, after deformation at 1020 °C – 0.05/s 
and 1020 °C – 0.1/s, were calculated for RR1000 and the 
LSF alloy, respectively. Although the length fraction of Σ3 
boundaries decreased significantly during deformation when 
dislocation-based plasticity mechanisms are operative, the 
strain energy stored within the microstructure triggered the 
formation of Σ3 boundaries via strain-induced boundary 
migration (SIBM) during subsequent annealing, Fig. 4b and 
c. Consequently, increasing the amount of stored strain energy 
within the microstructure of the alloys resulted in a higher 
increase in the fraction of Σ3 boundaries as seen when com-
paring the as-deformed to the annealed conditions.
With increasing deformation temperature and decreasing 
strain rate i.e. lower maximum flow stress, Fig. 3, the den-
sity of dislocations stored within the microstructure follow-
ing deformation decreases as dynamic recovery mechanisms 
become more active. Although, this leads to less annihilation 
of the pre-existing twins during deformation, Fig. 4a, the 
respective increase in length fraction of Σ3 boundaries post 
annealing is significantly lower, Fig. 4b, since a lower magni-
tude of strain energy is stored in the deformed microstructure 
and the driving force for SIBM is reduced.
Finally, in the superplastic flow regime where grain 
boundary rotation and sliding become possible, the fraction 
of pre-existing Σ3 boundaries remains unchanged following 
deformation as their coherency is not disrupted by dislocation 
activity, Fig. 4a. However, the absence of stored strain energy 
is not conducive for the formation of Σ3 boundaries during 
annealing and the increase in length fraction post-annealing 
is minimal and solely associated with the limited extent of 
grain growth, Fig. 4b.
The aforementioned increase in length fraction of Σ3 
boundaries during annealing with respect to the deformation 
mechanism is evident in the surface plot relative to sub-solvus 
annealing, Fig. 4b. During sub-solvus annealing, grain bound-
ary mobility is possible through SIBM but limited by the 
primary γ′ precipitates that prevent grain growth. Thereafter, 
leads to the formation of fine, recrystallised, grains located 
along the original HABs in the polycrystalline billet micro -
structure. A partially recrystallised ‘necklace’ microstructure 
consisting of fine recrystallised grains surrounding coarser 
unrecrystallised grains possesses a characteristic bi-modal

Discussion
The results form high-temperature grain boundary engi-
neering of two Ni-base superalloys, RR1000 14 and a low 
stacking-fault energy alloy, 19 revealed similar dependence 
on the preferential formation of Σ3 boundaries with respect 
to the deformation mechanisms involved prior to annealing. 
Furthermore, both the twin density and length fraction fol-
lowed similar evolutions as a function of the average grain 
size, deformation temperature, and strain rate. However, the 
direct comparison of the results for both alloys made in this 
investigation reveals significant differences that can be related 
to the stacking-fault energy and the distribution of the primary 
γ′ precipitates addressed in this section.
Compared to commercially available Ni-base superalloy 
RR1000, the experimental LSF alloy contained a significantly 
higher concentration of Co to further reduce its stacking-fault 
energy. With a lower stacking-fault energy, dislocation-based 
plasticity mechanisms were found to operate over a larger 
range of temperatures and strain rates as characterised by 
significantly higher flow stresses measured during deforma-
tion of the LSF alloy, Fig. 3. Compared to RR1000, higher 
deformation temperatures and lower strain rates were required 
to initiate superplastic flow behaviour in the LSF alloy. For 
both alloys, deformation conditions resulting in superplastic 
flow were found to impart negligible levels of strain energy 
within the microstructure and limited the degree of SIBM 
upon annealing. This served to inhibit the formation of Σ3 
boundaries. Although deformation parameters that induce 
dislocation-based plasticity and stored strain energy are 
favourable with respect to stimulating the rate of Σ3 bound-
ary formation during annealing, excessive levels of stored 
strain energy need to be avoided to suppress recrystallisation 
events. As the lower stacking-fault energy reduces disloca -
tion mobility and expands the temperature and strain rate 
regime over which dislocation-based plasticity mechanisms 
are operative, a broad range of processing parameters can be 
chosen to control the levels of intragranular strain imparted 
into the microstructure during elevated temperature defor -
mation. Upon subsequent annealing, dislocation dissociation 
and interaction of the partial dislocations with mobile grain 
boundaries can enhance the rate of Σ3 boundary formation 
to yield an optimised grain boundary character in the poly-
crystalline material.

ture of the experimental LSF alloy was distinctly different

Conclusions
Based on the results and observations presented in this study, 
the following conclusions can be reported:
(1) A lower stacking-fault energy reduces dislocation 
mobility and expands the deformation process-
ing regime over which dislocation-based plasticity 
mechanisms are operative. With proper control, these 
changes may be favourable for the development of 
elevated temperature GBE techniques.
(2) Deformation involving superplastic flow produces 
negligible levels of stored strain energy that lim -
its SIBM and the formation of Σ3 boundaries upon 
annealing. Therefore, deformation mechanism maps 
with expanded regimes over which superplastic flow 
occurs are not ideal for high-temperature GBE.
(3) A high density of coarse intragranular γ′ precipitates 
disrupts the formation of Σ3 boundaries and results 
in lower overall densities and fractions in the starting 
material. During deformation, migrating the γ′ pre-
cipitates in the random HAB network is necessary to 
obtain elongated twin boundaries.
2. During deformation, sufficient levels of dislocation-based 
plasticity were needed to enhance grain boundary mobility 
and move the coarse γ′ precipitates into the HABs. Upon 
annealing, straight elongated Σ3 boundaries appeared to 
form within the grains by utilising the stored strain energy 
for DDRX. Deformation conditions resulting in superplastic 
flow mechanisms in the LSF alloy most likely did not result in 
the relocation of the coarse γ′ into the HABs as interrupted and 
convoluted Σ3 boundaries were still observed after sub-solvus 
annealing.19
