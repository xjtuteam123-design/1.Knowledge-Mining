This study investigated a novel hypothesis on the inﬂuences of residual or pre-dislocation structures on creep life
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys. Different types of anisotropic dislocation structures were created by pre-
straining the samples at a relatively low temperature under different constant bias load conditions. After such
pre-straining treatment, the samples were subjected to creep testing at higher temperatures under uniaxial ten-
sion. It was found that the pre-straining treatment was effective in creating different anisotropic dislocation
structures without affecting the γ′ phase cuboid morphology and that the samples containing different aniso-
tropic dislocation structures have all signiﬁcantly reduced creep lives. The difference in the anisotropy of the dis-
location structures has little inﬂuence on the reduction of the creep life or the rafting morphology of theγ′ phase
cuboids. By using a Re-free alloy, the inﬂuence of the TCP phase precipitation on creep life is also clariﬁed. These
observations suggest that interruption to high temperature straining is the main contributor to the reduced creep
lives. This ﬁnding raises a serious concern on the validity, or reliability, of creep life assessment based on contin-
uous laboratory creep tests for real life applications, where the services are almost always interrupted into inter-
mittent sessions.

1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys have excellent ability to retain
mechanical properties at high temperatures and have good resistance
to oxidation and hot corrosion [ 1–3]. These attributes render them
wide applications as load bearing components at high temperatures,
such as turbine blades. These alloys derive their high temperature me-
chanical strength and creep resistance from a unique microstructure,
which consists of an L1
2 ordered γ′ phase in {100} cuboidal shapes
aligned along 〈100〉 directions and a continuous disordered fcc γ
phase matrix ﬁlling in the channels between the γ′ cuboids [ 4–7].
These two phases have different elemental enrichments, e.g., Al, Ti
and Ta tend to segregate into theγ′ phase whereas Cr, Co and Re segre-
gate to theγ phase [8–11]. The differences in chemical composition lead
to different lattice parameters of the two phases, causing a lattice misﬁt
at their coherent {100} γ′/γ interfaces [12–14].
When used as turbine blades, the Ni-based superalloys are subjected
to continuous centrifugal forces at high temperatures during service,
causing continued microstructural evolution and creep damages
[15–17]. Three major microstructural changes may occur during such
processes, including the formation of dislocation networks, directional
coarsening (rafting) of the γ′ phase cuboids and the precipitation of
the topologically closed packed (TCP) phases [ 18–23]. It is also well-
observed both in laboratory testing and in practice that the dislocation
activities occur at relatively low temperatures and over short periods,
rafting of the γ′ phase cuboids requires higher temperatures and long
straining times, and formation of the TCP phases occurs in later stages
of creep testing [24–27].
The formation of dislocation networks is driven by the need to relax
the misﬁts t r e s sa tt h eγ′/γ interfaces [28–30]. A uniaxial external load,
as typically being the case in service, alters the isotropy of the lattice
misﬁt among the three principal axes of the cuboids. As a result, the dis-
location network structures formed are anisotropic. For the same token,
it is easy to understand that under (external) stress free conditions, an
anisotropic dislocation network structure produces an anisotropic mis-
ﬁt ﬁeld on the γ′/γ interfaces.
At the same time, the directional coarsening of theγ′ phase cuboids
has also been suggested to be driven by the anisotropic misﬁts t r e s s e so r
potential energies among the γ′/γ interfaces, caused by the anisotropic
bias load applied during service (or creep testing) [31–33]. Under such
conditions, the interfaces of low mis ﬁt energies expand and those of
the high misﬁt energies contract [34–36]. This states that misﬁta n i s o t -
ropy among the three principal interfaces is an essential condition forγ′
cuboid coarsening and thus an important factor affecting the creep life
of the alloys [37,38].
In service (or creep testing), the anisotropic dislocation network
structure and the anisotropicγ′ cuboid coarsening structure are created
by the same external load. Considering the discussion above, it raises an
interesting question of how an anisotropic dislocation network struc-
ture generated under one stress state (bias load) will interact with the
γ′ cuboid rafting under a different stress state, thus affecting the creep
behavior and service life of an alloy. This study was conducted to inves-
tigate the in ﬂuences of different pre-existing anisotropic dislocation
network structures on the creep behavior of two representative Ni-
based single crystal alloys.
2. Hypothesis
Given that dislocation activities, thus the formation of dislocation
networks, may be activated at relatively lower temperatures and over
shorter times, it is thus possible for us create different anisotropic
dislocation network structures under different bias loading conditions
without affecting the γ′ cuboid structure, by using a low straining tem-
perature. Then the samples containing different pre-existing anisotropic
dislocation network structures can be subjected to the same creep test.
Such test will allow us to gain two important knowledges, i.e., (i) to
assess the impact of the various pre-existing dislocation structures on
the γ′ phase rafting structure and the creep life of the samples, and
(ii) to evaluate the hypothesis of using an “opposing” dislocation net-
work structure on the creep behavior of the alloys.
To verify this hypothesis, ﬁve different anisotropic dislocation net-
work structures are designed, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1 ,
including:
I. The three {100} interfaces all contain no dislocations.
II. The density of dislocations on different {100} interfaces follows
ρ(100) = ρ(010) b ρ(001).
III. The density of dislocations on different {100} interfaces follows
ρ(100) = ρ(010) N ρ(001).
IV. The density of dislocations on different {100} interfaces follows
ρ(100) = ρ(001) b ρ(010).
V. The three {100} interfaces all contain dislocations.
Another factor affecting the creep life of an alloy is the formation of
TCP phases. The TCP precipitates also interact with dislocations and the
coarsening of the γ′ cuboids. Therefore, to delineate the effect of TCP
phases, a Re-free superalloy, which is free of TCP phase formation at
high temperatures, and a Re-containing superalloy, which is capable
of precipitating TCP phases, are used. In addition, when used as turbine
blades, the service of Ni-based superalloys is often interrupted, thus this
study also provides an opportunity to investigate the effect of interrup-
tion of high temperature straining on the creep behavior of Ni-based
single crystal superalloys.

along the dislocation line whereas Ta, Ti and Ni, which are the γ′

segregation is attributed to two concurrent effects. Mis ﬁt dislocations,

elements (i.e., Cr, Co and Re), in the cores of the dislocations, which re-
side in the γ phase.

4.4. Interrupted creep test
To further investigate the factors that may contribute to the loss of
creep life of the pre-strained samples, two samples of the Re-free alloy
were pre-strained under the same condition of 750 °C and 750 MPa ten-
sile stress along [001], one for 2 h and the other for 100 h. The pre-
strained samples were then creep tested under the same conditions of
750 °C and 750 MPa tensile stress along [001] till rupture. Considering
that the temperature and stress conditions are the same for both the
pre-staining and the creep tests, this test routine eliminates the in ﬂu-
ences of different testing conditions. In this regard, the tests can be con-
sidered an “interrupted creep test”. In addition, the Re-free alloy is free
of precipitation of TCP phases. This eliminates the potential inﬂuence of
the formation of TCP phases on creep life. Furthermore, this low temper-
ature is chosen to avoid rafting of the γ′ phase (evidence in Fig. 11
below), thus to allow an opportunity to delineate the effect of disloca-
tions on creep life.

dislocation structures are highly anisotropic, both in terms of their
distribution and the dislocation t ype (Burgers vector), it is easy to
expect that noticeable local orientation changes or perturbations
may occur in the single crystal structure. On the other hand, it has
been reported that crystal orientation has direct in ﬂuence on the
creep life of Ni-based single crystal superalloys [ 41–43]. Thus, to in-
vestigate this, the local crystalline orientations of some samples
were analyzed by means of EBSD technique. Fig. 12 shows the
[001] orientation maps of three sa mples creep tested for different
times. It is evident that the orientation uniformity decreased
progressively with increasing the t ime of creep testing. The bulk of
the orientation variation is estimated to be within ~6°.

rafting, formation of TCP phases, and variation of creep temperature or
stress conditions, also demonstrated the detrimental effect on creep

5.1. Formation of anisotropic dislocation structures
This study demonstrates that by using a straining treatment at a low
temperature, it is possible to develop different dislocation network
structures without affecting theγ′ phase cuboid morphology or causing
TCP phase formation. The separation of the phenomena of γ′ phase cu-
boid rafting and dislocation structure evolution provides a rare opportu-
nity to delineate their inﬂuences on the creep behavior of the Ni-based
single crystal superalloys.
It has been proposed in the literature that different dislocation struc-
tures can be induced under different loading conditions during creep
testing [39]. The evidence presented in Fig. 4 conﬁrms this. It further
demonstrates that the different anisotropic distributions of dislocations
are not a result of the rafting process of the γ′ phase cuboids, but of di-
rect consequence of the different loading directions.
The experimental evidence shown in Fig. 4 demonstrates that, for a
negative mis ﬁt system, dislocation networks form preferentially in
channels perpendicular to the loading axis (the horizontal channels)
when under tension but in channels parallel to the loading axis (the ver-
tical channels) when under compression. This is due to that, for a nega-
tive misﬁt system where the γ channels are subjected to compressive
misﬁt stresses, an applied tensile stress in the vertical direction
([001]) decreases the compressive misﬁt stress in the vertical channels
and increases the compressive misﬁt stress in the horizontal channels.
The increased stress in the horizontal channels attracts more disloca-
tions. For an applied compressive stress, the situation is reversed,
i.e., more dislocations are accumulated in the vertical channels.
5.2. Rafting with anisotropic dislocation structures
It is evident that all the ﬁve Re-containing samples developed the
same N-type rafting structure after the creep testing, regardless of the
different pre-existing dislocation structures. This implies that the pre-
existing anisotropic dislocation structures have no or negligible in ﬂu-
ence on rafting of the γ′ phase cuboids.
It is understood that rafting of theγ′ phase cuboids during high tem-
perature straining is caused by mis ﬁt anisotropy [ 30,36,44]. Either an
anisotropic dislocation structure or an anisotropic external load
(e.g., the uniaxial tension applied for the creep test) may create a misﬁt
anisotropy. As presented in Table 3, the anisotropic dislocation struc-
tures formed in the pre-straining treatments introduce mis ﬁt anisot-
ropy. Taking the sample pre-strained under compression along [001]
(type III dislocation structure) for example, the dislocation-induced
misﬁt in the horizontal (001) channels is higher than that in the vertical
(100) and (010) channels, by a margin of 0.03%. Such misﬁ
t anisotropy
promotes the formation of P type raft structures. This obviously contra-
dicts the experimental observation of the N-type raft structure formed.
This implies that the type III dislocation structure in this sample cannot
be the determining factor for rafting in this sample.
The creep testing was conducted at a much higher temperature than
the pre-straining treatment, and dislocations have much higher mobil-
ity and reproduction rate at the creep testing temperature. As a result,
the dislocation structures created by the pre-treatments are expected
to quickly reconﬁgure under the new stress state during the creep test-
ing, i.e., to convert to type II as dictated by the tensile stress along [001].
The dislocation movement can be considered as a stress-induced diffu-
sion process, and the rate of dislocation movement is thus directly inﬂu-
enced by the diffusion coefﬁcient as well the magnitude of the applied
stress. Using the pre-exponential coef ﬁcient D
o = 1.27 × 10 4m2/s and
activation energy ΔG = 279.7kJ/mol [45], the self-diffusion coef ﬁcient
of Ni in Ni is estimated to be 6.67 × 10 −19m2/s at 750 °C and 2.91
×1 0−15m2/s at 1100 °C. It is seen that the diffusion coefﬁcient is nearly
four orders of magnitude higher during the creep testing than during
the pre-straining treatment. In addition, the duration of the creep test
is much longer than that of the pre-straining treatment. Therefore, it is
reasonable to expect that the pre-existing dislocation structures are
quickly converted into the type II structure as per the in ﬂuence of the
[001] tensile stress used during the creep test, as evidenced in Fig. 8.
This explains the reason why the pre-existing dislocation structures
have presented negligible inﬂuence on the raft structure formed.

5.4. The precipitation of TCP phase
It is also evident in Fig. 7 and 8 that the pre-strained Re-containing
samples developed P-type TCP precipitates after the creep test, whereas
the untreated sample was TCP precipitate free, despite the identical
chemical composition. This is attributed to inﬂuence of the dislocations
formed during the pre-straining treatments. All misﬁt dislocations have
an edge component, which attracts alloying elements to form Cottrell
atmospheres. It is evident in Fig. 5 that dislocations are richer in Co, Cr
and Re, which are all P-phase forming elements [4,6,46].
When the pre-strained samples are subjected to creep testing, the
pre-formed dislocations move away to reconﬁgure under the inﬂuence
of the new stress, leaving behind the alloying elements in the Cottrell at-
mospheres. This causes local composition over-saturation, which leads
to the precipitation of the TCP phase particles. For the untreated sample,
the creep testing temperature is high, which gives higher solubility of
the alloying elements in the matrix, thus no signi ﬁcant element segre-
gation is expected at dislocations, which in turn results in no TCP
phase precipitation.
5.5. Effect of pre-straining on creep life
It is evident in Fig. 4 that all the pre-strained Re-containing alloy
samples, regardless of the type of dislocation structure anisotropy, ex-
hibited shortened creep lives compared to the untreated sample.
These pre-strained samples differ from the untreated sample in three
main aspects: (i) they developed TCP (P phase) precipitates during
the process of creep failure, (ii) the loading conditions of the pre-
straining are different from that of the creep test except for sample II,
and (iii) the testing temperature of the pre-straining is different from
that of the creep test. All these three factors are possible to be the
cause of the creep life reduction.

also demonstrates the same effect, i.e., pre-straining signi ﬁcantly re-
duced creep life of the samples. Given that the Re-free alloy is free of

6. Conclusions
This work investigated a novel hypothesis on the inﬂuences of per-
existing anisotropic dislocation networks on the creep performance of
Ni-based single crystal alloys, and explored a unique heat treatment
process that allows the creation of designed dislocation structures with-
out affecting theγ/γ′ microstructure of the alloys. The main conclusions
may be summarized as below:
(1) By performing a straining treatment at a relatively low tempera-
ture (750 °C), different anisotropic dislocation network struc-
tures can be developed under different loading conditions
without affecting the γ′ phase cuboid morphology.
(2) The dislocation structures created by the pre-straining treat-
ments (at a lower temperature) are quickly recon ﬁgured under
the new stress state during the creep testing (at a higher temper-
ature), leading the pre-existing anisotropic dislocation structures
have no inﬂuence on the rafting behavior of theγ′ phase cuboids.
(3) The accumulation of a high density of dislocations on a {100} in-
terface causes local crystalline orientation heterogeneity. This is
due to the fact that the dislocations in a network on a {100}
plane are of the same type as being attracted by the interface
misﬁt and the minuscule orientation variations of each disloca-
tion are accumulated to cause measurable local crystalline orien-
tation heterogeneity.
(4) The pre-strained Re-containing samples developed P-type TCP
precipitates after the creep test, whereas the untreated sample
of the same alloy was TCP precipitate free. This is attributed to
the effect of pre-straining introduced dislocations, which func-
tion as local collectors (reservoirs) of alloying elements for the
formation TCP phase precipitates.
(5) The pre-strained samples, regardless of the type of dislocation
structure anisotropy or the presence of TCP precipitates, exhib-
ited shortened creep lives compared to the untreated samples
for both the Re-containing and the Re-free alloys.
