Abstract
This study addresses the effect of the trace Nb addition in
357 cast alloy and its subsequent role in driving the aging
and precipitation kinetics. The variations in microstructure
and mechanical properties are analyzed with a primary
focus on restoring the ductility in over age state without
compromising the strength characteristics. The prepared
cast alloys: (i) N-0 (without Nb) and (ii) N-1 (containing a
trace 0.035 wt% of Nb) display comparable optical
microstructure and Si eutectic phase characteristics. The
microstructural (TEM) analyses and comparison of
numerical features of the precipitates reveal that Nb
addition has signiﬁcantly stimulated the precipitation
kinetics by generating ﬁner and denser precipitates. As the
age hardening response is determined by the precipitation
kinetics; therefore, the N-1 alloy exhibits a quicker and
better age hardening response and superior thermal
endurance as compared to the N-0 alloy both before and
after the peak aging. Moreover, the trace Nb addition has
also reduced/delayed the over aging effects by suppressing
the further growth and transformation of the Mg
2Si pre-
cipitates (from b00 to b0). The tensile properties indicate
that the ductility is abruptly decreased for N-0 alloy, with
marginal improvement in strength characteristics from
peak to over age conditions. On the other hand, the trace
Nb (0.035 wt%) addition in N-1 alloy not only maintains
and restores the ductility values in both peak and over age
conditions, respectively, but also further improves the
strength properties as well. The fractographic
microstructures of tensile surfaces are logically aligned
with the obtained mechanical responses and microstruc-
tural features of both alloys.
Keywords: trace Nb addition, age hardening, precipitation
kinetics, ductility, 357 cast alloy
Introduction
A growing need to achieve better fuel economy and reduce
hydrocarbon emissions has urged the design engineers to
develop and fabricate lightweight and high-strength mate-
rials. The aluminum (Al) alloys emerged as a strong and
quite promising alternative for this driving potential. As a
result, numerous structural components of steel have been
replaced by the Al alloys during the past three decades.
1–3
Particularly, the utilization of the Al alloys has
tremendously increased in the automotive and aircraft
industries due to their remarkable features such as higher
strength-to-weight ratio, good formability, and superior
corrosion resistance.4–6 Although aluminum covers a broad
spectrum of alloys suitable for diverse applications, the
hypoeutectic cast alloys (A356/357) are speciﬁcally uti-
lized in the manufacturing of structural components for
automotive vehicles.
7–9 The mechanical properties of as-
cast hypoeutectic alloys are often inadequate; conse-
quently, the trace amounts of alloying elements
10–12 are
added, and/or heat treatment is conducted to attain the
desired levels of strength and toughness. 13–15 Heat treat-
ment is performed in two stages: (i) solution treatment at

high temperature, followed by the immediate quenching,
and (ii) aging at low temperature. 16–18 The primary
objective of the high-temperature treatment and subsequent
quenching is to modify the morphology of the eutectic
silicon, promoting its transformation into a globular shape,
facilitating homogenization, and creating a supersaturated
solid solution.
19–21 Subsequently, the low-temperature
treatment is executed to transform the supersaturated solid
solution and to promote the formation of nano-scale meta-
stable (Mg
2Si) precipitates.22–24
The appropriate range for artiﬁcial aging temperatures is
between 150 and 200 /C176C. Elevated temperatures beyond
this range may abruptly accelerate the precipitation
mechanism, potentially leading to increased brittleness and
a deterioration of ductility.
25–27 Even though the natural
aging (storing of samples at room temperature) appears to
be advantageous in improving the ductility of the cast
sections to some extent; nonetheless, it causes 10–20%
reduction in the strength characteristics.
28 Even though a
higher Mg (0.70 wt%) level promotes age hardening
response, the precipitation (Mg 2Si) kinetics, and yield
strength values remain unresponsive upon addition of Mg
beyond 0.50 wt% according to the thermodynamic analy-
ses.
29–31 Similarly, it is also declared that the morpholog-
ical (dendritic and equiaxed grain structure) variations do
not affect the aging behavior of the cast sections. Never-
theless, both the aging and precipitation responses are
considerably inﬂuenced by changing the amount of the
Mg.
32
Li et al. 33 analyzed the age hardening behavior and sub-
sequent precipitation sequence through differential scan-
ning calorimetry (DSC) and transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) techniques. They argued that the
sequential transformation of the Guinier–Preston (GP) into
meta-stable precipitates could be; the generation of
supersaturated solid solution ? formation of GP zones ?
transformation of GP zones into needle-like b
00 precipitates
? formation of rod-type b0 precipitates and generation of
equilibrium b phase. Donlon William 34 explored the ori-
entation relation of the precipitates with the aluminum
matrix and stated that nano-size precipitates are present in
the eutectic regions with [111]
Al||[001]Si, [111] Si||[001]Al,
and (11¯ 0)Al||(110)Si crystallographic orders. It is also found
that the size of the precipitates varied from 10 to 15 nm,
and their morphology remained unresponsive to the heat
treatment time and solidiﬁcation rate.
Numerous studies
35–39 investigated and presented the
comprehensive crystallographic analyses and formation
order of the precipitates in wrought (Al-Mg-Si) alloys in
pre-age, peak age, and over age conditions. However, the
literature related to the precipitation mechanism of the
hypoeutectic (A356/357) alloy was not substantially
focused and lacked a systematic analysis. However,
Chomsaeng et al.
40 conducted an ample study to evaluate
the crystallographic structure of the meta-stable precipi-
tates produced in an A356 cast alloy in peak age state.
After observing various orientations of the meta-
stable precipitates along different zone axes, it is claimed
that the (Mg
2Si) precipitates are identiﬁed along
[001]Al||[010]ppt zone axis. Contradicting to the previous
statement28 that rod-type b0 precipitates are produced upon
peak aging in A356 cast alloy, it is demonstrated that
needles-like b
00 precipitates possessing a monoclinic
structure are generated in the peak age state. 40
Subsequently, Rinderer et al. 41 recognized that the co-
clusters of Mg-Si together with the circular-shaped Mg-rich
GP zones are formed in under-age samples. The needle-like
b
00 precipitates are observed in peak age state; whereas, the
over aging causes the substantial coarsening of the pre-
cipitates, resulting in the generation of rod- and lath-type
morphologies of the b
0 and b precipitates, respectively. Sha
et al. 42 examined that natural aging (of 120 h) before
artiﬁcial aging considerably slows down and delays the
precipitation kinetics. Contrarily, the precipitation mecha-
nism is quite fast in as-quenched and instantly aged sam-
ples (at 180 /C176C) without any natural aging. A systematic
study
43 revealed that the pre-aging improves the ductility
value twice at the expense of strength characteristics.
Meanwhile, the number density of the b
00 precipitates is
increased, whereas the size is decreased as compared to the
single stage under peak age conditions. Conversely,
Choong do Lee
44 demonstrated that the over aging con-
tributes to the strength properties to some extent, but it
decreases the ductility values quite noticeably. Similarly,
Manickaraj et al.
25 demonstrated that incubation time
before artiﬁcial aging signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the precipi-
tation sequence in A356.2 alloy, where Mg–Si co-clusters
formed during incubation alter the kinetics of b
00 precipi-
tate formation during subsequent aging. This demonstrates
the critical role of aging parameters in tailoring mechanical
properties.
As our initial study,45 revealed that the over aging not only
enhances the size of the Mg 2Si precipitates but also causes
the partial transformation of needle-shaped b00 into rod-
type b0 precipitates. This phenomenon is the core reason
for the simultaneous reduction in hardness and ductility
accompanied by slight enhancements in strength charac-
teristics of the 357 cast alloy in over age condition. The
developed relations among precipitate size, ductility, and
quality index indicate that the precipitation mechanism
does not merely contribute to the attainment of the superior
strength properties; nevertheless, it also plays a crucial role
in determining the ductility of the 357 cast alloy. There-
fore, this study is an ongoing succession of the previous
research work with an aim to limit the growth (size and/or
length) and transformation of the Mg
2Si precipitates to
restore the ductility of the 357 cast alloy without com-
promising on the strength properties, speciﬁcally in over
age state. The investigations are conducted by analyzing

Materials and Methods
To prepare an Al-7.5Si-0.55Mg alloy, an Al ingot of
commercial purity (99.7 wt%) was cut into small pieces,
dried at 200 /C176C to take out moisture, and melted in a
graphite crucible using an electric resistance furnace at
690 /C176C. On complete melting of Al, the temperature was
raised to 740 /C176C, and master alloys of Al-20 wt% Si and
Al-10 wt% Ti were included. The melt was mixed for 1 *
2 min and let the temperature to be dropped up to 680 /C176C.
Then, pure Mg (99.9 wt% of purity) was added, dipped,
and pushed to the bottom of the crucible to avoid excess
burning of Mg. The temperature was again raised up to
720 /C176C, and an Al-10Sr master alloy was added as a
modiﬁer in both cast alloys (to include 300 ppm Sr);
whereas, an Al-5Ti-1B (0.20 wt%) was included as a grain
reﬁner in N-0 alloy. The addition quantity of an Al-2.8Nb-
0.25B master alloy was set to achieve 0.035 wt% of Nb in
N-1 alloy, and it served as a grain reﬁner as well. After the
addition of all alloying elements, the melt was mixed for
2–3 min to ensure the complete dissolution of all the
elements.
To remove hydrogen from the melt, a commercially
available degassing reagent, a mixture of chloride salts was
added and stirred continuously for 5 min. After degassing,
the slag was removed and the melt was left for homoge-
nization at 720 ±5 /C176C for 20 min and then poured into
Y-shaped iron molds (Figure 1 exhibits the schematic
diagram and dimensions) preheated at 200 /C176C for 3 h and
allowed to be solidiﬁed at room temperature. The compo-
sition analyses of both alloys were obtained through the
inductively coupled plasma (ICP) method and are listed in
Table 1 along with their designated cast (N-0 and N-1)
alloy codes.
The pieces cut from the bottom side of the cast parts by
sparing 10 mm edges (displayed in Figure 1) were solution
treated at 540±2 /C176C for 8 h and quenched in water at room
temperature. Tensile specimens of rectangular shape (15
mm gauge length, 3.5 mm width, and 2.2 mm thickness)
were cut from the solution-treated pieces using wire-cut
electric discharge machining. The aging was carried out at
175±1 /C176C according to the peak and over aging times
identiﬁed in the age hardening curves for both alloys, and
the samples were polished using a standard grinding
method. At least three samples for each (peak and/or over
age) condition were tested using Zwick/Roell-Z100 (20
kN) material testing equipment using a strain rate of 1 mm/
min to compute the average and standard deviation values
of the percentage elongation, yield, and ultimate tensile
strengths. The morphological features of the fractured
surfaces of the tensile samples were examined through
Phenom XL Desktop SEM equipment. The digital Vicker
hardness tester XHVT-10Z equipped with an image cap-
turing system was utilized to apply a load of 5 Kg for 15 s
and to record an HV value automatically. Moreover, the
reported average and standard deviations of HV values
were calculated from 10 indentations.
The metallographic samples were prepared through the
standard abrasion/grinding method by using wet silicon
carbide sand papers. To make the microstructural feature
clear, the ﬁnely polished samples were etched with a 3%
HF solution for just 3 s, washed instantly with ethanol, and
dried. Similarly, the polished samples were anodized in a
Barker solution (a diluted solution of 5 ml HBF
4 and 200
ml water) by passing 30V direct current (DC) for 110 s to
obtain polarized light images. The optical microstructures
(OM) and polarized light images (from anodized samples)
were examined and characterized through Zeiss Axio
Observer A1.

Age Hardening Responses
After the solution treatment (at 540 ±1 /C176C for 8 h) and
quenching, the age hardening responses of both alloys have
been accomplished at 175 /C176C while the numerical values of
the hardness tests are sketched in Figure 3. The Vicker
hardness (HV) values of N-0 and N-1 alloys in as quench
state are 80.8 and 80.7 HV, respectively, which demon-
strate that the solution treatment has produced similar
effect for N-0 and N-1 alloys irrespective of the fact that
N-1 alloy contains 0.035 wt% of Nb. Initially, a superior
aging response is exhibited by the N-1 alloy as compared to
the N-0 alloy during the ﬁrst 2 hours of aging and gained
(5.2 HV) higher hardness values. However, this margin
gradually started reducing during the further treatment and
squeezed up to 1 HV upon reaching the peak aging time (6
h); whereas, the attained peak hardness values of N-0 and
N-1 alloys after 6 h of aging are 119.7 and 120.7 HV,
respectively. Besides, it can be noted that by continuing the
aging treatment of N-0 alloy beyond the peak aging (6 h)
time, the hardness value started declining immediately and
decreased up to 114.2 from 119.7 HV after 12 h of aging.
On the other hand, the N-1 alloy shows minimal aging
effects for prolonged aging time after the peak aging and
maintains the hardness trend quite well with the least
variations up to 16 h of aging, and then starts declining.
Therefore, it can be said that the N-0 alloy is more sensitive
to the over aging treatment, whereas the addition of trace
Nb in the N-1 alloy reduced this susceptibility somewhat.

TEM and Precipitates Analysis
TEM micrographs in Figure 6 reveal that needles-like
precipitates can be seen in both (N-0 and N-1) alloys and
for both heat treatment (peak and over age) conditions.
However, the two variations can be easily identiﬁed among
the microstructures; (i) the size of the precipitates is
changing from peak to over age conditions for N-0 alloy
and (ii) small dot-like particles are scattered in all over the
area of N-1 alloy for both (peak and over age) conditions.
HAADF-STEM image of N-1 alloy in Figure 7 shows that
the dot-like particle is not spherical/round rather it has an
oval/egg-like morphology. Besides, HAADF-STEM map-
ping micrographs conﬁrm that this small oval shape par-
ticle is an Mg-Si-rich GP zone and/or precursor of b
00
precipitate.
The numerical measurements of the precipitates (listed in
Table 3 and sketched in Figure 8) illustrate that the length
values of the precipitates are signiﬁcantly increased (about
8.6 nm) from peak (23.5 nm) to over (32.1 nm) age con-
ditions while the number density values remain almost
same for N-0 alloy. Contrarily, a slight difference (1.5 nm)
in precipitates length is noticed for N-1 alloy from peak
(23.8 nm) to over (25.3 nm) age conditions; likewise, the
number density values are also not changed much. How-
ever, it is important to be noticed that the number density
values of N-1 alloy in both conditions are nearly ﬁve times
higher than the number density values of N-0 alloy for both
(peak and over age) conditions. It indicates that the trace
(0.035 wt%) addition of Nb in N-1 alloy caused the stim-
ulation of precipitation kinetics and considerably increased
the number density values.
The addition of Nb may have increased the dissolution rate
of the Mg and Si in the matrix during the solution treatment
and quenching, which resulted in the generation of highly
supersaturated solid solution. That is why, the N-1 alloy
exhibited a superior age hardening response in Figure 3
during ﬁrst 1–4 h as compared to the N-0 alloy and formed
denser precipitates. The similar phenomenon was observed
and reported by Wang et al.
51 that the addition of La in
A356 alloy not only accelerated the precipitation kinetics
but also reduced the size of the precipitates considerably.
The Nb addition in N-1 alloy also acted in the similar way
by increasing the number density of the precipitates and
simultaneously reducing their size by creating excess
number of nano-size (3–5 nm) Mg-Si-rich GP zones along
with b
00 precipitates.

the over aging effects offering more resistance to the

Evaluation of Mechanical Properties
The tensile test results in Figure 10 reasonably agree with
the microstructural characteristics and also verify our pre-
vious
45 claim that the size of the Mg 2Si precipitates sig-
niﬁcantly contributes in determining the ductility of the
357 cast alloy and the over aging causes a considerable
enhancement in the size of the precipitates. The N-0 alloy
revised and validated the previous study ﬁndings and again
the ductility was reduced spectacularly in over age state as
can be noted in Figure 10a. However, the trace Nb addition
in N-1 alloy (Figure 10b) restored the ductility value by
increasing the number density and restricting the growth
(size/length) of the precipitates without compromising on
the strength properties. The fractographic images of tensile
surfaces in Figure 11 are logically aligned with the
observed mechanical responses of both N-0 and N-1 alloys.
Conclusions
Based on the presented results and detailed discussion, the
ﬁndings of this study can be summarized as follows;
1. The grain reﬁnement potency of new grain reﬁner
Al-2.8Nb-0.25B is not much different from the
conventional Al-5Ti-1B reﬁner, both master
alloys yield identical grain reﬁnement results
for 357 cast alloy. Additionally, the two (N-0
without Nb and N-1 with trace 0.035 wt% of Nb)
cast alloys display comparable optical
microstructure and Si eutectic phase characteris-
tics as well under same heat treatment conditions.
2. However, the aging curves show some obvious
variations and N-1 (containing 0.035 wt% of Nb)
alloy exhibits quicker hardening response than
N-0 alloy (without Nb). Regardless of same peak
aging time (6 h) for both alloys, the Vicker
hardness trend starts declining instantaneously
after peak aging for N-0 alloy; meanwhile, the
N-1 alloy maintains the hardness curve with
minor variations up to 16 h of aging.
3. TEM analysis and numerical features of the
precipitates reveal that the precipitates size is
increased (from 23.5 to 32.1 nm) appreciably
upon over aging of N-0 alloy; whereas, the N-1
alloy shows minimal over aging effect with a
slight variation in size (23.8–25.3 nm) from peak
to over age states. Moreover, the small nano-size
(3–5 nm) Mg–Si-rich precursors of precipitates
are observed in both (peak and over) age states,
which enhanced the number density value of N-1
alloy about ﬁve times higher compared to N-0
alloy.
4. By correlating the age hardening responses and
characteristics of DSC peaks, it can be identiﬁed
that trace Nb addition in N-1 alloy effectively
retarded the transformation kinetics of b
00 into b0
precipitates as compared to the N-0 alloy.
5. Tensile results demonstrate that the ductility is
drastically reduced for N-0 alloy with slight
increments in strength properties from peak to
over age states. Nevertheless, the addition of
trace Nb in N-1 alloy maintained and recovered
the ductility values in both peak and over age
conditions with negligible variations.
Therefore, it can be deduced that the precipitation mech-
anism and the numerical features of the precipitates such as
number density and size (length) appreciably contribute in
deﬁning the ductility response of the 357 cast alloy.
