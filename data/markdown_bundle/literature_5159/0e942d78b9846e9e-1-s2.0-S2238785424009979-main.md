ABSTRACT 
Here, wire-arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) is employed to fabricate Al – Ce – Mg heat-resistant aluminum 
alloy, considering its capability to manufacture large complex structures more efficiently. Large-sized faceted 
Al
11
Ce
3 
intermetallic compounds are formed in the as-cast alloy with a hypereutectic microstructure. The 
microstructure is transformed to a eutectic microstructure by non-equilibrium solidification of WAAM, consisting 
lamellar α -Al/A1
11
Ce
3 
eutectics. The WAAM alloy shows enhanced mechanical strength and ductility at 200 
◦
C, 
compared with the as-cast alloy. The significant improvements of ultimate tensile strength and elongation for the 
WAAM alloy are correlated with its higher strain hardening rate, along with the transition from cleavage fracture 
to dimple fracture. The cracking of Al
l1
Ce
3 
particles occurs preferentially during the elastic deformation stage, as 
revealed by in-situ testing, and the strain hardening capability of the as-cast alloy is reduced. Pore defects, 0.15

and mergence, increases the pore volume fraction, and decreases the pore sphericity. Our results demonstrate 
that WAAM is strongly attractive for the fabrication of Al – Ce – Mg alloys, due to the enhanced high-temperature 
strength-ductility synergy. 
1. Introduction 
The industrial sectors, such as automobile and aircraft, recently call 
for lightweight aluminum alloys that can serve at high temperatures. 
The current commercial heat resistant aluminum alloys, e.g. Al – Si based 
A319 and Al – Cu based 2618 alloys) suffers from significant creep/yield 
strength loss at elevated temperatures exceeding 200 
◦
C, because of 
insufficient microstructure stability [ 1 ]. 
Al – Ce based alloys are recently eye-catching for their distinguished 
microstructure stability up to 400 
◦
C, which impedes the rapid decrease 
of mechanical property at high temperatures [ 2 , 3 ]. It has been reported 
that the hardness of the near-eutectic Al-12.5Ce alloy (wt.%) remain 
almost constant after heating at 400 
◦
C for up to 84 days, evidencing 
excellent microstructure coarsening resistance [ 4 ]. The good micro -
structure stability generally results from the limited solubility and 
diffusivity of Ce atoms in the aluminum matrix, and the microstructure 
coarsening caused by atomic diffusion is thus suppressed [ 5 , 6 ]. Besides, 
commercializing the Al – Ce alloys is generally unencumbered, due to the 
high abundance and the low price of rare-earth element Ce, which is rare 
earth mining by-product [ 5 , 7 ]. Unfortunately, the mechanical proper -
ties of conventional cast Al – Ce based alloys are insufficient, acting as the 
main obstacle for industrial application. 
The Al
11
Ce
3 
intermetallic compounds, as a result of eutectic reaction, 
are the main strengtheners of Al – Ce based alloys. Further solution and 
aging treatments are not required for mechanical property improvement 
[ 3 ]. Composition design and/or microstructure tuning are effective 
methods to develop high-performance Al – Ce based alloys. Several Al – Ce 
based alloy variants have been reported so far, such as Al – Ce – Fe alloy 
[ 8 ], Al – Ce – Ni alloy [ 9 ] and Al – Ce – Mg alloys [ 10 – 12 ]. In particular, the 
alloying of Mg is considered necessary for Al – Ce based alloys, because it 
improves the strength of the α -Al matrix via solid solution strengthening 
[ 10 – 12 ]. 
As a cast aluminum alloy, Al – Ce based alloys prefer the solidification 
processing routes, such as conventional casting and recently-developed 
additive manufacturing (AM). Previous literature [ 11 , 13 – 15 ] manifests 
that fusion-based AM, featured by a rapid solidification pathway,

2. Materials and methods 
A self-developed WAAM equipment was employed for the fabrication 
of Al – 15Ce – 3Mg (wt%) alloy component. It included a three-axis com -
puter numerical control machine tool, a tungsten inert gas (TIG) welding 
torch, a welding machine, a wire-feeding device, and the control system. 
The sketch of the WAAM equipment was illustrated in Fig. 1 a. In the 
WAAM process, an electric arc was initialized between the non- 
consumable tungsten electrode and Al-2024 alloy substrate, working 
as the heat source. The pre-alloyed Al – 15Ce – 3Mg (wt%) alloy wires 
were prepared using cast bars, followed by extruding and drawing 
procedures. The wires,1.6 mm in diameter, was melted by the electric 
arc, resulting in the formation of melt pools. The inert shielding Ar gas 
secured the tungsten electrode and melt pool from airborne contami -
nants, with a flowing rate of 20 L/min. The TIG torch moved along the 
rectangular route, guided by the machine tool. When one layer was 
finished via the accumulation of melt pools, the next layer was built 
continually on the top surface ( Fig. 1 b). A rectangular thin-walled 
Al – Ce – Mg alloy component was finally prepared layer-wisely. The 
WAAM processing parameters included 42.3 A arc current, 23 V arc 
voltage, 2.0 mm layer height, 700 mm/min wire feeding speed and 100 
mm/min travelling speed. For comparison, the as-cast counterpart alloy, 
with the same composition of Al – 15Ce – 3Mg (wt%), were synthesized 
via gravity casting, using ethanol-cleaned Al – 20Ce and Al – 20Mg master 
alloys as well as pure Al bulks as raw materials. The mixed raw materials 
were heated to 780 – 800 
◦
C, held for 20 min, and the liquid melt was 
poured into a cylindrical mold manually at ~750 
◦
C, with air cooling. 
The sampling positions for microstructure analysis and tensile testing 
were illustrated in Fig. 2 a. Microstructures were observed using field 
emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific 
Apreo C HiVac). The bulk sample for SEM observation was prepared by 
grinding and polishing, and a final OPS polishing was performed to 
improve sample surface quality. The elemental distributions were ob -
tained using a field-emission electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA, 
JXA-iHP200F, JEOL). 
To examine the high-temperature mechanical property, tensile 
testing was performed at 200 
◦
C using a Zwick Roell Z2.5 testing ma -
chine, according to the GB/T 4338-2006 standard. The strain was 
measured by a contact mechanical extensometer and the loading rate 
was constant (1 mm/min). The furnace temperature was controlled by 
three K-type thermocouples, and prior to mechanical loading, the 
specimen was held at 200 
◦
C for 5 min. The sampling position for tensile 
testing was illustrated in Fig. 2 a, and the specimen dimension was 
shown in Fig. 2 b. More than three specimens were tested for mechanical 
property repeatability. 
After tensile testing, top-view fracture surface and side-view micro -
structures of fractured specimens were analyzed by SEM. The micro -
structure nearby the fracture surface was observed by electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD), using EDAX Hikari Super EBSD camera 
equipped on SEM (ZEISS GeminiSEM 300). The EBSD scanning step size 
was 0.5 μ m, and the sample was prepared by ion milling with Cross 
Section Polisher (CP) to remove residual surface stress/strain. To reveal 
the strengthening mechanism, the microstructure nearby the fracture 
surface was further observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, 
JEM2100F, 200 kV) and the attached energy dispersive spectrometer 
(EDS-TEM). The TEM sample was prepared by mechanical grinding and 
thinning by ion-milling. 
To investigate the effect of porosity on the mechanical behavior, X- 
ray microscopy (XRM, ZEISS Xradia 520 Versa) was employed to track 
the porosity evolution of the WAAM-processed Al – Ce – Mg alloy. The 
XRM scanning parameters were 85 μ A target current, 70 kV beam 
voltage, 2.4 s exposure time, 6 W beam power, 5.2 μ m pixel size, and LE5 
source filter. 
To track the microstructure change during high-temperature defor -
mation, in-situ tensile testing was carried out in using a Kammrath- 
Weiss tensile module with a maximum loading of 5 kN, equipped in -
side a SEM (ThermoFisher Apreo 2S). Prior to installing the tensile

4.3. Porosity evolution during high temperature deformation 
Noteworthily, the differentials of UTS and elongation between 
WAAM and as-cast specimens are much larger than the that of YS 
( Table 1 ). This normally indicates the WAAM specimen has higher strain 
hardening capability than the as-cast specimen, as also revealed by 
Fig. 5 b. The deformation behaviors of Al – Ce – Mg alloys is discussed as 
follows. 
Porosity is the main metallurgical defect for aluminum alloys fabri -
cated by WAAM [ 36 ]. XRM is utilized to visualize the response of 
porosity to plastic deformation at high temperature. The porosity in the 
original WAAM (strain-free) specimen is reconstructed by XRM, see 
Fig. 10 a. The volume fraction of porosity — including gas pores and 
shrinkage cavities ( Fig. 10 b) — in the original AM-S specimen is ~0.15%,

caused by the huge difference of hydrogen solubility between liquid (7

released from liquid Al – Ce – Mg alloy melt. The presence of biofilms in 
solidifying aluminum can inflate due to the decrease of pressure and/or 
hydrogen segregation, and pore formation is considered to be a pure 
growth process according to previous research [ 39 ]. As delivered by 
Fig. 10 c, most of the pores are below 20 μ m in diameter, and above 0.8 
in sphericity. It suggests the amount of gas pores is much larger than that 
of shrinkage pores with lower sphericity, which is caused by hydrogen 
migration into the voids formed due to the volumetric difference be -
tween liquid and solid aluminum [ 41 ].
