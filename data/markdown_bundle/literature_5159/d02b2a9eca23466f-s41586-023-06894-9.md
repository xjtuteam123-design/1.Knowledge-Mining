Body-centred cubic refractory multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs), with several 
refractory metal elements as constituents and featuring a yield strength greater than 
one gigapascal, are promising materials to meet the demands of aggressive structural 
applications
1
–
6
. Their low-to-no tensile ductility at room temperature, however, limits 
their processability and scaled-up application
7
–
10
. Here we present a HfNbTiVAl
10
 alloy 
that shows remarkable tensile ductility (roughly 20%) and ultrahigh yield strength 
(roughly 1,390 megapascals). Notably, these are among the best synergies compared 
with other related alloys. Such superb synergies derive from the addition of aluminium 
to the HfNbTiV alloy, resulting in a negative mixing enthalpy solid solution, which 
promotes strength and favours the formation of hierarchical chemical fluctuations 
(HCFs). The HCFs span many length scales, ranging from submicrometre to atomic 
scale, and create a high density of diffusive boundaries that act as effective barriers 
 
for dislocation motion. Consequently, versatile dislocation configurations are 
sequentially stimulated, enabling the alloy to accommodate plastic deformation 
while fostering substantial interactions that give rise to two unusual strain-hardening 
rate upturns. Thus, plastic instability is significantly delayed, which expands the 
plastic regime as ultralarge tensile ductility. This study provides valuable insights 
 
into achieving a synergistic combination of ultrahigh strength and large tensile 
ductility in MPEAs.
Multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs), consist of many constituent 
elements in high concentrations with equal or near-equal atomic ratios, 
known as high-entropy alloys, and offer greater compositional flexibil
-
ity than conventional dilute alloys in exploring even better mechanical 
performance
11
–
14
. Although body-centred cubic (bcc) refractory MPEAs, 
consisting mainly of refractory elements, easily achieve high yield 
strengths close to or over 1 GPa at room temperature, one crucial bot
-
tleneck that hinders their processability and potential applications is 
their low-to-no tensile ductility
7
–
9
. This strength–ductility trade-off is 
not surprising, as straight screw dislocation planar slip dominates the 
plastic deformation, lacking dislocation to interact and multiply for 
strain hardening, which is essential for sustaining delocalized plastic 
strain
7
,
15
,
16
. Otherwise, strain localization and instability, such as neck
-
ing, occur immediately after yielding. One primary strategy for sup
-
pressing such instability is to keep the normalized strain-hardening 
rate 
θ
 at a high level (more than one) over an extensive regime
17
. So far, 
many conventional routes, such as twinning-induced plasticity and 
transformation-induced plasticity, have been made available to improve 
strain hardening in refractory MPEAs by tailoring their phase stability, 
which usually results in reduced strength
18
–
20
. A prototypical example is 
Ta
x
HfZrTi alloys, which achieved tensile ductility of up to 25% by making 
the constituent phase metastable to stimulate transformation-induced 
plasticity, but with relatively low yield strengths of several hundred 
megapascals
18
. Recent research has focused on exploring chemical het
-
erogeneities, such as short-range order and local chemical fluctuations 
(CFs), as a promising avenue to improve ductility in refractory MPEAs
3
,
21
. 
These chemical heterogeneities have shown the ability to retard dis
-
location motion by effectively trapping dislocations, which results in 
increased dislocation interactions and contributes significantly to the 
strain-hardening capability of the alloy. However, the strengthening 
effect of these heterogeneities was found to be limited due to their 
small sizes (less than about 3 nm). Consequently, the lack of other 
strengthening mechanisms led to relatively low yield strengths of below 
1 GPa. Thus, how to break the trade-off between strength and ductility

The current research shows that as the mixing enthalpy becomes 
more negative more chemical heterointerfaces are obtained in the 
solid solution (Supplementary Fig. 1). Elements with negative mixing 
enthalpies to the others have long been recognized to foster chemi-
cal affinity bonds among the alloying atoms, showing a tendency for 
chemical segregation or ordering3,42–46. The present study demonstrates 
that these multiscale and complex diffusive chemical heterointer -
faces, that is, CF , not only contribute to strengthening effect, but also 
stimulate further intragrain dislocation activities and promote disloca-
tion interactions. It is speculated that these alloys can not only use the 
strengthening effect of negative mixing enthalpies to achieve higher 
strengths, but also create further chemical affinity-related heterogene-
ous microstructures to sustain high strain-hardening rates at high flow 
stress levels. These genetic characters of negative mixing enthalpy in 
high-entropy solid solution systems show the potentials to achieve 
an exceptional strength–ductility combination, as demonstrated in 
the current research. On the basis of the aforementioned results, the 
idea of negative mixing enthalpy solid solution was proposed in this 
study. The definition and design route are described in Supplementary 
Notes 2 and 3.
In summary, a HNTVA10 refractory MPEA is developed and shows an 
exceptional strength–ductility synergy under tension. This is achieved 
through the negative mixing enthalpy strengthening effect and the 
related generation of unique hierarchical chemical heterogeneities 
spanning many scales. These heterogeneities play a crucial role in 
retarding dislocation motion while promoting dislocation interac -
tion and accumulation, thereby delaying plastic instability. The nega-
tive mixing enthalpy solid solution route is proved to be versatile and 
reproducible and sheds lights on other MPEA systems that can simul-

Methods
Material preparation and processing
Using high-purity Hf, Nb, Ti, V and Al (more than 99.95 wt%), 4 kg ingots 
of HfNbTiVAl0, (HfNbTiV)95Al5 and (HfNbTiV)90Al10 alloys were prepared 
by vacuum suspension melting in an argon atmosphere. Ingots with a 
nominal composition of (HfNbTiV)85Al15 were prepared by arc melting in 
a Ti-gettered high-purity argon atmosphere. T en remelting operations 
were performed on the ingots to improve initial chemical homogeneity. 
Using electric discharge machining, plates measuring 60 mm in length, 
10 mm in width and 4 mm in thickness were cut from the ingots. The 
plates were then wrapped in Ta foils and sealed in quartz tubes filled 
with pure Ar and sponge Ti before undergoing homogenization at 
1,300 °C for 10 h. After homogenization, the HfNbTiVAl0, (HfNbTiV)95Al5 
and (HfNbTiV)90Al10 plates were cold-rolled to a thickness reduction 
of roughly 62% to 1.5 mm. The plates were annealed at 1,200 °C for 
5 min under Ar protection for recrystallization before water-quenching 
them to ambient temperature (Supplementary Fig. 8). Owing to its 
brittle nature, the homogenized (HfNbTiV)85Al15 was directly annealed 
at 1,200 °C for 5 min under Ar protection and then water-quenched to 
ambient temperature, rather than being cold-rolled. For simplicity, 
we refer to the alloy (HfNbTiVAl0, (HfNbTiV)95Al5 and (HfNbTiV)90Al10) 
subjected to homogenization, cold-rolling and recrystallization as 
the ‘annealed alloy’ .
Microstructural and elemental characterization
XRD spectra were obtained using a Cu-Kα radiation source, scanned in 
the 2θ range of 20–100° at a speed of 0.01° s−1. Before XRD scanning, the 
samples were polished with 800- and 2,000-grit silicon carbide papers, 
followed by diamond polishing. An FEI-Quanta 650 field-emission SEM 
equipped with a TSL’s electron backscatter diffraction system was used 
to characterize the microscale structure and morphology. Samples 
for electron backscatter diffraction analysis were first polished with 
800-, 1,200- and 2,000-grit silicon carbide papers, followed by dia-
mond polishing and subsequent ion polishing. The high-resolution 
TEM characterizations of the cast, annealed and tensioned samples 
were conducted with an aberration-corrected FEI Titan-Themis instru-
ment and a double aberration-corrected FEI Titan-Themis-Z instru-
ment at 300 kV at the nanoscale and atomic scale with a Bestron TEM 
double-tilt mechanical holder. HAADF–STEM was performed with an 
FEI Titan-Themis-Z instrument containing a superXG2 EDS addon to 
analyse the atomic-resolution structure and the chemical distribution. 
High-resolution in situ TEM mechanical tests were conducted with a 
Bestron TEM double-tilt mechanical holder.
The STEM (FEI Titan-Themis-Z) was performed at 300 kV with a con-
vergence semiangle of 25.2 mrad. The apertures of the optics were 
C1 = 2 mm, C2 = 70 μm and C3 = 2 mm. The spot size was nine. The gun 
lens was 783 and the beam current was set between 25 and 30 pA. The 
dwell time was 2 μs per pixel with a map size of 610 × 582 pixels. The 
full EDS mapping process took roughly 0.7 h to achieve a reasonably 
high signal-to-noise ratio. Before the tensile test, the TEM specimens 
from the cast and annealed alloys were cut along the casting and roll-
ing directions, respectively. After different tensile strains, the TEM 
specimens were cut from the gauge section of the dog-bone-shaped 
tensile sample, and then mechanically ground to a thickness of about 
30 μm, followed by precise dimpling to a thickness of 10 μm with a 
GATAN 656 instrument and ion milling with a GATAN PIPS II 695 instru-
ment (Supplementary Fig. 8).
In situ scanning electron microscopy observation was performed 
using a FEI-Quanta 650 field-emission SEM operated at 20 kV. In situ 
tensile deformation of the sample was performed using a Gatan 
microtension tester at an initial strain rate of 1.0 × 10 −3 s−1. T ensile 
dog-bone-shaped samples with a cross-sectional area of 1.5 × 0.8 mm2 
and a gauge length of 2 mm were cut from the annealed alloy along the 
rolling direction by electrical discharge machining.
Measurements of mechanical properties
T ensile tests at room temperature were performed on an INSTRON-5966 
universal tensile testing machine at an initial strain rate of 1.0 × 10−3 s−1. 
Dog-bone-shaped tensile samples with a cross-sectional area of 
1.0 × 2.5 mm2 and a gauge length of 20 mm were cut from the cast and 
annealed alloys along the solidification and rolling direction by elec-
trical discharge machining (Supplementary Fig. 8). Elongation was 
measured with an extensometer.
In situ synchrotron high-energy XRD tests
High-energy X-rays with an energy of 60.05 keV, a wavelength of 
0.02065 nm and a beam size of 0.7 × 0.7 mm were used to obtain two- 
dimensional diffraction patterns in transmission geometry with 
a Perkin-Elmer large-area detector at Beamline 3W1 at the Beijing 
Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF). A Gatan microtension tester 
equipped with a home-built tensile jig was used for in situ tensile 
testing. One-dimensional XRD line spectra were generated using FIT 
two-dimensional software and the diffraction peaks were fitted to a 
Gaussian distribution. The in situ tensile tests were performed with 
a strain rate of 1 × 10 −3 s−1. The schematic of the in situ high-energy 
XRD tensile test is shown in the inset of Fig. 4b. The loading direction 
and transverse direction correspond to azimuth angles of 0° and 90°, 
respectively. The two-dimensional diffraction patterns were converted 
into one-dimensional high-energy XRD diffraction spectra by caking 
and integrating over a ±5° range along specified azimuth angles.

Atomic column IDA method
The STEM–HAADF image is chemical element discernible, and the 
brightness is roughly proportional to the exponential function of the 
atomic number, also known as the Z contrast image. So far, there are two 
main methods: quantitative HAADF library matching49–51 and statisti-
cal decomposition52. Here, we have developed a ‘nearest’ neighbour 
atomic column IDA method, which effectively reduces the undesir-
able influence of experimental noise and thickness. The IDA method 
uses HAADF intensity contrast to offer an unprecedented microscopic 
picture of the structure at the atomic level. IDA quantification consists 
of two main steps: (1) rescaling the image intensity by measuring the 
intensity of the incident electron probe but subtracting the vacuum 
intensity and (2) obtaining the site occupancy of atoms.
The imaging process is performed by a self-developed MATLAB code.
