1. Introduction
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) represent a highly promising 
class of novel alloy systems, which are composed of multiple refractory 
elements, such as Cr, Mo, Hf, V, Nb, W, Ta, Zr and Ti, in equal or near- 
equimolar ratios [ 1 , 2 ]. RHEAs exhibit excellent mechanical properties 
at room and high temperature conditions, such as resistance to high 
temperature oxidation [ 3 , 4 ] and corrosion [ 5 ], attributed to reduced 
diffusion rates and enhanced lattice distortion. Moreover, many RHEAs 
show superior high-temperature mechanical properties compared to 
traditional Ni-based superalloys [ 6 , 7 ]. RHEAs offer broad application 
prospects and represent promising alternatives to Ni-based superalloys 
for use in higher-temperature environments. Currently, the main fabri -
cation methods for RHEAs are vacuum arc melting [ 8 – 10 ] and powder 
metallurgy [ 11 , 12 ]. However, the high melting points and poor 
machinability of RHEAs make conventional fabrication both costly and 
geometrically restrictive, thereby limiting their application potential.
As additive manufacturing technologies continue to advance, 
increasing attention has been conducted on their application to the 
fabrication of high-entropy alloys [ 13 , 14 ]. Zhu et al. [ 2 ] and Zhang et al. 
[ 15 ] employed selective laser melting (SLM) technology to fabricate 
refractory high-entropy alloys. Xie et al. [ 16 ] utilized refractory high- 
entropy alloy powder as the raw material and employed selective elec -
tron beam melting (SEBM) to fabricate TaNbZrTi alloy, followed by an 
investigation of the mechanical properties of the alloy samples. Xiao 
et al. [ 17 , 18 ] prepared high-entropy alloy powder using a physical 
mixing method and subsequently investigated the manufacturing pro -
cess, microstructure, and mechanical properties of different RHEAs

2. Experimental methods
2.1. MWAAM equipment and deposition process
The multi-wire arc additive manufacturing equipment consists of a 
three-axis computer numerical control (CNC) machine tool, four wire 
feeding systems, a welding machine with hot wire power supply, a 
Tungsten inert gas (TIG) welding torch and a workbench, as shown in 
Fig. 1 . During the 3D printing process, the substrate is fixed on the 
workbench and preheated by moving the welding torch along the 
scanning path without wire feeding. After preheating, the alloy wire is 
fed to the TIG welding torch via the wire feeding mechanism, and then it 
is melted and deposited under the synergistic effect of the welding torch 
and the hot wire power. The entire printing process is conducted in an 
argon atmosphere with a purity exceeding 99.99 % to prevent oxidation 
of the metal at high temperatures. The printing process is recorded in 
real time by a Xiris welding camera and continuously monitored using 
Xiris WeldStudio 2.1.6 software.
The manufacturing process of the V
0.5
Nb
0.5
ZrTi RHEA is shown in 
Fig. 2 , where Fig. 2 (a) shows the relative positions of four metal wires. 
To achieve stable deposition during the manufacturing process, it is 
essential to maintain liquid bridge transfer, which involves continuous 
contact between liquid droplets and the molten pool, forming a stable 
liquid metal bridge [ 21 ]. The metal deposition process is illustrated in 
Fig. 2 (b) and (c). During the base current period, as shown in Fig. 2 (b), 
all wires converge at the center of the molten pool, where they melts and 
merges at the same point, generating a single pre-alloyed droplet. In the 
peak current period shown in Fig. 2 (c), the increased arc force causes 
the pre-alloyed droplet to separate from all four wires, and the molten 
droplets flatten to form a new molten pool surface and cover on the weld 
bead. This pre-alloyed droplet transfer mode enables full diffusion and 
chemical reactions of each metal element, thereby producing a part with 
favorable macroscopic morphology.

softening arises from the replacement of strain-hardened grains by new,

◦

During dynamic compression, the cyclic process of “ pinning to stress 
concentration to unpinning to stress drop to re-pinning ” occurs repeat -

scopic stress-strain curve a phenomenon known as the Portevin-Le 
Chatelier (PLC) effect [ 38 , 39 ]. When the temperature increases to 
300

5. Conclusions
This study successfully fabricates a ductile V
0.5
Nb
0.5
ZrTi RHEA using 
multi-wire arc additive manufacturing (MWAAM). The microstructure, 
as well as the quasi-static and dynamic compression properties of the 
alloy at various temperatures, were systematically investigated. The 
effects of temperature and strain rate on the compressive properties of 
the alloy are discussed in detail. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) By employing the low-cost and high-efficiency MWAAM tech -
nology, the crack-free and defect-free V
0.5
Nb

with the desired composition can be successfully fabricated.

0.5
Nb
0.5
ZrTi RHEA exhibits equiaxed 
grains oriented perpendicular to the deposition direction, with an 
average grain size of 162.8 μ m. The alloy forms a multiphase

(3) The V
0.5
Nb
0.5
ZrTi RHEA fabricated by MWAAM exhibits a 
multiphase microstructure consisting of two BCC phases and a V- 
rich Laves phase.
(4) The yield strength of this RHEA shows an enhancement ranging
