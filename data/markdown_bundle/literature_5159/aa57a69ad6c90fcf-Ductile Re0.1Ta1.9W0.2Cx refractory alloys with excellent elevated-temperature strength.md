1. Introduction
High-temperature structural materials are considered as critical 
components in national defense and industry. With the development of 
technology, there are stricter requirements on the performance of high- 
temperature materials in some components across aerospace and other 
sectors. For instance, the turbine inlet temperature of the fifth genera -
tion aero-engines can reach 2000 
◦
C; the service temperature of ultra- 
high speed aircraft nose and wing leading edges can reach 2200 
◦
C at 
a speed of Mach 8 [ 1 , 2 ]; and gas injected into turbine blades exceeds 
1600 
◦
C in the civil industry [ 3 , 4 ]. Nickel- or cobalt-based alloys are the 
most commonly used high-temperature materials, including INCONEL 
718, Haynes 230, and MAR-M 247. However, the maximum service 
temperature for these alloys is usually lower than 1200 
◦
C [ 5 ]. Con -
ventional refractory alloys based on tantalum-tungsten, molybde -
num-tungsten, and rhenium-tungsten alloys exhibit satisfactory ductility 
and machinability at room temperature.
Nevertheless, the strength of these refractory alloys tends to decrease 
obviously to less than 200 MPa when the temperature surpasses 1400 
◦
C 
[ 6 ]. As a result, it becomes challenging to fulfill the requirements of

properties [ 7 ], among which refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) 
with body-centered-cubic (BCC) structures demonstrate good

temperature to meet the requirements for use under extreme conditions 
and processing complex precision structures.
The trade-off between high-temperature strength and room- 
temperature plasticity poses a critical challenge in developing and 
applying high-temperature refractory alloys. Chemical composition 
optimization has been verified to be an effective approach to enhance 
the mechanical properties of the NbMoTaW RHEA. Additionally, it is 
promising to predict their mechanical properties using first-principles 
calculations and machine learning to aid in the compositional optimi -
zation of refractory high-entropy alloys [ 9 – 11 ]. The addition and sub -
stitution of light metal elements in NbMoTaW RHEA have been widely 
adopted as a common strategy [ 12 – 17 ]. Although this approach can 
considerably improve the compression plasticity of the alloy at room 
temperature, it often substantially decreases high-temperature strength. 
By introducing ceramic phases, the high-temperature strength and 
room-temperature compression plasticity of NbMoTaW RHEA can be 
improved simultaneously [ 18 – 22 ]. However, excessive ceramic phase 
can lead to significant decrease in room-temperature compression 
plasticity [ 23 – 25 ]. Tungsten-based refractory alloys are characterized 
by high strength and hardness, while tungsten typically exhibits brit -
tleness at room temperature and high ductile-brittle transition temper -
ature [ 26 , 27 ]. Ta-10W refractory alloy possesses excellent 
room-temperature compression plasticity, but its high-temperature 
strength is insufficient for practical applications. Increasing the pro -
portion of tungsten in Ta-10W alloy can improve the high-temperature 
strength of the alloy, but it also greatly decreases the 
room-temperature compression plasticity [ 28 ].
Rhenium (Re) has the second-highest melting point after tungsten 
and has been widely used in nickel-based high-temperature alloys [ 29 ,
30 ]. Compared with pure tungsten, W-Re alloys have better ductility and 
fracture toughness [ 31 , 32 ]. The addition of rhenium raises the recrys -
tallization temperature of the tungsten-based alloy while lowering its 
ductile-brittle transition temperature [ 33 ]. Moreover, rhenium miti -
gates lattice distortion, facilitates grain refinement, and enhances the 
solid solution strengthening effect [ 34 ]. Previous studies have demon -
strated that in comparison to MC-FCC carbides, M

experiments were cut from the ingots fabricated via vacuum arc melting.

The XRD specimens were roughly and finely ground with a series of 
sandpaper foils, and electrolytic polishing was then performed on the 
surface of the specimens to remove the surface stress and strain layers.

nitric acid + 43 % hydrofluoric) for 30 s at room temperature. Thin-foil 
specimens for TEM examination were mechanically ground and thinned
