Effect of Nb on pore structure and tensile property
of Ti–48Al cellular alloy

Abstract
TiAl based alloys have been investigated traditionally as potential high temperature structural materials. Another interesting ﬁeld where TiAl
based cellular alloys were fabricated by elemental powder metallurgy (EPM) is shown in this study. The effects of Nb addition forms (Nb elemental
powder and Nb–Al alloying powder) and contents on pore structures and tensile properties of Ti–48Al cellular alloy were investigated. It was found
that the phases identiﬁed are /H9253-TiAl and /H9251
2-Ti3Al when Nb content is less than 2%, and pore structures of Ti–48Al–2Nb alloy are similar to those
of Ti–48Al cellular alloy for both Nb addition forms. When Nb content is beyond 2%, an additional AlNb2 phase is observed, and pore structures
also change. Lots of particles form on the surface of the skeleton for adopting Nb elemental powder, and micropores appear along with big pores for
adopting Nb–Al alloying powder. With the increase of Nb content, tensile strength rapidly decreases for adopting Nb elemental powder. However,
the contrary phenomenon happens for the other form.

1. Introduction
An extensive research on TiAl based alloys has been per-
formed owing to their good high temperature properties and
low densities [1–4]. However, the inherent low-temperature
ductility of TiAl based alloys is poor, limiting their further struc-
tural applications [5,6]. Therefore, many efforts were made to
improve their ductility, such as adding alloying elements [7,8],
heat treatment [9], hot work [10], etc. It has been found that
Nb is the essential and effective element to improve mechanical
properties, especially, high temperature strength of TiAl alloys
[8]. Unfortunately, TiAl–Nb alloys are primarily utilized in the
automotive industry in a small scale and still step away from
aerospace ﬁelds [11].
It is interesting to fabricate TiAl based functional materials
such as cellular ﬁlters to avoid their poor ductility, which can
be widely used for seawater desalination [12], environmental
protection [13], pharmaceutical separation [14], chemical engi-

2. Experimental
The nominal compositions of the experimental alloys studied were
Ti–48Al–xNb (x = 0, 2, 4, 6) (at.%). Two addition forms of Nb were adopted,
i.e. Nb elemental powder and Nb–Al alloying powder. For elemental Ti and
Al powders, the mean particle sizes were in the range of 30–50 /H9262m and their
purities were higher than 99.5%. For Nb and Nb–Al powders, the mean parti-
cle sizes were smaller than 25 /H9262m, and their purities were higher than 99.7%.
The powders were mixed for 10 h using a V-blender without any additive. Pow-
der mixtures were die-pressed under 200 MPa to green compacts with 32 mm
diameter and 3 mm height. The compacts were initially sintered to 550–630
◦C
with the heating rate of 10◦C/min, and hold for 2 h in order to generate sufﬁcient
porous structure through the Kirkendall effect. Subsequently, the compacts were
heated to 1300
◦C with the heating rate of less than 5 ◦C/min, and hold for 1 h
under vacuum (10−3 Pa).
Differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) was adopted to understand the
reaction process with the heating rate of 10 ◦C/min. The Ti–48Al based alloys
obtained were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu K /H9251radiation to
identify the phase transformation. The pore structure was observed by scanning

4. Discussion
4.1. Pore formation mechanism of Ti–48Al alloy
For TiAl based cellular alloys, pores formed during diffusion
reactions and crystal growths belong to reactive pores, which are
the main part of pores in Ti–48Al alloy. Besides, a certain num-
ber of clearance pores are inherited from green compacts after
reactive synthesis of Ti and Al elements. The formation of reac-
tive pore is related with the reactive mechanism. According to
reactive mechanism between Ti and Al elements, the formation
of reactive pores has three phases as follows: (1) Ti powders
are covered with Al powders because Al powders are easier
to deform after mixing and cold pressing. Below the melting
point of Al, TiAl
3 forms by diffusion reaction between Ti pow-
der and Al powder. Meanwhile, Kirkendall effect happens due
to the diffusivity of Al is much higher than that of Ti in the
TiAl
3 phase [16,17]. The net movement and consumption of Al
must be balanced by the opposite net vacancy ﬂux, resulting in
a large number of vacancies near Al atoms. Excessive vacancies
will condense into pores to reduce the Gibbs free energy of the
system. With the growth and the boundary movement of TiAl
3
crystal, these vacancies recompose, congregate, grow up and
run through, then pores form. (2) When sintering temperature
increases to the melting point of Al, remanent Al powders melt
and react rapidly with Ti powders to form TiAl
3 continuously.
At the same time, there are better wetting between Al liquid and
Ti and TiAl
3 particles, which makes Al liquid ﬁlters into crys-
tal boundary of TiAl3 by the capillary force [18]. Large TiAl3
crystals are crashed into lots of small particles, and initial pores
are recomposed. (3) With the increase of sintering temperature,
further reaction between Ti and TiAl
3 leads to the formation of
the intermetallic compounds TiAl2,T i3Al and TiAl. In addition,
Ti3Al can further react with TiAl 2 to form TiAl. Finally, only
Ti3Al and TiAl phases are present[16]. Pores undergo the third
distribution and the second growth with the transformation of
these phases.
The distribution of the reactive pores is homogeneous
in the macrostructure. However, it is inhomogeneous in the
microstructure. The main reasons are: (1) the shape of Ti powder
is irregular, leading to the inhomogeneity of Kirkendall effect
between Ti and Al elements. (2) The inhomogeneity of diffu-
sion reaction is deteriorated since there is about 5% clearance
pores in the green compacts. (3) There are H
2 and adsorbing
water in the Ti powders due to their hydride-dehydride fab-
rication process. H
2 and adsorbing water escape in the early
phase of sintering process and form pores. Thereafter, it is
deduced that pore structure with homogeneous distribution will
be obtained if elemental powders with regular shapes and low
gas contents and green compacts with less clearance pores are
adopted.
4.2. Effect of Nb on pore formation mechanism of Ti–48Al
based cellular alloys
The effect of Nb addition on pore structures is negligible
when Nb content is 2%, which suggests that Nb element dis-
solves completely into Ti–Al skeleton by diffusion. However,
pore structures change when Nb content is beyond 2%. More-
over, the effect of addition forms on pore structures is different.
Lots of/H9253or /H9251
2 particles dissolved Nb form on the surface of pore
skeleton for adopting Nb elemental powders. The contact proba-
bility between Ti and Al powders decreases owing to the addition
of Nb powders, and pressureless sintering process is adopted,
which provide the premise for the formation of AlNb
2 phase.
Nb powders are covered with Al and Ti powders after mixing
and cold pressing due to their ﬁner granularity and lower duc-
tility. Moreover, the reactive ability between Ti and Al is higher
than that between Nb and Al because the diffusivity of Nb is
the lowest. Therefore, AlNb
2 phase is hardly observed on the
surfaces of the skeleton since it is covered with /H9253or /H92512 phases.
AlNb2 phase is detected inside the skeleton (Fig. 12a).

Another change of pore structure happens for adopting
Nb–Al alloying powder. According to the composition analysis
of micropore skeleton, it can be concluded that micropores are
generated from diffusion reaction between Ti powders and Al
in Nb–Al alloying powders. However, big pores are formed by
Kirkendall effect between Ti and Al elemental powders. The dif-
fusivity of Al element in Nb–Al alloying powder is smaller than
that in Al elemental powder due to the restraint effect of Nb ele-
ment. Moreover, the diffusivity of Nb is the lowest in Ti–Al–Nb
system [19]. Therefore, Nb content is higher in the skeleton of
micropores than in that of big pores. When Nb content is higher,
such as 4%, the diffusion of Nb element is incomplete and AlNb
2
phase forms. In addition, AlNb 2 phase may be the remainder
from Nb–Al alloying powder consisting of AlNb 2 and Al3Nb.
AlNb2 phase is observed in the skeleton of micropore by further
analysis (Fig. 12b).
4.3. Effect of Nb on tensile properties of Ti–48Al based
cellular alloys
It is well known that the tensile strength is related with the
porosity for cellular materials. A number of expressions can
be used to describe the porosity-strength behavior of cellular
materials. One of the simplest methods was proposed by Rice
[20] based on the minimum solid area approach and the resulting
porosity-strength dependence can be approximated closely by an
exponential function:
σ
b = σ0 e−bθ (1)
where σb is the tensile strength of the cellular material, σ0 the
tensile strength of the densiﬁed material with same composition,
θ the porosity of the cellular material and b is the constant.
Fig. 13 shows the relationship between porosity and Nb con-
tent. With the increase of Nb content, porosity increases for
adopting Nb powders. However, porosity decreases gradually for
adopting Nb–Al alloying powders even if more and more micro-
pores appear. According to the porosity-strength formula, it is
not difﬁcult to understand the relationship between the tensile
strength and Nb content for both Nb forms.

It has been found that Nb can signiﬁcantly improve the
mechanical properties of TiAl based alloys by the solid solu-
tion strength. In the pressureless sintering process, Nb cannot
dissolve into matrix completely when Nb content is beyond 2%,
and AlNb
2 phase forms. The existence of AlNb2 decreases the
mechanical properties due to its high brittleness [21]. There-
fore, the increase trend of tensile strength is not evident for
adding Nb–Al alloying powder when Nb content is beyond 2%.
However, the tensile strength decreases evidently for adding
Nb elemental powder. Generally, compared with the tensile
strengths of TiAl based dense alloys, those of TiAl based cel-
lular alloys at room temperature are poor, but they are yet good
enough for ﬁltration application.
5. Conclusions
(1) The pore structures of Ti–48Al–2Nb alloys are similar to
that of Ti–48Al alloy for both Nb addition forms.
(2) When Nb content increases to 4%, particles form on the
surface of skeleton and they become much more with the
increase of Nb content. However, another change of pore
structure is observed for adopting Nb–Al alloying powders.
