Study on the inﬂuence of temperature on the mechanical properties across multiple scales has been a 
focus on the research of Hastelloy-X (HX) alloys for the application in high-temperature structure com- 
ponents. In this work, Molecular Dynamics (MD) and Crystal Plasticity (CP) are put together to solve it 
from atomic scale to mesoscopic scale. MD research indicates that the deformation of HX alloy occurs 
in two stages at temperature below 300 K: initially, as stacking fault deforms, stacking fault can trans- 
form into twinning with increasing strain. When the temperature exceeds 300 K, deformation primarily 
forms a stacking fault. The twinning deformation path transforms from intrinsic stacking fault to extrin- 
sic stacking fault and then to twinning. A mesoscopic-scale CP model was developed using atomic-scale 
deformation mechanisms to bridge the gap between deformation mechanisms and experimental results. 
The CP results indicate a functional relationship between the strength of HX alloy and temperature. This 
relationship appears insensitive to crystal texture and grain shape. Incorporating grain morphology and 
texture into the model signiﬁcantly impacts the strength response of calculating HX alloy. After the ten- 
sile deformation of HX alloy at 300 and 1173 K, the atomic scale deformation results characterized by 
transmission electron microscopy are aligned with the MD simulation results. The relationship between 
strength and temperature predicted by CP results has also been validated. A thorough investigation into 
the deformation behavior of HX alloys across different scales, employing MD and CP models, introduces 
a novel approach for predicting the mechanical properties of superalloys.

1. Introduction 
Hastelloy-X (HX) alloy, an face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal 
structure nickel-based superalloy with the main element of Ni-Cr- 
Mo-Fe, is mainly applied in aerospace thermal protection struc- 
tures and engine sealing structures [1–3] . The service temperature 
of thermal protection structures for hypersonic vehicles instanta- 
neously reaches up to 900 °C [4] . The design of new-generation 
thermal protection structures aims to integrate material and struc- 
ture performance [5] . The new generation of hypersonic vehicle 
thermal protection structures designed by countries worldwide 
aim coincidentally at material-structure-performance integration 
solutions [6] .

The combination of additive manufacturing and traditional pro-

primary reasons for the certiﬁcation barrier. Firstly, the impact of 
crystal orientation on the mechanical properties of additively man- 
ufactured structures. The crystal orientation of additively manu- 
factured HX alloys is closely tied to the additive manufacturing 
method employed, and the path followed [10–12] . Secondly, the 
inﬂuence of temperature on the deformation mechanism of HX al- 
loys. These alloys are used for thermal protection of metals and 
can experience a variety of deformation mechanisms while op- 
erating within a temperature range from room temperature to 
900 °C. Each temperature variation brings about different defor- 
mation mechanisms in service [13] . In recent years, many scholars 
have studied the effect of temperature on the mechanical proper- 
ties of alloys at different scales to solve the above two problems

Based on Newton’s second law, Molecular Dynamics (MD) can 
simulate the atomic scale change during alloy deformation at dif- 
ferent temperatures [18–21] . Pan et al. [22] found a signiﬁcant 
negative correlation between the yield stress and the deformation 
temperature when using MD to study the deformation behavior 
of Iron-based medium entropy alloys. The effect of temperature 
on the stacking fault energy (SFE) was investigated, and the effect 
of temperature on stress was analyzed on the atomic scale. Chen 
et al. [23] used MD to study the effect of precipitation phase and 
temperature on the tensile behavior of single crystals in nickel- 
based single crystals. In the study, it was found that when the 
temperature was increased from 1 to 300 K, the yield strength de- 
creased with the increase in temperature; when the temperature 
was increased from 300 to 600 K, the yield strength increased with 
the temperature rising, and when the temperature was higher than 
600 K, the yield strength decreased with the increase of tempera- 
ture. The increase in yield strength is caused by the fact that the 
γ/ γ’ phase interface has a complete dislocation network to resist 
deformation. The decrease in yield strength is caused by the dislo- 
cation creeping motion. 
Some early attempts to develop Crystal Plasticity (CP) models 
exploring the effect of temperature on the deformation behavior 
of alloys are reported in Refs. [24–26] . A CP model for dynamic 
precipitated phases in alloys was proposed by Bignon et al. [27] . 
The model was applied to study the effect of the size of the pre- 
cipitated phase distribution on the mechanical properties of alloys. 
It can accurately describe the deformation behavior of aluminum 
alloy materials at 150 °C. The effects of precipitated phases, dislo- 
cations, and phase transitions were analyzed of temperature on the 
yield stress of high-entropy alloys by Zhang et al. [24] . A CP model 
was developed to predict the effect of temperature on the me- 
chanical properties of tensile models with different grain sizes. The 
model can accurately describe the deformation behavior of high- 
entropy alloys at different temperatures. We analyze the deforma- 
tion behavior of superalloys using both CP and MD techniques. The 
atomic-scale deformation behavior of HX alloys in relation to tem- 
perature is investigated using MD. Furthermore, the macroscopic 
mechanical behavior of the alloys in response to temperature is 
examined through CP. Connecting CP and MD outcomes is crucial 
in bridging the gap between the atomic and macroscopic scales. 
This connection enables us to comprehend the softening behavior 
of superalloys at various temperatures. 
This study has three main objectives using CP and MD: (1) the 
Atomic motion mechanism of HX alloys during stretching at differ- 
ent temperatures; (2) The effect of Texture on the tensile proper- 
ties of HX alloys; (3) The effect of temperature on the tensile prop- 
erties of HX alloys. This paper is structured as follows: ﬁrst, the 
simulation methods of the HX alloys are expounded ( Section 2 ); 
next, based on the analysis of the simulation results, the macro- 
scopic and microscopic mechanisms of temperature effects on the 
tensile properties of superalloys are discussed in detail ( Sections 3 
and 4 ); ﬁnally, some conclusions obtained from this study are 
given ( Section 5 ). The ﬂowchart depicting the article structure is 
shown in Fig. 1 . 
2. Computational methods and microstructure characterization 
MD and CP are calculated differently. Therefore, in this sec- 
tion, we will analyze in detail from two parts: the Computational 
method of MD and the Computational method of CP. Finally, the 
simulation results are compared with the microstructural charac- 
terization results. 
2.1. Computational method of MD 
The MD simulations were performed using the Large-scale 
Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) pack- 
age [28] . Lee and co-workers [29] gave modiﬁed embedded atom 
method (MEAM) potentials for six elements of Ni-Cr-Co-Mo-V-Al 
in machine learning of high-entropy alloy design models through 
neural networks. This paper uses this MEAM potential for all the 
parts involving MD simulation. The model was created in the form 
of Ni47 Cr25 Co2 Mo6 Fe20 (atomic ratio model for HX alloy). The ini- 
tial lattice length used for the model was 3.61 ˚A. Two main types 
of MD simulations were carried out: one was a tensile simulation, 
and the other was a generalized SFE simulation.

employed in MD simulations to investigate necking and eventual 
fracturing in a tensile model. The Open source software OVITO 
was utilized for performing visualization and defect characteriza-

The TEM samples were obtained by punching disks with a di-

4. Discussion and outlook 
This study aimed to investigate the inﬂuence of temperature on 
the deformation behavior of HX alloys. While previous studies have 
explored the inﬂuences of low temperatures on the deformation 
mechanism of these alloys [ 45 , 60 ], the fundamental cause for the 
disparity in mechanical properties between high and low temper- 
atures remains unclear. Our calculations at the atomic scale indi- 
cate that ISF structures emerge within the crystals when yielding 
occurs in HX alloys. Some researchers have found, through TEM 
characterization, that stacking faults and slip are the initial struc- 
tures that arise in the yielding process of HX alloys [ 45 , 60 , 61 ]. 
The atomic-scale calculations align with the experimental results.

5. Conclusions 
The deformation behavior of HX superalloys at different tem- 
peratures was studied by MD and CP. MD was tested in tension 
over a wide temperature range from 1 to 1200 K, and CP was 
tested in a wide temperature range from 300 to 1200 K. To explain 
the mechanical properties, the microstructural analysis after defor- 
mation was carried out by TEM. The following conclusions can be 
drawn. 
(1) MD studies show that the plastic deformation mechanism 
of HX alloys below 300 K is mainly twinning and stacking 
fault deformation. Reﬁning grain size through twinning de- 
formation can increase dislocation density, thereby leading 
to a more signiﬁcant low-temperature elongation rate and 
higher ultimate strength of HX alloy. When the HX alloy is

mechanism is deformation due to stacking faults. 
(2) The effects of temperature, grain shape, and crystal texture 
on the mechanical properties of HX alloys were investigated 
by CP. Determine the functional relationship between the 
yield strength of alloy and temperature. Bulk HX alloys with 
rolled textures have different tensile strengths and harden- 
ing processes in different directions. 
(3) The TEM characterization reveals that the primary deforma- 
tion mechanism of HX alloys at room temperature is the 
movement of stacking faults and dislocations. The persis- 
tent strain hardening is also attributed to the interaction be- 
tween dislocations and stacking faults, which aligns with the 
ﬁndings from MD simulations.
