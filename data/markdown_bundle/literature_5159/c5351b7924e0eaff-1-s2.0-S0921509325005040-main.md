 A B S T R A C T
The effects of Cu additions on microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCux (x = 
0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) high entropy alloys (HEA) prepared via the arc melting technique over a wide temperature 
range are investigated. The initial and postmortem samples are characterized by X-ray diffraction, scanning 
electron microscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, energy dispersive spectroscopy and transmission electron 
microscopy. The results indicate that with the increase of Cu content, the number of Cu-rich precipitates 
increases, and these Cu-rich precipitates share coherent interfaces with the matrix. Quasi-static tension tests 
reveal that the yield strength of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCux alloy is enhanced at lower temperatures or with higher 
Cu contents. Given the structure-based strength model, the improvement can be attributed to precipitation 
strengthening of uniformly dispersed nano-size Cu-rich precipitates. All alloys exhibit simultaneous superior 
strength and ductility at low temperatures. The investigation reveals multiple deformation mechanisms in 
Al0.1CoCrFeNiCux alloys with different Cu contents subjected to different degrees of deformation at different 
temperatures. Both Cu-rich precipitates and higher temperatures suppress the activation of deformation 
twinning. Dislocations, stacking faults, immobile Lomer-Cottrell locks, kink bands and deformation twins are 
found in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu0.1 alloy at 123 K, in contrast with only dislocations in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu0.5 alloy at 
673 K.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are an innovative category of mate-
rials, typically comprising five or more elements in approximately 
equiatomic ratios [1–8]. The unique compositions and chemical struc-
tures of these alloys offer the potential to achieve exceptional combi-
nations of mechanical properties [9–13]. The rational design of these 
alloys depends on a comprehensive understanding of the composition–
structure–property relationships within a vast compositional range [3,
4]. For HEAs as potential structural materials, extensive attention 
has been given to their strength and ductility at room temperature, 
as well as their deformability at both high and cryogenic tempera-
tures [14–18]. However, the yield strength of face-center cubic (FCC) 
structure HEAs is typically low, which limits their suitability for high-
performance structural applications [19–22]. Similar to conventional 
alloys such as steel or Ti alloys, the crystal structure, microstructure 
and mechanical properties are strongly related to the compositions of

The element Cu has been widely used as the precipitation strength-

For instance, the Super304H austenitic heat-resistant steel, which is

demonstrates exceptional properties due to the precipitation of nano-
scale Cu-rich phases in the austenitic matrix [23–25]. Considering 
that Cu is a widely used element for precipitation strengthening due 
to its low solubility and low price, recent studies have attempted 
to add Cu to HEAs to enhance material strength through precipi-
tation strengthening [26–30]. The element Cu exhibits a propensity 
to instigate liquid-phase separation prior to solidification, leading to 
the formation of a Cu-rich supersaturated solid solution interspersed 
with nano-sized precipitates [31]. Previous research suggests that the 
incorporation of Cu intensifies the strength in the CoCrFeMoNiCu𝑥

HEAs, attributable to the substantial positive mixing enthalpy of Cu 
and its weak bonds with numerous other metallic elements [26–28,32]. 
This results in Cu’s predilection to segregate towards the inter-dendrite 
region, subsequently generating Cu-rich precipitation phases [33]. In

solution-treated and quenched condition of the alloy. Clusters enriched 
with up to 80 at.% Cu are discernible, accompanied by a minor degree 
of Al enrichment adjacent to the Cu-rich regions. The clustering of 
Cu in this HEA can be leveraged to achieve a more uniform distri-
bution of fine-scale L12 precipitates, thereby augmenting precipitation 
strengthening at both room and elevated temperatures.
For widely used FCC HEAs, Al0.1CoCrFeNi alloy has demonstrated 
remarkable tensile ductility and promising ballistic performance but a 
low yield strength [34,35]. To enhance its yield strength, considerable 
efforts have been focused on altering the microstructure through severe 
plastic deformation [36–38] or heterogeneous structures [39]. How-
ever, there exist essentially no studies about Cu addition method in 
Al0.1CoCrFeNi alloys. Previous results showed that Cu-rich precipitates 
within the FCC matrix impede dislocation movement, resulting in a 
significant enhancement of strength [32,33,40]. There are two pressing 
questions to be understood: whether comprehensive properties, includ-
ing strength and ductility, can be achieved in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys, 
and how the Cu additions affect the microstructures and deformation 
mechanisms at different temperatures or Cu contents.
In this study, a series of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys with varying Cu 
contents are synthesized. The effects of Cu additions on microstruc-
tures, mechanical properties at different temperatures and deformation 
mechanisms are systematically explored. The increase in Cu content 
has a negligible influence on the grain size of as-cast alloys, but leads 
to the increase of Cu-rich precipitates. The significant improvement 
in yield strength is mainly ascribed to the precipitation strengthening. 
All alloys exhibit simultaneous superior strength and ductility at low 
temperatures. The temperature and Cu content affect the stacking fault 
energy of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys, resulting in various deformation 
mechanisms.
2. Materials and experiments
The Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 (x = 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) alloys used in this study 
are supplied by Beijing Yanbang New Material Co. Ltd. All alloys are 
fabricated by arc melting in a high-purity argon atmosphere. High-
purity (99.9 wt%) raw elements are weighed according to the nominal 
molar ratios and thoroughly mixed. The alloys are melted at 1950 K, 
and each ingot are remelted five times to ensure complete melting 
and homogeneity. After melting, the ingots are cast into water-cooled 
copper molds to form cylindrical alloys with dimensions of 30 mm and 
100 mm in length. For the convenience, the Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys 
with x = 0, 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5 are designated as Cu0, Cu1, Cu3, Cu5 
alloys, respectively.
Samples are dog-bone-shaped with gauge dimensions of 6 × 1.5 
× 0.5 mm3 are taken from the as-cast alloys. Tensile tests are then 
performed on a homemade material test system (MTS) with a strain 
rate of 10−3 s−1 at the 123 K, 298 K and 673 K. To correct the 
strain values, the digital image correlation (DIC) technique is employed. 
Three repeated tests are performed for each type of sample to ensure 
reproducible and reliable data. Moreover, based on a laboratory X-ray 
based in situ multi-scale measurement system [41], simultaneous X-
ray diffraction (XRD) and optical DIC measurements are conducted on 
different Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys at 298 K.
The microstructures of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys before and after ten-
sile loading are characterized via XRD, scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). XRD 
measurements are conducted on a Panalytical X-ray diffractometer 
with a Cu K𝛼 radiation source (0.15406 nm wavelength). SEM, EDS 
and EBSD are performed with an FEI Quanta 250 scanning electron 
microscopy equipped with the Oxford EDS or EBSD detectors. For the 
EBSD analysis, the samples are mechanically grounded, polished and 
electro-polished sequentially. The electro-polishing is conducted in a 
10% oxalic acid solution at a voltage of 20 V and a current of 0.8 A for 
90 s. For the TEM characterization, samples are mechanically ground 
to 80 μm , and then thinned to an electron-transparent thickness using 
a twin-jet polishing system with the same solution as for the EBSD 
characterization. TEM characterizations are performed in a JEM 2100F.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructures and elemental distributions
The microstructures of different Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys before ten-
sion loading are presented in Fig. 1. The inverse pole figure (IPF) maps 
reveal that all alloys consist of uniformly distributed equiaxed grains, 
and the average grain sizes are ∼239 μm , 275 μm , 216 μm and 223 μm
for Cu0, Cu1, Cu3 and Cu5 alloys, respectively. The distributions of 
grain boundaries misorientation are relatively uniform (Fig. 1i). In Fig. 
1g, the kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps of all alloys show 
negligible residual plastic deformation with low average KAM (𝜁av) 
value of 0.52◦, 0.49◦, 0.56◦ and 0.60◦ for Cu0, Cu1, Cu3 and Cu5 
alloys, respectively.
As shown in Fig. 2, some precipitates appear in the Cu1 alloy, 
and the number of precipitates increases markedly as the Cu content 
rises. In the Cu5 alloy, abundant precipitates with different sizes are 
observed, as shown in Fig. 2e. Given the EDS surface scanning results 
(Fig. 2f–k), the precipitate is rich in Cu, and depleted in Cr, Co, Fe and 
Ni, in contrast with the matrix.
Furthermore, the TEM characterization is also conducted on the Cu5 
alloy (Fig. 3). The dark ellipsoidal precipitates are approximately 50 nm 
in length and 25 nm in width (Fig. 3a). Fig. 3b–g also shows precipitates 
are rich in Cu. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns 
of matrix and precipitate reveal that they both have the FCC crystal 
structures (Fig. 3h and i). The High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) micro-
graph demonstrates that the precipitate and matrix share a coherent 
interface (Fig. 3j).
3.2. Mechanical properties at different temperatures
Fig. 4 displays the true stress−strain curves and corresponding 
strain hardening curves at 123 K, 298 K and 673 K of different 
Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys, revealing strong dependence of mechanical 
properties on Cu contents and loading temperature. All mechanical 
properties are summarized in Table 1. The calculation details of yield 
strength (𝜎Y), ultimate tensile strength (𝜎UTS) and elongation at frac-
ture (𝜀f ) are presented elsewhere [41,42]. The product of 𝜎UTS and 
𝜀f (𝜎UTS × 𝜀f ) is used to assess the overall mechanical properties 
of the materials [43,44]. At 123 K, all four Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys 
exhibit simultaneous superior strength and ductility. For each alloy, 
highest 𝜎UTS × 𝜀f value is obtained at 123 K. At 673 K, Cu3 and 
Cu5 alloys exhibit intermediate temperature embrittlement due to the 
Cu-rich precipitates, which is usually observed in Cu and Ni-based 
alloys [45,46].
For all Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloy at 123 K, the strain hardening curves 
exhibit similar trends including the initial decrease–shallower increase–
finally sharp drop. However, the shallower increase becomes less appar-
ent as the temperature rises or Cu contents increase. For Cu3 and Cu5 
alloys at 673 K, the strain hardening rates decrease monotonically. The 
variation in strain hardening rate is related to the plastic deformation 
mechanisms, which will be discussed in the next section.

4. Discussion
4.1. Strengthening mechanisms
Based on a structure-based model [38,56,57], the yield strength (𝜎Y) 
of different Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys at 298 K can be expressed as the 
sum of various strengthening mechanisms, including constant lattice 
friction stress (𝜎0), contributions from solid solution strengthening 
(𝜎ss), dislocation strengthening (𝜎dis), grain boundary strengthening 
(𝜎GB) and precipitation strengthening (𝜎ppt ). The specific model can be 
formulated as: 
𝜎Y = 𝜎0 + 𝜎ss + 𝜎dis + 𝜎GB + 𝜎ppt . (2)

regarded as solutes, while the Al0.1CoCrFeNi alloy serves as the solvent

4.2. Effects of Cu additions in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys
As the Cu content increases, the number of Cu-rich precipitates 
in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys significantly increases, which is consistent 
with other FCC HEAs [30,79,80]. Furthermore, several studies have 
observed the formation of Cu-rich inter-dendritic phases [27,28,81,82]. 
This formation is likely due to liquid phase separation prior to alloy 
solidification, caused by differences in the mixing entropy 𝛥𝐻mix of the 
constituent elements [33].
The yield strength of the Cu0 alloy in this study (Fig. 4) is similar 
to other cast Al0.1CoCrFeNi alloys from publications [44,83]. Increasing 
the Cu content results in an enhancement of the yield strength. Then 
the Cu5 alloy exhibits the highest yield strength (412 MPa) among 
all alloys, which is 193 MPa higher than that of the Cu0 alloy. The 
improvement of the yield strength is mainly due to the precipitates 
obstructing dislocation movement. Additionally, it is important to high-
light that adding Cu leads to a considerable reduction in fracture 
elongation. As shown in Fig. 10, abundant dislocations are accumulated 
around the Cu-rich precipitates, and the stress concentration induced 
via dislocation pile-ups may promote the initiation of micro-cracks. 
Thus, an increased number of Cu-rich precipitates results in premature 
failure and a decrease in plasticity [31].
Fig. 14 schematically summarizes the influence of Cu content and 
temperature on the deformation mechanisms of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 al-
loys. Generally, for FCC alloys, additional deformation mechanisms 
such as the deformation twinning or non-thermal phase transformation 
may appear when the stacking fault energy (SFE) decreases [84–88]. 
In this study, both the increases of temperature [89–91] and Cu con-
tent [92–94] contribute to the increase in SFE of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥
alloys.
For the Cu5 alloy at 673 K, the primary deformation mode is the dis-
locations slip. Dislocations are pinned by Cu-rich precipitates, thereby 
enhancing the material’s strength. As the Cu content or temperature 
decreases, elongation at failure gradually increases (Table 2). Thus, as 
the tensile loading proceeds, kink bands are activated to accommodate 
plastic deformations, and the interaction and entanglement of high-
density dislocations are observed in Cu5 alloy at 123 K or Cu1 alloy 
at 298 K. According to previous publications [38,95,96], in order to 
release the long-range stress field caused by the lattice distortion, 
dislocations aligned along the slip directions are arranged into walls 
perpendicular to the {111} slip planes, finally, the increasingly aligned 
dislocations walls ultimately evolve into kink bands. As the SFE de-
creases further in Cu1 alloy at 123 K, dislocations, SFs, immobile 
Lomer-Cottrell locks, kink bands and deformation twins are found. 
Nano-twins obstruct dislocation slip, and the dislocations aggregate at 
the twin boundaries to form high-density dislocations (Fig. 8a), which 
can further promote strain hardening [97,98]. For FCC alloys, the 
SFs [99,100] and L-C locks [101] can also hinder the dislocation slip. 
The L-C lock can connect more SFs, forming a ‘‘stacking fault network’’ 
that also acts as a strong obstacle to effectively pin the dislocation 
motion [102]. Although in this study, our in situ XRD experiments 
prove no phase transformation occurs in Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys, for 
FCC HEAs under more extreme conditions (dynamic loading or ul-
tralow temperature), phase transformation and even amorphization can 
occur [103,104].
5. Conclusions
In summary, via controlling the Cu content, different Al0.1
CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 (x = 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) alloys are obtained. Quasi-static 
tensile tests at 123 K, 298 K and 673 K are performed. The evolution of 
microstructure and deformation mechanisms is examined using various 
characterization techniques. The main conclusions are summarized as 
follows:
• With the increase of Cu content, all Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys 
show similar grain sizes, and the number of Cu-rich precipitates 
increases noticeably. The Cu-rich precipitates exhibit coherence 
with the matrix, and most of them are approximately 50 nm in 
length and 25 nm in width.
• All Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys show simultaneous superior strength 
and ductility at low temperatures. As the Cu content increases, the 
yield strength of Al0.1CoCrFeNiCu𝑥 alloys increases but ductility 
decreases. A structure-based strength model demonstrates that 
the enhancement in strength primarily results from the short-
range impediments of precipitates on dislocation slip. Dislocation 
pile-ups at precipitate-matrix interfaces promote the initiation of 
micro-cracks, leading to a drop in the fracture strain.
• The stacking fault energy (SFE) decreases with decreasing temper-
ature and Cu content. Consequently, Cu1 alloys at 123 K exhibit 
various deformation mechanisms such as dislocations, stacking 
faults, immobile Lomer-Cottrell locks, kink bands, and deforma-
tion twins, while Cu5 alloys at 673 K display predominantly 
dislocations.
