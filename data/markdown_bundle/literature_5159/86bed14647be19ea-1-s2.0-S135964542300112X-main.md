ABSTRACT 
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are developed for superior elevated-temperature strength and softening 
resistance. Additive manufacturing of RHEAs is attracting extensive attention because of their commendable 
potential in many applications. Yet, most additively manufactured RHEAs are brittle and their tensile properties 
at room temperature have rarely been reported. Here, tensile ductile RHEAs are fabricated by laser melting 
deposition for the first time. In the model TiZrHfNb
x 
( x = 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, in molar ratios) RHEAs, the as-printed 
alloys have an equiaxed-grained microstructure without any special process control or additional treatment. 
Increasing Nb contents stabilizes the body-centered cubic phase and suppresses the ω phase formation. Such 
phase engineering strategy transforms the mechanical performance from brittle to ductile fracture. As a result, 
the as-printed TiZrHfNb variant exhibits a room temperature tensile yield strength of ~1034 MPa and ductility of 
~18.5%. The solid-solution strengthening contributes to the high tensile yield strength, the local chemical 
fluctuations promote dislocation interactions and give rise to the large ductility. This work offers a new avenue 
for additive manufacturing of ductile RHEAs, which is conducive to their industrial application. 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys, as a newly developed metallic material, are 
composed of multiple constituents in nearly equivalent atomic fractions 
compared to conventional alloys with one principle element [1] . Among 
them, refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) consisting of metallic el -
ements with high melting points ( > 1600 
◦
C) have attracted substantial 
attention due to their compelling features qualifying them as candidate 
alloys for next-generation high-temperature applications. These prop -
erties include high melting points, high strength and extreme softening 
resistance at elevated temperatures. Senkov et al. reported that the yield 
strength at 1600 
◦
C of NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW RHEAs are 405 MPa 
and 477 MPa, respectively [2] . Such strong high temperature softening 
resistance outperforms most superalloys, such as Haynes 230 and 
Inconel 718 [3] . Although promising, RHEAs are fraught with 
difficulties during their preparation due to their extremely high melting 
temperatures. Arc melting is the most commonly used method for pre -
paring RHEAs [4] . However, we must melt the ingots repeatedly to 
achieve a homogeneous composition. Also, the significant difference in 
the elemental densities of RHEAs brings great difficulties to the control 
of melting parameters and processes. Consequently, the elemental 
segregation of arc melted RHEAs is usually quite severe [5] . Moreover, 
the size of ingots is strictly limited by the furnace and casting molds, 
making it hard to mass-produce RHEAs. 
Additive manufacturing, as a bottom-up technique, is transforming 
our ability to prepare metallic materials effectively. Compared to arc 
melting, additive manufacturing possesses more design freedom and 
efficient material usage. Several kinds of additive manufacturing tech -
niques have been developed, including power-bed-based selective laser 
melting, laser melting deposition (LMD) (or directed energy deposition)

have been reported lately, including steels [6 – 8] , Ti alloys [9 – 11] , Al 
alloys [12 – 14] , Ni-based superalloys [ 15 , 16 ] and high-entropy alloys 
[ 17 , 18 ]. Until recently, some attempts have demonstrated that additive 
manufacturing is a promising alternative for preparing RHEAs [19 – 26] . 
However, the currently reported additively manufactured RHEAs are 
generally brittle at room temperature, fail by cracking at low compres -
sive strains and suffer from limited formability. The additively manu -
factured NbMoTaW RHEA shows significant cracking and porosity 
throughout the height of the specimen, let alone its compressive prop -
erties [25] . The LMD-ed NbMoTa alloy exhibits only 5.8% compressive 
strain at room temperature due to metallurgical defects like porosities 
and intergranular cracks [27] . Also, there is almost no compressive 
strain in the selective laser melted VNbMoTaW RHEA owing to the 
inevitable cracks [22] . In addition to NbMoTa-based RHEAs, additively 
manufactured TiZrNb-based RHEAs have been also reported by re -
searchers. Crack-free and chemically homogeneous TiZrHfNbTa RHEA 
was fabricated by LMD [20] . However, the alloy fractured during the 
machining of threads in the grip section, indicating its embrittlement 
characteristics [19] . More crucially, for engineering applications of 
additively manufactured RHEAs, tensile properties are more valuable 
than compressive properties. Unfortunately, there have been no reports 
of tensile properties in the additively manufactured RHEAs. 
Here we demonstrate a phase engineering strategy that is versatile 
and potent for developing additively manufactured RHEAs with high 
tensile strength and ductility. TiZrHfNb
x 
( x = 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, in molar 
ratios) RHEAs were selected as the model alloys and single-track ex -
periments via LMD were carried out to determine the optimum process 
window. The as-printed RHEAs with equiaxed-grained microstructure 
were fabricated successfully under the optimized processing condition 
(laser power 1350 W, scanning speed 5 mm/s). Stabilizing the body- 
centered cubic (bcc) phase via increasing Nb contents inhibits the ω 
phase formation and avoids the resultant embrittlement. Consequently, 
such phase engineering strategy renders the printed TiZrHfNb RHEA 
outstanding tensile properties (tensile yield strength ~1034 MPa and 
ductility ~18.5%). The high tensile yield strength attributes to the solid- 
solution strengthening and the large ductility originates from the local 
chemical fluctuations which promote the dislocation interactions. This 
work is the first report to achieve tensile properties of RHEAs by laser 
additive manufacturing, which will provide new insights into the 
fabrication of ductile refractory alloys. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Elemental powders 
The elemental powders in the experiment included titanium (Ti, with 
a purity of 99.95%), zirconium (Zr, with a purity of 99.5%), hafnium 
(Hf, with a purity of 99.95%), niobium (Nb, with a purity of 99.95%) 
with sizes from 45 μ m to 150 μ m. Fig. 1 shows representative samples of 
the powder feedstock. The Ti, Zr and Nb powders were nearly spherical 
with good flowability, while the Hf powder was irregular. The powders 
were blended initially in a centrifuge tube by manual shaking. 
2.2. Laser deposition processing 
A laser deposition system was adopted in this work, equipped with a 
fiber laser system (the laser wavelength is 1080 nm and the maximum 
output power is 4500 W), a powder feeder, a KUKA six-axis robot and a 
cladding head. The TiZrHfNb
x 
thin-wall parts were prepared on a TA2 
titanium alloy substrate with a thickness of 5 mm. The printing 
TiZrHfNb specimen is shown in Fig. 2 a. The corresponding processing 
parameters were optimized as follows: laser power, 1350 W; scanning 
speed, 5 mm/s; powder feed rate, 4.8 g/min; working distance, 14 mm; 
offset value, 0.3 mm; layer number, 100.

3. Results 
3.1. Determination of processing window 
Single-track experiments were carried out with a wide range of 
process parameters to determine the optimum process window. The 
transverse section optical micrographs of single-track TiZrHfNb speci -
mens are presented in Fig. 3 . The rows represent a constant scanning 
speed and columns denote a constant laser power. Fig. 4 a defines the 
geometrical characteristics of a single-track. The measured track height 
( h ), track depth ( d ) and width ( w ) of the deposited specimens are sum -
marized in Fig. 4 b-g. The track height h , track depth d and width w in -
crease with increasing laser power and decreasing scanning speed. In the 
processing, the higher scanning speed would lead to a higher amount of 
spattering, which is not conducive to obtaining the desired alloy 
composition. As a result, a lower scanning speed (i.e., 5 mm/s) is 
considered to be applicable. With respect to laser power, the lower 
power (e.g., 900 W) is insufficient to melt the raw powders (see Fig. 3 a). 
However, the stirring of molten pool is too drastic at a rather high power 
(e.g., 2250 W), which leads to a large track depth and width ( Fig. 3 d). 
Moreover, the previous study has demonstrated that excessive heat 
input is prone to the vaporization of elements and probably causes high 
residual stress in the built specimen [30] . Therefore, the here optimal 
process parameters are determined to be as follows: laser power 1350 W, 
scanning speed 5 mm/s. The single-track experiments and measured 
geometrical characteristics of TiZrHfNb
0.6 
and TiZrHfNb
0.8 
RHEAs are 
exhibited in Fig. S1 – 4, which demonstrates that the optimum parame -

4.3. The origin of ductility 
Despite the strength isotropy, the tensile ductility shows anisotropy 
(see Fig. 9 a). The vertical TiZrHfNb specimen exhibit lower ductility 
(~10.0%) than the horizontal one (~18.5%). The equiaxed-grained 
microstructure excludes the effect of grain orientation on ductility. 
However, for the deformed TiZrHfNb RHEA along the vertical direction, 
several cracks are visible in the horizontal direction (i.e., the deposition 
direction), as shown in the lateral surface (Fig. S9). This may be 
attributed to the presence of porosity, as shown in Fig. S10. During laser 
processing, the molten metal does not completely fill the gaps in the 
inter-layer boundaries, which leads to the formation of pores near the 
inter-layer boundaries. These pores result in thin flat cracks and earlier 
fracture upon loading parallel to the build direction. The previous 
studies also demonstrated the deposited layers are the weakness in the 
loading condition [61] . Stress concentrations are prone to occur be -
tween the pronounced layers. Therefore, the weak adhesion of in -
terlayers causes ductility anisotropy in the two directions. 
In spite of the ductility anisotropy, the as-printed TiZrHfNb RHEA 
exhibits large tensile ductility in both directions ( > 10%). Our post -
mortem TEM analysis proves that the alloy is deformed primarily by 
screw dislocation movement (see Fig. 10 f-h). Dislocation jogs originate 
from the cross-slip of screw dislocations, indicating that the screw dis -
locations in the current RHEA possess a complex core structure. 
Therefore, the screw dislocations will undoubtedly spread over several 
intersecting planes [62] . Moreover, dislocation loops are frequently 
found in the specimen, which is beneficial to the enhanced 
strain-hardening capacity. Our recent study revealed that the dislocation 
loops were produced by the pinning effect from local chemical fluctu -
ations [39] . The local chemical fluctuations were visualized as (Hf, 
Nb)-rich and (Ti, Zr)-rich clusters in the arc-melted TiZrHfNb RHEA. A 
double cross-slip mechanism caused by the local chemical fluctuations 
was proposed for the good ductility of the arc-melted TiZrHfNb RHEA. 
The local chemical fluctuations are also found in the current as-printed 
TiZrHfNb RHEA (see Fig. 8 ). Additionally, these local chemical fluctu -
ations have also been demonstrated to be the symbol of short-range 
ordered regions and enhance dislocation interactions [63] . Therefore, 
the local chemical fluctuations in the as-printed TiZrHfNb RHEA are 
favorable for their enhanced ductility. 
5. Conclusion 
In summary, we for the first time reported a combination of high 
tensile yield strength and large ductility in the additively manufactured 
RHEAs. The as-printed TiZrHfNb
x 
( x = 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, in molar ratios) 
RHEAs exhibit the near-equiaxed grain microstructure. Increasing the 
Nb contents stabilizes the bcc phase and surmounts the ω phase 
embrittlement. Such phase engineering strategy can markedly switch 
the mechanical performance from brittle fracture to ductile fracture and 
enable the fabrication of ductile additively manufactured RHEAs. 
Specifically, we demonstrate that the as-printed TiZrHfNb RHEA ex -
hibits a tensile yield strength of ~1034 MPa and ductility of ~18.5% at 
room temperature. Furthermore, for the here printed TiZrHfNb RHEA, 
the high tensile yield strength mainly originates from the solid-solution 
strengthening, and the large ductility results from the multiple dislo -
cation interactions promoted by the local chemical fluctuations. Our 
work shows that phase engineering is an effective strategy for fabri -
cating strong and ductile additively manufactured RHEAs.
