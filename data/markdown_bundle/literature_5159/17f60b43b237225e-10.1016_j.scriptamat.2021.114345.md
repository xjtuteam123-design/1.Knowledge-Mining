a b s t r a c t 
A new Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy containing long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phases with good strength- 
ductility synergy was prepared by high temperature homogenization and rolling, which possesses a high 
ultimate tensile strength of 320 MPa and elongation of 17.0% and low work hardening rate during ten- 
sile deformation. Bulk LPSO phases with lamellar morphology exist in original grains after homogeniza- 
tion. Reﬁned grains, broken LPSO phases and high-density geometrically necessary dislocations provide 
a good strength for as-rolled alloy with the strength increment of 146.3 MPa, 51.7 MPa and 68.3 MPa, 
respectively. Good ductility and low work hardening rate are attributed to weak basal texture, activation 
of non-basal slips with high Schmid factors and annihilation of high-density dislocations. In situ tensile 
testing result shows deformation coordination by non-basal slips, deformation twinning, grain rotation 
and absorption of plastic deformation energy by LPSO phases, which suggests a good ductility as well.

In recent years, attention has been increasingly paid to Mg and 
its alloys in the aerospace and automobile industries due to their 
low density, high speciﬁc strength and stiffness high thermal con- 
ductivity and good damping [1–4] . Despite above advantages, the 
low absolute strength and poor formability caused by the limited 
available slip systems at room temperature limits the wide appli- 
cation of Mg alloys [ 5 , 6 ]. Therefore, the comprehensive mechani- 
cal properties of Mg alloys as effective structural materials need to 
be improved. Among various Mg alloys, Mg-rare earth (RE) alloys 
have been known to show ultrahigh strength and excellent high 
temperature stability [7] . In recent decades, the long period stack- 
ing ordered (LPSO) phases found in Mg-transition metal (TM)-RE 
ternary alloys can be remarkably beneﬁcial to develop the mechan- 
ical properties of Mg alloys [8] . For example, Mg 97 Zn 1 Y 2 alloy with 
LPSO phase developed by rapid solidiﬁcation powder metallurgy 
has an excellent high strength of 605 MPa and an elongation of 5% 
[9] . In general, LPSO phases have the same { 0 0 01 } basal plane and 
coherent interfaces with α-Mg matrix, which can provide a high 
yield strength. 
In addition to the Mg-Zn-Y alloys, a range of similar LPSO struc- 
tures, including 10H, 12R, 14H, and 18R-types were found in the 
Mg-Ni-Y alloys [10–13] . It is suggested that Mg-Ni-Y alloys exhibit 
superior mechanical and degradability [14] . Itoi et al. [15] prepared

a high strength Mg 90.5 Ni 3.25 Y 6.25 alloy with 18R-LPSO phase by hot 
rolling and annealing which exhibits a tensile yield strength (TYS) 
of 460 MPa and an elongation (EL) of 8%. It is found that the ulti- 
mate tensile strength (UTS) of Mg 96 Y 2 Ni 2 alloy can be increased to 
510 MPa by hot extrusion, which exhibits an EL of 7.2% [16] . How- 
ever, large amounts of Mg-RE alloys, especially for LPSO-containing 
Mg alloys, show a serious strength-ductility trade-off problem that 
the high strength usually sacriﬁce the ductility at room tempera- 
ture since the signiﬁcant hindrance effect on dislocation movement 
by introduction of high content of LPSO phases after hot working 
process [ 15 , 17 , 18 ]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a Mg-Ni-Y 
alloy with good strong-ductility synergy, and tailoring the content 
of LPSO phase and optimizing hot working process can be consid- 
ered as effective approaches [19] . 
In the present work, a new Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy containing 
18R-LPSO phase with good strong-ductility synergy was prepared 
by high temperature homogenization and rolling. The mechanical 
properties, microstructure evolution and deformation behavior of 
the hot-rolled alloy were investigated, and in situ SEM test was 
conducted to observe the process of plastic deformation. This work 
contributes to design of a low-RE alloyed wrought Mg alloy achiev- 
ing comprehensive mechanical properties by simple hot process- 
ing.

Mg (99.99%), Mg-30Ni (wt.%) and Mg-20Y (wt.%) master alloys in

in water. The homogenization temperature design referred to the 
calculation results of solidiﬁcation path by CALPHAD method (Fig. 
S1). The billet was cut into 60 × 20 × 12 mm 3 and hot-rolled at 
500 °C with the initial thickness reduction of 20% and subsequent 
thickness reductions of 15-20% per pass until a total reduction of 
80%. After each pass, the hot-rolled samples were reheated at 500 
°C for 10 min and quenched in water. Tensile test was conducted 
on an Instron 5982 testing machine at room temperature with the 
initial strain rate of 1 × 10 −3 s −1 . Tensile samples were machined 
along the rolling direction (RD) from the rolled sheet with a gauge 
length of 10 mm and a cross-section of 4 × 2 mm 2 . Microstruc- 
ture characterization of hot-rolled alloy was carried out by scan- 
ning electron microscopy (SEM, JSU-6700F) equipped with an en- 
ergy dispersive spectrometer (EDS), and the samples were mechan- 
ically polished and then etched in the 4% HNO 3 -CH 3 CH 2 OH solu- 
tion. The structure of LPSO and intragranular substructure were 
observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL 2100F) 
equipped with EDS, and the samples for TEM observation were 
obtained along RD and prepared by mechanical thinning down 
to about 40 μm and then ion beam milling (Gatan PIPS II 695). 
The deformed microstructure was also investigated on an elec- 
tron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) system with a step length 
of 0.6 μm for as-rolled sample and 0.12 μm for as-tensile sam- 
ple, and the data were analyzed with HKL Channel 5 software and 
the observation position was selected along RD. In situ SEM ten- 
sile testing for the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 was carried out inside 
a ﬁeld emission SEM (Zeiss-Sigma 500) equipped with a Gatan TM 
microtest tensile platform as well, and the sample was machined 
into dog-bone shape with a gauge length of 4.63 mm and a width 
of 3.5 mm and etched. 
Fig. 1 a shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the 
Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy. The as-homogenized alloy exhibits relatively 
mediocre mechanical properties with a YS of 143 MPa, an UTS of 
210 MPa and an EL of 7.7%. After hot rolling, the mechanical prop- 
erties are signiﬁcantly improved. The YS, UTS and EL increase to 
262 MPa, 320 MPa and 17.0%, respectively, and the corresponding 
UTS × EL value reaches 5.44 GPa • %, which is much higher than 
that of previous as-rolled Mg-Ni-Y alloy (4.21 GPa • %) [15] . Fig. 1 b 
shows the UTS × EL value corresponding to the Y content of var- 
ious hot-deformed Mg-Y-Zn/Ni alloys containing LPSO phases for 
comparison, suggesting that the present as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 
alloy has an excellent strength-ductility synergy with low Y con- 
tent as well [ 15-17 , 20-30 ]. 
True stress-true strain curves of Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloys corre- 
sponding to the tensile curves are shown in Fig. 1 c, and the 
strain hardening curve of as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy is shown 
in Fig. 1 d. No obvious yield platform can be observed in the true 
strain-true stress curve of Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloys, suggesting little 
hindrance to dislocation slips from solutes or particles [31] . More- 
over, it should be noticed that the work hardening rate ( θ = d σ
d ε ) 
gradually decreases during tensile deformation although the work 
hardening process still dominates. The θ rapidly drops and shows 
a nearly parabolic decrease. Then the θ remains almost zero until 
fracture, and the true strain exceeds 8% during this process. Such a 
phenomenon of dynamic softening suggests a dynamic equilibrium 
of stress from a dynamic recovery (DRV) process [32] . 
Fig. 2 depicts the microstructures of as-homogenized and as- 
rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloys. Bulk second phases distributed as a 
network with lamellar morphology can be observed after homog- 
enization, which are mostly long strips with a width of about 10 
μm and a lamellar spacing of about 1 μm ( Fig. 2 a). Chen et al. 
[33] reported that the average size of α-Mg grains in Mg-1.99Zn- 
8.02Y alloy can reach about 400 μm and LPSO phases with lamel- 
lar morphology are densely arranged in original grains after ho- 
mogenization at 510 °C for 20 h, which is similar to our observa- 
tion results. No other eutectic structure with α-Mg + Mg 2 Ni phases 
found in other Mg-Ni-Y alloys with lower Y/Ni ratios were ob- 
served in this alloy [34] . EDS result suggests that the chemical 
composition of lamellar phase is Mg 92.42 Ni 2.78 Y 4.80 (at.%), which 
has been identiﬁed as 18R-LPSO [16] . The content of 18R-LPSO is 
about 10%, which is similar to the calculation results (Fig. S1). In 
addition to these lamellar phases, some dispersed particles with 
a size of about 1 μm can be found in grains as well. EDS re- 
sult suggests a composition of Mg 90.23 Ni 0.13 Y 9.46 (at.%), indicating 
that they may be Mg 24 Y 5 phase [34] . Fig. 2 b presents the further 
HRTEM image with selective electron diffraction (SAED) pattern 
for investigation on the detailed LPSO structure and morphology. 
the alternative dark lamina can be regarded as an LPSO structure 
with a fringe interval of 1.61 nm [34] . The distinct orientation re- 
lationship between Mg matrix and LPSO phase can be identiﬁed as 
{ 0 0 01 } Mg ∥ { 0 0 01 } 18R . Such a structure may effectively limit dislo- 
cation movement and improve the strength. 
SEM observation for the microstructure of as-rolled 
Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy is shown in Fig. 2 c. Due to high temper- 
ature deformation, almost complete dynamic recrystallization 
(DRX) process occurs in the alloy during hot rolling, and the 
grains are fully reﬁned. Fig. 2 c also shows that rare kink bands 
of LPSO phase, which differs from other previous work [ 15 , 21 ]. 
Instead, the lamellar LPSO phases are broken and reﬁned under 
severe deformation and dispersed along the rolling direction in 
the matrix, which separate the DRXed grains as well. The broken 
LPSO phase morphology is shown in Fig. 2 d and corresponding 
EDS mapping results are presented in Fig. 2 e-g, indicating that the 
LPSO phase are distributed along the RD direction and separated 
with a distance of several microns. It is worth noting that the 
electron beam direction changes from { 11 ¯2 0 } 18R to { 0 0 01 } 18R 
[13] . Such phenomenon suggests that the basal plane of LPSO 
phase is parallel to RD, indicating that the LPSO phases select a 
preferred orientation during hot rolling. Besides, Fig. 2 h shows 
that high-density dislocation regions are uniformly distributed 
in the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy, implying a relatively high 
residual stress and mobile dislocation movement can be effectively 
hindered. 
Fig. 3 a shows the EBSD map of the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 al- 
loy. It is suggested that the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy mostly 
consists of broken LPSO phases and DRXed grains with an average 
size of 8.5 μm. Besides, the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy exhibits 
a weak and spread basal texture with a relatively low maximum 
intensity of 8.36 ( Fig. 3 b), which may result from texture weaken- 
ing effect due to the addition of Y [35] . Fig. 3 c shows the kernel 
average misorientation (KAM) map of the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 
alloy, which indicates that the as-rolled Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy ex- 
hibits a relatively high KAM value of 1.42 °. Generally, a high KAM 
value may suggest a severe degree of plastic deformation as a re- 
sult of high defect density [36] . It can be seen that the high KAM 
value regions are uniformly distributed in the alloy, indicating that 
a large amount of residual stress appears after hot rolling, which 
is consistent with the observation of Fig. 2 h. It is also suggested 
that the KAM value has a positive correlation with the geometri- 
cally necessary dislocation (GND) density ( ρGND ), which can be ex-

and b represents the Burgers vector of Mg (3.21 × 10 −10 m) [37] . 
Therefore, the ρGND is estimated as 2.57 × 10 14 m −2 , implying a

Figs. 4 a-d show the in situ SEM images of as-rolled 
Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy at different strains during the tensile test (cor- 
responding tensile curve is shown in Fig. S2). Equiaxed grains 
formed by complete DRX process and broken LPSO phase are dis- 
tributed at the grain boundaries. After yielding, it can be seen 
that the equiaxed DRXed grains (yellow line) are elongated along 
the deformation direction and rotated (white dotted line), as ob- 
served according to the grain shape, suggesting the activation of 
several slip systems during deformation [ 39 , 40 ]. The LPSO phases 
distributed in the grain boundary are elongated as well and ﬁrst 
fractured ( Fig. 4 b). Besides, dispersed particles can also be the loca- 
tion of crack initiation due to stress concentration at the phase in- 
terfaces. More slip lines can be observed in grains ( Fig. 4 c), which 
may be identiﬁed as non-basal slips according to the morphology 
and SFs results ( Fig. 4 e-h) [ 41 , 42 ]. Deformation twinning occurs 
during the subsequent deformation ( Fig. 4 d), indicating that plastic 
deformation coordination can be fully promoted. 
Above experimental results show that the as-rolled 
Mg 98.3 Y 1.3 Ni 0.4 alloy achieves a good strength at room tem- 
perature. The original grains are uniformly reﬁned by complete 
DRX during hot rolling, which can provide a signiﬁcant YS in- 
crement according to the Hall-Petch relationship. Besides, LPSO 
phases are broken and dispersed, and basal planes of LPSO phases 
are parallel to RD, which can provide a strong hindrance effect 
of activation of basal slips [43] . Moreover, the high density GNDs 
after rolling can hinder dislocation movement since a relatively 
strong lattice distortion, which can contribute to the improvement 
of strength as well. Detailed strengthening increments of various 
strengthening mechanisms were calculated as well (Fig. S3), which 
shows the strengthening contributions of grain boundaries, LPSO 
phase and dislocations are 146.3 MPa, 51.7 MPa and 68.3 MPa, 
respectively. 
The alloy shows good room temperature ductility as well. Grain 
reﬁnement can be conductive to improve the ability of grain 
boundary slips. Besides, due to the weak basal texture and high SFs 
of non-basal slips, numerous non-basal slips can be activated. The 
annihilation of original and newly proliferated opposite sign mov- 
able dislocations (MDs) and high density GNDs during the defor- 
mation promotes the occurrence of DRV process [44] , which bal- 
ances the work hardening effect to show a low work hardening 
rate and decreases the ﬂow stress to support continuous plastic 
deformation. Furthermore, according to the in situ tensile testing 
results, the microcrack initiation ﬁrstly occurs at LPSO/ α-Mg inter- 
face and the fracture of LPSO phase is prior to that of α-Mg matrix 
in the later stage of plastic deformation. Such phenomenon sug- 
gests that stress concentration is more likely to occur near the hard 
broken LPSO phases during plastic deformation which may result 
from the preferred orientation of LPSO phases. coordinated defor- 
mation of α-Mg grains can occur more easily by non-basal slips, 
deformation twinning and grain rotation. Therefore, a large amount 
of plastic deformation energy can be absorbed by LPSO phases be- 
fore fracture, which results in a good ductility of the alloy.

good strong-ductility synergy was prepared by high temperature 
homogenization and rolling. The as-rolled alloy exhibits a high UTS
