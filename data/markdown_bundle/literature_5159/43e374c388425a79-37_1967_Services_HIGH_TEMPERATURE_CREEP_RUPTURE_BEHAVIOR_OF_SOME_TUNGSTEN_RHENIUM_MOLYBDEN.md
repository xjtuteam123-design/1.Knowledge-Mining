ABSTRACT 
Thirty -four compositions of tungsten-rhenium-molybdenum alloys, 
fabricated. by powder metallurgy techniques, were evaluated for fabri - 
cability, strength, and ductility to assess their potential for use in 
high-temp'erature nuclear reactor' components. Compositions in atomic 
percent ranged from 15 to 75 W, 0 to 65 Mo, and 15 to 30 Re and in- 
volved both solid-solution and two-phase alloys containing varying 
amounts of the rhenium-rich sigma phase. After sfntering, most of 
the alloys presented no problems in working to 0.02-inch-thick sheet 
by hot-rolling 80 percent followed by cold-rolling 50 percent. 
Each alloy was constant-load, creep-rupture tested at 1600°C. and 
2200°C in hydrogen. At 1600°C, the alloys containing 10, 20, and 30 
atomic percent molybdenum exhibited a transition in the ductility and 
creep resistance in the region of 23 to 25 percent rhenium. Apparently, 
this was due, to the effect of the phase boundary in this region at 1600°C. 
A more pronounced effect of the phase boundary on the ductility was 
' 
observed at 2200°C for the same alloys; the ductility rose on either 
side of'the boundary. At 2200°C, a transition in the creep resistance 
occurred at the phase boundary with the solid-solution alloys being 
significantly stronger. 
Alloys containing 30 atomic percent W, when tested at both 1600°C 
and 2200°C, displayed a decrease in creep resistance with increasing 
amounts of rhenium (and decreasing amounts of molybdenum). The 
ductility was insensitive to composition at 1600°C but increased with 
increasing rhenium content at 2200°C. 
Rupture for all the alloys evaluated at both 1600°C and 2200°C ap- 
peared to be intergranular. Numerous voids were formed within the 
grain boundaries, transverse to the stress direction; these appeared 
to coalesce as strain progressed causing .fracture.

FABRICATION 
All the alloys were fabricated utilizing powder-metallurgy techniques4 
by the following basic procedure: 
1. Powders were- wet-blended in methanol and then vacuum dried., ' 
(The methanol prevented the tungsten powder from agglomerating, 
thus eliminating unalloyed tungsten areas in the finished product.) 
2. Blended powders were steel-die compacted at 1400 kg/cm2, pre- 
sintered for 2 hours at 1200°C in hydrogen, and sintered for 3 
hours at 2640 "C in hydrogen with a dewpoint of - -70" C. Densities 
of 93 to 9.5 percent of theoretical were obtained. 
3. Sintered sheet blanks (0.55 cm thick) were hot-rolled at 1400°C'

hour at'2640° C in hydrogen with a dewpoint of -70" C.

TESTING 
.9. 
Bend tests of the various alloys were performed at room temperature 
to evaluate their ductility in the as-processed condition and after an- 
nealing at various temperatures for different lengths of time. A bend- 
test fixture with a 4T bend radius was used in a tensile machine with 
the crosshead travel controlled at 0.13 cm per minute. 
Creep-rupture constant-load tests were performed in hydrogen at 
1600°C and 2200°C to evaluate the effect of composition on the creep 
resistance, rupture life, and ductility. All specimens were annealed 
for 2 hours at test temperature prior to loading to establish a reason- 
ably stable structure for the material being tested as well as to ther - 
mally equilibrate the test system. Specimen heating was accomplished 
by means of thermal radiation from an electrically heated tungsten 
tubular heating element. An L and N optical pyrometer, with its cali- 
bration traceable to NBS, was used for temperature measurements by 
sighting into a black-body tube located next to the test specimen. Con- 
stant temperature was maintained during the tests by controllirlg the 
heating element voltage. Creep-strain was obtained manually by means 
.of a twin optical extensometer unit focusing onto fiducial marks located 
at each end of the specimen gage section (2.54 cm long). The measure- 
ments yielded data from which a strain - time curve could be constructed. 
All tests were continued to rupture with the test system shut down auto- 
matically when specimen failure occurred.

Creep-rupture test results for the various W-Re -Mo alloys containing 
0 to 30 percent molybdenum (Group I) at 1600°C and 2200°C are given 
in Tables 3 and 4. Included are the times required to give various

the room-temperature elongation after rupture for the temperatures 
and stresses indicated. Typical creep curves for alloys containing 10 
percent molybdenum are given in Figures 2 and 3. In both figures, the 
curves show an increased creep resistance due to the decreasing 
amount of rhenium (increasing amount of W), as might be expected. 
This effect was not consistently observed in the data for the various 
other compositions. Figure 4 shows the effect of co~npositio~l on the 
rupture elongation (ductility) and the time required to give 5 percent 
strain at 1600°C. In general, there is a transition in the ductility and 
the creep resistance in the region of 23 to 25 percent rhenium. This 
is apparently the effect of the sigma-phase boundary existing in this 
region at 1600°C. Also, the ductility and creep resistance are de- 
creased in the two-phase region by the addition of molybdenum for the 
same rhenium content (decreasing tungsten content). In the single - 
phase region, for alloys with 20 and 30 percent molybdenum, both 
ductility and creep resistance are increased with decreasing rhenium 
content (increasing tungsten content) below about 20 percent rhenium. 
Figure 5 presents the test results for the same Group I alloys (W-Re 
with 0, 10, 20, and 30% Mo) tested at 2200°C. These data show a more 
pronounced effect due to the phase boundary. In this region, the duc- 
tility is at a minimum with a significant rise on either side of the 
boundary. A transition in the creep resistance also occurs at the 
phase boundary with the solid-solution alloys being significantly 
stronger.

SUMMARY 
Based on the information presented for various compositions of 
W-Re-Mo, no particular alloy composition was outstanding. Some 
general observations noted as a result of the work are: 
The W-Re-Mo alloy group with 0 to 30 percent molybdenum, after 
annealing at various temperatures for different lengths of time, 
showed useful, although not necessarily high, ductility in all the 
alloys at room temperature. At 1600°C, the alloys containing 20 
and 30 percent molybdenum with 18 and 17 percent rhenium, re- 
spectively, are promising alloys since both the creep resistance 
and ductility are favorable and these alloys are singlephase 
(solid-solution alloys). Moderate creep resistance and ductility 
at 2200" C were exhibited by the alloys containing 20 and 30 per- 
cent molybdenum and rhenium contents from 20 to 23 percent. 
Since these are solid-solution alloys, no precipitation at temper - 
ature will occur to influence the properties. 
2. All the W-Re-Mo allays with large amounts of molybdenuln (15 
and 30 percent tungsten), after annealing at various temperatures 
for various lengths of time, showed useful room-temperature 
ductility. High ductility was exhibited by the W - 25Re - 60M0, 
W - 30Re - 55M0, and W - 25Re - 45Mo alloys. At both 1600°C 
and 2200°C, only the W - 15Re - 55Mo had significant creep 
resistance with reasonable ductility. 
3. Rupture for all the alloys evaluated at both 1600°C and 2200°C 
appeared to be intergranular. Numerous voids were formed within 
the grain boundaries, perpendicular to the stress direction, and 
appeared to coalesce as strain progressed causing fracture. A 
significant portion of the total elongation at rupture is due to the
