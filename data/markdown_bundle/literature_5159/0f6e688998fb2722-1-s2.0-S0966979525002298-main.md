1. Introduction
With the rapid development of modern industry, particularly the 
aviation and aerospace industries, the exploration of materials with 
superior performance has drawn widespread attention in recent de -
cades. High ‒ entropy alloys (HEAs), as a new type of alloy, have become 
the focus of research by Professor Ye and a group of other researchers [ 1 ,
2 ]. Through rational alloy design, HEAs often achieve a range of 
excellent properties, including high strength, high ductility, corrosion 
resistance, and exceptional radiation resistance [ 3 – 5 ].
HEAs composed of various refractory elements (Ti, Zr, Hf, Ta, Nb, Cr, 
Mo, W, and V) are referred to as refractory high ‒ entropy alloys 
(RHEAs). RHEAs typically have a single body ‒ centered cubic (BCC) 
structure [ 6 ]. The high melting point ensures excellent strength at both 
room temperature and high temperature. However, owing to the high 
density of the elements in RHEAs, their specific strength is relatively 
low. Additionally, the high cost of refractory elements limits their 
further development and application in the aerospace industry [ 7 – 10 ]. 
To address the drawback of high density, studies on lightweight RHEAs 
have become more prevalent [ 11 ]. Compared with tradition RHEAs, 
light ‒ weight RHEAs not only offer greater strength but also have 
significantly lower densities, typically less than 8 g/cm
3
. For example, 
Stepanov et al. [ 12 ] developed the AlTiVNb lightweight RHEA, which 
has a density of only 5.59 g/cm
3 
and a yield strength of 1020 MPa.

NbTiZr medium ‒ entropy alloys have been extensively investigated 
by numerous researchers. In addition to its notable yield strength, the 
good compressive plasticity of these alloys suggests their potential for 
becoming a more advanced material [ 13 – 15 ].To simultaneously 
improve the strength of the alloy and reduce its density, the addition of 
Al is incorporated into this research design. The strength of the alloy 
increases with increasing interatomic bonding force between Al and 
other elements, and greater lattice distortion is formed by the addition of 
Al. The negative mixing enthalpy of the Al and other elements further 
enhances the solid solution strengthening effect. This can also result the 
formation of Al intermetallics and produce precipitation hardening 
[ 16 – 19 ]. Moreover, some researchers have investigated the role of Cr in 
NbTiZr alloys, demonstrating that it can improve yield strength and 
plasticity. Owing the smaller atomic size of Cr, it more easily dissolves 
into the lattice, resulting solid solution strengthening. Additionally, the 
relatively small atomic size of Cr compared with those of other elements 
results in the formation of Laves phases in alloys containing Cr [ 20 – 23 ]. 
For example, in the AlCr
x
NbTiV alloy developed by Stepanov et al. [ 24 ], 
the formation of the C14 Laves phase significantly increased the high ‒ 
temperature strength of the alloy. Owing to its excellent high ‒ temper -
ature properties, the Laves phase significantly impacts the mechanical 
properties of HEAs, making it highly attractive for the design of high ‒ 
temperature alloys.
Many researchers have investigated precipitated ZrAl intermetallic 
compounds and Laves phases in RHEAs [ 18 , 21 , 22 , 25 – 28 ]. Existing 
studies have focused mainly on the effects of intermetallic compounds 
on the performance of as ‒ cast alloys. Few studies have investigated the 
formation and processing of these alloys and examined the microstruc -

processing and their impact on the performance of the alloy. Only N.D.

4. Discussion
4.1. Microstructure evolution
In this study, annealing of the CA alloy led to the precipitation of 
Al
3
Zr
5 
and C14-Laves phases within coarse equiaxed grains. This 
microstructural evolution establishes a critical foundation for under -
standing the behavior of intermetallic compounds during forging pro -
cesses and their subsequent effects on alloy mechanical properties.
After forging at two different temperatures, the morphologies of the 
matrix phase, Al
3
Zr
5 
phase, and C14 Laves phase underwent significant 
changes. After forging at 1000 
◦
C, the alloy microstructure of the alloy 
was refined. The change in the C14 Laves phase indicates that during 
multidirectional forging, EDS analysis of the C14 Laves phase in Fig. 5 
(a2) revealed that Cr exhibited preferential segregation during the 
forging process, with its concentration being significantly higher than 
that in the HA alloy (as show in Table 3 ). This explains why the 
morphology of these precipitates changed. The diffusion of atoms 
strongly depends on temperature [ 37 ]. Therefore, high-temperature 
forging at 1000 
◦
C promotes the enrich of Cr, resulting the change of 
the C14 Laves phase. As the temperature quickly decreases, other ele -
ments are unable to diffuse into the C14 Laves phase lattice in time. 
Therefore, although the C14 Laves phase forms during forging, its 
elemental composition differs from that of the as ‒ cast annealed C14 
Laves phase, which results in the observed contrast difference.

Compared with the HA alloy, the forged alloy exhibited varying 
degrees of recrystallization at two distinct temperatures. Thermo -
mechanical processing can generate numerous dislocations, resulting 
dynamic recrystallization and grain refinement [ 38 , 39 ]. However, the 
effects of different forging temperatures on recrystallization are not 
uniform. The alloy forged at 1000 
◦
C undergoes complete recrystalli -
zation, whereas the alloy forged at 1200 
◦
C displays fewer recrystallized 
grains that are arranged in a chain ‒ like distribution around larger 
grains — an observation that diverges from typical metal recrystalliza -
tion phenomena. Notably, increasing the forging temperature di -
minishes the recrystallization ability of the alloy.
During high-temperature forging, dynamic recovery and dynamic 
recrystallization act as competing mechanisms. Increasing forging 
temperature enhances recovery in the alloy, while the concomitant 
dislocation annihilation reduces the stored energy available for recrys -
tallization. Consequently, excessively high forging temperatures may 
suppress recrystallization, which is consistent with the observations by 
Sun et al. in high-temperature titanium alloy forging processes [ 40 ].
The evolution of precipitates under various forging temperatures has 
been analyzed; more sub ‒ micron C14 Laves phases exist in the alloy 
forged at 1000 
◦
C, whereas the precipitates are confined solely to the 
recrystallized zone in alloys processed at 1200 
◦
C. The pervasive pres -
ence of submicron precipitates within the matrix impedes dislocation
