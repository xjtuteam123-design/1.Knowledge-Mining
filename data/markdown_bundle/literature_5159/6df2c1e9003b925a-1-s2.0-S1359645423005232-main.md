1. Introduction 
Additive manufacturing (AM) is a revolutionary technology that 
offers many advantages over traditional manufacturing technologies 
such as rapid prototyping, customization, reduced waste and enhanced 
design freedom. By selectively and precisely depositing materials in a 
layer-wise manner, AM enables the production of near-net-shape metal 
parts with intricate geometries [1] , making it highly promising for 
fabricating high-value components such as disks, turbine blades and 
vanes used in the hot sections of aircraft engines, land-based power 
generation turbines and petrochemical processing equipment. These 
critical structural applications require the materials to perform reliably 
at high temperatures. Due to the excellent mechanical properties at 
elevated temperatures, high-performance Ni-base superalloys have long

Solidification cracking occurs due to insufficient feeding of remaining 
liquid in the terminal stage of solidification, where the solidification 
shrinkage strain cannot be accommodated. Liquidation cracking is 
caused by liquid films enriching in γ

annealing treatment, which impedes the relaxation of thermal stresses 
by strengthening the recently solidified γ . Other advanced structural 
alloys, such as high-strength Al alloys [6] and refractory alloys with 
body-centered cubic structure [7] , suffer similarly from the hot-cracking 
issue. Hence, hot cracking is a critical issue that limits the range of en -
gineering materials suitable for AM [1] , hindering the widespread 
application and further development of AM technology. 
Numerous efforts have been devoted to mitigating hot cracking in 
AM, however, most strategies have compromised strength at ambient 
and/or high temperatures. For example, grain-refinement through the 
functionalization of powder surface can mitigate hot cracking in high- 
strength Al alloys [6] that operate at relatively low temperatures. 
However, if applied to high- γ
′ 
Ni-base superalloys, the resulting smaller 
grain sizes accelerate the strength decreasing rate at elevated tempera -
tures, negatively affecting high-temperature strength. Promoting the 
columnar to equiaxed grain transition by adjusting alloy compositions 
[8] or AM process parameters [9] can mitigate hot cracking. However, 
equiaxed grain microstructures are deleterious to high-temperature 
strength and creep properties, which is also unfavorable for 
heat-resistant materials. By alloying higher content of solid-solution 
hardening or grain-boundary strengthening elements into two existing 
solid-solution hardening-type commercial superalloys Hastelloy X [10] 
and Hastelloy 230 [11] , the hot-cracking in AM can be reduced. How -
ever, this strategy may cause serious embrittlement due to the formation 
of many brittle topologically-closed-packed (TCP) intermetallic phases 
in high- γ
′ 
Ni-base superalloys, particularly in the latter case with much 
more complex compositions than the former. Alternatively, substituting 
the γ
′
-forming elements (Al and Ti) with refractory element Nb [12] can 
mitigate hot cracking in AM. Unfortunately, this method significantly 
decreases the strength of the AM-ed materials. Reducing the content of 
the minor grain-boundary strengthening element Si to decrease the 
segregation levels at interdendrite regions can mitigate hot cracking in 
AM of IN738LC high- γ
′ 
Ni-base superalloy [13] . Yet, this strategy 
weakens grain-boundary cohesion strength. Murray et al. proposed 
several thermodynamic rules for overall composition design for 
hot-cracking resistance and developed a new CoNi-base superalloy for 
AM [14] . Although the developed alloy was manufactured without hot 
cracking via both laser and electron beam powder bed fusion

superalloys CM247 and IN738LC. Overall, such metallurgical ap -
proaches for mitigating hot cracking in AM of high- γ
′

encounter a severe trade-off between manufacturability and high 
strength, i.e., enhanced hot-cracking resistance usually compromises 
strength even at room temperatures.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Sample preparation 
Customized pre-alloyed powder of the designed MNiHEA is used in 
this study. The powder is fabricated by Xi ’ an Sailong AM Technology 
Co., Ltd (Xi ’ an, China), using the plasma rotating electrode process at -
omization technique. The pre-alloyed powder with high sphericity and 
smooth surface is shown in Fig. S1a. No satellite powder particles are 
observed, and each powder particle consists of fine dendrites (the inset 
of Fig. S1a). Besides, the powder particles show uniform distribution of 
the major elements Ni, Co, Cr, Fe, Al, Ti, W and Mo (Fig. S1c). The 
measured compositions of the powder are very close to the nominal

4. Discussion 
4.1. Resistance to hot cracking 
Many commercial high- γ
′ 
Ni-base superalloys with high content of Al 
and Ti, i.e., (Al + Ti) wt% > 6%, are highly susceptible to hot cracking, 
making them unmanufacturable or difficult-to-manufacture via AM 
[31] . This problem is further exacerbated when using the LPBF method 
due to extremely high cooling rates [3] . Yet in our study, the MNiHEA 
has shown excellent manufacturability via AM, despite its high (Al + Ti) 
content of ~7.4 wt%. There are no observable hot cracks in the AB and 
ABG states, even though no extrinsic hot-cracking-mitigation measures 
are applied. This observation suggests that the MNiHEA has an intrinsic 
resistance to hot cracking, which is key to its good manufacturability by 
the LPBF technique. To confirm the designated origins of the intrinsic 
hot-cracking resistance in our current MNiHEA, we analyze and inter -
pret the underlying mechanisms for all three types of hot cracking.

4.1.2. Resistance to liquidation cracking 
The resistance to the liquidation cracking is attributed to the absence 
of the low-melting-point phases like the carbides, γ / γ
′
eutectic, µ , η and 
B 2 in the cellular, dendrite and grain boundaries (It is uncertain if there 
are atomic layers of the MB2_C32 borides in these boundaries) [ 4 , 5 , 9 ]. 
Compared with the interior regions, the boundary regions solidifying at 
the final stage are more likely to have microsegregations, especially the 
enrichment of B, C and Zr elements. This causes the solidus temperature 
to drop and the liquid to be retained at a lower temperature. Hence, the 
boundaries, particularly the HAGBs are naturally the liquidation film 
sources [4] . It implies that in the boundary areas of the AB MNiHEA, the 
micorsegregations are relatively mild and insufficient to cause contin -
uous liquid films. There are three possible reasons. First, the sizes of the 
DCs and columnar grains of the AB MNiHEA are relatively small. In their 
boundaries, the degree of the microsegregations is insufficient to reach 
the local threshold composition for the formation of a liquid film [ 5 , 9 ]. 
For example, a critical columnar grain width of ~100 μ m has been 
identified in a nonweldable superalloy to prevent liquidation cracking 
[9] . When grain sizes are finer than this critical value, liquid films can be 
avoided. Second, the formation of the γ
′ 
phase is suppressed, and thus, 
there is no elemental redistribution between the γ
′
-forming elements (Ni, 
Al, Ti) and precipitate-lean elements (Fe, Co, Cr) during solidification. 
The remaining of the γ supersaturated solid-solution composition is 
overall homogeneous and can thus substantially mitigate and limit the 
microsegregation aggregation to the critical value that can lead to liquid 
film.

found in the ABG MNiHEA, indicating that strain-age cracking does not

mentioned above, the resistance to strain-age cracking may also be

5 ]. The precipitation-strengthening induced strength increment is

DC structure induced dislocation hardening dominate the strength. It 
makes the γ matrix strong enough to not only withstand thermal strains 
but also alleviate the ductility reduction due to 
precipitation-strengthening.

smaller SCI value, the suppressed phase separations of intermetallic 
compounds including the γ

by the IR-SPs, are intrinsically imparted by the thermodynamic and 
mechanical characteristics of the MNiHEA mentioned before. 
4.2. Strengthening mechanisms 
The yield strength of MNiHEA arises from various strengthening 
mechanisms. The solute atoms, grain boundaries, pre-existing disloca -
tions and coherent precipitates are all obstacles for the dislocation slip 
upon plastic yielding. The corresponding strengthening mechanisms are 
solid-solution hardening, grain-boundary strengthening, dislocation- 
hardening and precipitation-hardening, respectively. We carefully 
evaluate the strength contribution of each strengthening mechanism and 
its ratio to the yield strength of the four MNiHEA specimens (Supple -
mentary Note 2). Fig. 11 a summarizes the evaluation results. The most 
effective strengthening mechanism is solid-solution hardening, by which 
the lattice friction stress increases up to ~458 MPa. This value is ~260% 
higher than those of FCC single-phase CrFeCoNi-family HEAs [40] . Such 
a high lattice friction stress is probably due to the pronounced 
solid-solution hardening effect by the doping of W and Mo elements. The 
least effective strengthening mechanism is grain-boundary strength -
ening, which is more significant in the AB and ABG MNiHEA than those 
in the two ACG counterparts because the former two fabricated by the 
LPBF have the finer microstructures.

SP-ACG and IRP-ACG MNiHEA due to the dislocation-hardening effect, 
which contributes approximately 314 MPa. This strengthening effect is

particles can bring out ultrahigh strength while maintaining sufficient 
ductility. The three major strengthening mechanisms ‒ solid-solution 
hardening, dislocation-hardening and precipitation-hardening ‒ are 
all effective at elevated temperatures, indicating that this method and 
the synergy of multiple strengthening mechanisms may be also effective 
for enhancing high-temperature strength. 
4.3. Deformation and strain-hardening mechanisms for uniform ductility 
The uniform ductility of the AB and ABG MNiHEA is attributed to the 
adequate strain-hardening capability originated from the dislocation- 
slip and SF mediated deformation mechanisms in the precipitate-free 
and precipitate-bearing DC structures. At the onset of the plastic 
straining, the planar dislocation slip is the main deformation mechanism 
of the AB MNiHEA ( Fig. 11 b). The DC structure maintains, where the DC 
boundaries with the high-density multiple-Burgers-vector dislocations 
and the microsegregations are not only the dislocation nucleation sites, 
but also act as strong barriers to block the slipping planar dislocations 
[ 41 , 42 ]. The presence of the pre-existing dislocations with multiple 
Burgers vectors in the DC boundaries promotes the dislocation nucle -
ation and blockage [43] . The planar dislocation slip is mainly confined 
to small domains, i.e., the DCs, and thus the mean free path of the planar 
dislocations is reduced [42] . Moreover, the formation of the long-range 
planar dislocation pileup around the DC boundaries can intensify 
dislocation multiplication. All these dislocation behavior features facil -
itate dislocation accumulation and thus provide strain-hardening, 
resulting in the slowly decreasing strain-hardening rate with 
increasing strain. 
Such slip behavior of the DC structure and the resultant strain- 
hardening response are unlike those in the cold-rolled and low- 
temperature-annealed HEAs reserving the pre-deformation induced 
DCs [ 44 , 45 ], and those in the as-cast Al
0.1
CrFeCoNi HEA [46] and 316L 
stainless steel [47] with bare pre-existing dislocations. In those 
cold-rolled and low-temperature-annealed HEAs, the pre-deformation 
DC boundaries had no microsegregations and their dislocations were 
not with multiple Burgers vectors. The pre-deformation DC structure 
was thus unstable upon tensile straining so that dislocations can easily 
slip through the DC boundaries before being stopped at strong pinning 
sites like grain boundaries [42] . It led to larger mean free path of dis -
locations and the lack of effective blocking sites for dislocation pileup. 
Instead, the dislocations tended to move in the mode of cross slip. Owing 
to the lack of mutual trapping of dislocations, the dynamic recovery 
process, i.e., the annihilation of the encountering dislocations with 
opposite signs, prevailed. In those traditional as-cast Al
0.1
CrFeCoNi HEA 
and 316L stainless steel, starting from grain boundaries, the planar 
dislocations slipped over the whole grain interior before being piled up 
at the opposite grain boundaries. Due to the absence of the DC structure 
and the ultralow pre-existing dislocation density, the nucleation and 
blocking sites of deformation dislocations and the interaction between 
the pre-existing and deformation dislocations are rather limited. These 
dislocation behavior features caused negative effects on dislocation 
accumulation in both cold-rolled and low-temperature-annealed HEAs, 
and as-cast Al
0.1
CrFeCoNi HEA and 316L stainless steel; the dislocation 
multiplication was suppressed, and the dislocation annihilation was 
promoted. As a consequence, the dislocation accumulation was weak -
ened, and thus the strain-hardening rate of those cold-rolled and 
low-temperature-annealed HEAs decreased rapidly down to near zero 
and the resulting uniform elongation was quite low ( < 2%) [ 44 , 45 ], 
while those of the as-cast Al
0.1
CrFeCoNi HEA and 316L stainless steel 
also decreased quickly [ 46 , 47 ]. These comparisons suggest the unique 
and superior plastic-deformation stability of the AM-ed DC structure, 
which has been reported in LPBF AM-ed Cu [42] and 316L stainless steel 
[47] . 
With further straining, the DC structure is gradually unstable and 
disappears after fracture. The dislocation nucleation and slip are highly 
activated not only in the previous DC interior regions but also in the DC 
boundary regions. The case in the latter is continuously facilitated by the 
microsegregation atoms and multiple-Burgers-vector dislocations as 
they are also effective in promoting dislocation nucleation and blockage 
during the dislocation penetrating through and the destabilizing of the 
DC boundaries. The dislocations with increased density distribute more 
uniformly than those in the initial and 2.1%-strained states. The dislo -
cation mechanism by cross slip becomes significant, leading to the 
tension induced DCs with smaller sizes ( Fig. 11 b). The occurrence of 
dynamic recovery can be deduced based on aforementioned features of 
the dislocation behavior [48] . In addition, the deformation by the SFs is 
more activated. The number density of the SFs increases, and the SF 
structure changes to the SFB. The SFB contains dislocations, ISFs and 
ESFs, which is more complex than the parallel ISFs in the strain-free and 
low-strain states. Both the ISFs and ESFs are the obstacles that can block 
the moving dislocations [49] . Hence, the SFBs can provide a superior 
strain-hardening effect than the parallel ISFs. The enhanced dislocation 
and SFB-mediated strain-hardening delay the prevalence of dynamic 
recovery and continuously slow down the decreasing rate of the 
strain-hardening rate at high strain levels [44] . 
Unlike the AB MNiHEA, the ABG MNiHEA possesses stable DC 
structures even at the strain up to fracture ( Fig. 11 b). This enhanced 
plastic-deformation stability of the DCs is ascribed to the IR-NP particles. 
The high-density IR-NP particles inhibit the dislocation cross-slip and 
make the dislocation distribution uniform in the DC interiors even 
though the dislocation density increases notably. The resultant dislo -
cation patterns are mainly jogs and tangles with no tension induced DCs, 
dissimilar with those in the fractured AB MNiHEA. The high-density IR- 
NP particles have pronounced effect on the dislocation behavior espe -
cially considering that the ultimate strength of the ABG sample is ~200 
MPa exceeding that of the AB counterpart (the dislocation cross-slip 
tends to occur with increasing stress). These effects can be explained 
with four reasons below. First, there are high-density coherent precipi -
tate/matrix interfaces acting as not only obstructions that can hamper 
the dislocations to pile-up around them [50] , but also abundant dislo -
cation nucleation sources [51] . Second, the dislocations can cut through 
and slip in the high-density IR-NPs, where the dislocation slip and 
interaction are enabled, and the dislocation density increases [ 49 , 50 ]. 
Third, the SF-mediated plasticity is enhanced by the hierarchical ISF 
network with LC locks [52] . The ISFs on different slip planes and the LC 
locks all are blocking sites for the dislocation movement which can cause 
dislocation accumulation. Fourth, the DC boundaries with the 
high-density multiple-Burgers-vector dislocations and micro -
segregations themselves are dislocation nucleation sites and obstacles 
that can stop and trap the coming dislocations [41] . These multiple 
dislocation nucleation/blocking sites are high-density and are dispersed 
evenly throughout the material, which is highly favourable for dislo -
cation multiplication and unfavorable for dislocation annihilation, 
thereby significantly enhancing dislocation accumulation. As a result, 
the ABG MNiHEA shows the highest strain-hardening rate during the 
whole plastic deformation regime and the dislocations distribute more 
uniformly. It is worthy to note that with further increasing strain, the 
preservation or disappearance of the IR-NP-stabilized DC structure re -
mains mysterious, because the fracture strain effect on the plastic

5. Conclusions 
In this work, we have demonstrated the feasibility of synergizing 
intrinsic hot-cracking resistance and ultrahigh strength in AM of the 
MNiHEA, by carefully designing the compositions according to the 
thermodynamic rules. We have successfully fabricated crack-free, low- 
porosity and ultrastrong samples via LPBF, and the systematic experi -
mental characterizations and thermodynamic calculations have 
confirmed that the manifold characteristics, including the lower critical 
range, smaller SCI value, suppressed phase separations of the interme -
tallic compounds including the γ
′ 
by very high cooling rates, and mod -
erate hardening rate induced by the IR-SPs, are effective to achieve 
remarkable resistances to the various hot-cracking modes, despite the 
high (Al + Ti) content of ~7.4 wt%. The ultrahigh strength is attained by 
architecting the high-density nanoprecipitates strengthened DC struc -
ture through a combined process of LPBF and appropriate aging treat -
ment, surpassing the ACG counterparts and AM-unmanufacturable 
commercial high- γ
′ 
Ni-base superalloys CM247 and IN738LC. These 
findings also provide new insights into future alloy-design of next- 
generation hot-cracking-resistant high-temperature materials, such as 
HEAs, superalloys and refractory alloys with superior strength for AM. 
Our approach represents a new metallurgical concept via 
thermodynamics-guided composition design/modification for miti -
gating hot-cracking without compromising strength, which is cost- 
effective and generally applicable. The strength-ductility synergy can 
be further enhanced if the LOF pores are eliminated and/or the dislo -
cation density of the DC structure increases, where the processing pa -
rameters, e.g., laser power, scanning speed, and hatch spacing, can be 
further optimized via experiments and high-fidelity computational 
simulations of LPBF. In the future, integrating thermodynamics-guided 
materials design with computational simulations of AM is very prom -
ising though rather challenging, to design high-performance materials 
specifically suitable for AM to full exert the advantages of AM.
