ABSTRACT 
The present study investigates the microstructure, high-temperature creep rupture properties and oxidation 
resistance of three fourth-generation single-crystal Ni-based superalloys containing 0, 3.5 and 7 wt% of Ru. The 
addition of Ru changes the partitioning behavior of the alloying elements, decreases γ’ size and increases the γ / γ’ 
lattice misfit. The creep resistance keeps increasing as Ru content rises up 7 wt% under 1100 
◦
C/140 MPa. 
However, it was discovered that 7 wt% Ru alloy has inferior creep resistance compared with the 3.5 wt% Ru one 
under 1150 
◦
C/140 MPa, although no TCP precipitation was observed in this 7 wt% Ru-containing alloy. It was 
revealed that 7 wt% Ru-addition caused significant γ’ dissolution and induced the generation of creep cavities, 
which may account for the anomaly. Furthermore, the increase in Ru content up to 7 wt% keeps degrading the 
oxidation resistance of the superalloys by facilitating the formation of pores and cracks within the oxide scales, 
which also accelerate scale spallation. This work suggests that excess Ru addition may adversely affect both the 
mechanical properties and the oxidation resistance of the fourth-generation Ni-based superalloys. 
1. Introduction 
Nickel-based superalloys are extensively used in advanced aero- 
engines due to their excellent high-temperature performance [ 1 ]. As 
the increase in turbine entry temperature is key to improving engine 
efficiency [ 2 ], efforts have never been stopped to develop new genera -
tions of Ni-based superalloys, especially those of single-crystals, to 
permit a higher operating temperature for advanced engines. Previous 
investigations show that the high-temperature performance of superal -
loys would be significantly improved by Re addition due to solid- 
solution strengthening and a more negative γ / γ’ lattice misfit [ 3 – 5 ]. 
However, the formation of some brittle topologically close-packed (TCP) 
phases is simultaneously facilitated by Re. Dislocation can pile up at the 
boundary of these TCP phases during creep deformation, which causes 
stress concentration and leads to crack initiation. As a result, the creep 
properties of the superalloys are largely impaired [ 6 – 8 ]. 
Fortunately, recent studies have discovered that Ru addition could 
effectively inhibit the precipitation of TCP phases, improving the 
microstructure stability as well as the high-temperature creep resistance 
of superalloys [ 9 – 16 ]. Ru-bearing superalloys have ever since become a 
heated topic that attracted increasing research interest. Tan et al. [ 17 ] 
studied the high temperature creep properties of 0, 2 and 4 wt% Ru- 
added superalloys under 1150 
◦
C/100 MPa and 1180 
◦
C/70 MPa. As 
the Ru content increased, the creep strain rate decreased for both con -
ditions. It is indicated that Ru addition not only helped to reduce the 
interfacial dislocation spacing but also improved their stability and 
made them more homogeneously distributed. Song et al. [ 18 ] studied 
the creep behavior of two Ru-bearing (2.5 and 3.5 wt% of Ru) super -
alloys under 1140 
◦
C/137 MPa. While the 3.5 wt% Ru-containing alloy 
had better creep resistance compared with the 2.5 wt% one due to 
strengthened γ matrix and suppressed γ’ coarsening, TCP phases were 
still observed in both alloys. Zhao et al. [ 19 ] studied the creep behavior 
of two Ni-based superalloys with 0 and 2 wt% of Ru under 1120
◦
C/ 
130 MPa. By carefully comparing the dislocation configurations at 
different creep stages, they revealed that the enhanced creep resistance 
of the Ru-added alloy stemmed from the refined γ channel width, the

Considering the above, further investigations into the effects of Ru 
are required to answer the following key questions: 1) How does Ru 
addition affect the microstructure and phase stability, e.g. lattice misfit, 
γ’ size and TCP precipitation? 2) It is well-acknowledged that Ru addi -
tion could enhance the creep properties. To push the creep resistance 
limit of superalloys, can the creep resistance be further improved with 
higher Ru content ( > 5 wt%)? 3) Is the evaporation of Ru-oxides the 
primary factor that decreases oxidation resistance? If the answer is no, 
what is the corresponding degradation mechanism?

4.2. Effect of Ru on the oxidation resistance 
The experimental results also show that Ru addition promoted scale 
spallation and deteriorated the oxidation resistance. Generally, scale 
spallation stems from the release of stress generated during oxidation, 
that is, primarily the growth stress and the thermal stress. The growth 
stress is induced by volume change associated with the conversion of 
metals to oxides during oxidation. Usually, the Pilling-Bedworth Ratio 
(PBR, defined by the molar volume ratio of the oxide to the metal) 
[ 48 – 51 ] is applied to describe the volume change caused by oxidation. 
Since the molar volume of the superalloy changes with composition, the 
methods introduced in Ref. [ 50 ] were adopted for calculation. The PBRs 
for main oxides formed on three alloys are listed in Table 9 . The PBR of 
RuO
2 
will be discussed later. All the oxides have PBR values higher than 
1. Namely, the volume of oxides is larger compared with the consumed 
metal, yielding compressive growth stress and inducing the scale to 
rumple and crack [ 52 ]. It can be seen that Ru-addition could marginally 
increase the PBR values of main oxides. In this regard, relatively larger 
compressive stress should be generated for oxides growing on Ru-added 
alloys, leading to a higher cracking susceptibility as observed in Fig. 9 
(b).
