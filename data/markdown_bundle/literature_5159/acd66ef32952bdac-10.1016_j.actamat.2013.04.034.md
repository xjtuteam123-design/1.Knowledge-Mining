Abstract
A series of oxide-dispersion-hardened Fe–Al–Cr iron aluminides has been prepared by mechanical alloying techniques producing
compositions intermediate between binary Fe–Al and commercial Fe–Cr alloys. The very ﬁne oxide particles form by precipitation
on the {1 0 0} planes in the B2 ordered matrix, initially as a metastable face-centred cubic Y 2O3 phase which later transforms to an
Al-richer phase with monoclinic Al 2Y4O9 structure. The importance of this precipitation sequence in determining particle size and den-
sity is discussed. Mechanical properties are examined up to high temperatures, and strength is shown to depend mostly on the contri-
bution of the ﬁne particles, with also contributions from the ﬁne grain size, and from the ordered matrix. The roles of elastic
modulus variations, strongly aﬀected by alloy composition, and of matrix strength variations in determining material strength are
emphasized. Analysis of the factors determining particle nucleation rate and high-temperature strength leads to the development of pre-
dictive rules for improving microstructural reﬁnement and strength.

1. Introduction
There is a lot of interest in oxide-dispersion-strength-
ened (ODS) Fe–Cr alloys for structural applications at high
temperatures, such as in the nuclear industry, especially for
new nanostructured alloys based on Fe–Cr with small addi-
tions of Ti (and other elements) as well as Y
2O3 [1,2]. Such
materials show outstanding high-temperature strength [3,4]
and creep resistance [5–8], and are stable at high tempera-
tures as well as under irradiation [1,9,10]. The high strength
has been explained by contributions of matrix and grain
boundaries and by strong dislocation–particle pinning [3–
8]. It is believed that the milling process used to prepare
the alloys leads to the dissolution of the Y
2O3, which then
precipitates out as nanosized oxides during subsequent
processing or annealing [2,11,12]. These oxides have been
analysed on several occasions, but their structure and for-
mation mechanisms during heating remain uncertain: for
example, it has been suggested that they have simple NaCl
structure and are highly defective [13,14], or that they form
via some transient amorphous or disordered state [15].
FeAl intermetallics have been examined for high-tem-
perature applications [16–20], e.g. in the petrochemical
industries, where the high Al content provides better corro-
sion, oxidation or reduction resistance than obtained by Cr
additions [16,20]. While Al may not be a preferred element
for many nuclear applications under severe irradiation in
view of undesirable transmutations, there are many other
applications, both within and without the nuclear industry,
where this will not be a problem. In view also of the possi-
ble embrittlement of Fe–Cr alloys of high Cr content [21],
perhaps due to phase separation within the matrix [22],
interest has been shown in ODS ternary Fe–Al–Cr alloys,

2. Experimental details
Three ternary Fe–Al–Cr alloys with Al content in the
range 30–40% and Cr content in the range 5–10% (atomic
percent is used throughout to indicate metallic composi-
tion) were prepared by Plansee AG using similar industrial
pilot-scale milling conditions to earlier studies [18,19] on a
binary Fe–40Al alloy. Nominal alloy compositions were
Fe–39Al–10Cr, Fe–33Al–8Cr and Fe–34Al–7Cr (and Fe–
40Al), conﬁrmed as correct within several tenths of 1%
by chemical analysis. All alloys contain Y
2O3 additions in
the range 0.3–1% (nominal yttria addition is indicated as
volume percent). The alloys were consolidated by hot iso-
static pressing at 1100 /C176C and extruded to round bar at this
temperature. Alloys will be referenced throughout as 39Al–
10Cr–1 (indicating Al, Cr and Y
2O3 contents), as 33Al–8Cr–
1, 34Al–7Cr–0.3 and 40Al–1 (for the alloy without Cr). This
selection of alloy compositions allows the independent
examination of the role of Cr addition for constant Al and
Y
2O3, the role of Al variation for constant Cr and Y 2O3,
and the role of Y 2O3 variation for constant Al and Cr.
Samples were machined from the extruded bars for ten-
sile testing at room temperature, and for compression test-
ing from room temperature to 750 /C176C. For both tests an
initial strain rate of 2.8 /C2 10
/C04 s/C01 was used with some of
the high-temperature compression tests repeated at diﬀer-
ent strain rates in the range about 10
/C06–10/C03 s/C01 to deter-
mine the sensitivity of ﬂow stress to strain rate. At the end
of high-temperature tests the furnace was removed and the
sample cooled quickly under load, reaching temperatures
below 500 /C176C (where dislocation recovery becomes very
slow) within 2–3 min.
Microstructures of consolidated materials as well as
deformed materials were examined by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) on thin foils prepared by electropolishing using a
20% nitric acid in methanol mixture at -30 /C176C and about
16 V. Preliminary TEM studies of particles, as well as
examinations of dislocations, were carried out using a
JEOL 2010 instrument. Subsequent analyses of particle
chemistry and crystal structure were carried out using
JEOL 2100 and JEOL 3000F instruments operating in both
TEM and scanning transmission electron microscope
(STEM) modes, using also high-angle annular dark ﬁeld
(HAADF) and high-resolution electron microscopy
(HREM) imaging as well as energy dispersive X-ray spec-
troscopy (EDS) for local chemical analysis. Studies of dis-
location conﬁgurations in consolidated and in deformed
materials were carried out using both bright-ﬁeld (BF)
and weak-beam (WB) imaging modes.
3. Results
3.1. Mechanical testing
Tensile and compression testing showed a rounded yield
point with strong work hardening to 600 /C176C but little to no
work hardening from about 700 /C176C and above. Similar
yielding behaviour has been reported before for ODS Fe–
40Al [23,24]. The present publication deﬁnes ﬂow stress
as the stress where 0.2% plastic strain was imposed, which
corresponds to the yield plateau stress at low temperature
and the rounded onset of plasticity at high temperatures.
The upper inset in Fig. 1 illustrates the determination of
ﬂow stress for one material at intermediate temperature.
Flow stresses are shown in Fig. 1 for the four materials
tested showing generally a high strength from room tem-
perature to about 500 /C176C and then a steady loss of strength
at higher temperatures. The two alloys with high Al con-
tent (39–40%) showed a relatively small change of ﬂow
stress to 500 /C176C, while the two alloys with lower Al content
(33–34%) showed a gradual fall of stress over this 20–
500 /C176C range. Two alloys (with 39Al–10Cr and 33Al–
8Cr) show very high strength at room temperature, while
two others (40Al and 34Al–7Cr) were only moderately
strong at room temperatures. Despite the signiﬁcant
strength loss to high temperatures, the relative strengths
of the materials remained unchanged, i.e. the 39Al–10Cr–
1 alloy remains the strongest, the 33Al–8Cr–1 the second
strongest, etc., as seen clearly in the lower inset in Fig. 1 .

4.1. Oxide particle formation
Even though powders have not been studied here before
consolidation, it is expected, as reported previously
[2,11,12], that the ﬁne oxide particles form by precipitation
from supersaturated solution during the heat treatments
used for consolidation. It is obvious that control of such
treatments is important to determine particle size and num-
ber, while control of the initial milling stage determines the
chemical homogeneity [12]. It would, nevertheless, be inter-
esting to examine structures and chemical homogeneity in
milled powders before heat treatment. The ﬁne particles
inside the intermetallic matrix have the chemistry of com-
plex Y–Al oxides, possibly containing small amounts of
Fe and Cr, and appear to be present with two composition
types: Y rich and Al rich. Two diﬀerent precipitate crystal
structures have also been identiﬁed, namely an fcc structure
with lattice parameter of 0.526 nm and monoclinic YAM
structure with lattice parameters near 1.1, 1.08 and
0.7 nm. The fcc structure corresponds closely to metastable
fcc yttria (Y

It is suggested that the fcc structure is chosen by the pre-
cipitates because of its similarity with the underlying B2
matrix. There is a clear orientation relationship of precipi-
tate and matrix, and a coherent particle–matrix interface.
Precipitate nucleation is easier because of the coherent
interface, of low interface energy and hence low nucleation
barrier. Importantly, the low nucleation energy ensures a
large number of precipitates after processing heat treat-
ments. After nucleation, crystal growth is determined by
the diﬀusive accumulation of Y, Al and O, with precipitate
morphology dictated by rapid growth at the circumferen-
tial interface and slow disk thickening due to slow ledge
nucleation and motion [30,31]. Assuming the initial forma-
tion of these fcc precipitates, there appears to be a subse-
quent change of crystal structure to the monoclinic YAM
structure, and the new particles do not appear to be coher-
ent with the underlying matrix. Resistance to subsequent
coarsening will be determined by the low solubility or dif-
fusivity of the controlling atomic species. EDX analysis of
the particles has suggested that diﬀerent particles may have
diﬀering chemical compositions, rich in Y or rich in Al,
which may correspond to the two crystal structures deter-
mined. Further work is required to examine this point.
It is of interest to compare the morphology and size,
also number, of the disk particles with equivalent oxide
particles found in the present 40Al–1 alloy, as well as with
commercial ODS Fe–Cr alloys. The Fe–Al–Cr alloys all
show disk morphology particles, with diameter/thickness

and commercial Fe-Cr alloys show essentially equiaxed

alloys (see Tables 1–4 ), is larger at 18–20 nm for the
40Al–1 alloy and the commercial Fe–Cr alloy PM2000
[32], but smaller (11 nm) for commercial MA956 alloy
[33]. In view of the great importance of milling and process-

restrict the following discussion to a comparison of the
present binary and ternary alloys, prepared under very sim-

4.3. Comparison of present materials with other ODS Fe–
Cr(Al) alloys
A comparison of the strength of the Fe–Al–Cr alloys
with ODS Fe–Cr(Al) alloys shows that the present alloys
are of great interest in possessing good strength for the
entire range of temperatures studied, see Fig. 10 . The Fe–
20Cr-base PM2000 alloy [6,7] is weaker at all temperatures,
even in the ﬁne-grained state. Nanodispersion-strengthened
14YWT (Fe–14Cr-base ODS alloy with small additions of
Y, W and Ti) [3,4,6,7] is very strong at low temperatures in
the ﬁne-grained state, but is much weaker after recrystalli-
zation to a coarse grain. The rapid loss of strength of ﬁne-
grained 14YWT from 500 /C176C means that this material has
similar strength to the present alloys in the most-relevant
600–800 /C176C range. Nanocluster-strengthened alloys such
as 14YWT have very ﬁne grain size, 100–500 nm [3,4,6,7]
and very large numbers of 2–5 nm sized dispersoids [1–4].
Since these nanodispersion-strengthened alloys contain
many more oxide particles than the present materials
(e.g. 5 times ﬁner particles and 100 times more particles),
the athermal Orowan strengthening at low temperatures
can be as much as 5 times larger than for the present alloys.
This very ﬁne microstructure means, however, that
strength is more temperature dependent than for the pres-
ent alloys as thermally activated deformation processes,
such as Coble creep in grain boundaries or dislocation acti-
vation over particles, operate more easily [3,4,6,7]. It is well
known, for example, that coarse particles lead to less Oro-
wan strengthening, since their separation is large, but ther-
mal activation of dislocations over obstacles is too easy
when excessively ﬁne particles, e.g.<2–20 nm, are present
[49]. From this discussion it is clear that the present alloys
show good promise for high-temperature strengthening,
and future work will look at their stress–time dependence
and creep behaviour at high temperatures.
5. Conclusions
A series of Fe–Al–Cr alloys has been prepared using
mechanical alloying to mix in yttria and then processed
using standard consolidation techniques. Compared with
the binary Fe–40Al alloy, the various Fe–Al–Cr alloys have
slightly ﬁner, more numerous oxide particles. The disk
morphology of these particles is understood by the initial
formation of a metastable fcc Y
2O3 phase with low-energy
disk–matrix interface, which subsequently transforms to an
Al–Y oxide with YAM structure. Adjusting the Fe–Al–Cr
composition seems to control interface energy and particle
nucleation rate such that ﬁner oxides should be possible in
alloys of reﬁned compositions.
Strength is analysed in terms of the contributions of par-
ticle (Orowan) strengthening, grain boundary (Hall–Petch)
strengthening and ordered matrix strengthening. Orowan
strengthening is signiﬁcantly greater than Hall–Petch
strengthening, especially in the ternary Fe–Al–Cr alloys.
The temperature and composition dependence of both is
strongly dependent on elastic modulus, which varies
greatly with temperature and Al and Cr contents. Anoma-
lous strengthening of the FeAl matrix, especially for high
Al content, leads to a stable strength up to intermediate
temperatures for such alloys. The role of Fe–Al–Cr compo-
sition in controlling elastic parameters and strength is clear,
again suggesting ways to improve material strength.
