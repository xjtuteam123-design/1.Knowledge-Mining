ABSTRACT
Dual phase Ti-Fe alloys have been receiving attention for their favorable mechanical performance at room 
temperature; however, their strength significantly deteriorates at high temperatures. This study aimed to 
enhance the room temperature strength of Ti-Fe alloys through rhenium (Re) addition, and to investigate its 
effect on high temperature strength. By powder metallurgy and thermomechanical processing, Ti-4Fe alloys with 
various Re content were fabricated. As a result, 0.2 % yield strength increased from 750 MPa (Ti-4Fe) to 1433 
MPa (Ti-4Fe-4.0Re), while elongation decreased from 30 % to 7 % at room temperature. This improvement was 
primarily attributed to an increase in the area fraction of the β phase, followed by Re solid solution strengthening 
in the β phase and boundary strengthening. At 500 ℃ , the 2.5 wt% Re addition improved the ultimate tensile 
strength of Ti-4Fe from 258 MPa to 514 MPa. These findings demonstrate the superior strengthening effect of Re 
at both room and high temperatures compared to other alloying elements reported in the literature, offering 
valuable insights for the development of high-performance Ti alloys.
1. Introduction
Titanium (Ti) alloys, especially α + β alloys, have been widely 
applied to various industries owing to their excellent mechanical prop -
erties, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility [ 1 , 2 ]. To stabilize the β 
phase (stable above 882 ℃ in pure Ti [ 3 ]) and tailor alloy characteris -
tics, numerous β -stabilizing elements have been introduced. For high 
strength alloys, iron (Fe) has been receiving considerable attention for 
its low cost, little toxicity, and large solid solution strengthening effect 
[ 4 – 11 ]. Thus, mechanical properties of α + β Ti-Fe based alloys have 
been widely studied [ 8 – 18 ]. For instance, Umeda et al. investigated 
microstructures and room temperature tensile properties of binary Ti-Fe 
alloys [ 17 ]. The 6 wt% Fe addition to commercially pure Ti (CP-Ti) 
increased the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) from 600 to 1182 MPa 
mostly due to grain refinement and an increase in the area fraction of the 
β phase hardened by Fe solid solution. Despite this substantial 
strengthening, the deterioration in elongation to failure (ETF) was 
modest, i.e., from 34 % to 29 %. Therefore, considering the strength- 
ductility trade-off relationship, this sufficient elongation allows further 
strengthening of Ti-Fe alloys by incorporating additional alloying ele -
ments. In another study, Fujii et al. reported that a Ti-1.5Fe-0.5O-0.05 N 
alloy exhibited approx. 950 MPa in UTS, which was comparable to a Ti- 
6Al-4V alloy (approx. 1000 MPa) [ 16 ]. However, the UTS of Ti-1.5Fe- 
0.5O-0.05 N deteriorated to approx. 250 MPa at 400 ℃ , nearly half of Ti- 
6Al-4 V (estimated to 525 MPa). Similarly, there is a report that the Fe 
addition to Ti-6Al-4 V led to lower flow stress at 800 ℃ [ 19 ]. The 
deterioration of high temperature strength could be owing to the fast 
diffusivity of Fe [ 5 ]. These reports on the Ti-Fe based alloys showed the 
excellent effect of Fe on the room temperature strength of Ti alloys; 
however, there is scope for improvement of room temperature strength 
and drawback on high temperature strength in Ti-Fe alloys.
To improve both in room and high temperature mechanical perfor -
mance, the addition of refractory elements has been widely recognized 
as an effective approach. The refractory elements are a group of metals 
with high melting points above 2200 ℃ , including tungsten (W), 
rhenium (Re), tantalum (Ta), molybdenum (Mo) and niobium (Nb). 
From the viewpoint of alloying elements for Ti alloys, they behave as β 
phase stabilizers, increasing strength of the β phase by their solid

been employed in some commercial heat-resistant Ti alloys such as IMI-

Chlebus et al. studied microstructures and tensile properties of bi -
nary Ti-Re alloys additively-manufactured by laser powder bed fusion 
(L-PBF) [ 32 , 33 ]. Since the Re addition led to grain refinement of lath 
martensite, the yield strength increased from 659 MPa (CP-Ti) to 1197 
MPa (Ti-4Re). Meanwhile, the ETF drastically decreased from 13.5 % to 
1.5 %. This decline in ductility was partly attributed to undissolved Re 
particles, resulting from the insufficient energy input during L-PBF, 
which is inadequate to fully dissolve Re with its inherently slow diffu -
sivity and a high melting point. In contrast, Bahador et al. applied heat 
treatment to sintered Ti-Re alloys, demonstrating complete solid solu -
tion of Re in the β phase [ 34 ]. In addition, applying hot extrusion to the 
heat-treated alloys, a Ti-6Re alloy showed sufficient ductility in addition 
to the high strength, i.e., 13 % and 1040 MPa in ETF and 0.2 % offset 
yield stress (0.2%YS), respectively. This study suggests that Re is the 
promising element to improve room temperature strength of Ti alloys.
With regard to the high temperature mechanical performance, 
Shayakhmetova et al. demonstrated that the UTS of a Ti-5Al-5Mo-5V- 
3Cr alloy (Ti-5553) was increased from 317 MPa to 417 MPa at 600 
℃ with 1 wt% Re addition [ 35 ]. This report indicates the excellent 
potential of Re on improvement of high temperature strength. However, 
the applicability of this effect across other Ti alloy systems remains 
uncertain. Notably, while refractory elements typically improve high- 
temperature strength due to their low diffusivity, certain cases (such 
as a Mo-induced increase in the β phase fraction) may enhance effective 
diffusivity and reduce high-temperature strength [ 38 ]. Therefore, the 
effect of Re on high-temperature mechanical performance should be 
further investigated in different Ti alloy systems.
Inspired by the excellent potential of Re to improve mechanical 
properties of Ti alloys over a broad temperature range, this study aims to 
enhance room temperature strength of Ti-Fe alloys with Re addition and 
then to explore its effect at high temperatures. Ti-4Fe alloys with 0 – 4 wt 
% Re were fabricated using powder metallurgy and thermomechanical 
processing, followed by microstructural and crystallographic charac -

2.1.2. Alloying
The mixed feedstock powders were consolidated by spark plasma 
sintering (SPS, SPS-1030S, SPS Syntech Co.). The feedstock was filled 
into a carbon container with an inner diameter of 36 mm, and then 
heated to 1000 ℃ at a rate of 20 ℃ /min. The temperature was held for 
1 h under vacuum (6 Pa) with a uniaxial pressure of 30 MPa.
The sintered sample was then subjected to a solution treatment (ST) 
at 1450 ℃ for 1 h followed by water quenching, aiming to dissolve Re 
homogeneously in the Ti – Fe matrix. Finally, the solution-treated sample 
was hot-extruded to obtain a fully dense specimen using a 2000 kN 
hydraulic press (SHP-200 – 450, Shibayama Machine Co.). Preheating 
was performed at 850

6 mm/s and an extrusion ratio of 18.5 (container diameter: 37 mm, die 
diameter: 10 mm).
2.1.3. Impurity oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen contents
Oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen impurities are known to signifi -
cantly influence the mechanical behavior of Ti alloys [ 39 ]. Especially, 
their solid solution can cause enhancement of strength and deterioration 
of ductility. Thus, their concentrations were quantified in both raw and 
extruded materials using a gas analyzer (EMGA-830 OK; HORIBA). 
Oxygen and hydrogen contents were measured by non-dispersive 
infrared detection, while nitrogen was detected via thermal 
conductivity.
As shown in Fig. 1 , the CP-Ti powders contained 0.12wt.%O, 0.04wt. 
%N, and 0.002wt.%H, satisfying industrial standards [ 40 , 41 ]. The Fe 
particles exhibited high nitrogen content (1.18wt.%N). Previous studies 
reported that using the same Fe particles increased nitrogen content in 
Ti – Fe alloys, contributing to solid solution strengthening without 
inducing significant embrittlement [ 17 ]. Thus, the Fe particles were 
used without further treatment.
In contrast, the Re particles had a high oxygen content (2.64 wt% O), 
which posed a risk of embrittlement. Thus, to reduce the oxygen content, 
heat treatment was conducted on the as-received Re particles under 
hydrogen atmosphere, which successfully decreased the oxygen content 
down to 0.33 wt%. Consequently, as shown in Fig. 2 , the concentrations 
of oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen in the extruded samples were nearly 
constant across all compositions, indicating that impurity effects on 
subsequent properties were negligible.

In addition to the texture, the Re content also affected phase fractions 
and grain sizes ( Fig. 5 ). The β phase area fraction proportionally 
increased from 19.7 % (Ti-4Fe) to 76.1 % (Ti-4Fe-4.0Re) at the incre -
ment of 14 % per 1 wt% Re, clearly demonstrating the β -stabilizing effect 
of Re. As for the grain size, fine α and β grains ( < 2.1 µ m) were observed 
in Ti-4Fe and Ti-4Fe-1.0Re. However, the further Re addition led to 
significant grain coarsening of the β phase. This transition is associated 
with the suppression of CDRX, as evidenced by the disappearance of 
globular β grains in Ti-4Fe-1.5Re (IPF maps in Fig. 4 ). According to the 
model proposed by S. Gourdet and F. Montheillet, CDRX is governed by 
three elementary mechanisms, i.e., strain hardening, dynamic recovery, 
and high-angle boundary (HAB) migration [ 45 ]. Since dynamic recovery 
and HAB migration are diffusion-controlled processes, CDRX could be 
hindered by the slow diffusive Re, which is the potential reason for this 
grain coarsening.

enriched in the β phase in Ti-4Fe-(0 – 2.5)Re, which was consistent with 
the finding of Bahador et al. [ 34 ]. They showed complete partitioning of 
Re into β grains in Ti-Re alloys extruded in similar conditions to the 
present study. This partitioning occurs mostly during the preheating 
prior to hot extrusion. In terms of Ti-4Fe-4.0Re, even though the pre -
heating caused Re enrichment in the β phase, part of the β phase 
transformed into the α phase after hot extrusion, requiring Re to be 
partitioned from the transformed α phase back into the remaining β 
phase. However, due to the slow diffusivity of Re and the limited time 
and temperature window for diffusion in the extrusion process, some Re 
solutes could be trapped in the transformed α phase. Consequently, Ti- 
4Fe-4.0Re exhibited the α lattice contraction.
Regarding the lattice expansion in the β phase, this trend seemed 
contradictory, as solute atoms smaller than Ti are typically expected to 
induce lattice contraction. Indeed, Bahador et al. showed that the Re 
addition led to a decrease in a lattice parameter of the β phase in binary 
Ti-Re alloys [ 34 ]. However, lattice distortion in ternary alloys cannot be 
discussed simply with the atomic size relationship because chemical 
interactions between solute elements also play a significant role [ 47 , 48 ]. 
For instance, the W addition caused lattice contraction of a β phase in a 
binary Ti-W system [ 49 ], while it induced lattice expansion in Ti-2Fe- 
(0 – 3)W alloys [ 50 ]. Therefore, the present lattice expansion was pre -
sumably affected by the chemical interaction between Fe and Re.

3.4. Strengthening mechanisms at room temperature
Since mechanical properties of metals are strongly related to mi -
crostructures and crystal structures [ 58 ], the potential strengthening 
factors of Ti-4Fe with the Re addition can be inferred from the materials 
characterization in Section 3.1 to 3.3 . These mechanisms include: 
(i) Boundary strengthening (grain size effect).

4. Conclusion
This study has successfully enhanced the room temperature strength 
of Ti-4Fe through Re addition and demonstrated that Ti-4Fe-2.5Re 
exhibited superior tensile strength at both room and elevated tempera -
tures compared to previously reported Ti – 4Fe-based alloys. These re -
sults establish Re as an effective alloying element to overcome the 
limited high temperature strength of Ti – Fe alloys. The key findings are 
summarized as follows: 
1. The extruded materials consisted of α and β phases with no detect -
able Re segregation. The α phase exhibited an < 10 10 >
α
//ED 
textured globular grains with the low Re addition ( ≦ 1.0 wt%). With 
higher Re additions ( ≥ 1.5 wt%), more acicular α grains and inter -
granular α grains formed, accompanied by a < 0001 >
α
//ED texture. 
The β phase exhibited an < 101 >
β
// ED texture regardless of Re 
content. The Re addition increased the β phase fraction and its grain 
size.
2. The lattice parameters of the α phase were almost constant at 0.290 
nm and 0.461 nm in a - and c -axes, respectively. Ti-4Fe-4.0Re 
exceptionally exhibited lattice contraction in c -axis, suggesting Re 
solid solution in the α phase. On the contrary, a linear lattice 
expansion was observed in the β phase with increasing Re content, 
attributed by the Re solid solution and chemical interaction between 
Fe and Re.
3. The Re addition increased 0.2%YS from 750 MPa (Ti-4Fe) to 1433 
MPa (Ti-4Fe-4.0Re), while ETF decreased from 30 % to 7 % at room 
temperature. In addition, the Re addition maintained or slightly 
enhanced strength up to 500 
◦
C, as observed by comparing Ti – 4Fe 
and Ti – 4Fe – 2.5Re.
4. Compared to other alloying elements reported in the literature (e.g., 
Cu, Si, Zr), Re provided a greater strengthening effect on Ti – 4Fe al -
loys at room temperature. In terms of high temperature strength (400 
and 500 ℃ ), the strengthening effect of Re was superior to those of 
Cu, Si, W and TiC.
5. The improvement of room temperature strength with Re addition 
was attributed to three primary mechanisms: solid solution 
strengthening of the β phase by Re, boundary strengthening, and 
increased β phase fraction. Boundary strengthening dominated in 
Ti – 4Fe – 1.0Re, while increased β phase fraction was the major 
contributor in Ti – 4Fe – 1.5Re and Ti – 4Fe – 2.5Re.
