abstract
High strength, amorphous or partly nanocrystalline, multiphase intermetallic alloys with average com-
position of Al 65Cu20Ti15 have been processed by mechanical alloying of the elemental powders followed
by consolidation using high-pressure (8 GPa) sintering either at room temperature or at 300–450 ◦Cf o r
1 min. Examination of the microstructural constituents using X-ray diffraction and transmission electron
microscopy shows that the alloy pressed at 400
◦C is primarily amorphous, while nanocrystalline inter-
metallic phases, Al 2Cu, Al 4Cu9 and Al 5CuTi2 evolve from the amorphous matrix on pressing at 450 ◦C.
The pressing temperature of 400 ◦C appears most promising, leading to products with negligible porosity,
increment in the Young’s modulus by about 85% with respect to that of Al alloys, as well as impressive
hardness (7.9 GPa) and compressive strengths (1490 MPa). However, absence of ductility and poor frac-
ture toughness (3.8 ± 0.8 MPa√m) appear as the major challenges to be met for widespread application
of these alloys.

1. Introduction
Al-based amorphous or nanocrystalline alloys are of interest due
to the prospect of achieving two to three times greater compres-
sive strength than that in the known conventional age-hardenable
Al alloys [1]. The high strength level of the primarily amorphous
alloys is found to be accompanied by reasonable ductility or tough-
ness, if isolated ductile nano-metallic phases are precipitated in the
amorphous matrix and aid in shear band multiplication or deﬂec-
tion during deformation [2]. While partial crystallization in some
Fe-alloys may induce brittle failure (say in Fe–B–Si amorphous alloy
[3–5]), controlled crystallization in Al–Ni–Y amorphous alloy [6,7]
is known to partly increase the plastic strain to failure and tough-
ness. Rapid solidiﬁcation is a well-known method for achieving
either an amorphous or a nanocrystalline microstructure in the
ternary or quaternary systems containing Al, rare earth (RE) and
transition metals (TMs) [6–8]. Mechanical alloying is an alternative
non-equilibrium solid-state processing route known to produce
either a number of Al-based amorphous or nano-intermetallic dis-
persed amorphous Al–Cu–TM [9–1 1]or Al–TM–Si alloys [12–14].
In rapid solidiﬁcation or mechanical alloying, the product is either

2. Experimental procedure
A mixture of elemental Al, Cu, and Ti powders (with each con-
stituent having at least 99.5 wt% purity and about 50–100 /H9262m
particle size) was subjected to mechanical alloying in a Retsch
PM 400 high-energy planetary ball mill with 10:1 ball to pow-
der weight ratio using tungsten carbide (WC)-coated vial and balls
(10 mm diameter) to yield a single phase Al
65Cu20Ti15 product.
Milling was carried out in wet (toluene) medium to prevent the
agglomeration of powders and to retard oxidation beyond the ini-
tial stage of milling. The sequence of phase evolution during the
different stages of mechanical alloying was studied by X-ray diffrac-
tion (XRD) analysis using a Pan-Analytical X’Pert-Pro diffractometer
with Cu K /H9251(0.1542 nm) radiation. Furthermore, the powder mix-
tures were subjected to differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)
using a PerkinElmer Thermal Analyzer operated at a heating rate
of 50
◦C/min in inert atmosphere to examine the thermal stabil-
ity and evolution of the phases with increasing temperature. The
DSC data of pure Al crucible was obtained using the same heating
rate to determine the base line, and was subtracted from that of the
alloy. To identify the phase transformations associated with the for-
mation of speciﬁc DSC peaks, the powder samples were sealed in
vacuum inside glass tubes and heated at the rate of 50
◦C/min to
≈20◦ more than the DSC peak temperature and held isothermally
for 5 min. Subsequently, the glass tubes containing the powder
samples were taken out of the furnace and rapidly cooled in air
through convection, to disallow any further transformation than
that inside the furnace. The heat-treated powder samples were
examined using XRD to identify the phases formed during heating.
The alloy powder milled for 25 h was ﬁrst cold-compacted at
a uniaxial pressure of 100 MPa in vacuum (4 × 10
−8 MPa) using a
manual press. These cold pressed cylindrical preforms of 5 mm
diameter and 5–6 mm height were mounted in ceramic contain-
ers, and positioned between tungsten carbide anvils, where heating
was carried out using graphite susceptor coils. The samples were
sintered at 300, 400 and 450
◦C under a uniaxial pressure of 8 GPa
for 1 min. Following the application of heat, the samples having
diameter of 5 mm were furnace cooled to room temperature, and
subsequently stripped from the die for further characterization.
The sintered samples were examined using XRD analyses using
the experimental conditions similar to that used for the milled
powders, to identify the phases present. Density of the compacted
sintered samples was measured using a helium pycnometer (Accu-
Pyc1330). Based on density measurements, the samples prepared
by hot pressing at 400 and 450
◦C were found suitable for further
study of their structure–property relationships. Microstructure of
the compacted and sintered samples was studied using a JEOL
JSM-5800 scanning electron microscope (SEM) and this study was
supplemented with chemical analyses of phases using the energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Electron transparent thin foils were
prepared for studies under transmission electron microscope (TEM)
using mechanical polishing followed by argon ion thinning using
a GATAN precision ion mill for 30 min. These foils prepared from
the composites sintered at 400 and 450
◦C were examined under
a JEOL JEM 2100 high resolution transmission electron microscope
(HRTEM) operated at 200 kV, under bright and dark ﬁeld as well
as high resolution lattice imaging conditions. Selected area diffrac-
tion (SAD) was used to identify the phases present in the sintered
products.
The hardness and Young’s modulus of the alloys were measured
with the help of a nano-indenter (Nano-indenter-XP, Nano-
instruments Inc., USA) operated at a load of 500 mN load, and the
data (with a standard deviation of 1%) obtained from 15 to 20 inden-
tations at different locations on the same sample were averaged.
Fracture toughness of the alloys was measured at room tempera-
ture through the measurement of lengths of cracks at the corners of
indentations. As the crack lengths exceeded three times the length
of half-diagonal of their corresponding indentation, the empirical

Compression tests were carried out on specimens with

3.2. Density
Plots showing the variation of density and porosity of the com-
pacts with the temperature of hot pressing are shown in Fig. 7 .
On examination of the plots, it is obvious that the samples hot
pressed at 400
◦C have demonstrated the highest density, which
also coincides with the least porosity (both closed and open) con-
tent. The lowest density is observed in the samples, consolidated
at room temperature due to incomplete densiﬁcation and presence
of defects. Furthermore, it is interesting to note that the porosity
increases by as much as 5% and the density drops by a similar
amount as the temperature of pressing is increased from 400 to
450
◦C. It may be argued that formation of crystalline intermetal-
lic phases such as Al 2Cu with lower density (3.4 g/cm 3) due to
exposure at 450 ◦C, could result in a decrease in bulk density of
the alloy. However, the increase in the porosity content suggests
incomplete densiﬁcation, which could probably be explained con-
sidering the formation of the nanocrystalline intermetallic phases
at 450
◦C. An earlier study by Senkov et al.[18] on compaction of the
Al89Gd7Ni3Fe1 amorphous alloy powder by equal channel angular
extrusion has also shown that the presence of brittle intermetal-
lic phases inhibits densiﬁcation. The intermetallic phases are much
harder than the powder particles with amorphous structure, do not
deform under the action of the applied pressure, and also reduce
the plasticity of the amorphous phase. It is known that forma-
tion of hard, non-deformable particles in a matrix of soft particles
could signiﬁcantly reduce the consolidation ability of the powders
[27,28]. Moreover, the surface and grain boundary diffusivities of
the intermetallic phases with melting points much higher than that
of Al are expected to be different from those of the amorphous
Al
65Cu20Ti15 alloy nano-particles. Moreover, the energy provided
by heating of the sample is utilized in crystallization and nucleation
of intermetallic phases, rather than in densiﬁcation. The nucle-
ation and growth of crystalline intermetallic phases within the

4. Conclusion
The structure and mechanical properties of the Al
65Cu20Ti15
alloy in either amorphous or partly crystalline form have been
examined and the following conclusions are drawn. The alloys
show near-complete amorphous structure on pressing for 1 min
at temperatures between the ambient and 400
◦C. Pressing at
450 ◦C for 1 min leads to a microstructure constituting a mix-
ture of amorphous and nanocrystalline, intermetallic precipitates
including Al
4Cu9,A l2Cu and Al 5CuTi2. The Young’s modulus, hard-
ness, indentation fracture toughness and compressive strength of
the alloy pressed at 400
◦C have been found as 130 GPa, 7.9 GPa,
3.4 ± 0.8 MPa√m and 1490 MPa, respectively, which are higher than

of the alloy pressed at 450 ◦C is probably because of the brittle
character of the crystalline intermetallic phases, present in the
microstructure.
