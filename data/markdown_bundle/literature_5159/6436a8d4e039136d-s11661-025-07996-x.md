REVIEW ARTICLE
A Perspective on the Historical and Contemporary
Development of Refractory Alloys
CAROLINA FREY, KAITLYN M. MULLIN , JOSEPH JANKOWSKI ,
LEAH H. MILLS , NOAH R. PHILIPS , and TRESA M. POLLOCK
Refractory metals and their alloys are promising structural materials for operation in elevated
temperature environments ( /C21 1200 /C14 C). Despite extensive historical research during the
mid-twentieth century, challenges in achieving a balanced set of properties, including
fabricability, oxidation resistance, and high-temperature strength, hindered broader implemen-
tation of this alloy class. Recently, modern interest in these materials has been renewed by the
refractory multi-principal element alloy (RMPEA) design philosophy and new developments in
processing pathways, such as additive manufacturing (AM), that could enable their production.
This review provides a critical comparison of the historical and modern approaches to
refractory alloy design and processing, highlighting the shared mechanisms between conven-
tional refractory alloys and RMPEAs. Key areas discussed include deformation mechanisms at
low, intermediate, and elevated temperatures; the importance of tractable processing pathways
in the successful implementation of new materials; and design considerations for developing
alloys for AM that are derived with insights from the welding literature. Knowledge gaps and
critical challenges are identiﬁed, and opportunities are proposed to accelerate the development
of next-generation refractory alloys.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE refractory metals (Nb, Ta, Mo, W) and their
alloys have been of interest for high-temperature struc-
tural applications since the mid-twentieth century. This
material class has experienced several signiﬁcant histor-
ical disruptions in their development, driven by evolving
motivations and applications for these materials. Break-
throughs in processing capabilities in the 1950s occurred
concurrently with the demanding materials require-
ments associated with the beginning of space explo-
ration, leading to rapid advancements throughout the
1950s and 1960s.
1 In the United States, several policy
changes in the early 1970s, such as a shift away from
space-based nuclear reactors, resulted in an abrupt
curtailment of refractory alloy research.
2 In the decades
since, the ﬁeld of refractory metallurgy has remained
largely stagnant. 3 Nearly all refractory alloys in service
today were developed in the original burst of activity
that supported early spaceﬂight.
Several recent shifts in the landscape have brought
refractory alloys back into focus. An emerging private
space industry, 4 increased investment into fusion and
ﬁssion reactors, 5,6 and continued demands for more
eﬃcient, higher temperature turbine engines 7,8 have all
increased commercial demand for new high-temperature
structural materials. Coincident with increased commer-
cial pressure, academic interest in the refractory metals
was renewed in the early 2010s due to the introduction
of refractory ‘‘high-entropy alloys’’ (HEAs).
9,10 The
high-entropy design strategy proposed that materials
with new and unusual behaviors could be developed by
increasing their compositional complexity and maximiz-
ing their conﬁgurational entropy.
11–13 While many of
the original claims of the HEA hypothesis have not been
supported by further study,
14,15 the HEA concept can be
credited with reinvigorating research into refractory
metals after decades of inactivity. To acknowledge their
compositional complexity, but also the reduced role of
entropy, many in the academic community now refer to
these materials as refractory multi-principal element
alloys (RMPEAs) or refractory complex concentrated
alloys (RCCAs). In this review, RMPEA is used.

RMPEAs face the same challenges as conventional
refractory alloys, including poor oxidation resistance,
diﬃculties in balancing room temperature tensile duc-
tility with high-temperature strength, and diﬃcult pro-
cessing requirements. Contemporary research eﬀorts
would therefore beneﬁt from an increased awareness
and familiarity with the alloys, methods, and conclu-
sions of the earlier research on refractory alloys. In
addition, examples of interesting phenomena in
RMPEAs are hypothesized to be a function of their
compositional complexity; however, the modern char-
acterization techniques utilized to uncover these phe-
nomena may also result in new discoveries in
conventional, more dilute refractory alloys. The primary
objectives for this article are therefore four-fold:
(1) To integrate the historical body of refractory
literature into contemporary research efforts.
(2) To emphasize the importance of fabricability in
the successful implementation of new materials
and evaluate opportunities offered by new pro-
cessing pathways.
(3) To highlight unique properties and behaviors that
emerge from recent research on the higher
dimensional compositional space of RMPEAs.
(4) To identify knowledge gaps and propose future
opportunities for research on refractory alloys.
The ﬁrst portion of this review will focus on the research
and development of conventional refractory alloys and
their processing strategies. A brief history of refractory
alloy development from the 1950s to the 1970s and the
driving forces behind their development in the United
States, the former Soviet Union, and China are
described. Fundamental properties of the pure refrac-
tory metals are reviewed, followed by design strategies
and behaviors of conventional refractory alloys and the
characteristic processing methods that enabled their
production. A number of excellent reviews have been
published on conventional refractory alloys and, there-
fore, this perspective aims to emphasize key details for
the current community and integrate them into the
modern context. The authors highlight the following
historical references as particularly inﬂuential: ‘‘A
Decade of Progress in Refractory Metals’’ by Ault,
1
which covers the history of refractory alloys in the
United States from 1955 to 1965; the proceedings of a
1983 symposium hosted by Oak Ridge, 16 titled ‘‘Refrac-
tory Alloy Technology for Space Nuclear Power Appli-
cations’’; and the proceedings of a TMS symposium held
in 1993, 17 titled ‘‘Evolution of Refractory Metals and
Alloys.’’
The second half of this review will focus on compar-
ing the landscape of modern refractory alloy develop-
ment to its historical counterpart. The concept of
high-entropy alloys has led to the examination of
multi-component composition spaces not addressed in
early research on refractory alloys. The properties in
these compositional spaces, to the extent known at
present, are reviewed and compared to conventional
refractory alloys. Modern characterization and compu-
tational techniques bring new insights on phase consti-
tution, short range order, and precipitation
strengthening of refractory alloys. Similarly, emerging
processing advances, particularly in the area of additive
manufacturing, oﬀer new opportunities to broaden the
‘‘processable’’ compositional space. Finally, opportuni-
ties for future research on refractory alloys are
proposed.
II. HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT
OF REFRACTORY ALLOYS
There are several deﬁnitions that can be used to deﬁne
‘‘refractory metals’’ as a material class. The deﬁnition
used here follows that of Buckman,
18 deﬁning refractory
metals as metals with (i) melting points above 2000 /C14 C,
(ii) body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal structures, and
(iii) oxide melting temperatures below the melting point
of the pure metal. This limits the materials under
discussion to Nb,* Ta, Mo, and W. Under other
deﬁnitions, this category may also include Re, Ru, and
Rh, which possess high melting temperatures but which
have hexagonal close packed (HCP) crystal struc-
tures,
20–22 and Ti, Zr, Hf, V, and Cr, which are frequent
alloying elements for Nb, Ta, Mo, and W. Due to
mutual solubility, relatively high melting points, and
metallurgical compatibility, the Group 4, 5, and 6
elements plus Re are often considered the basic building
blocks of refractory alloy design.
Research on refractory metals and their alloys was
driven primarily by their potential for high-temperature
structural applications, particularly for nuclear propul-
sion and power systems (both for space-based and
land-based reactors), and rocket and re-entry materials.
Refractory metals and their alloys are also key materials
for other applications, such as superconducting radio
frequency (SRF) cavities for particle accelerators,
23
superconducting wire, 24 lamp ﬁlaments, 25,26 integrated
circuits,27 rotating X-ray anodes, 28 high-temperature
springs,29,30 and as corrosion-resistant materials in
chemical processing, 31 though these applications have
not motivated research on the same scale as the
programs targeted for high-temperature structural
applications.

trends based on their shared BCC crystal structure and
their locations on the periodic table. The mechanical
behaviors of BCC metals are characterized by a strong
temperature dependence of strength and ductility. As
temperatures increase from low (cryogenic) to interme-
diate temperatures, there is a steep decline in yield
strength and a corresponding change in the plasticity
behavior from brittle to ductile. The transition to ductile
behavior is associated with a speciﬁc temperature that is

III. COMMERCIAL PROCESSING
OF REFRACTORY ALLOYS
A. Ingot Metallurgy
The enduring value proposition that distinguishes
refractory alloys from ceramics and composites is the
ease with which they can be integrated into a wide range
of useful forms. When fabricable, they can be easily
bent, stretched, and joined to meet a wide range of
application needs while maintaining unique high-tem-
perature properties. As such, they are available in a wide
range of semi-ﬁnished forms: plate, sheet, foil, bar, tube,
wire, forged, and recently, powder. These varied shapes
can be further reﬁned by spinning, bulging, deep
drawing, wire/tube drawing, welding, brazing, riveting,
machining, bending, to name the most common. As a
general rule, these processes follow a similar pathway:
primary melting, primary fabrication (breakdown), and
secondary fabrication. This sequence addresses the
preliminary breakdown of the low-ductility initial
microstructure, followed by a sequence of operations
which progressively reﬁne the microstructure, improving
the behavior of the material for both end-use and
intermediate process operations essential for manufac-
turing useful articles.
For Nb- and Ta-based alloys, Ingot Metallurgy (IM)
begins with primary melting, wherein the alloy compo-
sition is established through casting of a large ingot and
subsequent processing. Techniques like VAR and EB
melting are commonly used both in combination and
individually. Multiple remelts are required to achieve
suﬃcient mixing for a homogeneous ingot. Although
Mo-based alloys can be produced via melting, powder
metallurgy is much more common. Casting of W is rare
for large-scale production.
42 Mo and W, and their
alloys, are typically reduced from oxides into metal
powder,274,275 which is then consolidated into large
preforms via some combination of hot and cold press-
ing, and sintering. Further discussion of powder pro-
cessing is provided in Section III–B.
After consolidation, both Group 5 and 6 alloys are
largely produced using processes in common with other
major alloy families. Starting with classical ‘‘hot work’’
of forging or extrusion to break down ingot structure,
often at >0:5T
m, though W processing at this stage is
better described as ‘‘warm work’’; progressing through
thermal treatment to recrystallize the microstructure,
recover dislocation structure, or manipulate precipitates;
and then repeating TMP to reach the desired properties
and product form, generally at progressively lower
temperatures. The Group 5 alloys are readily cold
worked and can be processed in a wide range of forms.
Mo and W generally must be worked initially at elevated
temperatures to establish a suﬃcient population of
dislocations, which then enables working at progres-
sively lower temperatures. In some cases, deformation
processing can be completed at room temperature.

alloys

B. Powder Processes
Powder metallurgy (P/M) has long been identiﬁed as
an attractive processing method for producing refrac-
tory alloys in near-net-shaped forms. Various P/M
techniques, including cold pressing, furnace or direct
sintering and subsequent TMP, spark plasma sintering,
and hot isostatic pressing (HIP), have successfully been
utilized for refractory alloy production.
286–290 Histori-
cally, P/M processes have been industrially implemented
for Mo- and W-based alloys. Large-scale application of
P/M to Nb and Ta has been restricted by challenges in
producing high-quality powder at a feasible cost,
necessitating a brief review of powder production
methods for each of these metals.
Mo and W powders are derived from chemical oxide
precursors, which are subsequently reduced in a hydro-
gen atmosphere to their elemental form.
291 In contrast
to the Group 6 elements, Group 4 and 5 elements form

from the alloy. The conversion from Nb and Ta oxides

their oxides, followed by electron beam reﬁning, alloy-

in this process increases processing costs and cumulative
powder contamination.

deliver satisfactory homogeneity or cleanliness in the
consolidated structure.

deformation processing and do not take advantage of
the potential of P/M processing.

IV. CONTEMPORARY DEVELOPMENT
OF REFRACTORY ALLOYS
In recent years, academic interest in refractory alloys
has been renewed by the arrival of refractory mul-
ti-principal element alloys (RMPEAs). The worldwide
research focus on RMPEAs is the most signiﬁcant eﬀort
to study refractory alloys in decades, with hundreds of
compositions reported.
305,306 RMPEAs utilize the same
palette of elements as conventional refractory alloys and
the motivations for investigating RMPEAs remain
consistent with historical drivers: development of new
high-temperature materials that can surpass the capa-
bility of Ni-based superalloys and operate at tempera-
tures /C21 1200
/C14 C. Their targeted applications include gas
turbine engines, land- and space-based ﬁssion reactors,
fusion reactors, hypersonic vehicles, and heat exchang-
ers, a list that would be as applicable in the 1960s as it is
today.
307 Despite these similarities in composition and
target applications, there is a disconnect between current
eﬀorts in the RMPEA research community and the
highly relevant work performed on conventional refrac-
tory alloys. The second half of this review seeks to
discuss similarities between RMPEAs and conventional
refractory alloys, highlight gaps, and propose opportu-
nities for research that are drawn from both our
historical and contemporary knowledge.
A. The Emergence of Refractory High-Entropy Alloys
RMPEAs were originally introduced as refractory
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs). Formally proposed in the
mid-2000s by Cantor et al.
12 and Yeh et al. ,11,308 the
‘high-entropy’ design strategy focused on compositions
near the center of multi-component phase space, with
approximately equiatomic mixtures of multiple con-
stituents. High-entropy alloys (HEAs) were a shift from
the traditional principal element approach, where a
major element like Nb is selected for desirable properties
and then alloyed in relatively dilute quantities to
optimize properties. In the multi-principal element
approach, all elements are considered solutes and no
dominant ‘‘matrix’’ element exists. The name is derived
from the hypothesis that the higher conﬁgurational
entropy that arises from the complex chemical solution
would result in unusual and improved properties.
308,309
The original deﬁnition of an HEA was strictly based
on composition. For an alloy to be an HEA, it was

B. Thermophysical Properties
Characterization of thermophysical properties, such

modeling eﬀorts, including the simulation of high-tem-

systems-level design, such as design of compatible
coatings for high-temperature environments.
160 Exper-

C. Mechanical Behavior and Dislocation Mechanisms
The mechanical properties of RMPEAs appear to
follow the trends observed in conventional alloys, with
alloying elements having similar eﬀects in both classes of
materials.
327 For example, additions of Mo and W,
which possess higher shear moduli, melting tempera-
tures, and DBTTs, promote high strength and creep
resistance in RMPEAs while degrading ductil-
ity.
306,328,329 V has one of the smallest lattice parameters
of the refractory elements (only Cr is smaller). In the
historical literature, this makes V a potent addition to
improve the room and intermediate-temperature
strength of conventional Nb-based alloys due to its
high lattice mismatch with Nb.
70 It has a similar eﬀect in
RMPEAs.330 The temperature dependence of yield
strength in pure and conventional dilute BCC alloys,
which are characterized by three regimes at low,
intermediate, and high temperature (Figure 3), is also
found in RMPEAs 331,332 (Figure 11).
The solid solution strengthening widely utilized in
conventional refractory alloys (see Section II–B)i s
further enhanced using the RMPEA design strategy. 332
This is due to an increase in atomic lattice distortion
with increasing compositional complexity. In contrast to
conventional alloys, many RMPEAs retain signiﬁcant
strength at intermediate (0 :2 /C0 0:4 T=Tm) homologous
temperatures, and for a subset of RMPEA composi-

Figure 11).327,332 The mechanisms that control strength
at ultrahigh-temperatures ( >0:7 T=Tm) are diﬃcult to
study and have rarely been evaluated for RMPEAs or
conventional alloys.

intermediate temperatures (Figure 11) indicates that
there may be diﬀerences in dislocation behavior com-

RMPEAs, suggesting that alternate (or at least modi-

tion.

D. Phase Evolution and Short Range Order (SRO)
With multi-component RMPEA compositions often
being approximately equiatomic, new thermodynami-
cally stable ternary and higher order phases might be
expected. However, in general, the equilibrium phases
formed by RMPEAs are consistent with the phases
present in their constituent binary and ternary phase
diagrams. This behavior was obscured in early investi-
gations of RMPEAs due to the use of as-cast, sputtered,
or rapidly solidiﬁed samples, which have non-equilib-
rium microstructures.
306 For example, the pure Group 4
(Ti, Zr, Hf) elements possess both HCP and BCC
allotropes, and exhibit BCC miscibility gaps in several of
their binary systems with Group 5 and 6 elements.
Therefore, RMPEAs with additions of Group 4 ele-
ments frequently form HCP and secondary BCC phases
at low-to-intermediate temperatures.
345 Cr, which forms
Laves phases in its binary systems with Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb,
and Ta, promotes the formation of Laves phases in
RMPEAs.
306
While the equilibrium phase behavior of RMPEAs is
comparable to that of conventional alloys, the compo-
sitional complexity of RMPEAs may promote the
formation of metastable phases and local elemental
partitioning or ordering, i.e., short range order (SRO).
SRO has also been referred to as high/medium/low
energy clusters (H/M/LECs),
348 or ordered oxygen
clusters (OOCs) when promoted by oxygen. 349,350

ordering to a B2 crystal structure.

resulting B2 phase is frequently metastable and can

E. Second Phase Strengthening
It is unlikely that signiﬁcant property advancements
will be achieved in refractory alloys, including
RMPEAs, without moving from single-phase to two-
phase alloys. 361 The development of new coatings that
are protective for longer at higher temperatures would
unlock the potential of legacy alloys already developed,
but improvements to the underlying mechanical prop-
erties require new strategies. Precipitation and disper-
sion strengthening are already utilized extensively in
Mo- and W-based alloys, but major opportunities exist
for Nb- and Ta-based alloys. Re-evaluating legacy
carbide-strengthened Nb- and Ta-based alloys is one
opportunity highlighted in Section V–C–i. Another
opportunity to consider is new intermetallic strengthen-
ing phases that were unexplored in previous cycles of
alloy development.
Strengthening by a large volume fraction of coherent
intermetallic precipitates is a highly appealing strategy
for materials targeted for high-temperature applications.
Microstructural stability is one of the key properties
that determines the lifetime of a material, and the low
interfacial energies of coherent precipitates help reduce
the driving force for coarsening.
362,363 This strategy is
inspired by c=c0 Ni-based superalloys, which are the
prototypical materials for extreme environments that
combine thermal, mechanical, and chemical stres-
sors.7,362 Legacy refractory alloys did not pursue this
strategy because useful intermetallic phases are not
formed within mixtures of Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta, Cr,
Mo, W, and Re. 364,365 These elements can form Laves
and sigma phases, but these phases are incoherent and
highly brittle and, therefore, not usually suitable as
strengthening precipitates. Accessing other intermetallic
phases, particularly those derived from the BCC lattice,
requires additions of non-refractory elements such as Al,
Co, Fe, Ru, or Pt. From this perspective, RMPEAs have
greatly pushed the envelope of potential strengthening
phases in their pursuit of compositional complexity.

F. New Processing Pathways
The recent revolution in additive manufacturing
(AM) and concurrent rapid advances in powder pro-
duction methods present new opportunities to overcome
the persistent historical fabrication challenges associated
with ingot metallurgy of refractory alloys. Modern
developments in AM, also known as 3D printing, oﬀer
economic advantages and design freedoms over con-
ventional ingot metallurgy, which has expanded the
industrial interest in and adoption of refractory alloys in
recent years.
406 The subsequent sections will brieﬂy
review developments in AM, integrate ﬁndings from the
historical welding literature, and discuss modern powder

1. Additive manufacturing
AM oﬀers signiﬁcant potential for cost-eﬀective

possible avenues for microstructural control. Though
these methods have reached appreciable maturity in
fabricating other alloy classes, such as Al- and Ni-based
alloys, printing of refractory alloys is currently in
nascent stages. Academic publications regarding refrac-

Ta, Mo, W),

V. OPPORTUNITIES FOR FUTURE RESEARCH
The increasing demand for structural materials that
can reliably operate at temperatures beyond 1200 /C14 Ci n
the aerospace, defense, and energy sectors is likely to
create new opportunities for research on refractory
alloys, including opportunities for both conventional
alloys and RMPEAs. Given the challenges inherent to
refractory alloys, new computational and experimental
techniques combined with a systems design approach
are essential. Inevitably, designs will be driven by
multiple objectives that are linked to application envi-
ronments where material property and performance
metrics are not always easily deﬁned.
Six important areas of future research are discussed in
the following sections in an approximate order of
priority. Some of these areas discuss opportunities to
improve our mechanistic understanding of these alloys;
others are targeted to address gaps in reported material
properties that are needed for modeling and design.
Where helpful, speciﬁc laboratory-scale methods and
areas for improvement are suggested. Universally,
small-scale or computational methods that can probe
properties at elevated temperatures and across compo-
sitional spaces are needed.
A. Oxidation Resistance
Poor oxidation resistance and the slow development of
new coatings are the key limiting factors that prevent
adoption of refractory alloys above current service
temperatures.
While oxidation resistance is not discussed in depth in
this review, for many applications poor oxidation
resistance and the lack of an appropriate coating is the
limiting factor. A critical lesson learned during historical
eﬀorts in refractory alloy-coating development is that
oxidation protection is a system-level property. Devel-
opment of new alloy-coating material systems with
desirable performance attributes such as longer life,
higher operating temperatures, greater achievable geo-
metric complexity, and lower cost should be done with
speciﬁc applications and fabrication processes in mind.
Depending on the end use, the following properties may
need to be optimized in concert with a substrate alloy:
environmental resistance, viscosity, interface toughness,
chemical compatibility, interdiﬀusion, CTE, and creep
resistance.
More broadly, the exploration of oxidation-resistant
coatings is beneﬁting from modern computational and
theoretical tools, combined with the decades of empir-
ical observations in the superalloy space. These can all
be leveraged to produce new coating concepts.
485 For
example, borosilicide coatings for Mo-based intermetal-
lic alloys have been developed, which provide protection
by a borosilica layer.
486,487 This is similar to strategies
being developed for Ultra-High-Temperature Ceram-
ics.488 RMPEAs with improved oxidation resistance
have been explored by revisiting the concept of complex
or multi-cation oxides, 489 which were found to provide
some degree of protection in early work on oxida-
tion.
490,491 Most recently, alumina-forming coatings for
Nb-rich alloys have been designed with the assistance of
computational thermodynamics. 485 These coatings have
thermal expansion properties that closely match Nb-rich
refractory substrates, making them attractive for further
development for intermediate temperature, longer-life-
time applications. A remaining challenge is to develop
deposition techniques for these coatings that are

Designing alloys with suﬃcient printability should be a
priority.
Traditional manufacturing techniques impose signif-
icant constraints on allowable alloy properties. As an
alternative processing pathway, AM could enable the
industrial production of novel alloys with exceptional
strength and creep resistance and only moderate levels
of ductility. Similar to the historical down-selection of
refractory alloys to those with suﬃcient weldability,
modern refractory alloy development may face a similar
down-selection with regard to their printability. The
drive toward additive manufacturing demands greater
attention to the fundamentals of solidiﬁcation, the role
of interstitial elements, and structure–property relation-
ships. This research opportunity has been hindered at
the academic level by the cost and availability of
small-scale powder batches of novel compositions.
Recent developments in ultrasonic atomization and
other lab-scale academic methods to produce highly
pure and spherical powders are promising to address
this gap,
480–482 but further development is needed.
A large design challenge for this space is the lack of
fundamental properties relevant to solidiﬁcation, such as
elemental partition coeﬃcients and solidus and liquidus
temperatures.

C. Exploration of the Refractory Composition Space
Combined experimental and computational approaches
will most eﬀectively accelerate the discovery of new alloys
and material behaviors.
The higher dimension compositional space that has
inspired RMPEAs promises unique mechanical proper-
ties, derived from diﬀerences in elastic properties,
dislocation dynamics, and the potential for precipitation
strengthening. Yet, after twenty years of intensive
research eﬀorts, these spaces remain relatively unex-
plored. Full exploration of the vast compositional
spaces represented by RMPEAs will proceed most
eﬃciently with a combined computational and experi-
mental feedback loop. Workﬂows that automate
small-scale processing for screening of compositions
that can subsequently be produced by intermediate-scale
processing paths and ultimately industrial-scale
approaches need to be developed. Successful develop-
ment of new alloys will beneﬁt from (1) advances in
compositional design that leverage reactive metal com-
pounds and precipitation strengthening and (2) compu-
tational and experimental methods to rapidly screen and
fabricate promising compositions; the following sections
address these two opportunities.
1. Role of interstitial solutes
A critical issue in the ﬁeld of refractory alloys is the
role of interstitial solutes, especially C and O. Interstitial
solutes are typically missing in thermodynamic assess-
ments and their concentrations are frequently not
reported in experimental investigations. By analogy,
the exclusion of oxygen from thermodynamic databases
of BCC refractory alloys is akin to omitting carbon in a
steel database; oxygen and its partitioning have pro-
found inﬂuence on dislocation behavior, ductility, and
strengthening at low temperatures. An example men-
tioned previously is dynamic strain aging, which can
cause instability at industrial metalworking tempera-
tures as well as reduce ductility during transients to and
from elevated service temperatures.
100 At higher tem-
peratures, the morphological evolution of the reactive
metal compounds, especially oxides and carbides, is key
to controlling ambient and intermediate-temperature
plasticity and high-temperature creep. Given the critical
role of interstitials, it should be mandatory for the
refractory alloy research community to report the inter-
stitial content of their alloys.
A deeper understanding of thermodynamics of inter-
stitials, including binding energies and solubility in
multi-component systems, would support designs that
control the DBTT and associated dislocation and
plasticity mechanisms. While the reactive metal com-
pounds ( e.g., carbides) can be introduced for strength-
ening or grain reﬁnement, this strategy has not been
explored in detail in conventional alloys with modern
tools or in the more complex compositional space of

D. Plasticity and Low- to Intermediate-Temperature
Mechanical Properties
At ambient temperatures, the required levels of plastic
deformation are determined by the intended processing
pathway and application lifetime
At low and intermediate temperatures, strength may
not be the primary design consideration. Instead, the
mechanical properties of greatest relevance are those
that depend on the material’s capacity for deformation,
requiring ﬁnite levels of ductility and fracture toughness.
Some level of intrinsic plasticity is essential for a
material to avoid unexpected failures during service, or
earlier during material processing and component fab-
rication. A case study worth considering is the intro-
duction of intermetallic gamma titanium aluminides
into aircraft engines.
508 Rotating blade components in
aircraft engines contain stress concentrations at features
such as the dovetails that connect to the disk, which has
stress concentration factors K
t = 2 to 3. The stress
concentrations can be blunted by local plastic ﬂow if the
material reproducibly achieves above 2 pct plastic strain
in tension at ambient temperatures and low fractions of
the melting temperature. Similarly, with some level of
ductility, ﬂaws and cracks that develop during process-
ing or as a result of impact do not reach lengths
suﬃcient for cyclic crack growth during subsequent
thermomechanical cycling in service.
509 To address these
potential failure modes, it is essential that fracture
toughness, cyclic fatigue, and fatigue crack growth
properties be evaluated.
There has been limited research on fatigue and fatigue
crack growth in refractory alloys in general, and this is
an area of priority for future research. Low and high
cycle fatigue studies have only been reported for pure
Nb, Ta, and Mo, and on the refractory HfNbTaTiZr
and C103 alloys.
121,510–518 The majority of these studies
have been conducted by bending fatigue and do not
consider a wide range of temperatures or loading
conditions. The available data suggest that BCC refrac-
tory metals and single-phase alloys follow the trend that
fatigue strength scales with the yield strength of the solid
solution alloy and is a high fraction of the yield
strength.
519,520 No fatigue studies have yet been con-
ducted on the two-phase BCC/B2 alloys to examine
whether they are more prone to strain localization and
have fatigue strengths that are a lower proportional
fraction of their yield strength.
121 There is also limited
fatigue crack growth data on refractory alloys, with only
a few studies that focus on Mo-, Nb-, and W-based
alloys.518,521–524 In general, the Paris Law exponents are
higher compared to other classes of metallic systems.
With regard to processing, higher levels of intrinsic
plasticity are often needed. For additive manufacturing,
the cyclic thermomechanical stresses combined with
solute segregation during solidiﬁcation require some
level of plasticity from the solidus temperature down to
ambient. One measure of tolerance is the residual
ductility in the as-printed material. Superalloys that
resist cracking during printing have up to 4 to 5 pct
ductility in the as-printed condition

as-printed ductility of C103 is 15 to 40 pct. 412,426,526

Finally, there is a need to connect fundamental
dislocation observations to macroscopic properties, with
models that predict tensile ductility and toughness with
at least moderate ﬁdelity. The intrinsic ability of a
material to deform plastically is limited by the character
of dislocations and their ability to glide through the
crystalline structure. The thermally activated motion of
screw dislocations is a characteristic factor associated
with low ductility in many refractory alloys. High
concentrations of alloying additions, characteristic of
RMPEAs, oﬀer the possibility of approximately equal
resistance to the motion of screw and edge disloca-
tions,
119,122,527 accompanying a high level of lattice

observed to inﬂuence the homogeneity of slip in the

refractory composition space. In situ digital correlation
experiments could provide critical information at the
individual grain and slip band level. Coupled with
crystal plasticity simulations, these techniques could
provide the insights needed to build predictive models.
At every stage, investigations of plasticity require careful
monitoring and consideration of the role of interstitial
elements.

Evaluation of a range of thermophysical properties is
necessary to support systems-level design and modeling
A large suite of material properties across a wide

VI. CONCLUSION
The high melting points of conventional alloys and
RMPEAs present opportunities for the development of
new materials for extreme thermomechanical environ-
ments. Higher operating temperatures inevitably trans-
late to improved performance and eﬃciency, and unlock
new possibilities for engineering design. However, suc-
cessful acquisition of balanced elevated temperature
properties and development of a suite of high-temper-
ature processing paths remain as major barriers.
Conventional refractory alloys and RMPEAs face
comparable challenges and exhibit many similarities in
their underlying mechanisms, to a greater degree than
has been acknowledged in RMPEA research. While this
could imply a lack of new opportunities, the wealth of
historical literature presents an excellent foundation for
the development of new materials. To be utilized
eﬀectively, the early literature needs to be reassessed in
light of modern theories and instrumentation. The
abrupt changes in investment that characterize refrac-
tory alloy development have left many historical
assumptions and proposed mechanisms unexplored.
For example, modern computational and experimental

In many ways, the contemporary era of RMPEA
research is an inversion of the ﬁrst era of refractory
metallurgy. The RMPEA community has access to
state-of-the-art characterization equipment and compu-
tational tools that did not exist in the 1960s, providing
an opportunity to understand the fundamental behav-
iors of refractory alloys beyond their macroscopic
physical properties. However, unlike the commercial
manufacturing facilities of the past, the RMPEA com-
munity currently lacks the facilities necessary to produce
and test refractory materials for their intended applica-
tions. As a result, the RMPEAs that have received the
greatest amount of attention are those that demonstrate
signiﬁcant cold working capability in the as-cast condi-
tion, which is antithetical to superior high-temperature
properties.
Additionally, while it is likely that there are new
opportunities in higher dimensional compositional
spaces, the drive for compositional complexity in
RMPEAs has also obscured the nuance and diversity
of refractory alloys. RMPEAs are currently collected
together as a single group of materials that have
potential for generic high-temperature applications,
but the material solutions required for operating tem-
peratures of 1200
/C14 C vs. 1600 /C14 C are fundamentally
diﬀerent. Each additional constraint—a desired lifetime
of 10, 100, or 1000 hours, an air or vacuum operating
environment, welding requirements, and so on—guides
alloy development along diﬀerent avenues and should
segregate refractory alloys into diﬀerent perfor-
mance-based classes. This mindset is reﬂected in the
application and processing-informed discussion of
refractory alloys in the Soviet, post-Soviet, and Chinese
literature, which identiﬁes classes of refractory materials
such as weldable Nb- and Mo-based alloys, high-tem-
perature spring alloys, low-density alloys for rocket
nozzle extensions, and oxidation-resistant cladding
alloys.
29,30,62,65,234,236,268 Ultimately, it is not the com-
position that deﬁnes an alloy, but its balance of
properties and speciﬁc application. To that point,
further clarity is needed from those involved at the
systems level on what are meaningful property targets
for relevant applications.
Applications in the nuclear, space, and propulsion
industries will continue to drive the demand for
improved refractory alloys. Building on the rich inter-
national history of refractory alloy development, new
alloy design strategies that embrace physics-based pre-
dictive models as well as AI/ML approaches promise
continued progress. This, however, will require invest-
ments in powder-based synthesis and 3D printing as well
as thermomechanical processing approaches, and high
temperature and high-throughput testing and charac-
terization that is informed by requirements at scale.
Oxidation resistance remains the Achilles heel of refrac-
tory alloys and new solutions—where protective coat-
ings are designed concurrently with the substrate
alloy—are needed. Additionally, considering the grand
challenge and complexity of refractory alloys, improved
academic–industry and international collaborations
would strongly enhance progress towards a new gener-
ation of alloys.
