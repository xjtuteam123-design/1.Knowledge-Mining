ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
The Site Preferences of Transition Elements
and Their Synergistic Effects on the Bonding
Strengthening and Structural Stability of c¢-Ni3Al
Precipitates in Ni-Based Superalloys:
A First-Principles Investigation
RASIM ERIS¸, M. VEDAT AKDENIZ, and AMDULLA O. MEKHRABOV
Advanced mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys strongly depend on the site preferences
of alloying X elements in c¢-Ni3Al-X precipitates, which are associated with the partial bonding
characteristics between Ni, Al, and X atoms. Therefore, in the current work, the site occupancy
tendencies of transition X metals were revealed via ﬁrst-principles ab initio calculations at 0 K.
Bonding features of Ni-Al, Ni-X, and Al-X pairs were simulated by using the charge density
diﬀerence (CDD), electron localization function (ELF), and density of states (DOS) methods,
respectively. According to simulations, higher atomic size X elements preferably occupy Al sites
of c¢-Ni3Al-X intermetallics and lead to strong covalent-like directional bondings between
themselves and their nearest neighbor (NN) Ni atoms along h110i directions. However, if these
larger X metals substituted for Ni sites, the bonding properties would diﬀer by plane due to the
nature of the L1 2-type crystal structure of c¢-Ni3Al-X precipitates. Considering all transition
elements, refractory metals ( i.e., X = Re, W, Mo, Ta, or Nb) appear as the most eﬀective
strength inducers, improving the structural stability of c¢ phase, even if Ni site substitution of X
= Re atoms would start to increase structural instability. On the other hand, relatively small
alloying X elements having electron conﬁguration similarities with Ni ( i.e., X = Co, Cu, Rh, Pd,
Ag, Ir, Pt, or Au) are more likely to worsen bonding strengthening. Instead, these transition X
metals creating metallic bondings with NN Ni atoms would contribute to ductility and
malleability of Ni-based superalloys. Furthermore, depending on the relative atomic size of
c¢-former and refractory elements, the phase and site preferences of refractory atoms would alter
in multicomponent systems. As a result of the attractive or weak repulsive forces between
Re-Re, Re-Mo, and Re-W pairs, the structural stability of the constituent phases would
deteriorate and harmful topologically close-packed (TCP) phases would precipitate.

I. INTRODUCTION
NI-BASED superalloys are extensively used materi-
als for turbine blades/discs and power generation
industries due to their enhanced creep properties orig-
inating from their novel two-phase ( c¢-c) microstruc-
tures. In these materials, L1 2-type ordered c¢-Ni3Al
precipitates are coherently dispersed in FCC-type dis-
ordered Ni-rich c matrix and cause yield strength
anomaly by resisting deformation at elevated service
temperatures.[1,2] In recent years, to further improve the
mechanical strength of Ni-based superalloys, the eﬀects
of transition metal element addition on the structural
stability of c¢ precipitates have been analyzed. [3–6] In
particular, the phase and site substitution behaviors of
alloying elements have been widely reviewed via numer-
ous computational, theoretical, and experimental
approaches.[1,7–30] According to these studies, Co, Cr,
and Ru atoms preferentially participate into c matrix
and promote solid solution strengthening, [7–10] while
Nb, Pt, Ta, and Ti atoms are strongly involved in c¢
precipitates and lead to precipitation hardening. [1,7–9,11]
Interestingly, the phase preferences of these frequently
added elements do not seem to be aﬀected by the
addition of other transition metals. However, refractory
elements that increase creep resistance of Ni-based

synergistic eﬀects with other impurity atoms on the
bonding strengthening and structural stability of c¢
intermetallics were discussed in detail.
II. COMPUTATIONAL MODELING
AND FIRST-PRINCIPLES CALCULATIONS
A. Methodology
First-principles density functional theory calculations
solving the Kohn–Sham equations based on the gener-
alized gradient approximation after Perdew, Burke, and
Ernzerhofer exchange correlation were employed as
implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package
(VASP).[32] For c¢-Ni24Al8 alloy, plane wave cut-oﬀ
energy and a set of k points were converged to 315 eV
and 9 9 9 9 9, respectively. In the convergence test, the
smearing type was selected as Methfessel–Paxton with a
smearing width of 0.2 eV, while self-consistent-ﬁeld
convergence was 1 9 10/C0 7 eV. In order to estimate the
eﬀects of magnetism on the electronic structures of
c¢-Ni3Al-X intermetallics, both nonmagnetic and
spin-polarized calculations were executed in real space
projection. For X = Zr or Nb atoms, semicore s and p
states were treated as valence states, respectively.

2. Density of States
As represented in Figures 6 and 7, pDOS calculations
are performed to correlate the electronic structures and
energetic states of pure and impurity induced
c¢-Ni3Al-(X) systems, where X = Ti, Co, Cu, Ru, Ta,
W, Re, or Pt elements are representatively
selected.[9,45,50] In DOS diagrams, the y-axis indicates
the contributions of p and d electron states ( i.e., Al-p,
Ni-d, and X-d) with respect to the energy values given in
the x-axis, where origin refers to Fermi energy, EF.
Hence, the electron states left and right to EF corre-
spond to bonding (occupied) and antibonding (unoccu-
pied) states, respectively. Sun et al .[46] reported that the
bonding mechanism in pure c¢-Ni3Al intermetallics
consists of strong metallic Ni-d/Ni-d p bondings, which
are formed as a result of perfectly overlapped Ni-d/Ni-d
states shown by black and orange in Figure 7(a).
Additionally, being one of the most important bonding
features determining the mechanical strength and
structural stability of these phases, the hybridization
between Al-p and Ni-d states constitutes covalent-like
directional bondings. [22,23,46,50–52] As clearly seen in
Figures 6(a) and 7(a), this hybridization occurs via
Al-p/Ni-d peak overlaps, i.e., a sharp bonding peak and
an antibonding peak at /C0 3 and 1.2 eV, respectively. [46]
Other weaker bonding peaks are approximately located
at /C0 3.75, /C0 2.5, and /C0 2 eV, agreeing with a recent
study.[23] Another aspect of the electronic structure of
the pure c¢-Ni3Al system is a ‘‘pseudo-gap valley,’’ which
separates the p-d bonding and antibonding states at
approximately 0.5 eV above EF. The presence of this
valley, therefore, proves the existence of covalent-like
directional bondings indicated by the orange region
between centrally located Ni and its NN Al atoms
(Figure 4(b)).[46,51,53,54]
In impurity induced c¢-Ni3Al-X systems, transition X
metals can easily lose electrons from their d orbitals.
This charge transfer between X and NN Ni atoms plays
an important role in the formation of strong bondings
such that X-d peaks close to EF overlap with the d
electron states of host Ni atoms and make the major
contribution to tDOS by dominating the electron
states.[52] If the pseudo-gap valley between the peaks at
both sides of EF is sharper and narrower, the cova-
lent-like characteristic of bondings becomes more pro-
nounced.[55] Figures 6(b) through (i) show pDOS
diagrams of c¢-Ni3Al-X intermetallics, in which center
Al sites are replaced by representative X metals.
Evidently, pseudo-gaps occurring only for X = Ti, Ta,
W, or Re additions are sharper and narrower in the
order of X = Ti < Ta < W < Re. Moreover, numerous
overlapped bonding and antibonding peaks of these
systems follow the same sequence, suggesting that X =
Re impurity atom is the most eﬀective strength inducer
in c¢-Ni3Al-X intermetallics via covalent-like bondings
followed by W, Ta, and Ti atoms. On the other hand,
pseudo-gaps do not seem to be present for X = Co, Cu,
Ru, or Pt additions such that X-d states of these
elements lie on EF and the substitution of these element
atoms for Al sites would lead to metallic bondings
instead of covalent-like bondings. When the aforemen-
tioned X metals substitute for center Ni sites, both Al-X
and Ni-X pairs are created in (010) and (001) planes,
respectively. Thus, Figures 7(b) through (i), presenting
pDOS curves of NN Al-p, Ni-d, and X-d states, prove
that X = Ti, Ta, or W additions lead to covalent-like
directional bondings through Al-p/X-d and Ni-d/X-d
hybridizations, while remaining X = Co, Cu, Ru, or Pt
metals do not induce such strong bondings due to a lack
of pseudo-gaps and antibonding peaks. Interestingly,
among refractory impurities, only X = Re atoms seem
to show a diﬀerent bonding strengthening behavior as
the preferable site is changed from Al to Ni. It is
believed that this situation arises from the fact that the
antibonding peaks of X-d states gradually shift from
higher to lower energy values in the order X = Ti > Ta
> W > Re > Ru > Co > Pt > Cu, as these elements are
arranged in the same sequence from left to right in the
periodic table (Figures 6 and 7). In the case of Al site
occupancy, Re-d becomes the ﬁrst state right to EF,
leading to the best bonding strengthening eﬀect

IV. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, the site occupancy properties of
transition X metals in c¢-Ni3Al-X precipitates and their
synergistic eﬀects with Re atoms on the bonding
strengthening and structural stability of c¢-Ni3Al-ReX
phases were analyzed in detail. Remarkable results are
listed as follows:
1. X = Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Nb, Mo, Tc, Hf, Ta, W, Re, or
Os metals preferentially substitute for Al sites, while
X = Pd, Pt, or Au elements tend to occupy Ni sites.
The site preferences of remaining X = Mn, Fe, Co,
Cu, Ru, Rh, Ag, or Ir metals are composition
dependent; i.e., these elements may substitute for
both Al and Ni sites.
2. In c¢-Ni3Al-X precipitates, the main impurity
induced strengthening effect occurs when relatively
large X metals preferentially occupy Al sites.
Simultaneously, strong covalent-like directional
bondings are formed between themselves and their
NN Ni atoms along h110i directions due to d-d type
hybridizations. In the case of Ni site substitutions of
smaller X metals, metallic bondings are created
within Ni-X pairs.
3. Refractory elements, such as X = Re, W, Mo, Ta,
or Nb, would dramatically enhance bonding
strength properties due to their high number of
semifilled d states.
4. Having nearly filled d states, X = Co, Ni, Cu, Rh,
Pd, Ag, Ir, Pt, or Au elements would enhance
ductility and malleability of Ni-based superalloys.
