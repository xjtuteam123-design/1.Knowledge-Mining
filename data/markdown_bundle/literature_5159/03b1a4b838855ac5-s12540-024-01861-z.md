Abstract
The process of printing components layer by layer is additive manufacturing, which has brought about a paradigm shift in 
the design, production, and distribution of goods to consumers. Metal additive manufacturing has executed an industry-
changing manufacturing production strategy. In the current review article, the printability of tungsten and tungsten alloys 
are summarized by examining several crucial parameters, viz, scanning strategy, volumetric energy density, and particle 
morphology. The high melting point of tungsten renders its melting through conventional casting extremely challenging. 
Traditional manufacturing methods have been employed for quite some time to address these challenges. In this article, 
only additive manufacturing techniques are surveyed. Powder bed fusion, directed energy deposition, and binder jetting are 
revolutionary production methodologies employed for most of the research on the 3D printing of tungsten and its alloys. Only 
a few articles regarding the printability of 3D parts produced from tungsten-heavy alloys are available. This review probes 
deeper into the impact of process conditions on the balling phenomenon, the attained relative density, and the propensity for 
cracking in pure tungsten and its alloys.

1 Introduction
Naturally occurring tungsten (W) is discovered in the min-
eral’s wolframite and scheelite. The term "tungsten" finds 
its etymological origin in the ancient Swedish term "heavy 
stone," which refers to a mineral known to contain tungsten. 
D-block is where it is classified in the periodic table. Among 
all the metals, tungsten possesses the highest melting tem-
perature (3420 °C) [1]. Furthermore, it exhibits remarkable 
mechanical properties even at elevated temperatures, high 
hardness, high Young's modulus, high resistance to tempera-
ture fluctuations, low thermal expansion, exceptional high-
temperature conductivity, and creep resistance. Furthermore, 
it demonstrates remarkable resistance to acidic environ-
ments, including nitric acid, sulfuric acid, and hydrofluoric 
acid. Its refractory characteristics result in the lowest vapor 
pressure among all metals. Electrical emitters, kinetic energy 
penetrators, thermionic emitters in spacecraft, ion propul-
sion jets, ignition tubes for the main rocket engine, medical 
radioactivity shielding materials, airborne antenna bases, 
construction parts in space nuclear reactors, and pinhole 
collimators are all instances in which tungsten-based alloys 
are utilized in high-temperature demanding applications 
[2]. W is chosen as the preferable plasma facing material 
[3] due to its poor tritium retention inside the material and 
high threshold energy for sputtering by hydrogen isotopes 
[4]. Furthermore, there is a rising interest in using tungsten 
as a reinforcing phase in metallic composite materials for 
advanced plasma-facing and structural heat sink materials. 
Improving the properties of such composite materials makes 
it possible to boost the effectiveness of plasma facing com-
ponents [5], which are subjected to intense heat, particle, and 
neutron loading while functioning in nuclear reactors [6 ].
Tungsten recrystallizes at a relatively lower temperature 
and is brittle even at room temperatures, it also exhibits a 
high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT). DBTT 
denotes the threshold temperature at which tungsten and its 
alloys manifest brittle behaviour. A high DBTT is the primary 
factor contributing to tungsten fracture, reducing its manu-
facturing capabilities. Impurities, lattice defects, and pores 
predominantly cause the DBTT. The brittleness exhibited by 
tungsten at room temperature is hypothesized to result from 
the body centred crystal (BCC) structure's restricted number 
of active slip systems. In tungsten and its alloys, intergranular 
fracture is facilitated by segregating impurities, including 
Oxygen, Phosphorus, and Nitrogen, towards grain boundaries 
(GB). Several methods have been attempted to minimize the 
DBTT of tungsten. These methods involve the utilization of 
ultrafine-grained, nanocrystalline microstructures, thermome-
chanical processing, production of tungsten-based composites, 
and alloying of tungsten [6]. The DBTT is reduced by alloying 
elements, including Iridium (Ir), Zirconium (Zr), and Rhe-
nium (Re). Furthermore, Titanium (Ti), Tantalum (Ta), and 
Vanadium (V) did not have an impact on the DBTT but rather 
enhanced its mechanical properties. It has been observed that 
tungsten's DBTT is increased by adding Yttrium (Y) [7].
Several researchers examined the impact of alloying ele-
ments on the thermal behaviour of tungsten. The following 
three alloy types stand out: (1) W doped with Potassium inte-
grates nanoscale Potassium bubbles that stabilize grain bound-
aries and dislocations, consequently enhancing its strength 
at high temperatures [8]; (2) W strengthened with Rhenium 
solid solution, specifically W26Re, where the redox changes 
the morphology of dislocations and affects the behavior of 
interstitial atoms, resulting in increased ductility at low tem-
peratures [9]; and (3) W strengthened with oxide dispersion, 
where La2O3 or Y2O3 [10] influences the high-temperature 
strength.
The W–Ni-Fe (WHA) alloy holds significant strategic value. 
Many conventional manufacturing techniques have been used 
to manufacture parts made from W-based materials in powder 
form, such as powder metallurgy (PM), hot isostatic press-
ing, and metal injection moulding. When the operating tem-
peratures of PM processes are constrained, residual porosity 
develops in W-based materials due to their elevated melting 
temperature and limited densification capability. Furthermore, 
pretreatments are crucial to produce the necessary configura-
tions of specific specialized instruments. The nanosized parti-
cles of tungsten-based parts tend to aggregate due to the strong 
Van der Waals laws of attractive forces. This aggregation leads 
to the development of diverse microstructures, and the PM 
processing coarsens the extremely fine nanostructures. How-
ever, because tungsten is brittle at room temperature, these 
techniques are inappropriate for fabricating tungsten compo-
nents with intricate geometries. The physical and mechanical 
properties of pure tungsten at room temperature are recorded 
in Table 1 Moreover, a heterogeneous microstructure is formed 
during PM processing due to the coarsening of ultrafine nano-
structures. Because of these limitations, additive manufacturing 
(AM) can be a promising manufacturing process for tungsten.

cating complex geometries with high precision. Moreo-
ver, AM consumes less material compared to conventional

subtractive manufacturing techniques. AM, frequently 
called 3D printing, is among the twelve state-of-the-art 
technologies that boosted the Industrial Revolution 4.0. 
[11]. Although AM approaches have been used over the 
previous two decades, the direct manufacturing of porous 
structures and prototypes is still in its infancy. As a result 
of advancements in applied science, component qual -

approach of the AM process is to construct a component

design-to-manufacturing process. In contrast to traditional 
subtractive manufacturing, AM utilizes a standard feed-
stock consisting of granules or wire. Numerous businesses 
and industries, including the aerospace, medical device, 
implant, military, and automotive sectors, have extensively 
used AM techniques [13] due to these exceptional benefits. 
The evolution of the AM process from the early 1970s to

According to the International Organization for Stand -
ardization and the American Society for Testing and Materi-
als 52,900:2021 standard [15], AM techniques are divided 
into seven groups.
(1) Sheet Lamination (SHL)
(2) Material Jetting (MJT)
(3) Material extrusion (MEX)
(4) Binder Jetting (BJT)
(5) Vat photopolymerization (VPP)
(6) Powder Bed Fusion (PBF) and
(7) Directed Energy Deposition (DED).
The PBF and DED are the two most adopted technologies 
in metal AM, direct metal deposition, directed light fabri-
cation, laser solid forming, laser engineered net Shaping, 
Wire + Arc AM, and Electron Beam AM are additional sub-
categories of DED [16]. The PBF production is additionally 
subdivided according to the energy source employed to fuse 
the powder layer; for instance, whenever a beam of electrons 
is used, the process is referred to as electron beam melting 
or electron beam powder bed fusion. When a laser energy 
source is employed, the process is addressed as selective 
laser sintering, selective laser melting, and direct metal laser 
sintering [17]. DED and PBF are two prominent AM tech-
niques for printing tungsten components that can generate 
denser metallic components for a different industry sector. 
Variations in powder management mechanisms impact the 
surface characteristics and intricacy of parts produced using 
distinct additive manufacturing techniques.

3 Parameters Affecting the Printability 
of Tungsten
The major process parameters that significantly affect the 
quality of 3D printed objects are spot size, scanning speed, 
laser power, and scanning strategy.
3.1 Scanning Strategy
The scan strategy relates to the geometric configuration 
that the energy beam adheres to while traversing the layers 
of powder particles in PBF [17]. A schematic of various 
scanning strategies is shown in Fig.  2a–g [17]. The scan-
ning approach for a solitary-layer scan differs significantly 
in the context of scanning sequence, scanning directions, 
scanning duration, scanning vector length, scanning vector 
rotation angle, and hatch distance. These factors substan-
tially impact the scanned layer's crystallographic texture 
and grain structure. Layer-by-layer fabrication generates a 
substantial temperature gradient in the build direction (BD), 
facilitating crystal growth in the opposite direction of heat 
extraction. The scanning strategy employed during PBF 
can substantially impact heat transfer and result in distinct 
microstructural evolutions. Therefore, selecting the appro-
priate scanning method can promote the development of 
uniform microstructural features and improve its respond-
ing behavior to service conditions. A few deficiencies, such 
as balling phenomena and significant residual stresses, can 
be avoided by modifying the scanning strategy parameter 
while working in AM technology. The tendency to form 
individual spherical droplets instead of a continuous scan 
track is known as the balling phenomenon. Implementing 
the remelting scanning method, Zhou et al. [18] found that 
balling events decreased, and the solidified surface became 
smoother.
3.2 Volumetric Energy Density
In 3D printing, volumetric energy density (VED) is a crucial 
manufacturing characteristic for fabricating layers. It exhib-
its a direct relationship with laser power and an inverse rela-
tionship with hatch distance, scanning velocity, and power 
layer thickness. These four critical process parameters are 
independently controllable.
(1) The quantity of energy transmitted to the particle layer 
by the laser energy source is denoted as laser power. 
The laser energy must not be excessively low or high; if 
it is too low, powder particles will not melt sufficiently, 
and if it is too high, succeeding deposited layers will 
evaporate due to laser penetration, resulting in keyhole 
pores forming.
(2) The scanning or traversal speed is the velocity at which 
the energy beam point travels. It is of considerable 
importance in facilitating the densification and micro-
structure formation of the molten powder layer. A high 
scanning speed will result in balling due to surface

4 Powder Bed Fusion
The powder bed fusion (PBF) is a fabrication procedure that 
utilizes thermal energy to sinter or melt metallic powder par-
ticles together. A wide range of PBF technique machines are 
accessible, which differ according to the build chamber type 
(which can be a controlled atmosphere or a vacuum), the 
heating source (which can be an electron beam or a laser), 
and the method of metal powder deposition and smoothing 
for each layer [24]. The PBF was divided into two categories 
according to the thermal energy source: (a) Selective Laser 
Melting (SLM or LPBF, a schematic of the same is shown 
in Fig. 6 [25]) and (b) Electron Beam Melting (EBM or EB-
PBF). The SLM was a product of the Fraunhofer Institute. 
The PBF uses powder as a feedstock material. Build geom-
etry data is supplied as a Computer-Aided Design model 
during the LPBF process, and it is subsequently divided 
into thin layers utilizing a designated program. Each divided 
layer undergoes additional reconstruction to generate a suita-
ble scan path. By scanning a laser beam selectively along the 
developed scan paths, the previously paved particles on the 
substrate are melted. The building stage is lowered height

4.1 Single Tracks of Pure Tungsten Using LPBF
For the first time, Vrancken et al. [27] investigated micro-
cracking in pure tungsten by printing single-scan tracks on 
a tungsten plate using the LPBF process and in-situ high-
speed video. Laser power of 300 and 450 Watt and scan-
ning speeds of 50, 100, and 300 mm/s were used. In this 
LPBF, two varieties of cracks are observed: Longitudinal 
fractures, whose direction is analogous to the scan direction, 
and branched or transverse cracks, which are at an angle 
to the direction of the laser. As shown in Fig.  7 [27], the 
solidification structure becomes obscured at high scan rates 
due to the melt pool's irregularities on the melt's surface. As 
a result of low temperature, fractures form along the GBs, 
which weakens the tungsten by opening the grain surfaces. 
The distance per crack was found to be proportional to the 
laser power and inversely proportional to the scanning speed, 
with a reduction in the number of cracks and an increase in 
the laser power due to an increase in the solidification time. 
A rise in the power parameter causes the molten tungsten 
to cool to its minimum temperature for a prolonged period, 
thereby providing an extended period for the initiation of 
surface cracks, as represented in Table 2 [27].
Ren et al. [28] analyse the collective effect of scan veloc-
ity and laser power on the formation of tungsten single tracks 
with a particle size between 5 and 25 µm on a preheated (200 
°C) stainless steel substrate. Single tracks were printed onto 
the substrate using the parameters P and ʋ, creating irregu-
lar, balling, and regular tracks. The morphology of all three 
scan tracks was succinctly summarized in the P-ʋ window 
of Fig.  8 [28]. It is evident from Fig.  8 that an increased 
scanning speed results in the balling phenomenon, while 
a reduced or increased laser power causes irregular tracks 
[28].

4.2 Pure Tungsten of 3D Parts Using LPBF
4.2.1 Effect of Volumetric Energy Density
The 3D printing of pure tungsten was characterized by Guo 
et al. [31] using VED as a process parameter. Tungsten pro-
duction requires VED = 667 J/mm3, 833 J/mm 3, and 1000 
J/mm3. A decrease in microcracks and liquid droplets was 
observed as the laser power and VED raised, as indicated 
by Fig. 11a–f [31]. Additionally, a uniform melt pool flow 
was observed in the microstructure at high P and VED. The 
lack of information regarding balling phenomena, common 
in the AM of tungsten metal, may be due to the spherical 
morphology of the powder particles utilized in this study. 
Spherical particles have a high absorptivity for lasers and 
a relatively high packing density compared to polyhedral 
powder [31]. A consistent molten track was witnessed in 
the spherical powders, these results match the findings of 
Zhou et al. [18] concerning the powder morphology. The 
increased VED and laser power provide a greater heat input 
to the powder particles, raising the melt pool's heat content. 
As a consequence, the dynamic viscosity of the molten pool 
decreases, and the molten pool flows more easily, result -
ing in a consistent flow front. Tungsten's DBTT fluctuates 
between 200 and 400 ºC [27]. It is postulated that the micro-
cracks produced during the LPBF manufacturing of metal 
components are thermal fractures resulting from thermal 
shrinkage due to the high DBTT [32]. A rise in the VED 
leads to a concomitant decrease in pore formation, resulting 
in a density of 19.0 g/cm 3 and a relative density of 98.4%. 
The investigation showed a maximum microhardness of 474 
HV at a VED of 1000 J/mm3 [31].
Enneti et al. [22] studied the impact of process variables 
on tungsten powder densification under the assumptions of a 
30 µm powder layer thickness and a laser power of 90 Watt. 
The maximum final density attained through this method is 
between 59 and 75%, and Zhang et al. [33] reported com-
parable outcomes. Table  3 [22] presents the final densities 
of the W samples investigated in this study. It is evident 
from the table that the final densities vary inversely with 
scan speed and hatch distance. Scan speed is the determining 
factor, and one of the observations from the morphology of 
the tungsten powders suggests that the powders are deviat-
ing from a spherical shape (as shown in Fig.  12a); pores 
(as shown in Fig.  12b) will become trapped between the 
tungsten powders during the LPBF recoating operation for 
the subsequent layer printing process, this results in a low 
density of printed W samples. Table 3 indicates that samples 
with high density result from low scan speed and low hatch 
distance. Figure 12c illustrates the balling effect in the SEM 
microstructure of samples with high scan speed [22].

4.2.2 Effect of Scanning Strategies
The impact of scanning paths on the melting of pure tung-
sten in LPBF of AM was studied by Gu et al. [34] The con-
struction of samples was carried out utilizing remelting, 
zigzag, and chessboard scanning strategies (illustrated in 
Fig. 2). Layer-by-layer scanning of metallic powders was 
conducted with a 67-degree rotation between each layer to 
diminish thermal gradients, layering overlap, and residual 
stress. The researchers deduced from Fig.  13a–f [34] that 
the chessboard scanning strategy yields significant balling 
phenomena and lengthy fractures that traversed the scan-
ning tracks. A smooth surface with diminished fissures was 
observed regarding the zigzag scanning strategy, while the 
balling events remained unaltered. By utilizing the remelt-
ing scanning approach, the scanned surface acquires distinct 
scanning traces and fewer fissures; consequently, the balling 
effect is substantially diminished. The compressive strength 
and microhardness of the three scanning strategies were 
evaluated; remelting produced the lowest microhardness 
compared to the zigzag scanning approach, which had 
the maximum HV. When compressive strength was deter -
mined, the zigzag scanning approach produced the highest 
value compared to the other two scanning strategies; the 
chessboard scanning strategy yielded the lowest value. The 
microhardness and compressive strength are illustrated in 
the Fig. 14a–c [34].

the pores. This ultimately leads to the formation of pores.

6.1 Printability of Tungsten Heavy Alloys
In the latest study, Xie et al. [78] studied the DED technique 
to create samples with a high concentration of pure tungsten. 
By carefully adjusting the process parameters, samples with 
tungsten concentration up to 99.78 wt%. were fabricated suc-
cessfully. Before Xie et al. several attempts were made to 
deposit pure tungsten, but researchers faced challenges such 
as incomplete melting, porosity, and cracks in the deposited 
parts. In this study, the authors construct single and mul-
tiple tracks on carbon steel using pure tungsten powders. 
The metal powders employed in this study have a polygonal 
shape and a size range from 20 to 150 μm. The scanned 
single tracks display a consistent and smooth appearance, 
with slight variations in width caused by adjustments in laser 
power and scanning speed. On the other hand, the multiple 
track samples exhibit a surface that is free from any pores 
and balling and only has minimal roughness. Some pure 
tungsten particles can be observed at higher magnification 
because the input laser energy density was low. For power, 
higher than 2000 Watt (ʋ = 400 mm/min) or scanning speed 
lower than 600 mm/min (P = 3000 Watt), the microstruc-
ture displayed satisfactory melting and fusion. Multiple sin-
gle tracks are deposited by adjusting the laser power while 
maintaining a constant scanning velocity and vice versa. The 
macrostructures of the scanned tracks are analysed, and the 
conclusions are tabulated in Table 4 [78].
Jeong et al. [79] investigated the printability of 3D struc-
tures using pure tungsten powder by applying the DED 
technique. They examined the build rate and surface topog-
raphy of 3D-printed structures. They sought to establish a 
connection with the powder feed rate (g/min) and carrier 
gas velocity (m/s) as process parameters. Suppose the build 
rate does not match the step increment in the deposition 
process. In that case, it can result in a situation where the 
incoming powder particles do not align with the laser spot 
at the focal point when building the subsequent layer on 
top of the previously deposited layer. Instead, it might meet 
at an offset of the end; because of this, there is insufficient 
melting of the powder and a decrease in the width of the 
fused layers. It yields a wall with a triangular-shaped cross-
section instead of the expected rectangular shape. The study 
of surface topography involved keeping the laser intensity 
and constant scanning velocity while adjusting the powder 
feed rate and carrier gas velocity. They observed a correla-
tion between the powder feed rate and carrier gas velocity, 
which substantially impacted the surface topography. The 
temperature of the melt pool drops as the powder feed rate 
increases. Similarly, when the carrier gas velocity increases, 
the powder particles flight period reduces. Due to the reduc-
tion in powder heat content before reaching the melt pool, 
the melt pool is hydrodynamically unstable, in addition to 
the high surface tension of tungsten. This instability pre-
vents the formation of a substantial temperature gradient, 
causing the molten column to break up and forming lumps 
in the scanned track during laser cladding. They could print 
samples with 98.4% of the predicted density by fine-tuning 
the laser power, scanning velocity, powder feed rate, and 
carrier gas velocity. The hardness achieved ranged from 3.8 
to 3.9 GPa [79].

Liquid Phase Sintering. The powder particles mentioned in

7 Binder Jetting
This technique spreads a flowable powder onto a build plat-
form, and a polymer adhesive is selectively deposited onto 
the powder bed in the shape of the desired part. The binder 
jetting (BJT) AM process can fabricate metal parts by selec-
tively printing a liquid binding agent into a powder bed. The 
build plate is lowered for each layer, and the subsequent 
layer of powder is equitably distributed. The process is then 
repeated. The desired element is produced by binding the 
successive layers together. The powder construction box is 
placed in an oven to cure the adhesive after printing is com-
plete. After curing, the green body can be extracted from the 
surplus loose powder for post-processing. The part is then 
dewaxed and can be sintered conventionally. After print -
ing, compressed air can eliminate the unbound loose powder 
in the bed that surrounds the part. This powder is used to 
support overhanging structures during the build. The binder 
jetting of AM process enables the creation and production of 
intricate geometries that are either impossible or exceedingly 
difficult to produce through machining or other manufactur-
ing methods. Furthermore, BJT is well-suited for optically 
reflective and thermally conductive metals which can be 
challenging to process using PBF processes, as BJT does 
not require an energy beam to process the material. A sche-
matic of the BJT process is shown in Fig. 33 [82]. The high 
melting temperature and limited solubility of tungsten in 
comparison to the matrix alloy elements of Nickel, Iron, and 
Copper have presented significant challenges to numerous 
research groups that have attempted to print WHAs. Most 
of these research groups have observed some vaporization 
of the lower melting temperature elements, cracking in the 
finished microstructure, difficulty attaining total density, and 
poor mechanical properties [83].
Binder jetting provides a viable alternative to the PBF and 
DED routes that can circumvent these substantial barriers. 
It does not necessitate a high-temperature melt pool gener -
ated by the laser process's intensive energy for densification. 
Consequently, the vaporization of the matrix alloy should 
be mitigated through a liquid-phase sintering and binder jet 
printing method. Despite its promise, the industrial applica-
tion of BJ3DP to the production of WHA parts is currently 
impeded by the low strength of printed parts in the as-cured 
state and the poor mechanical properties of sintered compo-
nents when sintering using conventional sintering techniques 
[84, 85].

ticle rearrangement and particle interlocking, it is difficult to 
create particle-to-particle contact. Hence, particle physical

8 Conclusions
Additive manufacturing is one of the leading techniques 
for developing tungsten and its alloys. Most of the research 
has been carried out in the past twenty years using powder 
bed fusion technology compared to directed energy depo-
sition manufacturing techniques. In the powder bed fusion 
technique, cracking of scanned tracks is unavoidable. The 
most influencing process variables are energy density, 
scanning speed, scanning strategy, and powder particle 
size. Even though process parameters had been precisely 
chosen, most of the work found cracks and balling phe-
nomena in scanned microstructure tracks due to variations 
in powder particle sizes. In comparison, the spherical pow -
ders dominate the irregular powder morphology in terms 
of relative density. Changing the scanning path to remelt-
ing scanning alone made the scanned surface exceptionally 
smooth with fewer cracks. Tungsten has a very high duc-
tile to brittle transition temperature, which makes cracking 
in tungsten relatively more straightforward. Adding alloy -
ing elements to the pure tungsten reduces the susceptibil-
ity of cracking. Ren et al. produced crack-free specimens 
using an electron beam powder bed fusion technique at a 
very high substrate preheating temperature. Incomplete 
fusion was found in one electron beam powder bed fusion 
manufactured sample; later, it was overcome by increasing 
the energy density of the source. Very few research works 
are available for the direct energy deposition of tungsten 
alloys; gas velocity and powder feed rate are the main fac-
tors affecting the surface topography in this carrier. A thin 
wall of 90W-7Ni-3Fe alloy with encouraging outcomes 
and no visible cracks in the microstructure was success-
fully printed using direct energy deposition. The tensile 
properties and hardness (844 MPa, 5.9 GPa for matrix, and 
7.89 for tungsten) achieved are more than the liquid phase 
sintering process, but with poorer plasticity. Binder jetting 
of tungsten heavy alloys produced a pore-free sample with 
detailed designs printed without supports. However, the 
strength (770 MPa) is lower than that of the powder bed 
fusion and directed energy deposition.
In additive manufacturing of tungsten heavy alloys, 
processing parameters like powder characteristics (mor -
phology and distribution), scanning strategy, and volu-
metric energy density (laser power, scan speed, hatch 
distance, and layer thickness), play an important role in 
deciding the soundness of the printed samples. Even by 
changing the scanning strategy from remelting to zigzag, 
there is a profound increase in compressive strength. But 
in the remelting strategy more appearance of defects are 
recorded. In the electron beam powder bed fusion pro -
cess, upon proper exercising of the temperature gradient 
in build direction for a smaller build area one can achieve 
equiaxed microstructure, as a consequence of which there 
is an enhancement in the mechanical properties of the built 
wall. The same can be followed for the directed energy 
deposition technique to get a defect-free 3D printed wall. 
The ductility of the printed wall in case of tungsten heavy 
alloys majorly depends on the solubility of tungsten in 
the matrix phase. If the tungsten solubility in the matrix 
phase is high, more will be the ductility of the sample. 
The tungsten heavy alloys components manufactured via 
powder bed fusion and directed energy deposition pro-
cesses may contain a lot of defects like pores, entrapped 
gases, cracks in the matrix phase, and non-smooth tung-
sten particles. Post-heat treatment of the additive manu-
factured tungsten heavy alloys would result in elimination 
of pores and will also help in entrapped gas removal and 
smoothening of tungsten particles. Thereby post process 
heat treatment becomes very essential in case of tungsten 
heavy alloys to enhance the quality of the 3D printed wall 
and its microstructure.
