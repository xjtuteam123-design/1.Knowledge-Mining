Abstract
The presence of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases is known to deteriorate the mechanical properties of Ni-based 
single crystal (SX) superalloy, which not only depletes the strengthening refractory elements from the γ' phase, but also 
contributes to the material failure due to the weak TCP/matrix interface. This study explores the interface between σ and 
γ' in a creep-fractured Ni-based single crystal superalloy, with a specific focus on the atomic interfacial structures and Co 
segregation. The σ phase was observed to be orientated with γ' as both [110]σ//[110]γ' and [410]σ//[011]γ', but always pos-
sess a habit plane of (001) σ//(111)γ'. Furthermore, significant Co segregation occurred between the σ and γ', leading to the 
reinforced interfacial strength. Theoretical calculation indicates that the enhanced interfacial strength can be attributed to the 
accumulated charge density across the σ/γ interface with the Co segregation. These findings provide insight into the TCP/
matrix interface and the role of interfacial solute segregation, which would benefit the design of Ni-based SX superalloys.
Keywords TCP/matrix interface · Co segregation · Ni-based single crystal superalloys · DFT calculations
1 Introduction
Ni-based single crystal (SX) superalloys have excellent high-
temperature mechanical properties and thus are widely used 
as turbine blades [1 , 2]. Ni-based SX superalloys typically 
possess a structure of solid solution γ phase (Ni) reinforced 
by L12-ordered γ' precipitates (Ni3Al) [3, 4]. Upon thermo-
mechanical exposure, the microstructure of alloy tends to 
form severe rafting, deteriorating the mechanical properties 
greatly [5, 6]. To enhance the high-temperature creep resist-
ance, various refractory elements, including W, Re, Cr, Ru 
and Mo, etc., have been widely added into the Ni-based SX 
superalloys as effective solid solution strengtheners [7–11]. 
Some solute atoms (e.g., W [12], Re [13, 14]) can also segre-
gate to the γ/γ' interfaces, especially at the sites of misfit dis-
locations, and contribute to the strengthening [13]. However, 
excess refractory elements would induce the precipitation 
of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases after prolonged 
exposure to high temperature [12], which is harmful to the 
fracture resistance of superalloys due to the consumption 
of strengthening elements in alloys and the introduction of 
weak TCP-matrix interfaces.
The commonly observed TCP phases in Ni-based SX 
superalloys include the tetragonal σ phase, rhombohedral 
μ phase and orthorhombic P phase. The structure [13– 17], 
composition [18, 19], internal defect [20, 21] and orientation 
relationship with the matrix [19, 22, 23] of different TCP 
phases have attracted considerable attentions. Typically, σ 
phases (with Re, Cr, Fe, Co, W and Ni as the main con-
stituents) have been shown to be the most frequently formed

2 Experimental and Simulation Methods
2.1 Materials
A Ni-based SX superalloy with a chemical composition 
of Co 5.5–6.9, Cr 2.5–4, Mo 2.1–3.5, W 5–6.5, Al 5–6.5, 
Ta 5.5–6.5, Re 4–5.5, Ru 2–3 (all in wt%) and the balance 
Ni was used in this study. Before creep tests, the materials 
underwent a three-step heat treatment process: 1340 °C/10 h/
AC → 1100 °C/4 h/AC → 870 °C/16 h/AC (AC: air cooling). 
The creep rupture test was carried out at 1100 °C under 
137 MPa tensile stress. The creep life is 487 h. After the 
creep fracture, the alloy was cooled in the air. The elongation 
ε of parallel section and area shrinkage Ψ at fracture surface 
were measured.
2.2 Microstructure Characterization
For SEM imaging, the samples were mechanically polished 
firstly and then etched chemically with a solution of 30 ml 
 C3H8O3, 20 ml HNO3 and 10 ml HF. A field emission SEM 
(Hitachi SU-70) was used for surface observation. For 
STEM characterization, the alloy was firstly ground to less 
than 30 μm thick, then dimpled into a disc with a diameter 
of 3 mm, and finally electrolytically polished at 253 K with 
10 vol.% HClO4–90 vol.% C2H5OH solution. A probe Cs-
corrected scanning-transmission-electron microscope (FEI 
Titan 80–200) operated at 200 kV was employed. High-
angle-annular-dark-field (HAADF) images were captured 
with a probe convergence semi-angle of 21.4 mrad and a 
collection semi-angle from 53 to 200 mrad.
2.3 DFT Calculations
All calculations were performed using the Vienna ab-
initio simulation package (VASP) [31, 32] with the DFT 
method. The electron–ion interaction was described using 
the Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) method. The 
exchange–correlation contributions were accounted for 
using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within 
the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). The plane 
wave cut-off energy was set to 400 eV for the basis set, 
and the Monkhorst–Pack method was employed to gener -
ate the k-point sampling grids. Specifically, for the bulk σ 
phase, a 6 × 6 × 12 k-point grid was used, while for bulk Ni, 
a 12 × 12 × 12 k-point grid was utilized. For the (001) σ sur-
face, a 6 × 6 × 3 k-point grid was employed, while for the 
(001)σ//(111)γ interface, a 2 × 2 × 1 k-point grid was utilized. 
A vacuum layer with a thickness of 10 Å was added to all 
surface and interface slabs. In the (001)σ surface models, the 
lattice constants on the a-b plane were set as a = b = 9.71 Å 
and γ = 90°. In the (001)σ//(111)γ interface models, the lattice 
constants on the a-b plane were a = 13.09 Å, b = 13.33 Å, 
and γ = 90°. The bulk phase elements were arranged in a 
ferromagnetic configuration, and the spin effect was consid-
ered in all calculations with ISPIN set as 2. The geometrical 
optimization was achieved when the total energy converged 
within 1.0 × 10−5 eV/atom and the maximum stress con-
verged within 0.01 eV/Å. The differential charge density 
images were generated using the VESTA software.

3.2 Atomic Structures of σ/γ' Interface
The presence of TCP phases is known to deteriorate 
mechanical properties of Ni-based SX superalloy, which 
not only depletes the strengthening refractory elements 
from the γ' phase due to the formation of TCP phases, but 
also contributes to the crack initiation due to the weak TCP/
matrix interface [37, 38]. With the chemical variations of 
TCP, the atomic structure of TCP/matrix interfaces can 
be very different, which may influence their mechanical 
responses. Careful characterizations show that for both 
σ1 and σ2 phases observed above, there exist two types of 
interface terminations where the (001)σ plane connects with 
the (111)γ' plane, as shown in the HAADF images, viewed 
along [110]σ//[110]γ' for (Fig. 5a, b) and [4 10]σ//[011]γ' for 
(Fig. 5c, d), respectively. The directions of [110]σ and [410]σ 
are parallel to [110]γ' and [011]γ', respectively. This results 
in the appearance of both [110]σ and [4 10]σ views when 
observed along < 110 > γ'. For the first interface configura-
tion (Fig. 5a, c), the σ phase terminates at the layer 1 or 4 of 
σ unit cell and is classified as T1. For the second interface 
configuration (Fig.  5b, d), the σ phase (for both σ 1 and σ2) 
terminates at the layer 2 or 3 of σ unit cell. The magnified 
image in Fig.  5e reveals the presence of complete coher -
ency between the σ 1(σ2) and γ' phases at the interface. The 
insets in Fig.  5e outlined by white dashed frames show the 
simulated HAADF-STEM image of the two types of inter -
face, well illustrating the experimental observation. The 
parameters used in the HAADF-STEM simulations were as

aging at 1070 °C [44]. Furthermore, experimental results

4.1 Work of Separation
Work of separation (W sep) for the σ/γ interface represents 
the energy necessary for separating the σ/γ interface into 
two free surfaces. Meanwhile, a higher W sep serves as an 
indication of a more stabilized interface. Figure 8 shows the 
work of separation as a function of Co coverage on the σ/γ 
interface. For the clean σ/γ interface without Co segrega-
tion (Co coverage = 0), Wsep is calculated to be 3.47 J/m 2 
for 001–1-top interface and 3.89 J/m 2 for 001–3-fcc inter -
face, respectively. These results suggest that among the two 
interfacial termination configurations, 001–1-top is weaker 
than 001–3-fcc. Moreover, the two types of interfaces are 
found to be more susceptible to fracture when compared to 
the γ/γ' interface (the W sep of γ/γ' interface is calculated to 
be 8.68 J/m2). This might contribute to the deleterious effect 
of TCP phases on the mechanical properties of Ni-based SX 
superalloys. When the segregation of Co atoms occurs on 
interface, a strengthening effect on the σ/γ interface can be 
observed for both termination types and the corresponding 
Wsep increases monotonically with the increase of Co cover-
age (Fig. 8).

5 Conclusions
The microstructure and composition of σ phase and its 
interface with the matrix phase in a crept Ni-based single 
crystal superalloy were investigated systematically by both 
experimental and theoretical studies. This work is expected 
to shed light on the design of Ni-based superalloys.
1. In the crept Ni-based superalloy, the σ phase exhibits 
two distinct orientation relationships with the γ' matrix: 
 [110]σ//[110]γ' and [410]σ//[011]γ', while maintaining a 
consistent habit plane of (001) σ//(111)γ'. Atomic-scale 
characterization reveals two interfacial termination 
types: T1 and T2.
2. DFT calculations demonstrate significant differences 
in interfacial stability between these terminations. The 
001-3-fcc interface exhibits a higher work of separation 
and lower interface energy, indicating stronger atomic 
bonding and enhanced stability compared to the 001-1-
top interface. This structural heterogeneity directly influ-
ences the mechanical integrity of the σ/matrix interface, 
with the T2 termination being more resistant to decohe-
sion.
3. Experimental observations reveal pronounced Co segre-
gation at the σ/matrix interface. DFT calculations show 
that Co can also serve as a cohesion enhancer of the σ/γ 
interface, which originates from the accumulated charge 
density and the shortened interatomic bindings across 
the σ/γ interface. These atomic-scale interactions collec-
tively enhance interfacial cohesion, thereby significantly 
improving resistance to crack initiation and propagation 
under thermomechanical stress.
