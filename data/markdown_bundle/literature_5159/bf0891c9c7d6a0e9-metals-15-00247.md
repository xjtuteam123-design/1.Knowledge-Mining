Abstract: The excellent mechanical properties of high-entropy alloys, especially under harsh
service environments, have attracted increasing attention in the last decade. FCC-based
and refractory high-entropy alloys (HEAs) are the most extensively used series. However,
the strength of FCC-base HEAs is insufficient, although they possess a great ductility and
fracture toughness at both room and low temperatures. With regard to the BCC-based
refractory HEAs, the unsatisfactory ductility at room temperature shadows their ultrahigh
strength at room and high temperatures, as well as their excellent thermal stability. In order
to strike a balance between strength and toughness, strengthening mechanisms should be
first clarified. Therefore, typical mechanical performance and corresponding strengthening
factors are systemically summarized, including the solid solution strengthening, second
phase, interface, and synergistic effects for FCC-base HEAs, along with the optimization
of principal elements, construction of multi-phase, the doping of non-metallic interstitial
elements, and the introduction of kink bands for refractory HEAs. Among which the design
of meta-stable structures, such as chemical short-range order, and kink bands has been
shown to be a promising strategy to further improve the mechanical properties of HEAs.
Keywords: high-entropy alloy; mechanical property; strengthening mechanism; microstructure
1. Introduction
The outstanding mechanical properties and service performance of high-entropy
alloys (HEAs) in a much wider temperature range than traditional alloys have attracted
increasing attention, especially under harsh environments [1]. Since the concept of HEAs,
which refers to a stable structure of a simple solid solution with a higher configurational
entropy raised from multiple elements (usually five) in an equimolar or near equimolar
ratio, was first put forward in 2004 [2–4]. It has promoted a shift in research focus from the
corner of the alloy phase diagram to the center region, thus breaking conventional alloying
concepts and cracking a brand-new avenue in future metallurgical fields [5]. It is worthy
of note that both the definition and research scope of HEAs have evolved dynamically. In
early investigations, HEAs were stringently defined as single-phase solid solutions with
a high configurational entropy, and were composed of more than five principal elements

in approximately equiatomic ratios (configurational entropy ∆Sconfig ≥ 1.61R, where R
represents the gas constant) [4]. The high configurational entropy renders the high-entropy
system more prone to form a single phase structure. Moreover, during deformation, it
exhibits a relatively high dislocation nucleation energy barrier. The high-entropy system
demonstrates more prominent lattice friction and solid-solution strengthening effects.
Consequently, initial research on HEAs predominantly aimed at maximizing entropy.
Subsequent studies have indicated that medium entropy alloys ( ∆Sconfig ≈ 1.10R) with
three or four components, such as CrCoNi, also exhibit excellent properties akin to those
of traditional HEAs [ 6]. Medium-entropy alloys rely more on the precipitation phases
that emerge under slightly lower configurational entropy, as well as the regulation of
nanotwins and stacking fault energy (SFE), particularly short-range ordered structures, to
achieve the strengthening effect. Medium-entropy alloys can attain mechanical properties
equivalent to those of HEAs without reducing the entropy value [ 7]. Therefore, alloys
composed of less than five components or with non-equiatomic compositions have also
been incorporated into the research ambit of HEAs recently. The research emphasis has
gradually shifted from merely pursuing high configurational entropy to the synergistic
effects of multiple strengthening mechanisms during the deformation process, providing
more novel directions for the performance design of HEAs [8].
The high entropy in thermodynamics, sluggish diffusion in kinetics, and severe lattice
distortion, along with cocktail effects, due to the distinct elementary compositions and
microstructures, are widely believed to contribute to the excellent properties of HEAs, and
in turn create a capacious space for new HEA designs [9]. The essence of the cocktail effect
lies in the nonlinear synergy between elements in a multi-principal component system,
and its performance improvement far exceeds the linear superposition of the contribution
of a single component [ 7]. This phenomenon is due to the modulation of multi-scale
electronic structures, the synergistic amplification effect of lattice distortion, the synergistic
strengthening of chemical short-term ordered structures, and the multi-body coupling of
dynamic effects such as the roughened diffusion landscape caused by multiple components.
This is different from the linear superposition of traditional alloys [10].
At low temperatures, brittleness is insurmountable in most structural materials, while
face-centered cubic (FCC)-based HEAs such as CoCrFeMnNi and CrCoNi possess both
high ductility and fracture toughness [11,12]. FCC-based HEAs are mainly composed of
basic elements from 3d family elements like Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, and Mn, and present higher
plasticity and toughness but lower strength. In fact, the close atom radii and lower mixing
enthalpy of 3d family elements induce a lower SFE and higher dislocation mobility [13–15].
When the FCC-based HEAs deform at room temperature as well as at low temperature,
dislocation slipping, stacking fault generation, twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP), and
transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP), among other strengthening mechanisms, could be
triggered. For these alloys, the only demerit might be their insufficient strength. However,
solution strengthening, grain-size strengthening, strain hardening, and so forth could be
additionally adopted to further improve their mechanical properties [16,17]. For instance, a
series of synthesized deformation modes make HEAs prone to experience a TWIP and/or
TRIP , and thus notably increase their strength without sacrificing ductility [ 18]. As for
hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structures, they are mainly precipitated as by-products of
FCC-based HEAs during heat treatment or TRIP reaction, and could accordingly enhance
the strength of master alloys by virtue of their intrinsic hard and brittle nature. Nevertheless,
the content of HCP phases should be reasonably controlled to avoid the instability fracture
in the early stage of deformation [19].
In comparison with FCC-based HEAs, refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), which
mostly consist of refractory elements from the IV , V , and VI families including W, Mo,

Nb, Ta, Ti, Zr, Hf, V , present superior strength at both room and high temperatures,
enabling them to have enormous potential in applications such as turbine blades, nuclear
material, and kinetic energy penetrators, among others [20,21]. Since greater differences
in atomic size and electronegativity exist among refractory elements from the IV , V , and
VI families, RHEAs are chiefly composed of a body-centered cubic (BCC) single phase,
and sometimes include disordered HCP and ordered intermetallic compounds such as
B2 and Laves phases [22]. At room temperature (RT), RHEAs are characterized by their
higher strength, higher hardness, and lower ductility [23]. At high temperatures, the phase
stability, strength, and resistance to high-temperature softening and oxidation of RHEAs
are all superior to those of traditional nickel-based superalloy and refractory alloys, which
is ascribed to the kink band coupling with the solid solution strengthening configuration to
inhibit the slip of planar dislocations at both short and long distances [24–26]. Therefore,
the insufficient ductility at RT and the softening preference at high temperatures become
the bottlenecks limiting the increasing applications of RHEAs [27]. The improvement in
ductility and toughness is most likely at the cost of strength, especially in high temperature
conditions [25,28]. At present, the tailoring of basic elements and their contents, the doping
of interstitial elements, the engineering of the second phase as well as strain structures
on a micro-scale have been conducted in order to strike a balance between strength and
ductility. As to the deformed structures, the effects of tangled dislocations and kink bands
on mechanical properties are of significance [29,30].
In order to further improve the service performance of FCC-based HEAs and RHEAs,
the elements and microstructures must be customized deliberately and reasonably. There-
fore, it is necessary to review the latest results on the strengthening mechanisms underlying
the mechanical properties to help better understand and design HEAs in future. First,
the advantages and disadvantages of mechanical properties during deformation for both
FCC-based HEAs and RHEAs were discussed, and the reasons for their unique perfor-
mances were also explored, including the influences of solid solution type, interstitial
elements, and solution temperature, size, distribution of second phases on the ductility, and
toughness. Then, the roles various strengthening mechanisms play in the final mechanical
properties were clarified. Thereafter, the design principles and optimizing directions for
manufacturing HEAs combining strength and toughness were accordingly proposed.
2. FCC-Based HEAs
2.1. Mechanical Properties of FCC-Based HEAs
FCC-based HEAs have attracted increasing attention due to their notable ductility
and fracture toughness at low temperatures [ 31–35]. Typical FCC-based HEAs include
CoCrFeMnNi [36], CrFeCoNi [ 37], CrCoNi [ 38], and VCoNi [ 39], which present great
plasticity under room and low temperatures but with inadequate strength. Recently, the
FCC-based HEAs that have received the most attention are CoCrFeMnNi and CrCoNi.
Gludovatz et al. conducted systematic research about the mechanical performances of
both CoCrFeMnNi and CrCoNi HEAs under various temperatures and found that ductility
and strength improved as temperatures dropped, especially the fracture toughness of
CrCoNi [14,36,38,40]. The yield strengths (σy), fracture strains (εf), and fracture toughness
of the two kinds of HEAs at RT were approximately 500 MPa, 0.55 and 210 MPa.m 1/2,
respectively. The σy increased by 50%, the εf increased from 0.7 to 0.9, and the fracture
toughness increased to 270 MPa.m1/2 for CrCoNi as temperature decreased from 293 K
to 77 K. In the same case, the σy and εf also enhanced by 85% and 27%, respectively, for
CoCrFeMnNi, except for stable fracture toughness.
In addition to CrCoNi and CoCrFeMnNi HEAs, many other kinds of FCC-based HEAs
present an outstanding balance between strength and toughness at low temperatures, which is

mainly attributed to multiple enhancements during the deformation process [41,42]. It is noted
that both CrCoNi and CoCrFeMnNi show a ductile fracture at RT and low temperatures,
resulting from microvoid coalescence, with a noticeable stretch zone formed at the crack
initiation stage. At RT, their deformations are dominated by dislocation slip, especially the
<110> slips along the crystallographic plane of {111} [43]. The strain hardening effect caused
by the denser dislocation network and cell structure, among others, could improve theσy and
impede the necking process, and, thus, the toughness [44,45]. In addition, nanoscaled defor-
mation twins in CrCoNi at RT could be observed but not for CoCrFeMnNi, which is ascribed
to the lower SFE but nearly identical critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) of CrCoNi in com-
parison with CoCrFeMnNi, making it easier to reach the CRSS of twinning in CrCoNi [15,40].
At low temperatures, the SFE decreases with temperature, but the CRSS of twinning is almost
insensitive to a continuous cooling environment. Since the SFE of CrCoNi is much lower than
that of CoCrFeMnNi at cryogenic temperatures (such as 77 K), nanoscaled twins are more
prone to form in CrCoNi HEA [42,46,47]. Another example is V10Cr10Fe45Co20Ni15: as the
temperature drops from 298 K to 77 K, the ductility , strength, and fracture toughness of the al-
loy increase simultaneously (Figure 1a,b). This performance improvement mainly comes from
the deformation twins that appear when the material is deformed at 77 K (Figure 1c,d) [33].
At low temperatures, the movement of thermally activated dislocations is suppressed due to
the reduced mobility of atoms. The SFE is essentially temperature-dependent. The vibration
entropy decreases at low temperatures, and the SFE also decreases accordingly . Affected by
the reduction in SFE, the deformation mechanism changes from the traditional dislocation slip
to TWIP/TRIP [48]. Therefore, although dislocation slip resistance generally increases at low
temperatures, the change in deformation mode compensates for the reduction in dislocation
mobility , and both strength and plasticity are improved [33,49].
This phenomenon also indicates that FCC-based HEAs with a lower SFE are more
likely to trigger the multiple strengthening mechanism combining dislocation slip, stacking
fault, and TWIP with TRIP at low temperatures, which could effectively retard the localized
necking and improve the mechanical performance [50]. In contrast to RT, TWIP becomes
the leading mechanism with only few HCP structures involved in the deformation process
at 77 K [40]. The multiple strengthening mechanisms underlying the plastic deformation
process are undoubtedly critical to the striking damage tolerance of FCC-based HEAs. For
instance, the reported CrCoNi shows a much lower SFE at 20 K, and its fracture toughness
boosts to 459 MPa.m 1/2, which is also attributed to the strengthening effect of ultrafine
twins and deformation-induced HCP structures [50]. In fact, the dynamic Hall–Petch effect
resulting from twins with a finer size plays a significant role in reducing the mean free path
of dislocation and promoting the strain hardening rate. Admittedly, the excellent fracture
toughness of CrCoNi is closely related to a special series of stable strain modes at 20 K [42].
As mentioned above, FCC-based HEAs often possess a remarkable ductility and
fracture toughness, but the unsatisfactory strength will definitely attract greater atten-
tion to the engineering of compositions, phases, and microstructures without sacrificing
their plasticity.

2.2.1. Solid Solution Strengthening
HEAs are composed of multi-principal elements, which could be mutually dissolved
easily in general, meaning that no solute and solvent could be distinguished clearly. There-
fore, the traditional solid solution strengthening mechanism, which considers the atomic
size and elasticity modulus mismatches of only one solute between the solvent, is not
suitable for HEAs [51,52]. Instead, the enhancement from each individual kind of solute
should be taken into account [53]. Actually, the effect of solid solution strengthening is not
as significant as expected because the similar atomic sizes and electronegativity of basic
elements from the 3d family. The dislocations migrate at a high speed under a lower Peierls
stress and the whole deformation process is dominated by {111} (110) dislocations [54]. In
view of this, Varvenne proposed a model by raising the volume mismatch and shear modu-
lus to enhance FCC-based HEAs [55]. More recently, researchers tried to introduce atoms
with a higher mismatched volume and shear modulus into the HEA matrix to improve the
effect that the solid solution strengthening mechanism exerts [56].
As for CoCrFeMnNi, doping elements that have a higher volume mismatch, such as
Pd and Ru, or higher shear modulus, like V , could noticeably improve its strength [57–59].
Kumar et al. added various contents (0.25–5.0 at.%) of Al into CoCrFeMnNi and found that
the strength could increase by up to 50% but with a sharply reduced ductility. The enhanced
strength was mainly ascribed to the high volume mismatch and lattice distortion resulting
from the difference in size of Al and other principal elements (more than 12% in average),

Likewise, the elements of C, Cu, Cr, V , Ni, and Ti are also frequently adopted to
strengthen FCC-based HEAs [62]. It is worth noting that the enhanced strength is attributed
to both the solid solution strengthening mechanism and the hard precipitation phase due
to the limited solubility of these alloying elements. Furthermore, the effects of phase
boundary and twinning on the deformation should not be overlooked [63]. Meena et al. ex-
amined the fracture toughness of Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 and Fe49.5Mn30Co10Cr10C0.5 [64]. The
results indicated that the fracture toughness increased from 205 MPa.m1/2 to 301 MPa.m1/2
after doping with C, which is assumed to be a contribution of interstitial solid solution
strengthening, the second phase, and TRIP , as well as grain boundary strengthening.
Therefore, multi-phase HEAs have greater potential to be tailored than single-phase ones,
although the brittle phase should be paid more attention to avoid causing premature
fractures [65,66]. For instance, Du et al. [ 67,68] proved that the ductility and strength of
(Fe20Co30Ni10Cr20Mn20)100−x-xCu could be simultaneously improved after incorporating
trace amounts of Cu. When the proportion of Cu was 2 at.%, the εf was 0.82. Once the
content of Cu increased to 8 at.%, the strengthening effects of dislocation and twinning were
restricted by the Cu-rich phase. The (Cu, Ni)-rich phases aggregated in grain boundaries
would induce the early fracture due to their low hardness. In this case, the εf dropped
to 0.3 and thus weakened the strengthening effect. In addition, the precipitation of the
second phase consumed the solutes in the FCC-based matrix, which might also attenuate
the benefits from solid solution strengthening and Hall–Petch [69].

For instance, the addition of Al in moderation could improve the strength of AlxCoCrFeNi.

composite susceptible to brittle fracture failures.
In addition to introducing second phases before a deformation, taking advantage of
the TRIP effect during the deformation process is favorable for strain hardening [ 6,85].
TRIP could promote meta-phase transforms into a stable state and thus optimize the strain
hardening ability [86]. In the cases of FCC-based HEAs, the FCC phases are likely to change

2.2.4. Synergistic Strengthening of Multiple Mechanisms
As seen in Figure 6, not only the grain refinement, but also the solid solution strength-
ening induced by Mo atoms could increase strength, indicating a synergistic strengthening
mechanism. In order to explore the positive synergistic effects, the high-pressure torsion,
heavy drawing, high-speed stamping, rolling, and forging, among others, have been devel-
oped to enhance FCC-based HEAs by introducing shear stresses into microstructures [101].
These strategies have proven to be successful in making strength and toughness compat-
ible. For instance, Ma et al. [ 71] modified the size and distribution of precipitations in
the surface layer of CoCrNi through deep rolling. The pre-embedded nano-twins could
interrupt the continuous growth of precipitations and thus refine grain size, due to which
the ductility loss was largely reduced. Moreover, the metastable microstructures controlled
by TRIP/TWIP have been extensively adopted to strengthen FCC-based HEAs. These
customized metastable FCC-based HEAs tend to generate twins and HCP structures when
suffering under external loads due to the relatively lower SFE. The fine grain boundaries
and twin boundaries could maintain continuous strain hardening and retard the fracture
failure during the deformation process by reducing the mean free path of dislocations,
contributing to an effectively dynamic Hall–Petch and second phase strengthening. Bet-
ter yet, there may be no negative impact working on the ductility under this combined
strengthening approach [87]. Wang et al. [62] constructed a metastable HEA by adjusting
the unstable stacking fault. The resultant integration of the heavy lattice distortion, solid
solution strengthening, TRIP/TWIP , and a second phase achieved excellent strength and
toughness [31,73]. Accordingly, the reasonable design of metastable microstructures in
FCC-based HEAs is a promising way to simultaneously enhance strength and ductility [56].

3.1. Mechanical Properties of RHEAs
As shown in Figure 7, BCC RHEAs usually exhibit high strength but low ductility at

strengthening effect, suggesting that most of them have a lower fracture toughness. Except
for a few RHEAs with principal elements from the IV family that possess an ultrahigh
fracture toughness, other types of RHEAs such as WMoNbTa(V) and WMoNbTiCr could
hardly sustain elongation of 10% [26,119,120].

3.2.2. Construction of Multi-Phase
Refractory HEAs are mostly used in high temperature and pressure environments,
meaning that deformation behavior and mechanical properties at high temperatures should
be paid more attention. Similar to FCC-based HEAs, the second phase could contribute
extra strengthening. First, the homogeneously distributed fine phase could effectively
bend and cut the dislocations [151]. As the temperature gradually decreases from 1400 ◦C,
Al0.7CoCrFeNi precipitates BCC and FCC at different temperatures, and σ phase and
B2 phase L12 greatly affects the strength of the alloy at different temperatures [ 152,153].
Thereafter, the reasonable manipulation of the size, fraction, and dispersion of second
phases is conducive to the ductility of RHEAs. Second, the covalent bonds of long-range
or short-range ordered second phases influence the actually shear modulus [ 61]. The
performance of σy at high temperatures is dramatically impacted by these two factors [154].
As shown in Figure 12a,b, multi-phase refractory HEAs present a higher and more stable
yield resistance than single-phase ones at low and moderate temperatures [ 18]. When
raising the temperature, the dissolved second phase weakens the strengthening effect and
thus drops the σy, especially in second phases with a lower solvus temperature. Since the
multi-phase often reduces the liquidus line, strength might decrease earlier and faster as
temperatures rise. When the temperature is very high, the σy keeps steady again, which
may be related to the inherent properties of the principal elements [155].
However, the yield strength might also increase at a certain temperature range due to
new, harder precipitates [156–158]. Both the precipitation and dissolution of second phases
could influence the dislocation movements during the deformation at various temperatures.
Therefore, the temperature ranges of precipitation and dissolution of second phases should
also be controlled according to the specific serving environment, other than their size and
distribution in the construction of multi-phase structures in refractory HEAs.

3.2.3. Doping of Non-Metallic Interstitial Elements
As aforementioned, there is a trade-off between high-temperature strength and RT
ductility for refractory HEAs [115,141]. In addition to external factors such as temperature
and loading rate, the strength–ductility balance mainly depends on intrinsic characteristics
like interstitial elements and second phases, among others [ 159]. Senkov et al. [ 160]
believed that the improvement in RT ductility might be at the cost of the σy at RT and high
temperatures. For instance, the addition of elements from the VI family could weaken the
solid solution strengthening effect in refractory HEAs. The ductility of HEAs composed
of ordered B2 matrices and A2 cubic precipitation could also be improved by inducing
microstructure transfers into a basket-weave structure consisting of lathe B2 precipitation
and an A2 matrix via heat treatment. Likewise, strength also drops [ 148,161,162]. At
present, the investigation into solid solution strengthening and second phase strengthening
is primarily based on empirical models, but the effect of non-metallic interstitial elements
on dislocation movement and mechanical properties is still not better understood [163]. In
practice, non-metallic interstitial elements such as O, H, B, N, and C are inevitable during
the metallurgical process. Most recently, the influence of doping these elements in trace
amounts on mechanical properties has been explored [121,164,165]. In terms of single-phase
HEAs, there are two main factors contributing to the solid solution strengthening hardening.
First, the random distribution of principal elements induces a local composition fluctuation
and lattice distortion, generating an energy barrier to hinder dislocation movements [148].
Second, interstitial impurities such as N and O could also bring about lattice distortion and
chemical fluctuation [166]. Lee et al. [155] improved the σy of TiVNbTa by 300 MPa through
doping 1.6 at.% interstitial elements (O and N). The non-metallic elements were found
to exist as interstitial atoms since no nano-scale precipitation was observed. The effects
these interstitial elements play are the same as solid solution strengthening from principal
elements. The actual strengthening efficiency lies in parameters such as mismatches in
atomic volume and shear modulus.
However, the solubility of interstitial elements in the matrix and the interaction be-
tween them should further be considered since not all interstitial atoms exist as a solid
solution strengthening without other kinds of precipitation. For example, elements from
the VI family have an ultrahigh melting point and high temperature strength, making it
difficult for interstitial atoms (N, O) to dissolve into the lattice intervals due to a relatively
smaller gap between atoms and a much denser electronic configuration. The redundant
non-metallic elements will precipitate at grain boundaries or form oxides and nitrides with
a low thermal stability, resulting in brittleness [167]. In comparison, principal elements
from the V family are likely to bond with interstitial elements (N, O) to form stable com-

brittleness [168]. The principal elements from the IV family have larger atom intervals and
could dissolve many more interstitial atoms. The stable bonds between principal elements

time, which was undesired in practice. Although the effect of trace interstitial elements on
the mechanical properties and thermal stability of refractory HEAs is of significance, it is
critical to exploit them reasonably in developing refractory HEAs with a higher strength
and ductility [63].
3.2.4. Introducing of Kink Bands
The plastic deformation of BCC-based refractory HEAs at RT is dominated by the slow
slip of 1/2<111> screw dislocations. Apart from the contribution of dislocation pinning
resulting from solute atoms and local chemical fluctuations to a high strength at RT, the
phase constitution, grain size, casting microstructure, deformation microstructure, and
dislocation configuration make a difference to the mechanical properties, among which
there is a special form known as a kink band [61,171]. Generally, BCC structures show a low
homogeneous tensile ductility and insufficient strain hardening since it is difficult for them
to accommodate enough dislocations. The unstable cracks give rise to brittle cleavage or
quasi-cleavage fractures, indicating a very low toughness in refractory HEAs [26,172]. If the
matrix consists of high-density IV family elements, the local ductility could be moderated
through localized edge dislocation arrays. The introducing of elements from the IV family
exerts great impact on the core configuration and oscillation of screw dislocations as well
as unstable SFE [148]. Accordingly, the energy barrier the edge dislocation slip needs to
overcome is increased, promoting the piled-up edge dislocations to evolve into inclined
boundary pairs of kink bands. This structure can inhibit the dislocation runaway and local
plastic flow through the change of local slip resistance in the same slip plane, which is an
important factor for the high ductility and fracture toughness of refractory HEAs [30,173].
Some kinds of refractory HEAs exhibit an ultrahigh fracture toughness at RT. For
instance, the σy of HfNbTaTiZr is 929 MPa at RT and could be maintained at 295 MPa
at 1000 ◦C [ 28]. Although the tensile εf is only 0.16, the fracture toughness is up to
210 MPa.m1/2 at RT. In this case, it is characteristic of ductile fractures with micro-void
nucleation and aggregation (Figure 13a). Slant parallel bands with misorientation ranging
from 18◦ to 35◦ could be observed at the crack tip, suggesting they are kink bands instead
of twins (Figure 13b,d); the kernel average misorientation (KAM) is severe around grain
boundaries and kink bands, meaning where the strain and dislocation density are very high
(Figure 13c) [118]. Since the dislocation movements need to first conquer the energy barriers
of grain boundaries and kink bands, the existence of these obstacles will definitely increase
the resistance of crack propagation and hence the fracture toughness of HfNbTaTiZr [130].

pairs, which are formed by edge dislocations within the kink bands, nucleate in regions

interface, and the uneven stress between adjacent grains causes the grains to rotate in
different directions. Due to the difference in the original orientation, the degree of rotation
of each grain is also different. Some grains show significant orientation differences before

different slip systems within the grain are driven to rotate to their respective stress–stable
directions. In this process, the kink band rotates around the rotation axis to help the
material adapt to the lattice rotation, and the orientation difference between the matrix
and the kink band is accommodated inside the kink band [175]. In BCC, kink deformation

extend the kink bands, which is capable of better accommodating high strains at crack
tips [117]. Most of the geometrically required dislocations along the crack path are confined
to the kink band boundaries. During continuous deformation, the kink band could bring
lattice along to adapt to the high strain at crack tips through rotations, which is effective in
dispersing damage and slowing necking. In addition, kink bands promote the formation of
low-angle grain boundaries and eliminate the nucleation sites for micro-voids in grains by
providing a favorable moving path for dislocations, which is believed to be one of the most
important sources for high toughness [177]. The pinning and depinning of dislocations
usually results in a zigzag behavior in the stress–strain curves [178].

alloys with high toughness [179–182]. Refractory HEAs that consist of principal elements

4. Summary
The vast majority of FCC-based HEAs possess sound ductility and fracture toughness
at both room and low temperatures but inadequate strength. In comparison, BCC-based
refractory HEAs usually exhibit ultrahigh strength at room and high temperatures as well
as an excellent thermal stability, except for an unsatisfactory ductility at RT. Therefore,
further improving the mechanical properties of HEAs is of great significance for meeting
extended requirements in practice.
Both the strength and ductility of FCC-based HEAs like CoCrFeMnNi and CrCoNi
could be raised with decreased temperatures, mainly profiting from the synergistic strength-
ening effects of dislocation slip, stacking fault, TRIP , and TWIP . In order to further enhance
the strength of FCC-based HEAs, general approaches are always necessary. First, expand-
ing the mismatches in atomic volume and shear modulus by adjusting the constituents.
Second, introducing short- and/or long-range ordered phases to increase the resistance of
dislocation movements. Third, enhancing the interface by exploring element separation,
cell walls, fine grains, and newborn twins, among others. Finally, constructing meta-stable
structures. Escalating the solid solution strengthening by optimizing principal elements has
proven to be a great means. But the solubility of additives in the matrix should be paid more
attention. As to the second phase, the ultrafine CSRO is more effective than the coarse ones
in slowing the migration rate of dislocations, suggesting a better efficiency in dispersing
damage and reducing ductility loss. Interfaces such as grain and twin boundaries are also
widely used. At present, the design of meta-stable structures is attracting more attention
and has been demonstrated to be promising in striking the balance between strength and
ductility through TRIP and TWIP .

The high strength of single-phase refractory HEAs is primarily ascribed to a relative
lower migration of the edge dislocation and pinning effect due to lattice distortion and
sluggish diffusion. During isothermal deformation, the σy depends heavily on the tempera-
ture and strain rate. As temperature increases, the strength presents an “S-shape” tendency.
At moderate temperatures, the weakened dependency of the shear modulus and Burgers
vector on the temperature promotes a plateau to form. The actual temperature range of
this plateau is correlative with the absolute melting points of refractory HEAs. With the
strain rate reducing and temperature increasing, the yield strength decreases, which is
attributed to the depinning of dislocations, as well as recovery and recrystallization at
high temperatures. Additionally, a lower strain rate would provide more time for com-
pleting this softening process. The main strategies to improve the strength of refractory
HEAs can be concluded as the type and concentration of principal elements, multi-phase
structures, and interstitial elements, along with kink bands, among which the principal
elements are of most importance. A high mismatch in atomic volume and shear mod-
ulus is favorable for enhancing the interaction between the misfit of solute volume and
the compressive stress field of dislocations. The core configuration of screw dislocations
could impact their migration path and rate, and even the anisotropy of elastic properties.
Moreover, strength and ductility are also affected by the chemical interaction between
solute and dislocations. The intermetallic phase, atom clusters, and CSRO might change
the actual shear modulus. In comparison to single-phase refractory HEAs, the multi-phase
structure exhibits a higher strength at high and moderate temperatures. But an ultrahigh
temperature will accelerate the dissolution of phases that play a strengthening role at low
temperatures. The contribution of interstitial elements is mainly from their position and
state in the matrix. In addition to impurity, vacancy, grain boundary, and other defects,
the kink bands that usually nucleate at a high-density dislocation zone could effectively
relax the concentrated stress by bringing lattices along to rotate, one of the most important
inducements for ultrahigh fracture toughness and excellent ductility of refractory HEAs
consisting of principal elements from the IV family. Admittedly, the reasonable exploitation
of these strengthening mechanisms is the premise for improving the mechanical properties
and applications of HEAs.
