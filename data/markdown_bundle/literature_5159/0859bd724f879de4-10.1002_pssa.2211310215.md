Laves-Phase Superalloys? 
BY 
J. D. LIVINGSTON 
Dedicated to Professor Dr. PETER HAASEN on the occasion of his 65th birthday 
The mechanical properties of Laves phases, and of alloys containing Laves phases, are reviewed with 
an eye to their potential as high-temperature structural materials. Several two-phase alloys show 
promising combinations of high-temperature strength and low-temperature toughness, and extensive 
room-temperature deformability of the cubic Laves phase by mechanical twinning is demonstrated 
in one series of alloys.

1. Introduction 
Intermetallic compounds are being considered for application in future turbines planned 
to operate at temperatures higher than those attainable with present-day superalloys. 
Although most past studies have been on compounds with structures that are ordered forms 
of f.c.c., b.c.c., or h.c.p., most intermetallics have more complex structures. By far the largest 
class of such compounds is that of the Laves phases. 
There are hundreds of binary and ternary Laves phases, with melting points ranging 
from 7 "C (KNa,) to over 2000 "C (TaCr,, ZrW,). Some show promise for non-structural 
applications, such as (Tb, Dy)Fe, for magnetoelastic transducers [l], (Hf, Zr)V, for super- 
conducting magnets [2], (Cr, Fe)Zr, for hydrogen storage [3], and Mo(Co, Si), for wear 
resistance [4]. Although Laves phases have a reputation as an embrittling grain-boundary 
phase in steels, superalloys, and other structural alloys, Bhandarkar et al. [5, 61 showed 
many years ago that a dispersion of TaFe, Laves-phase particles dispersed within the grains 
of a ferritic steel produced good high-temperature strength without low-temperature 
embrittlement. Recent studies suggest that similar results may be obtainable in other alloy 
systems.

3. Mechanical Properties of Single-Phase Alloys 
In considering the C15 structure, one recent review article notes [18] “the structure is 
complicated and likely slip systems are far from obvious”. However, slip and twinning 
systems in both C15 and C14 phases have been known experimentally for some time, and, 
as expected from the arguments above based on stacking sequences, are analogous to those 
observed in f.c.c. and h.c.p. structures, respectively. 
In C15 compounds, as in f.c.c., the dominant slip systems are (111) (110) and the 
twinning systems are { 11 l} (1 12), as demonstrated by Moran [19] by deformation of single 
crystals of MgCu,, the prototype C15 compound. Moran also found, however, that ductile 
behavior was limited to temperatures above O.65Tm in compression and O.8Tm in tension 
(T, melting temperature). Similar ductile-brittle transition temperatures have been reported 
for polycrystalline MgCu, [20 to 221 and TEM analysis of dislocations in deformed MgCu, 
(Fig. 2) has confirmed {Ill) (110) to be the dominant slip systems [21,23]. 
The most extensive body of work on plastic deformation in a Laves phase has been that 
of Paufler and others at the Technical University of Dresden [24 to 301, who have studied 
single crystals of MgZn,, the prototype of the hexagonal C14 structure. As in h.c.p., the

The prototype of the dihexagonal C36 phase is MgNi,. As with MgCu, and MgZn,, 
plastic deformation is observed only at high homologous temperatures [31]. TEM of 
plastically-deformed MgNi, (Fig. 4) and C36 Mg(Cu, Zn), compound revealed many 
stacking faults [31], consistent with the expected low stacking-fault energy of the C36 
structure, which is intermediate between hexagonal and cubic stacking. 
Studies on a number of other Laves phases have confirmed ductile-brittle transition 
temperatures at 0.6Tm or higher. A recent example is the work of Saka et al. [33] on C15 
(Tb, Dy)Fe,, of technological interest because of its giant magnetostriction. However, a 
number of Laves phases have shown promising strength and creep resistance at high 
temperatures, possibly because the high-coordination TCP structure limits diffusion rates. 
Sauthoff [33] reported promising high-temperature strength for TiCr,, Ti(Cr,Si),, 
Nb(Ni, Al),, and Ta(Fe,Al), (Fig. 5) and Anton and Shah [34] reported good creep resistance 
for NbCr,, NbCo,, and related compounds. Good oxidation resistance was observed in a 
number of these compounds, even in several containing neither aluminium nor silicon. It 
was suggested that the slow oxidation rate may have resulted from the afore-mentioned 
slow diffusion rates. 
Significant solution strengthening was found in a number of Mg(Cu,X), C15 compounds 
[21]. Compounds with promising high-temperature properties, such as NbCr, and NbCo,, 
also have many alloying possibilities [35]. 
Despite promising properties at high temperatures, Laves phases have the disadvantage 
shared by most intermetallics and ceramics, i.e., brittleness at low homologous temperatures. 
Since even single crystals of Laves compounds share this problem, their brittleness does 
not derive from grain-boundary weakness or a limited number of slip systems. In all 
probability, the brittleness results directly from the complex TCP structure and a resulting 
high Peierls resistance to dislocation motion. Dislocation glide in Laves phases requires 
“synchroshear” that involves more complex atomic motion than required in simpler 
structures [lo, 26, 36, 371. As a result, development of “superalloys” based on Laves phases 
will probably require two-phase microstructures to produce adequate low-temperature 
ductility and/or toughness.

A ferritic steel with good high-temperature strength produced by a dispersion of TaFe, 
particles also had good ductility at low temperatures [5,6]. More recently, two-phase Cr-Nb 
alloys comprised of NbCr, and Cr-rich solid solution were found to have both high strength 
at high temperatures and adequate ductility at low temperatures (Fig. 6) [40]. A NbCr,-based 
alloy toughened with Nb-rich solid solution has also been proposed for high-temperature 
application [41]. Some encouraging properties have also been reported for Ti-Cr-Nb alloys 
hardened by TiCr, [42]. In these alloys, however, it has not been determined to what degree, 
if any, the Laves phase itself is plastically deforming at room temperature. 
Vanadium-rich alloys containing substantial volume fractions of a C15 HfV,-based phase 
can be extensively deformed by rolling at room temperature [2]. It has recently been 
confirmed that the Laves phase in these alloys indeed deforms plastically at room 
temperature, and established that the major deformation mode is { 11 l} { 112) twinning 
(Fig. 7) [37]. Since glide of the partial dislocations involved in mechanical twinning in C15 
compounds also requires synchroshear, it is clear that at least in this one Laves phase, 
synchroshear is possible at room temperature. Further study is required to establish whether 
room-temperature plastic deformation is possible in other Laves phases, or whether it is 
limited to HfV,-related compounds, which have anomalous elastic properties associated 
with low-temperature lattice instabilities.

5. Conclusions 
The great number of known Laves phases, their wide alloying possibilities, and limited but 
promising data on high-temperature creep and oxidation resistance, suggest that Laves 
phases should be among those phases to be considered for hardening future higher- 
temperature superalloys. Brittle behavior at low temperatures, even in single crystals, 
indicates they probably will be unacceptable structural materials as single-phase alloys. A 
few studies of two-phase alloys suggest the possibility of improving low-temperature ductility 
and/or toughness while maintaining substantial high-temperature strength. The demonstra- 
tion of room-temperature plastic deformation by mechanical twinning in a Laves phase 
within two-phase V-Hf-Nb alloys is especially promising. Further research is necessary 
to determine whether such deformability can be induced in other Laves phases by control 
of structural parameters such as stacking-fault energy. 
To provide a stronger foundation for development of structural alloys hardened by Laves 
phases and other complex intermetallics, more basic data on the structure and properties
