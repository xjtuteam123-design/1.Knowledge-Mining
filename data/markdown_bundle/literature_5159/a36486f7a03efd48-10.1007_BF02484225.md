The numerous publications dealing with high-temperature surfacing focus mainly on the issue of resistance to 
high-temperature corrosion. Very few studies have been dedicated to the wear resistance of high-temperature 
materials. Meanwhile, items fabricated from high-temperature alloys have to operate under a wide gamut of 
service conditions which require that they should have not only high heat resistance and high-temperature 
strength, but also improved wear resistance. The situation is aggravated still more because at elevated 
temperatures the contacting surfaces are more likely to seize. The failure of materials when they are raised to 
high temperatures adds to the danger of abrasive wear. In a search for ways and means of enhancing the wear 
resistance of parts fabricated from high-temperature alloys and operating under contact friction, a technique 
was investigated in this study for the laser surfacing of ZhS26 high-temperature alloy with refractory carbides. 
In [1, 2], an attempt is reported to use a laser beam in the 
surfacing of high-temperature nickel alloys with refractory 
carbides. Exposed to laser radiation, the carbides introduced 
into the melt undergo oxidation, thermal destruction, and dis- 
solution in the molten matrix metal. This enhances the hard- 
ness of the alloy in the laser-affected zone, but fails to impart 
long-term strength to the surfaces which operate in contact 
friction. 
At the operating temperatures (about 1000~ the diffu- 
sion processes in high-temperature nickel alloys are stepped 
up and the surface layer is rapidly depleted of the alloying 
elements. 
An optimal way to solve the depletion problem is to pro- 
duce a surface layer reinforced with refractory carbides intro- 
duced into the melt which is formed when the surface of the 
alloy is exposed to the laser beam. 
The ZhS26 high-temperature nickel alloy (0.13 - 18% C,

appearance of coarse carbides is this. When the slip is pre- 
pared and applied to the workpiece, the powdered carbides 
settle out to form conglomerates which differ in size and con- 
sist of original fine carbides sticking together. In the sub- 
sequent laser-beam treatment, the individual particles of the 
conglomerates melt on the surface and fuse together. 
The carbide regions thus produced are inhomogeneous in 
both composition and properties. Under exposure to the laser 
beam, the fusion in the bulk of a carbide conglomerate pro- 
ceeds more readily in its looser parts and at the boundaries of 
the fine original carbides. At the same time, an amount of 
nickel finds its way from the base metal into the microvolume 
of the molten carbide. During the subsequent solidification, a 
carbide is formed in that microvolume with a stoichiometric 
composition corresponding to the formula (W, Ni)C. Differ- 
ent amounts of nickel may be incorporated in the carbide, de- 
pending on temperature fluctuations and solidification time. 
The microprobe spectral analysis reveals sharp variations in 
the amount of nickel over the cross-section of individual car- 
bide constituents. The nickel present in the carbide reduces 
the hardness of the latter, which is why the hardness of the 
carbides in the laser-surfacing layer ranges from 1500 to 
2500 HV. 
Thus the carbides which reinforce the surface layer in the 
laser-treated ZhS26 alloy are original ones (that is, fused into 
the metal surface from the slip).. It is highly improbable that 
such carbides can crystallize from the liquid phase in the la- 
ser-beam treatment. 
Nevertheless, the carbides do crystallize when the surface 
layer given a coat of slip is treated with the laser beam. For 
example, x-ray diffi'actometric examination reveals the pres- 
ence of carbides of the W2C type nonexistent in the slip 
powder. 
In addition to carbides, the fusion zone of the slip-coated 
specimens shows the presence of other constituent phases as 
well (Table 1). 
The hardness of those phases is markedly lower than that 
of the carbides. The hardness distribution across the thickness 
of the layer exhibits no definite pattern. Because of the in- 
tensive convective mixing of the molten bath and lack of 
time for its homogenization, the structural constituents 
may occur at random in any section of the slip coating. 
The hardness of the layer likewise varies at random. On 
the whole, however, the laser-surfacing layer is harder 
than the base metal. 
The chemical composition of the structural constitu- 
ents in question was assessed using the point-by-point mi- 
croprobe spectral analysis technique (Table 1). That car- 
bides of the (W, Ni)C type should carry far more tungsten 
than nickel is a foregone conclusion and needs no expla- 
nation. The noncarbide structures carry different amounts 
of tungsten because the latter dissolves in the molten bath

From the foregoing, it may be concluded that the surface 
layer gains in hardness due to the following factors: 
(1) the presence of reinforcing carbides; 
(2) the solid-solution hardening of the gamma phase due 
to its supersaturation with the metallic constituents of the 
molten carbides; 
(3) dispersion hardening associated with the precipitation 
of fine carbides of the W2C type and small fragments of the 
gamma phase (Ni3AI) upon solidification. 
To assess the temperature stability of the surface layer re- 
inforced with carbides of tungsten, molybdenum, and tita- 
nium, the specimens were annealed at 1070~ for 5 rain to 2 h. 
When heated in an air, the ZhS26 high-temperature alloy 
suffered gas corrosion. The carbides produced by laser-sur- 
facing likewise underwent oxidation. For example, the mo- 
lybdenum carbide began to be oxidized even at 500~ the 
tungsten carbide at 700~ and the titanium carbide at 600- 
900~ [3]. Because of this, the surface layer suffered chip- 
ping fully or in part. 
Microstructural examination of annealed specimens 
shows that tungsten carbide-reinforced layers produced by a 
laser beam with energy 4 - 5 J are unstable. They suffer chip- 
ping even after annealing for 5 min. The layers produced by a 
laser beam with energy 6 - I 0 J remain intact after annealing 
at 1070~ for 2 h. In both structure and properties, they are 
almost identical with the layers that have not been annealed. 
In [4], the stability of layers deposited by the laser-surfac- 
ing process was determined using the cutting-tool test proce- 
dure. Specimens simulating the leading edge of a gas-turbine 
engine blade in size and shape were laser-surfaced with car- 
bides under various conditions. The results of the tests are 
given in Fig. 3. The highest wear resistance was shown by the 
carbide layers produced with a laser-beam of energy E = 7 J 
and defocused to A f= 3. The lowest wear resistance was 
found in the carbide layers produced with a laser beam of 
lower energy (E = 5 J). 
A possible explanation for this pattern is that the low-en- 
ergy beam was unable to bond the carbide-reinforced layer to 
the base metal strongly. Because of this, when the carbide- 
surfaced specimens were tested for wear resistance, the car- 
bide layer chipped and the solid carbide particles are de- 
tached, thus leading to a faster wear compared to specimens 
not carbide-surfaced. An increase in the laser-beam energy to 
7 J led to a stronger bond with the substrate. In that case, car- 
bide surfacing served to enhance the wear resistance of the 
layer considerably. 
CONCLUSIONS 
(1) Laser surfacing of ZhS26 high-temperature alloy with 
tungsten carbides produces mainly original carbides of tile 
WC type and the ?'-phase alloyed (to various degrees) with 
tungsten. 
(2) Laser surfacing enhances the hardness and wear resis- 
tance of the ZhS26 alloy due to the presence of the original 
carbides, the solid-solution hardening of the ?'-phase, and dis- 
persion hardening associated with the precipitation of the ?"- 
phase and fine carbides of the W2C type.
