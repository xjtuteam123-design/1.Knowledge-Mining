The Viability of the Refractory High-Entropy Alloy

George J. Wise, Nicole L. Church, Hon Tong Pang, Robert P. Thompson, Howard J. Stone,

1. Introduction
Conventional metallurgical systems exploited for engineering
applications have typically relied on a single principal base
element, with the physical properties tailored by the addition
of minority elements in relatively small concentrations.
However, extensive optimization of these systems has meant that
they are currently nearing the limit of their capabilities. As such,
new alloy systems need to be explored if step changes in high-
temperature performance are to be realized for next-generation
applications in the power generation,
propulsion, and petrochemical sectors.
The ﬁeld of so called “high-entropy”
alloys (HEAs) ﬁrst proposed by Yeh
et al. [1] explores regions of composition
space with multiple principal elements,
often in equiatomic or near-equiatomic
ratios. Despite initial suggestions that the
high entropy of mixing in multicomponent
alloys would promote solid solution for-
mation, there are often inconsistencies
with regard to heat treatment condition
and phase identi ﬁcation, with both single-
phase and multiphase microstructures
reported.[2– 6] While several of the founding
principles have since been questioned, [7,8]
the multiple principal element alloy design
space still offers unique possibilities to
develop materials with signi ﬁcant perfor-
mance advantages compared to currently
utilized conventional metallurgical systems with a single
principal element.
Most systems reported in the literature have typically been
based on ﬁrst-row transition metals[8–11] or refractory metals,[12– 16]
with the former conferring limited high-temperature advantage
compared to conventional steels or Ni-base superalloys. [8] In con-
trast, refractory HEAs (RHEAs) have shown properties that indi-
cate that they are a genuine source of potential new materials for
elevated temperature applications as they are based on elements
with extremely high melting points. Many of these elements also
have high mutual solubility, which can result in the formation of
single-phase solid solutions over extended temperature ranges.
However, many of the single-phase RHEAs exhibit a ductile-to-
brittle transition above ambient temperature, as well as much
lower absolute strengths than those which can be achieved in
multiphase RHEAs.[13,14,17,18]
Multiphase RHEAs containing Al as one of the principal
alloying elements can form microstructures comprising both a
body-centered-cubic A2 structure and an ordered superlattice
B2 structure [where A2 and B2 refer to crystal structures
in the Strukturbericht notation]. [19] The ﬁrst reported A2 – B2
RHEA, and one that has received considerable attention, is
AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr.[20– 27] Here, the A2 structure forms cuboidal
precipitates within a B2 matrix, creating microstructures that are
visually similar to the γ– γ 0 microstructure commonly observed in
Ni-base superalloys, but with an ordered matrix and disordered

There is signi ﬁcant motivation to produce new structural materials for
high-temperature service, so that enhanced ef ﬁciencies can be realized for next-
generation power and propulsion applications. High-entropy alloys incorporating
refractory elements have been shown to be a viable source of potential new
materials for these applications. Despite this, signi ﬁcant work is required in order
to evaluate the suitability of these materials for operation in extreme environ-
ments of temperature and mechanical load. Herein, AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr alloy is
ﬁrst reported on, for the ﬁrst time providing an in situ determination of the B2
solvus, correlated to the changes in mechanical properties. In addition, a low-
temperature (800 °C) regime of catastrophic accelerated oxidation is identi ﬁed,
which is discussed in context of the potential future applications for this alloy.
These results highlight signi ﬁcant limitations in AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr and related
alloys, which make them unsuitable for commercial application in their current
form.

ity on the order of 50% at elevated temperatures, the ordered
matrix phase has been reported to limit room-temperature duc-
tility to ≈10%,[22] and as such there have been efforts to invert the
microstructure and improve fracture toughness. [28– 30] Despite
success in inverting the microstructure by modi ﬁcations to

microstructures with an A2 matrix but no ordered superlattice
phase.[30,31] This suggests a low B2 solvus temperature in
these modi ﬁed systems, which could potentially limit the high-
temperature capabilities of these alloys.
At present, the alloy AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr currently appears to
show the most favorable microstructure for high-temperature
structural applications. Both the reported compressive yield
strengths and the speci ﬁc yield strengths of AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr
were greater than commercial superalloys from room tempera-
ture to 1200 °C with a rapid decrease in strength observed above
≈900 °C.[22] It has been hypothesized that this could be due to the
dissolution of the B2 precipitate phase; however, the temperature
at which this dissolution occurs is yet to be experimentally
determined.
The only constituent binary system that may contain the B2
intermetallic precipitate is the Al – Mo system, with evidence to
suggest the B2 phase ﬁeld extends to temperatures in excess
of 2000 °C. [32] However, several of the constituent ternary
systems also contain the B2 structure, forming AlTi 2Mo,
AlTi2Nb, and AlTi 2Ta. These phases are unstable above 1200 °C
and are thought to be metastable at lower temperatures. [33– 37]
This raises questions about the stability of the B2 superlattice
structure in AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr. For example, signi ﬁcant phase
coarsening and the precipitation of large volume fractions of an
Al– Zr intermetallic phase have been reported following furnace
exposures between 800 and 1200 °C. [38] In simpli ﬁed alloy sys-
tems this intermetallic has been shown to form rapidly, within
an hour at elevated temperatures.[39] Critically, Al– Zr-based inter-
metallic phases are known to exhibit limited plasticity, which
may compound the limited room-temperature ductility seen in
this alloy. [40]
Another key requirement for any high-temperature material is
an ability to form a self-protecting exterior scale, such that it can
withstand in-service conditions, even when coated. However,
despite the promising mechanical properties exhibited at ele-
vated temperatures, there have been few studies to date assessing
the oxidation performance of AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr. The data
available generally suggest poor oxidation behavior, with one
study reporting mass gains of 12.91 mg cm /C0 2 after a furnace
exposure at 800 °C for 15 h and of 155 mg cm /C0 2 after exposure
at 1000 °C for 40 h and another reporting interface controlled
(linear) kinetics at 1000 °C. [41,42] Similar oxidation behavior
has been reported for a number of other RHEAs, but many of
these studies have not investigated homogenized material. [42,43]
It is highly likely that any solidi ﬁcation-induced microsegrega-
tion invariably present in cast ingots would in ﬂuence the result-
ing oxidation performance. Consequently, there are still many
key questions that remain outstanding about the oxidation
resistance of this class of alloys.
In this work, we report on the phase stability, microstructural
evolution, mechanical properties, and oxidation performance of
the RHEA AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr and, in particular, provide the
ﬁrst accurate assessment of the B2 solvus through in situ syn-
chrotron X-ray diffraction experiments. Elevated temperature
mechanical testing has correlated the microstructural changes
with loss of mechanical performance, while oxidation testing
has revealed the alloy suffers from a highly damaging pesting
regime around 800 °C. These ﬁndings have signi ﬁcant implica-
tions as to the viability of the material for high-temperature struc-
tural applications, for which the material is currently unsuitable
without signi ﬁcant further development.
2. Experimental Section
The alloy studied in this work was produced by vacuum arc melt-
ing of constituent elements with purities 99.95% or higher,
according to the nominal composition given in Table 1. The con-
stituent elements were melted on a water-cooled copper hearth
under an inert atmosphere. The cast ingot was inverted and
remelted multiple times to reduce macroscopic inhomogeneity
and ﬁnal re ﬁning was achieved via remelting with an optical
ﬂoating zone furnace (Crystal Systems Corporation, Japan).
The ingot was subsequently wrapped in titanium foil, placed
in an Al 2O3 crucible, and homogenized for 24 h at 1400 °C in
a laboratory-scale vacuum furnace (Advanced Furnace
Technology Ltd., UK). Temperature control was achieved using
a C-type thermocouple (W 26%-Re, W 5%-Re) located near the
heating element. Within the hot zone (measuring ≈100 mm in
diameter /C2 100 mm high) the temperature uniformity was
within /C6 2 °C as determined by scanning of the hot zone with
a separate N-type (Nicrosil-Nisil) thermocouple during calibration.
Bulk compositional analysis for the major elements was
performed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission
spectroscopy by a certiﬁed external laboratory. Oxygen interstitial
content was measured using combustion infrared analysis by the
same laboratory.
Samples were prepared for electron microscopy by mounting
in conductive phenolic resin and surface grinding using succes-
sively ﬁner SiC papers. Final surface preparation was achieved by
polishing to a 0.04 μm ﬁnish using a colloidal silica polishing
suspension, buffered to pH 7. Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) was performed to acquire backscattered electron (BSE)
micrographs using a Zeiss GeminiSEM 300, operated at 15 kV
with a 30 μm aperture. Elemental segregation was identi ﬁed
via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using an
Oxford Instruments X-Max N 50 solid-state EDX detector, an
accelerating voltage of 25 kV, and a 120 μm aperture. Electron
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) data and forward scatter detector
(FSD) images were collected on the same instrument, using an
Oxford Instruments SYMMETRY EBSD detector, with the result-
ing inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps generated using the
AZtecCrystal software.

Synchrotron X-ray diffraction (sXRD) data were acquired on
the ID15a beamline at the European Synchrotron Radiation
Facility using a Pilatus3 2M CdTe detector. The energy of the
monochromatic beam was calibrated using a NIST CeO 2 sample,
corresponding to a wavelength of 0.12782 Å. Samples were illu-
minated in a transmission Debye – Scherrer geometry and data
were collected continuously with an exposure time of 10 s, while
the sample was rocked by /C6 5° to improve grain sampling and
counting statistics. Heating and cooling were performed at a rate
of 60 °C min/C0 1 in a Linkam TS1500V furnace under ﬂowing Ar at
35 mL min/C0 1. The heating rate was chosen to minimize any
potential effects of oxidation on the sample at elevated temper-
atures, while providing suitable temperature resolution. 2D dif-
fraction data were reduced to 1D by integration over the entire
azimuthal range. These data were subsequently analyzed using
custom ﬁtting scripts in the WaveMetrics Inc. software package
IGOR Pro 9. The temperature was calibrated by comparing the
change in diffraction peak positions to the thermal expansion of a
sample heated in a dilatometer.
Vickers hardness measurements were obtained on a sample
polished to a surface ﬁnish of ≈5 μm, using a Qness Q30A þ
hardness testing machine, using a 10 kg applied load. Indents
were placed well away from the edge of the sample and spaced
apart at least three times the indent diameter, in accordance with
ASTM E92.[44] The data are reported as the average obtained from
ﬁve indentations, with the error given as the standard deviation
of the results.
Samples for mechanical testing were produced via electrodi-
scharge machining as right circular cylinders measuring 4 mm
in diameter by 6 mm in length, ground to a 15 μm surface ﬁnish.
Compression testing was performed using a TA Instruments
DIL805 dilatometer, operated in deformation mode with
Al2O3 measuring pushrods and Si 3N4 deformation platens.
Previous work has demonstrated that this method offers excel-
lent temperature and environmental control. [45] All tests were
performed after evacuating the chamber to at least 5 /C2 10/C0 4 mbar
and then backﬁlling with argon to ≈250 mbar. Temperature con-
trol was achieved via the use of an S-type thermocouple, spot
welded directly to the sample surface. Heating was performed
inductively, using a 5 K s /C0 1 heating rate to the test temperature,
followed by a 2 min dwell period for thermal equilibration prior
to the onset of deformation. Deformation was conducted at a
quasistatic strain rate of 2 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1, in accordance with
ASTM E209. [46]
The oxidation behavior of the alloy was assessed via a combi-
nation of isothermal furnace exposures and thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA). Furnace exposures were performed at 800,
900, 1100, and 1200 °C in laboratory box furnaces, calibrated
to /C6 1 °C using an N-type thermocouple. Circular samples
≈7 mm in diameter by 2 mm thick were cut, ground to a
5 μm ﬁnish, and cleaned with acetone. After the samples were
thoroughly dried (for at least 24 h), they were inserted into the
furnaces preset to the test temperature. TGA experiments were
performed on rectangular samples prepared using the same
method, measuring ≈5 /C2 6 /C2 0.5 mm, with cross-sectional areas
measured using an optical microscope and image analysis soft-
ware. Data were acquired using a Setaram Setsys Evolution 18
TGA, under ﬂowing synthetic air (Air Products Synthetic Air
Zero) at a rate of 30 mL min /C0 1. Heating to the test temperature
was performed at 100 K min /C0 1 for these experiments. At the
higher exposure temperatures of 1100 and 1200 °C, evaporative
losses from the Pt wire used to suspend the test crucible were
accounted for based on calibration data obtained from isothermal
exposures on empty crucibles. An additional sample was heated
to 1200 °C at a slower rate of 10 K min /C0 1, to provide more
information regarding the oxidation kinetics as a function of
temperature.
3. Results
3.1. Composition and Microstructure
Compositional analysis of the ingot produced was performed by
a certi ﬁed external laboratory, with the results given alongside
the nominal composition in Table 1. The measured composition
was deemed suf ﬁciently close to the nominal value, with a
relatively low oxygen interstitial content.
The microstructural condition of the RHEA following the
homogenization heat treatment (1400 °C – 24 h) is presented in
Figure 1 . Equiaxed grains on the order of 50 – 100 μm were
present, with the precipitation of a blocky dark BSE contrast
phase primarily at the grain boundaries. Also present at the grain
boundaries was evidence of discontinuous precipitation,
comprising two phases, one light BSE contrast and one dark
BSE contrast. Within the grain interiors a very ﬁne-scale
two-phase microstructure was detected, consistent with the bas-
ketweave A2 – B2 microstructure commonly reported for this
alloy.[22,38]
To identify the compositional segregation associated with the
dark BSE contrast phase, EDX data were acquired, with the asso-
ciated elemental concentration maps provided in Figure 2 . The
dark BSE contrast phase was enriched in Al and Zr, consistent
with the precipitation of an Al – Zr-based intermetallic which are
commonly reported to form rapidly in this alloy, as well as in
simpler alloy systems. [38,39] These regions were concomitantly
depleted in Mo, Ta, and Ti with no measurable enrichment or
depletion in O. It was also noted that a small enrichment in
Nb was detected in the same regions as the enrichment in
Al and Zr, which is putatively attributed to the close proximity
of the Nb and Zr X-ray emission lines used for element
identiﬁcation.
Additional EDX data obtained from the grain boundary
discontinuous precipitation suggested similar enrichment in
Al and Zr, highlighting that the dark BSE contrast phase in these
regions is also likely to be an Al – Zr intermetallic.

The RHEA was then heated and cooled at a constant rate to
identify the B2 solvus temperature. The {111} B2 superlattice
reﬂection was monitored ( Figure 4 ), with the peak area taken
as a proxy to volume fraction. On heating, the peak area decreases
slightly with temperature up to ≈1000 °C. This small decrease in
peak area could be associated with two possible phenomena.
Firstly, this could be indicative of a decrease in B2 volume
fraction with temperature, similar to the progressive decrease
in volume fraction observed for other precipitate species such
as γ 0 in Ni-base superalloys. However, a decrease in the
measured peak area would also be expected from thermal
scattering even if the volume fraction of the B2 phase remained
constant. In practice, the observed decrease in peak area is likely
to be due to a combination of both phenomena. Above ≈1000 °C
there is a sharp decrease in intensity suggesting a decrease in
volume fraction of the B2 precipitates. At 1214 /C6 10 °C the B2
superlattice reﬂection could no longer be resolved from the base-
line suggesting that the B2 precipitates are no longer present in
the microstructure.
On cooling, the reverse process occurs. From 1206 /C6 10 °C,
there was an increase in volume fraction of the B2 superlattice
reﬂection. Below ≈900 °C, the volume fraction remains approxi-
mately constant.
3.3. Mechanical Properties
Room-temperature mechanical properties were investigated via a
series of hardness indents. The average Vickers hardness was
625 /C6 4 HV10, consistent with other reports in the litera-
ture.[47,48] No cracking was observed at the corners of the hard-
ness indents, where the stress concentration is expected to be
greatest. However, all samples exhibited cracks that extended
from the edges of the indents. EBSD was used to acquire crys-
tallographic information around one of the indents, with these
data given in Figure 5 .
In this case, the crack was observed to closely align with a
grain boundary, suggesting a low grain boundary toughness at
room temperature in this alloy. Interestingly, this grain boundary
did not appear to contain a signiﬁcant fraction of the Al4Zr5 inter-
metallic phase. Furthermore, within the indent, intermetallic
precipitates could be identi ﬁed suggesting their presence may
not signi ﬁcantly limit room-temperature compressive ductility
as previously suggested. Toward the bottom of the indent, with

in previous work. The exact nature of this discrepancy is unclear
but may be related to differences in production and testing
methodologies.
3.4. Oxidation Performance
The oxidation performance of the alloy was assessed via a com-
bination of furnace exposures and thermogravimetric analyses.

a solid, but expanded oxide product with a substantial associated
volume change. In contrast, the sample exposed at 800 °C suf-

loss of cross section and structural integrity. The spallation of the

To investigate the oxidation behavior of the alloy further, TGA
data were obtained at the four test temperatures, with the speci ﬁc
mass gains reported as a function of exposure time in Figure 8.
Also included in Figure 8 are comparative data obtained at
1100 °C by Pang et al. [49] for the single-crystal Ni-base superalloy
CMSX-4.
At all the exposure temperatures studied, the speci ﬁc
mass gain initially increased with exposure time, as expected
for isothermal oxidation experiments. Despite this, at 800 °C,
the speci ﬁc mass gain exhibited many sharp drops, in addition
to a signiﬁcant increase in oxidation rate after ≈72 h of exposure.
When removed from the instrument, this sample demonstrated
the same catastrophic oxidation observed at 800 °C during the
furnace exposure. At 900 °C and above, the speci ﬁc mass gain
data did not exhibit any of the sharp drops, and the samples
remained solid on removal from the instrument after testing.
The sample exposed at 900 °C initially demonstrated near-
parabolic oxidation kinetics; however, after ≈42 h the oxidation
rate increased toward linear behavior, before leveling off as
the exposure time approached 100 h. At 1100 °C, similar behavior
was observed, with initial near-parabolic behavior rapidly becom-
ing linear after ≈3.5 h of exposure time. The speciﬁc mass gain at
1100 °C began to level off after ≈22 h, followed by a small
decrease over the remainder of the test duration, which was ter-
minated after 50 h of isothermal exposure time. The changes in
slope observed as the oxidation kinetics approximate linear
behavior before leveling off may be attributed to the loss of sam-
ple cross-section as oxidation proceeds. This would be expected
from any potential changes in the oxidation mechanisms or
species produced as the two oxidation fronts on each side of
the sample meet in the center and consume the underlying metal
substrate. Indeed, on removal from the instrument, the cross-
section of these samples had been completely consumed by
oxidation. The decrease in speci ﬁc mass gain observed at
1100 °C is consistent with volatilization of some of the oxide spe-
cies produced, after the metal substrate has been consumed and

4. Discussion
The B2 solvus temperature of AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr has been mea-
sured as 1214 °C, which to the authors ’ knowledge is the ﬁrst
time this has been reported for this alloy. The loss of the ordered
precipitates coincides with the dramatic reduction in strength
observed in the compression data presented in Figure 6, provid-
ing evidence as to the origins of the high-temperature strength in
this alloy. These results indicate that the presence of the B2 phase
is fundamentally important for the mechanical performance at
elevated temperature.
The Al4Zr5 intermetallic phase was present after homogeniza-
tion, with recent studies demonstrating that cooling rates in
excess of 30 °C min /C0 1 from the homogenization temperature
are required to reduce the volume fraction of intermetallic spe-
cies to less than 1%. [50] These results indicate that it is unlikely
that precipitation of the intermetallic phase can be avoided
during typical manufacturing processes. This phase persists to
temperatures much higher than the B2 solvus and as such,
can be inferred to provide only a limited strengthening contribu-
tion. During the elevated temperature compression testing, the
samples displayed model barreling, indicating that the interme-
tallic phase did not inhibit plastic deformation within the sam-
ples. Nevertheless, at room temperature, there was evidence of
cracking associated with the hardness indent. Interestingly, the
cracking did not initiate from the corners of the indent, where
the stress concentration levels would be expected to be highest.
Instead, EBSD analyses revealed that the observed cracking had
propagated along a grain boundary, suggesting a low grain
boundary toughness in this material.
Whilst a low grain boundary resistance to crack propagation is
considered deleterious for high-temperature service in structural
applications, there are several routes by which this could be
addressed. Polycrystalline alloys typically include minor addi-
tions of grain boundary strengthening elements such as C, B,
or Zr, which increase the cohesion of the boundaries through
elemental segregation and the precipitation of species such as
carbides and borides. [51,52] Whilst effective, these elements are
known to be melting point depressants, which may limit the
maximum improvements in temperature capability that could
be realized. [53] By analogy with conventional Ni-base superalloy
metallurgy, the addition of these elements can be avoided if grain
boundaries are removed altogether, typically achieved by careful
control of withdrawal rates when casting the material so as to
produce single-crystal components.
Conventional refractory metal-based alloys, such as titanium –
zirconium– molybdenum (TZM), oxidize very rapidly and are
highly susceptible to catastrophic oxidation as the Pilling –
Bedworth ratios of the elemental refractory metals are extremely
high. This means that there is a substantial volume expansion
upon oxide formation, which commonly leads to fracture and
spallation.[54] It has been suggested that the multi-elemental
nature of RHEAs could lead to superior behavior through the
formation of complex oxides, especially if an alloy contains sig-
niﬁcant quantities of elements such as Al, Cr, or Ti. [55– 61] Indeed,
some RHEAs have been shown to provide very encouraging
behavior with parabolic or near-parabolic kinetics, including
AlNbTiZr and AlNb 1.5Ta0.5Ti1.5Zr0.5.[43,62]
Despite these previous reports, in the present work, the oxi-
dation behavior of AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr was found to be rather
poor. The absolute values of the speci ﬁc mass gains recorded
at all temperatures in the TGA data were greater than those
reported for the commercially utilized second-generation
single-crystal Ni-base superalloy CMSX-4 oxidized at 1100 °C. [49]
The speci ﬁc mass gains at 1100 °C reported in this work after
25 h of thermal exposure were ≈2.5 orders of magnitude greater
than those reported for CMSX-4. Interestingly, despite these
extremely high speci ﬁc mass gains, the form of the TGA data
suggested that the oxidation kinetics were initially moving
toward a parabolic regime. As shown in Table 2, the alloy exhib-
ited near-parabolic oxidation rate exponents ranging from 1.627
to 1.844 when oxidized at temperatures between 800 and
1200 °C. Similar correlations have been reported previously,
for example, in NbCrMo0.5Ta0.5TiZr, where a mass gain in excess
of 120 mg cm/C0 2 was recorded following 100 h at 1000 °C despite
near-parabolic kinetics ( n = 1.67) being achieved. [61] The oxide

The changes in slope observed in the speci ﬁc mass gain data

sumption of the 0.5 mm-thick cross section, and it is noted that
the near-parabolic oxidation kinetics characterized in the initial
stages of isothermal oxidation would be expected to continue if
the samples were thicker. The mass loss associated with volatili-
zation of the oxide species observed at 1100 °C would also
unlikely be observed on thicker samples, where it would be
masked by the large mass gains. As such, the changes in slope
are considered as testing artefacts relating to the sample dimen-
sions. Despite this, the observations suggest that that the use of
this alloy in oxidative environments is unfeasible, especially
given that components are likely to contain thin sections with
dimensional tolerances on the order of /C6 0.1 mm or less. This
is especially pertinent given that the oxidation data presented
at 800 °C appears to identify a regime of catastrophic accelerated
oxidation. The poor performance at 800 °C is most likely a result
of “pesting”, whereby fast disintegration of the metallic substrate
occurs due to severe intergranular oxidation, typically character-
ized at temperatures between 600 and 800 °C. The sharp drops
observed in the TGA data at 800 are related to this behavior and
result from the excessive spallation of oxide products outside the
suspended crucible. Whilst the mechanisms governing pesting
behavior are not well understood, it has been suggested that pest-
ing may be related to reduced ductility of the metal substrate at
lower temperatures, which is unable to accommodate the high
volume expansion that occurs when forming the growing oxide
species.[63,64] This is also compounded by the fact that the stable
oxide species forming at lower temperatures typically have larger
unit cells. It has also been suggested that in HEAs pesting can be
made worse by the magnitude of lattice distortions, which can
vary with compositional ﬂuctuations in the material, causing
inhomogeneous oxygen diffusion leading to serious stress con-
centrations in certain regions of the material.[65,66] Despite this, it
is noted that characterization of the lattice distortions in a given
system is required to unambiguously attribute this to the cause of
pesting.[67]
Whilst pesting behavior has been reported previously in other
RHEA alloys, for example, in Hf0.5Nb0.5Ta0.5Ti1.5Zr,[68] this is the
ﬁrst time this behavior has been reported in AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr.
It has been suggested that pesting resistance may be improved by
producing thick, protective external scales. [63,65,69] However this
would likely require extensive compositional modi ﬁcation. The
removal of all grain boundaries, which exhibit accelerated oxida-
tion at lower temperatures, may also confer resistance to pesting,
although it is noted that pesting has still been observed in some
single-crystal materials.[70] The observation of this phenomenon
in AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr is particularly interesting as it does not
conform with recent suggestions of how it can be suppressed.
Chang et al. [71] reported that small levels of Al ( ≈6 at.%) could
suppress the spallation observed in an HfNbTaTiZr alloy. Yet
in the present work spallation occurs despite a much higher
Al content of 22 at.%. This observation indicates that bulk Al con-
centration alone is not a viable approach to controlling
spallation. Similarly, it has been suggested that the formation
of complex oxides helps to prevent spallation. [58] However,
despite AlMo 0.5NbTa0.5TiZr forming such oxides, spallation
was still observed. Consequently, further work is required to
understand the order in which the different oxide species form
at particular temperatures, to help establish why pesting occurs,
and aid the development of mitigation strategies.
Based on the good high-temperature compressive strength,
these alloys may be well suited to structural applications in inert
environments, where oxidation performance is not a major con-
cern, for example, the alloys used for isothermal precision forg-
ing operations. Current commercial alloys such as TZM have
much lower absolute mechanical strengths below 1200 °C, but
due to the thermal stability of the strengthening carbides present
in the microstructure they retain useful mechanical performance
up to ≈1600 °C.[72] Whilst the loss of the B2 structure means that
the maximum temperature capability compared to TZM is
somewhat reduced, the signi ﬁcant increase in strength for appli-
cations below 1200 °C means that much lower volumes of mate-
rial would be required in the production of such components and
tool life may well be extended.
However, even in inert environments, a remaining issue is the
thermal stability of the B2 phase. The observed discontinuous
precipitation appeared to contain an Al – Zr intermetallic phase.
As no additional reﬂections of any other intermetallic phase were
observed in the sXRD data, it is presumed that this intermetallic
is also Al 4Zr5. The formation of this phase would be expected to
deplete the surrounding material in Al and Zr. The coprecipitat-
ing phase is therefore likely to be enriched in elements that
segregate to and stabilize the A2 structure. This type of discon-
tinuous precipitation is classi ﬁed as a Type 3 reaction in the
framework described by Williams and Butler. [73] In these reac-
tions, neither of the two phases precipitating from the grain
boundary are identical to those ahead of the precipitation front.
Instead, one (the Al 4Zr5 intermetallic) is of a distinct crystallog-
raphy and composition, and the other (the A2), is crystallo-
graphically related to one of the phases in the bulk of the
microstructure but compositionally distinct. Both discontinu-
ously precipitating species are necessarily more thermodynami-
cally stable than the phases in the bulk of the material. This
means that given suf ﬁcient time, the Al 4Zr5 and A2 structures
would be expected to consume the A2 – B2 microstructure, an
important consideration when li ﬁng components for long-term
high-temperature service.
Similar discontinuous lamellae have been reported to form in
Ni-base superalloys and have been linked to a loss of hardness,
signiﬁcant decreases in high-temperature ductility, and the initi-
ation of grain boundary cracking. [74,75] The precipitation of inter-
metallic species resulting in impaired mechanical performance
is also reported in Ni-base superalloys, which are often alloyed
beyond the thermodynamic stability limit for the formation of
topologically close-packed (TCP) phases.[76] Despite this, TCP for-
mation in conventional commercial superalloys is often kineti-
cally inhibited over typical service lifetimes, which can be on
the order of several thousand hours. However, the rapid time-
frames over which Al 4Zr5 have been reported to form in the lit-
erature may suggest that long-term use at elevated temperatures,

5. Conclusion
Refractory metal high-entropy alloys have been reported to pro-
vide performance advantages over conventional alternatives,
however their suitability for high temperature service has not
been thoroughly characterized within the literature. This
study reported on the elevated temperature performance of
AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr, which is the archetypal alloy in this class.
An accurate measure of the B2 solvus temperature has been
identiﬁed for the ﬁrst time within this work, occurring at
1214 /C6 10 °C. The correlation of this value with the elevated tem-
perature mechanical performance has provided direct evidence
for the origins of the high temperature strength in this alloy,
and that loss of the B2 structure limits the elevated temperature
mechanical performance. Furthermore, discontinuous precipita-
tion which appeared to comprise of Al 4Zr5 and an A2 structure
highlighted the metastability of the B2 phase, ultimately limiting
the useful timeframes over which this alloy can be utilized.
Catastrophic oxidation performance, several orders of magni-
tude worse than commercial single crystal Ni-base superalloys,
combined with the identi ﬁcation of a pesting regime at interme-
diate temperatures raises concerns regarding the feasibility
of this and related alloys in oxidative environments. Whilst
AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr can undoubtedly outperform most conven-
tional systems when considering elevated temperature mechani-
cal strength, the poor oxidation performance and limited
grain boundary toughness at room temperature mean that these
systems are unsuitable for commercial application in their
current form.
