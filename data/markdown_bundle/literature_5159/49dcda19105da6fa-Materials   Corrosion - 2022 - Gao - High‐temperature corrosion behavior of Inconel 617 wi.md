Abstract
In this study, the Ni ‐cladding layer was deposited on Inconel 617 substrate as a
corrosion‐resistant layer by gas tungsten arc welding. The corrosion behavior of
Inconel 617 alloys with and without Ni ‐cladding layer, and individual
Ni‐claddings was studied at 850°C in molten FLiNaK salts with a high Ni 2+
concentration. After corrosion, FeNi 3 layers cover the surface of all alloy
specimens. Inconel 617 suffers from severe Cr depletion, whereas Ni ‐claddings
with low Cr content demonstrate significantly improved corrosion resistance.
For Ni‐clad Inconel 617, if Ni‐claddings are broken, the coupling of Ni‐claddings
and Inconel 617 can accelerate the corrosion of Inconel 617 by galvanic
corrosion, inducing a mass transfer of Co from Inconel 617 to Ni ‐claddings
through the molten salts.

alkaline corrosion, EPMA, high temperature corrosion, nickel, superalloys, welding
1 | INTRODUCTION
Corrosion of structural alloys in molten salt reactors
(MSRs), where they directly contact the corrosive molten
salt media at elevated temperatures, is a significant
challenge for material scientists. [1] MSR is constructed
primarily of the UNS N10003 alloy due to its outstanding
mechanical characteristics and resistance to molten salt
corrosion.[] However, the maximum allowable tempera-
ture for UNS N10003 alloy is 704°C, based on the
American Society of Mechanical Engineers Boiler and
Pressure Vessel (ASME ‐BPV) Code, which precludes its
use at elevated temperatures (above 704°C). [3] Inconel
617 alloy was recently added to “ASME‐BPV III ‐5
High‐Temperature Reactors, ” with a codified service
temperature of 950°C. [4] Therefore, it has been suggested
that it could be used as a structural alloy in advanced
nuclear reactors. [3,5] However, corrosion tests conducted
on Inconel 617 alloy in molten halide salts such as FLi-
NaK (LiF –NaF–KF) salts
[6] and KCl ‐MgCl2 salts[7] at
850°C revealed that this alloy is highly susceptible to
molten salt corrosion, with Cr, Mo, and Co depleted from
the alloy matrix. [8] The corrosion of alloys in molten salts
is well ‐known as selective dissolution of active elements
(such as Cr) at the alloy/molten salt interface into molten
salts, resulting in vacancy defects near the interface and
diffusion of the internal alloying elements outwards the
surface. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that In-
conel 617 with a high Cr content (above 20 wt%) has poor
corrosion resistance in high ‐temperature molten salts,
that is, it is impossible to apply Inconel 617 alloy directly
in molten salts without additional protection.
Cladding protective coatings on the surface of an al-
loy with excellent high ‐temperature mechanical perfor-
mance but low corrosion resistance to molten salts is an
effective way to improve corrosion resistance, as widely
demonstrated in the Incoloy ‐800H alloy. Olson et al.
[9]
reported that a Ni layer electroplated on Incoloy ‐800H
alloy could effectively decrease the mass loss of Incoloy ‐
800H alloy in FLiNaK salts at 850°C. Dai et al. [10] in-
vestigated the high ‐temperature corrosion behavior of
Incoloy‐800H alloy with Ni ‐claddings formed by gas

claddings considerably improve the compatibility of
Incoloy‐800H alloy with molten salt, which is similar to

Therefore, cladding protective coatings may also be sui-

[11] showed that
Inconel 617 alloy with Ni ‐claddings exhibits a low con-

results suggested the Ni ‐cladding should have excellent
corrosion resistance to molten salts, no experimental
evidence has been provided. Additionally, if the protec-
tive Ni ‐claddings are broken, whether the molten salts
will be much more corrosive to Inconel 617 alloy is
unclear.
On the basis of the above idea in mind, the corro-
sion behavior of Inconel 617, Ni ‐claddings, and Inconel
617 with Ni ‐cladding layer (Ni ‐617) in the FLiNaK salts
at 850°C, were systematically investigated through the
static immersion corrosion tests. The FLiNaK salts
with high concentrations of oxidizing impurities
were used to simulate an extremely corrosive en-
vironment, and the corresponding mechanisms were
also discussed.
2 | EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
2.1 | Materials
A multilayer Ni ‐cladding layer was deposited by GTAW
with restraint on a 23 ‐mm‐thick Inconel 617 plate in the
present work. The welding method was described in
detail in our previous work.[ 11] The chemical composi-
tions of Inconel 617 and the ERNi ‐1 filler wire are listed
in Table 1. Table 2 lists the welding parameters used in
this investigation. The Ni ‐claddings have three layers and
a thickness of 4.5 mm.
The corrosion test specimens with dimensions of
15 × 10 × 2 mm were machined from Inconel 617, Ni ‐617
composite plate, and Ni ‐claddings (near the outermost
layer), as illustrated in Figure 1. The corrosion specimens
and their regions carefully analyzed are listed in Table 3,
classified by the chemical compositions. For example, the
two sides of the Ni ‐claddings specimen can be considered
as two corrosion regions, named NITOP (outermost
surface side of Ni ‐claddings with low Cr concentration)
and NIBOT (away from the outmost layer side of
Ni‐claddings with high Cr concentration). Before the
corrosion experiments, all specimens were ground using
water‐resistant SiC abrasive papers up to 2000 grits and
polished using 0.05 μmA l
2O3 powders and then cleaned
with deionized water and alcohol. The specimens' masses
were measured using an electronic balance with 0.1 mg
accuracy.
2.2 | Static corrosion test
The static immersion corrosion test was performed in a
high‐temperature furnace at 850°C. Figure 2 illustrates
the experimental capsule used in this study. Three cap-
sules, respectively for three kinds of specimens, were
used in the static corrosion test. In the enclosed corrosion
experiment, a double ‐layer crucible with a graphite inner
layer and 316 stainless steel (SS) outer layer was used as
the corrosion experiment device. The inner wall of the
graphite crucible, the graphite cover, and the insulation
gasket were cleaned with alcohol and dried for 6 h at
600°C in the vacuum annealing furnace. The outer 316
SS crucible was cleaned in stages with deionized water
and alcohol and then dried for 30 min at 80°C. After
cooling all components to room temperature, they were
transferred to the glove box for later use. Each alloy was
tested using three parallel specimens. A ceramic in-
sulating sheet was used to connect the alloy specimens to
avoid direct contact between the alloys and the graphite
crucible (Figure 2). The graphite crucible was first filled
with 150 g FLiNaK (46.5LiF –11.5NaF–42KF, mol% salts)
and then covered and placed into the SS crucible. The
crucible was then welded and sealed. To avoid the in-
troduction of excess water and oxygen impurities, all the
above operations were performed in a glove box pro-
tected by argon. The sealed SS crucible was removed
from the glove box and heated for 400 h at 850°C in the
muffle furnace. After the corrosion test was completed,
the specimens were rinsed with 1.0 mol/L aluminum
nitrate solution to eliminate the remaining FLiNaK salts,
followed by deionized water and alcohol cleaning.
The microstructures were characterized using a
scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss LEO1530VP)

3.2 | Micromorphology
3.2.1 | Specimen surface
Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of all the specimens
before and after corrosion in molten FLiNaK salt at
850°C for 400 h. When compared to the alloys before
corrosion, the three characteristic peaks of the alloy
matrix ((111), (200), and (220)) after corrosion shift to
higher 2θ, indicating a decrease in the lattice of the alloys
caused by metallic element depletion from the surface of
the alloys.
[12] It should be noted that the characteristic
peaks of FeNi3, which are next to those of austenite, were
observed on the XRD patterns of the three types of spe-
cimens after corrosion. Fe tends to form γ (Fe, Ni)
through complete dissolution in nickel at 700°C, ac-
cording to the Fe ‐Ni phase diagram.
[13] Therefore, Fe and
Ni, which source from molten salt, can be alloyed to form
FeNi3 on the alloy surface. Additionally, FeNi 3 exhibits a
minor peak shift in comparison to the austenite matrix
due to its different chemical composition.
As shown in Figure 4a,c,e,g, it can be observed that a
layer of new products appears on the surface of all the
specimens, which is consistent with XRD results that the
FeNi
3 is formed on the surface of all specimens after the
corrosion experiment. Small corrosion pores are also
observed on the surface of all specimens, suggesting that
the specimens underwent corrosion.
3.2.2 | Cross‐section of the corrosion layer
As shown in Figure 4b,d, numerous pits or holes, which
are distributed along the grain boundaries (GBs), appear
in the near ‐surface area of the Inconel 617 (Figure 4b)
and NIBOT (Figure 4d). In comparison, there is no
visible corrosion in the near ‐surface area of NITOP
(Figure 4f). For the Ni ‐617 specimen, the pits or holes in
the near ‐surface and intergranular corrosion at
the IN617BASE are more severe than those at the
Ni‐claddings side.
Figure 5 shows the cross ‐sectional EPMA image and
element distribution mappings of the Inconel 617 and
Ni‐claddings. It can be observed that substantial Cr
depletion occurs on the surface and along the GBs in the
corroded Inconel 617 and NIBOT, while NITOP exhibits
a relatively slight depletion of Cr. Since severe inter-
granular corrosion of Cr can be observed throughout the
corroded Inconel 617, the complete Cr ‐depleted layer is
measured to assess the corrosion degree of the Inconel
617 in this study. As shown in Figure 5d, the maximum
depth of the complete Cr ‐depleted layer near the surface
of Inconel 617 is approximately 84 μm. It is worth noting
that the Fe concentrations in the Cr ‐depleted region are
greater than those in the bulk for both alloy specimens
(Figure 5j–l). For the Inconel 617 specimens, Ni con-
centrations are relatively greater in the Cr ‐depleted re-
gion than those in the other area. The aforementioned
ICP‐OES results (Table 4) revealed that the Cr con-
centration in molten salt increases sharply after corro-
sion, consistent with the severe depletion of Cr from the
Inconel 617 in Figure 5. Besides, the existence of Fe ‐ and
Ni‐rich layers on the surface of all the specimens is also
related to the decrease in the contents of Fe and Ni in the
salts after corrosion (Table 4). This result is also con-
sistent with the experimental XRD analysis, which re-
vealed the formation of FeNi
3 on the surface of all three
specimens during the corrosion test.
The cross ‐sectional element mappings of the Ni ‐617
are shown in Figure 6. A significant amount of Cr is
depleted near the surface and along the GBs in both
IN617BASE and IN617NI1 (~14.9 wt% Cr). Compare to
the Inconel 617 specimens, IN617BASE exhibits more
severe depletion of Cr, which is marked by the increasing
depth of surface complete Cr ‐depleted (~129 μm), in-
dicating that IN617BASE suffers from more severe cor-
rosion than the Inconel 617 specimens during the
corrosion test. However, IN617NI2; (~8.1 wt% Cr) only
shows a relatively slight depletion of Cr, and no sig-
nificant loss of Cr can be observed for IN617NI3 (~4.0 wt
% Cr). These results suggest that the corrosion degree of
the specimens in molten FLiNaK salt is closely related to
their Cr concentration. Besides, a Co ‐rich layer can be
found on the surface of Ni ‐claddings (Figure 6o), with a
thickness approximately equal to the depth of the
Cr‐depleted layer. Moreover, Co atoms diffuse into the
matrix of Ni ‐claddings along the GBs. These results
imply that a mass transfer of Co between Inconel 617 and
Ni‐claddings through molten salts occurs during the
corrosion test, leading to the more severe corrosion of
IN617BASE.

4 | DISCUSSION
4.1 | Corrosion of the Inconel 617 and
the Ni ‐claddings
As mentioned in Section 1, many previous studies have
revealed that 617 alloys have poor corrosion resistance in
high‐temperature molten salt, specifically exhibit as
higher mass loss and deeper corrosion depth. The results
of this study demonstrate that Ni ‐claddings, as a pro-
tective barrier to molten salts, can protect the 617 alloys
from attack by molten FLiNaK salts. It should be noted
that the Cr and Co content in the Ni ‐claddings affects the
corrosion resistance. Dai et al.
[10] found that, the chem-
istry criterion for claddings compatible with molten salts
should be Fe + Cr ≤ 11 wt%. NiTOP (Co + Cr = 6.8 wt%)
shows excellent corrosion resistance to the molten salt,
while NIBOT (Co + Cr = 11.6 wt%) shows relatively poor
corrosion resistance, which suggests that Co + Cr ≤ 11 wt
% should be helpful to screen the chemistry of claddings,
similar to Dai's criterion.
The significant depletion of Cr along the surface and
GBs of the Inconel 617 indicates that more severe cor-
rosion proceeds for the Inconel 617 during the corrosion
experiment (Figure 4). Generally, the FLiNaK salts can-
not corrode the Ni ‐base alloys because the fluorides of
alloying elements have larger free energies of formation
than those of LiF, NaF, and KF.
[6] Therefore, the corro-
sion of metal samples was attributed to the electro-
chemical dissolution of alloying elements caused by the
corrosive impurities in molten FLiNaK salt. It was found
that Cr

4.2 | Galvanic corrosion between 617
alloys and Ni ‐claddings
It should be noted that Ni ‐617 shows much more severe
corrosion than 617 alloys without Ni ‐claddings, indicating
much more severe corrosion could happen if Ni ‐claddings
were broken in the molten FLiNaK salts. Many investiga-
tions have demonstrated that additional materials can affect
the corrosion of alloys in molten fluoride salts. [6,17–24] The
galvanic corrosion process in this study can be initiated by
differences in corrosion potentials between electrically
linked materials and elemental transfer generated by an
activity gradient between different materials, which can be
described as two stages: 1. Th e accelerated corrosion of
IN617BASE (Figure 6a,b); 2. The transfer of Co from
IN617BASE to Ni‐claddings (Figure 5o). The related reac-

5 | CONCLUSION
1. Inconel 617 exhibits obvious intergranular corrosion
in molten FLiNaK salt with high Ni 2+ concentration,
which is attributed to the dissolution of Cr, Co, and
formed ions in the salt.
2. Ni ‐claddings exhibit much more excellent corrosion
resistance than the Inconel 617, and the high ‐
temperature corrosion resistance of the Inconel 617
can be significantly improved by the Ni ‐claddings.
3. If the Ni ‐cladding is broken, the interactions of the
Inconel 617 and the Ni ‐claddings can increase the
intergranular corrosion of base 617 alloys and induce
a mass transfer of Co from Inconel 617 to Ni ‐claddings
through the molten salt.
