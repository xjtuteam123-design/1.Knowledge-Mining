Abstract
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) possess unique strengthening mechanisms, and a vast design possibility, distinguish-
ing them from conventional alloys. Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), a subclass of HEA, offer better 
high-temperature structural stability, making them ideal replacement for traditional high-temperature alloys. This 
review systematically evaluates the critical role of empirical alloy design parameters and processing methods 
in influencing the microstructure and mechanical properties of RHEAs. Key parameters, including enthalpy of 
mixing (ΔH mix), atomic size difference (δ ), valence electron concentration (VEC), Pauling electronegativity, 
and melting point are analyzed for their impact on phase formation and mechanical performance. Furthermore, 
the effects of various processing techniques, such as arc melting, powder metallurgy, and magnetron sputtering, 
are explored for their ability to optimize the microstructure and mechanical response. The review highlights the 
interplay between these factors, offering pathways for improving yield strength, ductility, and high-temperature 
softening resistance. These insights provide a foundational understanding of the factors governing the perfor -
mance of RHEAs for high-temperature applications and outline pathways for future research and development.

1 Introduction
The need for heat-resistant materials in aircraft engine turbo superchargers led to the invention of the super 
alloy, an alloy with excellent mechanical properties at high temperatures. Ni-based super alloy is one of the 
kind of structural material which provides a distinct combination of mechanical and physical properties at 
temperatures below 1100 °C [1 ]. After exposure to hot gases, the turbine rotor inlet temperatures exceed 
1400 °C. The components made of Ni-based superalloys, therefore, require cooling to avoid the melting of the 
constituent phases and prevent structural damage of engine component [2 ]. Besides this, working efficiency is 
significantly reduced. Therefore, there is a crucial need to explore a material having ability to withstand the 
high temperature [3 , 4]. The qualities of new material may require a balance between room temperature tough-
ness, high-temperature strength, density, excellent wear and oxidation resistance [5 ]. The traditional alloying 
system allows one or two principal elements to dominate physical, chemical, and mechanical properties of an

Refractory alloys comprised of group VI metals, such as molybdenum (Mo) and tungsten (W), show ductile

if VEC < 4.5 and vice versa [25]. To validate the electron theory, the Hf0.5Nb0.5Ta0.5Zr RHEA, characterized as 
a single-phase BCC, low VEC (~ 4.29) and minimal lattice distortion, was developed. Hf0.5Nb0.5Ta0.5TiZr was

3.2 Atomic size difference (δ)
The large disparity in atomic size induces severe local lattice distortion, thereby influences the strengthening 
behavior of RHEAs, as given in Fig. 7, which shows a direct correlation of lattice distortion and internal stresses 
in Nb-based alloys [29]. The structural stability of RHEA also requires δ less than 8.5% [30, 31], to form single-
phase structure. The decrease in δ from 6.11 to 5.54 of single-phase Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx results in a decline in yield 
strength from 1160 to 980 MPa, respectively. The results indicate the decrease in yield strength is proportional 
to the degree of lattice distortion [32]. NbMoTaWVCr RHEA demonstrates a ultra-high compressive yield com-
pressive strength of 3416 MPa, attributed to solid solution strengthening caused from lattice distortion. The 
alloy has a δ of 4.76 [33]. Numerous other reports indicate that the large atomic size differences contribute to 
solid solution strengthening. However, the connection between atomic-scale lattice distortion and macro-scale 
mechanical properties has not been fully established through experimental verification. Only few experiments 
have been conducted to gain an in-depth understanding of the lattice distortion and yield strength, by utilizing 
synchrotron X-ray/neutron diffraction (XRD/ND), atom-probe tomography (APT), and scanning transmission 
electron microscopy (STEM) techniques [34]. For instance, the remarkable yield strength of 1518 MPa in NbTa-
TiVZr RHEA results from the significant effect of lattice distortion. The lattice distortion in NbTaTiVZr RHEA 
is validated from multiple experimental techniques using synchrotron XRD, ND and HRTEM-FFT images and 
found to be consistent with theoretical value [35].

3.4 Electronegativity (Δχ )
Δχ has been identified as a crucial factor in predicting the formation of disordered solid solution RHEAs. 
According to the Hume–Rothery principles for binary alloy systems, the formation of solid solutions is gov -
erned by low atomic size disparity and Δ χ, along with comparable entropy values. This principle has been 
extended to high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [41]. As shown in Fig.  9a, a distinct separation is observed in the plot 
between areas representing solid solutions (characterized by low lattice mismatch and low electronegativity 
difference) and intermetallic phases (with large lattice mismatch and higher electronegativity differences). 
For instance, due to low values of atomic size difference (δ ) and Δχ, single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) 
solid solutions was formed in WReTaMo RHEA [42]. The atomic size discrepancy of WReTaMo was calcu-
lated to be 1.67%, with a Δχ of 0.32. Furthermore, Ti35Zr27.5Hf27.5Nb5Ta5, RHEA was specifically designed 
to promote transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) using the Bo–Md diagram. This diagram incorporate 
the bond order (Bo), taking into account the atomic radius and χ of constituent elements [43]. In contrast, 
Fig. 9b illustrates no significant difference in Δχ between single-phase and multiphase structures, prepared 
via vacuum-arc melting. Thus, while Δχ is a useful parameter, it must be considered in conjunction with other 
empirical factors while determining the phase structure of RHEAs.

HfNbTaTiZr (2250 °C), MoNbTaW (2885 °C) and MoNbTaVW (2690 °C) RHEAs [47]. However, the general trend 
displays a modest improvement in mechanical properties with Tm.
There are two different mechanisms for high-temperature deformation: dislocation glide and diffusion control. 
The yield strength decreases more quickly with increasing temperature in the diffusion-controlled regime than in 
the glide-controlling regime. In single-phase BCC RHEAs, the transition from dislocation glide to diffusion con-
trol takes place at ~ 0.6Tm. Therefore, single-phase RHEAs with higher Tm are stronger at high temperatures. The 
strength of multi-phase RHEAs is determined by the combined effect of solid solution, precipitation, and dispersoid 
strengthening, with smaller contributions from other mechanisms. The transition to the diffusion-controlled regime 
occurs in a wide temperature range of 0.56–0.7 Tm for such RHEAs. As precipitation and dispersoid strengthening 
can be observed at temperatures as high as 0.7 Tm. The high-temperature strength of RHEAs diminishes when the 
volume fraction of secondary phases drops below 20%. Consequently, lightweight RHEAs possess relative lower Tm 
and therefore, multi-phase structures are better suited for high-temperature applications (T < Ts) compared to their 
single-phase counterparts.
Extensive research in the literature has provided valuable insights into designing the chemical composition of 
RHEAs to optimize phase structure and mechanical properties. The phase structure is strongly influenced by the 
enthalpy of mixing, while the δ parameter has been shown to significantly affect the mechanical strength of the alloy. 
Additionally, valence electron concentration within a specific range has demonstrated improvements in room tem-
perature ductility. Further details of RHEAs empirical parameters and resultant phase structure are givenin Table 1.

4.1 First‑principles calculations
Elasticity is a fundamental property directly related to an alloy mechanical behavior and serve as a key indica-
tor in screening RHEA compositions. Due to challenges in developing uniform, defect-free crystals, to measure 
elastic constants, information on elastic properties is available only for a limited portion of materials. First-
principles calculation is an efficient method to accurately calculate these parameters [48]. For instance, the 
elastic properties were studied in an effort to enhance ductility and reduce density of the VMoNbTaW RHEA, 
[49] by a Monte Carlo simulated maximum entropy structures. This work concludes that a high concentration 
of Mo + W contribute to high elastic constant (C44), bulk modulus (B), elastic modulus (E), and shear modulus 
(G) values and suggesting W replacement by Mo due to its relative low density. Further V is critical in improv -
ing ductility of VMoNbTaW RHEA. TiZrNbHf RHEA has gained researchers attention due to its excellent 
phase stability, in cold rolled and after heat treatment. DFT calculations have been performed on TiZrNbHf 
alloys to systematically evaluate electronic structure, lattice distortion and mechanical properties [50]. The 
elastic constants (C11, C12 and C44) were computed by DFT that serve as an indicator of mechanical stability 
and preferred deformation mode. In BCC lattices, C11 denotes uniaxial deformation, whereas C12 and C44 
denote crystal shear deformation. In case of TiZrNbHf RHEA, C11 (156 GPa) is notably greater than C12 
(97 GPa) and C44 (45 GPa), indicating that shear deformation is more likely to occur. The revealing of lattice 
distortion in RHEAs by experimental methods remains challenging. TiZrNbHf RHEA DFT calculations shows 
that the relative position between atoms changes, when the unit cell is relaxed and unrelaxed. The Zr and Hf 
interatomic distance is found significantly larger than that of other pairs as shown in Fig.  12, and have a great 
influence on the lattice distortion of TiZrNbHf alloy. The structural stability is related to bond strength in an

4.2 CALPHAD modeling
CALPHAD is state-of-the-art alloy design tool due to its ability to accurately extrapolate Gibbs energies [53]. 
This makes it particularly valuable in the study of RHEAs, where the compositional complexity makes experi-
mental investigation and theoretical calculations, such as density functional theory (DFT), expensive and time-
consuming. The CALPHAD can predict thermodynamic and physical properties, such as RHEAs density and 
liquidus temperature, providing a computational efficient alternative to first principles calculations [54]. Study 
have proved that RHEA density calculated using CALPHAD and ROM technique are in good agreement. How-
ever, the liquidus temperatures calculated using ROM deviate from CALPHAD values. One of the CALPHAD 
core strength lies in its ability to predict phase stability as a function of temperature. For instance, the instability 
in TiZrHfNbTa RHEAs phase is observed from 600 to 1000 °C. The phase stability is assessed and improved 
using CALPHAD approach [55]. The study revealed that Hf and Ta are sensitive to the RHEA phase stability. As 
a result, a Ti30Zr30Hf16Nb24 RHEA, depleted in Hf- and Ta, was designed with outstanding phase stability, and 
remained a single-phase BCC structure after heat treatment at 600 °C for prolong duration (200 h). The synergy 
between CALPHAD and DFT, further enhances alloy design capabilities. For instance, during the WMoNbVCrₓ 
RHEAs design, spin-polarized DFT (SP-DFT) was applied to calculate lattice parameters, elastic moduli, and 
thermodynamic parameters of proposed single-phase BCC structures, using CALPHAD model. The computa-
tionally designed alloys are experimentally validated proving the effectiveness of integrated CALPHAD and 
DFT approach [56]. The CALPHAD is also integrated with machine learning to address complex RHEAs design 
problems. Since there is limited knowledge on RHEAs dual BCC/FCC-phase structures. An initial screening 
is performed using, and the CALPHAD developed and existing binary alloy phase diagrams. 504 BCC/FCC-
phase RHEAs are identified. Machine learning, predict that 52 of them exhibit a BCC/FCC-phase structure. The 
predicted results were experimentally validated through phase structure [ 57]. The CALPHAD is exceptionally 
useful in RHEAs chemical composition screening, predicting phase stability, and developing specific phases for 
high mechanical strength after integration with DFT and machine learning.
5 Processing technologies, microstructure and mechanical behavior
5.1 Preparation methods
The method of alloy preparation has a significant impact on mechanical properties of RHEA. The large differences 
in melting temperature and the electronegativity of the constituent elements causes macro-segregation, which 
complicates the alloy’s fabrication [58, 59]. Vacuum arc melting is the earliest and most prevalent manufacturing 
technique being used in research and industry [60]. In vacuum-arc melting, segregation, heterogeneous micro-
structure, and porosity are difficult to control. Therefore, multiple alloying techniques that includes induction 
melting, selective laser melting, hot isostatic pressing (HIP), and spark plasma sintering (SPS) are introduced 
to overcome the casting problems [61, 62]. Arc-melted NbMoTaW RHEA features large grains with a diameter 
of approximately 200 µm and contains pores. This alloy has a hardness of 446 Hv. In contrast, the NbMoTaW 
RHEA produced by selective laser melting (SLM) exhibits a higher hardness of 826 Hv and has an average 
particle size of 13.0 µm [63]. Using in situ sequential laser metal deposition (LMD) homogeneous mixture of

5.3 Effect of heat treatments
Various heat treatment processes, such as homogenization, annealing, and aging, are employed to modify the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of alloys. Homogenization is a heat treatment method used to elimi-

compressive engineering properties of Hf₀.₅MoNbTaW and HfMoNbTaW RHEAs were investigated in their as-
cast and as-homogenized states [21]. Notably, HfMoNbTaW exhibited the most significant improvement in yield 
strength after homogenization, increasing from 430 to 571 MPa at 1600 °C. This enhancement is attributed to the 
elimination of Hf-precipitates and other microstructural inhomogeneities, resulting in improved solid solution 
strengthening. The heat treatment also reduces solid solution induced strengthening effect after precipitation of 
secondary phases. For instance, after annealing, the yield strength of NbMo14Ti18 and NbMo14Ti18Fe2 RHEAs 
decreases by 11–16% and 5–13%, respectively, after precipitation of Ti-rich particles and a minor increase in 
grain size [87]. Instead in most studies, secondary precipitate area is characterized as a barrier to deformation 
and enhances the yield strength. Likewise, a study compared the microstructure, and mechanical properties 
of as-cast and annealed Al0.5CrNbTi2V0.5 RHEA [88]. The XRD pattern suggest the nucleation of C14 Laves

interconnected with longer annealing times. Whereas, the yield strength increases by 57% respectively, after

5.4 Effect of microstructure
Microstructure is an alloy extrinsic character effecting mechanical properties The combination of process meth-

the featuring microstructures produced through elemental addition/modification. Nb addition into Ti2ZrHf0.5V

5.5 Effect of Si addition
The introduction of Si has shown a substantial reduction in density and enhanced mechanical properties. 
Concurrently, Si has lower ∆H mix with refractory elements, which facilitate the formation of multi-phase 
structures. For instance, (VNbTiTa) 100-x Six where (x = 0, 2.5, 5, 10) RHEAs is studied for microstructure 
evolution and mechanical properties at temperature up to 1200 °C. The Si addition has caused phase trans-
formation from single-phase BCC to BCC/eutectic structure and later, M5Si3 precipitates are formed in the 
eutectic regions [94]. Consequently, the RHEA experiences a remarkable enhancement in yield strength from 
~ 720 MPa to ~ 1671 MPa, while its plasticity decreases from approximately 50% to about 11%. However, 
(VNbTiTa)97.5Si2.5 RHEA provided best combination of yield strength and ductility up to 1000 °C. The yield 
strengths are 1023 MPa and 505 MPa at room temperature and 1000 °C, respectively with ductility exceed 
30% of strain. In another study, a low density (~ 6 g  cm−3) VNbTiTaSi RHEA composed of BCC/M 5Si3 
eutectic structure [95], report an extraordinary compressive yield strength of 2400 MPa at room tempera-
ture. Eutectic structure is also reported in AlMo0.5NbTiVSix, along with M5Si3 intermetallic, which result in 
improvement in yield strength to 390 MPa, and 442 MPa at 800 °C and 1000 °C, respectively [96]. Incor -
porating Si in RHEAs create a certain volume fraction of eutectic structure and M5Si3 intermetallic, which 
increases the number of phase interfaces. This hinders dislocation movement and enhances high-temperature 
strength [97]. Nevertheless, an excessive presence of eutectic and M5Si3 phase results in decreased plasticity. 
The eutectic disappears after cold rolling followed by recrystallized heat treatment of Ti50V20Al10Nb(20-x)Six 
RHEA, leading to the formation of nanoscale M5Si3 phase, distributed along grain boundaries. This enhances 
strain-hardening capability and fracture elongation of RHEA [ 98]. Subsequently, the recrystallized RHEA 
containing 1.0 atomic % Si exhibits favorable tensile yield strength of 916 MPa and fracture elongation of 
22.8%. Further details on temperature-dependent mechanical performance of RHEAs are given in Table  4. 
Hence, the thermomechanical treatment has successfully evaded strength ductility tradeoff in in Si-doped 
RHEAs through re-engineering of grain morphology, and open new research horizon in developing Si-doped 
RHEAs for high-temperature applications.
5.6 Effect of C addition
The addition of carbon in create a synergistic strengthening effect between the BCC matrix and the carbide 
precipitates, significantly enhancing its mechanical properties. For instance, the MoNbW(TaC) 0.5 RHEA 
demonstrated an impressive yield strength of 800 MPa at 1200 °C [99], further improved in NbTaW0.4C0.25 
RHEA [100] to 825 MPa at 1450 °C, with 12.4% fracture strain at room temperature, higher than those of 
NbMoTaW RHEA [61]. However, NbTaW0.4C0.25 RHEA has low high-temperature softening resistance. 
To consider NbTaW0.4C0.25 RHEA for practical applications, the improvement in high-temperature soften-
ing resistance is certainly important. A newly developed Re0.1Hf0.25NbTaW0.4C0.25 RHEA [101] created by 
incorporating Re and Hf elements, exhibit yield strength of 1026 MPa at 1450 °C and 523 MPa at 1700 °C. 
Additionally, it demonstrated a room temperature fracture strain of 10.2%. The microstructure analyses reveal 
three-dimensional, continuous, carbide structure, acting as a load-bearing component, and ductility provided 
by matrix phase. The M2C phase can significantly influences the high-temperature strength. Accordingly, 
 Re0.1Ta1.9W0.2C0.25 RHEA, derived from Re0.1Ta1.9W0.2, is prepared [102]. At 1450 °C, Re0.1Ta1.9W0.2C0.25 
RHEA high yield strength (~ 710 MPa) represents 105.8%, increases from Re0.1Ta1.9W0.2 RHEA. Along with 
the eutectic structure consisting of Ta2C and BCC, fine, plate-like precipitates are observed within the BCC 
matrix phase. Plate-like Ta2C morphology effectively boost the high-temperature strength without compromis -
ing its compression plasticity. Table  5 illustrates the mechanical properties of C containing RHEAs performed 
in recent past. To sum up, the carbide containing RHEAs has successfully achieved excellent high-temperature 
mechanical strength, but a focus on enhancing ductility and tensile properties needs more attention.

6 Plasticity in RHEAs
6.1 Compressive plasticity RHEAs
For an alloy to be suitable for engineering applications, it must have good plasticity. Recent reports indicated 
a number of RHEAs demonstrating impressive compressive ductility and strength, as given in Table  6. Ti 
addition has increased VNbMoTaW RHEA room temperature ductility from 1.7% to 16% and maintained 
yield strength of 1515 MPa [ 103]. A single-phase BCC TaNbHfZrTi RHEA is developed with substantial 
enhancements in room temperature ductility (> 50%) and yield strength (1597 MPa) [76]. The TiZrNbMo3V3 
and AlNbTiVZr0.5 RHEAs have shown high yield strength of 1312 MP and 1430 MPa, respectively, without 
compromising the plastic strain (50%) [104]. Various single and multi-phase RHEAs have successfully demon-
strated high strength and ductility, but they disadvantaged from high density. The C containing RHEAs suffer 
from similar issue, as given in Table  5. On other hand, some Si containing light-weight RHEAs are developed, 
but the compressive ductility remains below 40%. Recently, a novel low density (~ 6.9 g  cm−3) chemically 
complex Al12Nb25.5Ta12Ti27.5Zr25.5 intermetallic, characterized as single-phase ordered B2 structure exhibited 
extra ordinary yield strength (1398 MPa) and compressive strain (> 50). The ordered B2 structure provided 
high strength, while ductility arose from the formation of delocalized dislocation bands, due to high-entropy 
effect. Although, the RHEA has manifested great strength–ductility synergy at room temperature, but, losses 
strength after 600 °C. Even though, it is clear from this work that complex refractory intermetallic alloys 
provide better mechanical properties than disordered solid solutions [105].

6.2.2 Spinodal decomposition
The spinodal decomposed modulated structure benefits in hindering dislocation motion, causing dislocations 
to decelerate, disperse, and interact extensively, significantly enhancing strain hardening and tensile ductility. 
Spinodal decomposition causes single-phase BCC structure to produce coherently interfaced two BCC networks 
with distinct compositions and lattice strains. HfNbTiV RHEA is first developed RHEA with characteristics of 
spinodal decomposed structure, due to the miscibility gap originated from large atomic size difference [34]. This 
results in high yield strength (1100 MPa) and tensile strain (28%) to failure. In another study, spinodal decomposed 
(TiZrNbTa)98.5Al1.5 RHEA display a typical basket-weave fabric morphology, showing an excellent strength-
ductility synergy with a yield strength of 1123 MPa and ductility (∼ 21%) [106].
6.2.3 Phase transformation‑induced plasticity (TRIP)
Phase metastability has been introduced to increase the tensile ductility of BCC RHEAs. A BCC phase has been 
intentionally destabilized leading to the formation of HCP phase in a strain-localization region. A phase stability 
index “Bo–Md diagram” was originally used to gauge the stability of β-phase in titanium alloys. This has been 
successfully applied to activate stress-induced martensitic transformation in Ti35Zr27.5Hf27.5Nb5Ta5 RHEA. High 
strain-hardening capability and tensile ductility were successfully achieved by modulating the stability of the 
single-phase BCC solid solution. Inside the metastable BCC matrix, twinned stress-induced martensite phase 
resulted in strain hardening and retarded strain localization. The UTS and elongation of 995 MPa and 23% were 
achieved, respectively [43]. Ta, a BCC stabilizer element, was gradually reduced in TaxHfZrTi RHEA, which 
led to an interleaved HCP and BCC dual-phase structure. Phase transformation enabled dynamic stress–strain 
partitioning between BCC and HCP structures and induced vigorous plastic deformation within the grain. This 
suppressed premature cracking and produce intense strain hardening and ductility [107]. The Ta0.4HfZrTi alloy 
demonstrated the maximum tensile strain (30.6%) among RHEAs, with UTS 1126 MPa. The TRIP proved to be 
superior approach in developing ductile RHEA; however, the yield strength and UTS lags behind in performance 
compared to RHEAs with admirable tensile strain, as given in Table 6.
6.2.4 Ordered interstitial complexes
A novel strain-hardening effect is introduced using ordered interstitial complexes. The nanoscale clusters are 
enriched in atomic complexes of O, Zr, Ti, driven by short-range substitutional ordering in the surrounding lattice. 
As a result, a strong strengthening effect is observed for the O-doped RHEA [108]. The yield strength increases 
from 750 MPa for the base TiZrHfNb RHEA to 1110 MPa for (TiZrHfNb)98O2. Meanwhile the elongation almost 
doubled from 14.2% to 27.7%, respectively. The impressive improvement in mechanical response is associated 
with the change in shear deformation mechanism from planar to wavy slip, which facilitates double cross-slip, 
as a result generates additional dislocations through the formation of Frank–Read sources [109]. Despite that, 
the (TiZrHfNb)98O2 RHEA is unsuitable for high-temperature applications due to its susceptibility to oxidation. 
It is suggested that incorporating oxidation resistant elements, such as Al, Si and Cr, could improve the EHEA 
oxidation resistance. The design strategies discussed here have direct implications for real-world applications, 
including high-temperature aerospace components, nuclear reactor materials, and wear-resistant coatings, where 
phase stability and mechanical robustness are crucial [66].
Based on the unique structural characteristics and mechanical properties of RHEAs, there are broad appli-
cation prospects in future fields where strength and ductility are critical. It includes areas such as aerospace, 
nuclear energy, gas turbines, petrochemical metallurgy, concentrated solar power systems (CSP), mechanical 
manufacturing and biomedical. In specific, the RHEAs excellent high-temperature mechanical properties 
may benefit in new-generation aircraft engines components and gas turbine blades. For examples, VNbCrMo 
demonstrates high yield strength (1000 MPa) at 1000 °C. Re and C contained RHEAs outstands super alloys 
in high-temperature mechanical stability. For example, Re0.1Hf0.25NbTaW0.4C0.25 alloy [101] exhibit excellent

7 Summary and key challenges
This review highlights the potential of RHEAs as next-generation structural materials, offering superior 
mechanical properties compared to conventional superalloys. Through phase prediction based on empiri -
cal thermodynamic parameters, such as valence electron concentration, enthalpy of mixture, and atomic 
size difference, many novel RHEAs have been developed. Single-phase body-cantered cubic RHEAs with 
intrinsic ductility can be achieved within specific parameter ranges, including VEC values between 4.3 and 
4.6, δ < 8.5%, and ΔHmix > − 15 kJ  mol−1. Additionally, the melting temperature (T m) significantly influences 
diffusion-controlled deformation, typically occurring at 0.6T m, providing RHEAs an advantage over traditional 
refractory alloys. Over time, the primary single-phase BCC structures of RHEAs have evolved into mixed-
phase systems comprising dual-phase and intermetallic structures. Fabrication techniques such as arc melting, 
mechanical alloying, and additive manufacturing remain central to their production, with powder metallurgy 
demonstrating notable improvements in grain refinement and mechanical strength, including nearly doubling 
the yield strength compared to VAM-prepared alloys. Despite substantial progress, several key challenges 
hinder the practical implementation of RHEAs. Theories regarding the strengthening mechanisms, particu-
larly the evolution of microstructures under high-temperature conditions, remain under debate. Balancing 
high strength with sufficient plasticity, especially under tension, continues to be a major obstacle. Critical 
performance metrics, including dynamic mechanical properties, fatigue resistance, and creep behavior, are yet 
to be fully explored. Furthermore, understanding and optimizing corrosion, oxidation, irradiation, and wear 
resistance are essential for their industrial adoption. Addressing these challenges through focused research and 
innovative solutions will pave the way for the broader application of RHEAs in advanced engineering systems.
