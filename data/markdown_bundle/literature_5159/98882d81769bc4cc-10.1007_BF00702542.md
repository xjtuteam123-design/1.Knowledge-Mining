INFLUENCE OF NONFERROUS METALS IMPURITIES ON THE STRUCTURE 
AND HIGH TEMPERATURE STRENGTH OF NICKEL BASED FOUNDRY ALLOYS 
V. V. Sidorov and A. M. Kulebyakina UDC 620.17:620.18:669.245'26'25'27'28'4'76 
The influence of nonferrous metal impurities (Pb, Bi, and so on) on the structure 
formation and high temperature strength properties has been examined for nickel 
based foundry alloys produced by equiaxial and controlled casting procedures. It 
is shown that an alloy with an equiaxial structure is more sensitive to impurities 
than is an alloy that is cast with a columnar structure, and that this is related 
to its structural formation characteristics. The adverse influence of impurities 
lon the structure formation during the casting of eutectic alloys has been determined. 
Nonferrous metal impurities (Pb, Bi, etc.) have an adverse effect on the properties 
of nickel based high temperature strength alloys. 
In the current work we have examined the influence of individual nonferrous metal im- 
purities on the structure and properties of Ni alloys with 5% Cr, 9% Co, 12% W and 1% Mo, 
and which were alloyed with C, Ni, Ti, and AI. The alloys had an equiaxial or controlled 
structure which was produced by cooling the melts at various rates.

Analyzing these results shows that the equiaxial structure alloy is more sensitive to 
contamination with the lead impurity during the creep process than is the case for the con- 
trolled structure alloy. When specimens cut from the metal with a controlled structure 
in the transverse direction were tested, the durability reduction for the metal with the 
lead impurity was more obvious than was the case for specimens cut in the longitudinal direc- 
tion. Moreover, as the directional crystallization velocity increased from 4 to 20 mm/min, 
the durability reduction rate for metal with the lead impurity decreased. 
It should be noted that the lead addition significantly reduces the long-term metal 
ductility for all the melting options that were examined. However, while the equiaxially 
structured metal has a 6 value of <3%, the relative elongation for the metal with a con- 
trolled structure meets the structural strength requirements (6 > 3%), although the rela- 
tive elongation is reduced. 
It is accepted that nonferrous metal impurities, and lead in particular, are virtually 
insoluble in the nickel matrix of high temperature strength alloys. In addition, as a con- 
sequence of their reduced activity, chemical compounds are not formed with the alloy com- 
ponents and, therefore, the impurities are present in the free condition in the metal. This 
confirms the research results obtained using Auger electron spectroscopy [i], where it was 
shown that the lead is not present in the form of discrete compounds in the nickel alloy, 
but in the virtually free lead condition. It can be assumed that during the controlled 
hardening process, part of the lead is forced by the growing crystals into the melt, as 
was shown in Fig. i. In this context, the residual lead content in the metal with a con- 
trolled structure was somewhat lower than was the case for th~ metal with an equiaxial 
structure. In addition, when the melt was subjected to lengthy controlled hardening under 
vacuum conditions, the lead could be partially volatilized, and this has been confirmed 
by the analytical results for the metal. When 0.001% Pb was added to the alloy~ the residual 
concentration was as follows: 0.0015% in the metal with an equiaxial structure, and 0.0006

respectively. It should be noted that in every case examined, the lead concentration in the 
upper portion of the plate was somewhat higher than in the lower portion.

After the long-term tests had been carried out on the metal specimens with equiaxial and 
controlled structures, to which the maximum lead concentration (0.001% Pb by calculation) 
had been added, the structure close to the failure zone was investigated. 
Pridantsev [2] believes that the formation and development of breakdown sites in 
creep conditions is related to the development of single pores, which then form chains. 
Avalanche metal failure commences at the point when a specific number of pores form at the 
grain boundaries, and which then blend to form cracks. 
It has been established that the nature of specimen failure is dependent on the melt 
crystallization conditions. Cracking develops incastings with an equiaxial structure under 
creepconditions,: and propagates along the grain boundaries which are located in a transverse 
direction relative to the applied load direction (Fig. 3a). 
There are no grains formed during the controlled crystallization of transverse boundaries, 
hence cracking takes place at the carbides and propagates through these grains (Fig. 3b). 
Presumably, in this situation, the transverse cracking would propagate, during the controlled 
solidification, through the elongated dendritic structure, the particle size of which in- 
creases as the crystallization rate increases (Fig. 3c, d). However, we have established 
that the cracking induced in metal cast with a controlled structure does not develop in 
the transverse direction, because in this case there are no transverse boundaries, 
and crack propagation is restrained by the elongated dendritic axes along the 
crystallization plane (Fig. 3c, d). Thus, during the creep processes in metal 
with a controlled structure, a specific number of short length pores are formed, 
these being only slightly elongated in the transverse direction, although not 
coalescing together (Fig. 3b). In this context, the reduction in the durability of 
metal containing lead as an impurity and having a controlled structure is less than for 
metal with an equiaxial structure. 
In addition, it is also necessary to take into account the internal stresses that arise 
in the material during production and which may lead to localized grain boundary failure 
[3]. Alloys having an equiaxial structure have a lower ductility level than alloys with 
controlled structures. This prevents stress relaxation due to matrix flow, and causes the 
stresses to be concentrated close to the grain boundaries. In this context, any grain 
boundary weakening (due to pore formation, for example) develops to a greater extent in 
alloys with an equiaxial structure. 
Moreover, the extent of the grain boundaries in controlled structure alloys is signifi~ 
cantly smaller, all these boundaries being elongated in the load application direction, 
which will also reduce the probability of forming localized grain boundary defects [4]. 
As a result, impurities in metal with a controlled structure will not have such an adverse 
effect on the durability of the alloys as is the position with equiaxial structures, and 
experimentalidata from durability and structure assessments on the alloys (Figs. 2 and 3) 
confirm this. 
Impurities have a particularly adverse affect during unidirectional solidification of 
nickel alloys with a y + y' + MC eutectic structure.

It is recognized that in order to achieve unidirectional growth of the eutectic phases, 
it is necessary to establish and stably maintain a plain crystallization front, i.e. having 
plane isotherms close to the crystallization temperature. Thus, an orientated heterophase 
fibrous, lamellar or more complex structural type may be formed. 
An important factor that typifies the capacity of eutectic alloys for forming a com- 
posite structure, is the GL/R ratio, where G L is the temperature gradient at the crystalliza- 
tion front and R is the growth rate. For each alloy there is a specific GL/R value, above 
which a stable plane crystallization front exists, and below which, the front becomes cellular 
or dendritic. Thus, the critical GL/R ratio determines the conditions for controlled crystal 
growth: a severe temperature gradient G L at the crystallization front and a low growth rate 
R are important for limiting the impurity concentration. 
If the impurity concentration in the solid phase is less than in the melt from which 
this solid phase is formed, then the impurity will accumulate in the melt forward of the 
crystallization front. Since the time for uniformly distributing the impurity over the 
remaining melt volume is inadequate, a concentration gradient is formed and maintained for- 
ward of the crystallization front during the crystallization process, and this produces 
the so-called concentrational melt supercooling [5]. 
If the crystals grow without any melt supercooling, the plane crystallization front 
is maintained. If, as a result of the increased impurity concentration in the metal, con- 
centrational supercooling takes place, the crystallization front forms a cellular struc- 
ture. With any further increase in the concentrational supercooling level, the cellular 
structure transforms to the dendritic condition, such that greater GL/R values are re- , 
quired to maintain the plane front stability as the impurity concentration increases [6]. 
In order to determine the conditions for forming structure defects during controlled 
crystallization, experimental heats of the eutectic alloy were produced with nonferrous 
metal impurity additions (as calculated): 0.00050, 0.00075, and 0.0010% Pb plus 0.00030, 
0.00045, and 0.00060% Bi. The eutectic alloy from one original heat was remelted in stages 
in a batch furnace with a prepared charge that contained a specific impurity amount in each 
case. The ingots obtained were used to cast specimens with a controlled structure. It 
was established from macrostructure investigations (Fig. 4) that the metal without any 
addition had a unidirectional elongated composite zone. The crystallization front was con- 
trolled by the presence of impurities in the metal: all the heats having Pb and Bi impuri- 
ties had a cellular crystallization front. This is related to concentrational supercooling 
developing forward of the crystallization front due to impurity accumulation in that area 
and movement ceasing at some point in time. It should be noted that the adverse effects 
due to lead and bismuth on the crystallization front formation are apparent even at a concen- 
tration as low as 0.0001% and above. This, is probably related to the virtual total in- 
solubility of Pb and Bi in the nickel melt.

The formation of a cellular structure in an eutectic alloy that contains an impurity 
markedly reduces the durability during long-term strength testing at t = II00°C: the time 
to failure for metal with a lead or bismuth addition is five or more times lower than for 
metal with no such addition and which crystallizes with a plane front (see Table i). 
It has been shown (Fig. 2) that the lead impurity has a more adverse effect on the 
relative durability of the eutectic alloy than is the case for other alloys that were ex- 
amined, even those with an equiaxial structure. This is related to the fact that when nickel 
based alloys that have a wide crystallization temperature range (=80-120°C) solidify, a 
dendritic structure is formed that is unaltered when an impurity, for example lead, is added 
in the above given quantities. In this context, lead only affects the grain boundaries 
condition. Depending on the lead concentration at the boundaries and on the location of 
these boundaries in relation to the applied load, the properties of a given alloy will also 
change. 
Eutectic alloys have a narrow (=I0°C) crystallization range and they crystallize with 
a plane front. In this situation, the lead which promotes concentrational supercooling 
causes stability failure in the plane front and the formation of a cellular structure. Since 
the basic contribution to the strength of eutectic alloys is via the unidirectional carbide 
fibers, the breakdown of such a structure when cells are formed causes a severe drop in 
the alloys' durability, i 
Conclusions. i. Nickel based alloys of the Ni plus 5% Cr plus 9% Co plus 12% W plus 
1% Mo type with an equiaxial structure are more sensitive to nonferrous metal impurities 
than are alloys that are cast with a controlled structure. This effect is due to the struc- 
ture formation characteristics for these alloys. 
The most adverse effect is that due to impurities on the formation of a composite struc- 
ture when the eutectic alloys are cast: the plane crystallization front loses stability 
due to concentrational supercooling and a cellular structure is formed, which results in 
a significant fall in the durability.
