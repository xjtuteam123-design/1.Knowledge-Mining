ABSTRACT
We demonstrate that based on NbTa as the base solution, ternary equiatomic HfNbTa and TiNbTa single-phase
body-centered-cubic (BCC) solid solutions are achieved at arc-melted alloy ingots. These add interesting new
members into the group of ternary refractory medium-entropy alloys (RMEAs), previously available only in
NbTiZr. Moreover, HfNbTa and TiNbTa are the ﬁrst two ternary RMEAs that exhibit tensile ductility, with
HfNbTa having a combination of yield strength and tensile strain on par with its high-entropy counterpart, the
Senkov alloy TiZrHfNbTa. We also show that alloying Hf into the base NbTa solution manifests a much higher
strengthening potency compared with Ti. This di ﬀerence can be rationalized using empirical parameters and
models accounting for the size and modulus mis ﬁts among constituent elements.
1. Introduction
With multiple principal elements, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) o ﬀer
opportunities to discover interesting alloys in an extended composi-
tional space [ 1–4]. Among such complex concentrated alloys (CCAs), a
family of them are composed of ﬁve or more refractory metals in equal
proportion, including titanium, zirconium, hafnium, vanadium, nio-
bium, tantalum, molybdenum and tungsten, etc. [ 5–11]. These are
grouped as the refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), which are
single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) solid solutions. These RHEAs
exhibit a high yield strength ( σ
y) in the range of 900 –1600 MPa at room
temperature (RT). RHEAs are currently of much interest, because they
may o ﬀer su ﬃciently high strength at elevated temperatures to rival
superalloys [ 12], and are potentially useful for biomedical implants
[13–17].
A sore spot with the RHEAs reported so far is that at RT they usually
are ductile only in compression. Tensile ductility is more important for
applications bearing high loads and for forming and shaping operations,
but is unfortunately rarely available from RHEAs. In this regard, the
RHEAs in the Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta system stand out, as they are the only ones
showing appreciable tensile ductility [ 18–20]. The as-cast BCC equia-
tomic TiZrHfNbTa alloy shows a σ
y of 820–835 MPa [18] (which can be
further elevated via subsequent cold-rolling and annealing [ 19]), to-
gether with a tensile elongation to failure of ∼9%. This level of RT
ductility is acceptable for certain applications. Our work presented
below therefore focuses on alloys in the Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta sub-family of
RHEAs.
Rather than dealing with all combinations in this sub-family, our
next step is to further narrow down the scope of our study to a subset:
ternary medium-entropy alloys (MEAs), i.e., refractory MEAs composed
of three elements out of Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta. Ternary MEAs are interesting
for at least the following four reasons. First, reducing the number of
principal elements makes the system simpler but does not necessarily
sacriﬁce mechanical properties. For example, the quaternary TiZrHfNb
medium-entropy alloy (MEA) in its as-annealed state exhibits a σy of
879 MPa and an elongation of ∼15% [ 21], a combination comparable
to its higher-order TiZrHfNbTa quinary alloy. In other words, the ab-
sence of Ta did not adversely a ﬀect tensile properties. Similar situation
has also been noticed in the face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs: in the
family based on FeCoCrNiMn (the Cantor alloy) [ 22], it turns out that
the ternary CoCrNi MEA presents the highest σ
y, ductility and fracture
toughness [23], better than quaternary FeNiCoCr or NiCoCrMn, and the
quinary Cantor alloy. As such, the tensile properties of MEA/HEA are
not simply determined by the number of components or simply an al-
gebraic interpolation of constituents. The stremlined ternary MEA can
even bear representative properties typical of a family of HEAs. Second,
the smaller number of constituent elements makes it easier to under-
stand the thermodynamics and phase diagrams in the system, and more
amenable to modeling research on mechanisms in general. Third, the
optimization of strength and ductility, particularly when at high

Then, which three elements should we choose among Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-
Ta for our pursuit of ternary RMEAs? We next remove Zr from the mix,
leaving the selection pool to be Ti-Hf-Nb-Ta. The reason not to involve
Zr is that, in the presence of Nb-Ta, previous studies have already found
Zr [24] to dramatically reduce the tensile ductility of NbTa. The loss in
ductility in fact is related to the drastic rise of the ductile to brittle
transition temperature ( T
BDT) when the alloying element Zr is over-
concentrated in pure Nb [ 15,25].
Besides, an interesting perspective is that Ti-Hf-Nb-Ta o ﬀers an
opportunity to examine the strengthening potency of a “solute”,H f
versus Ti, in the base binary BCC Nb-Ta “solvent”. This perspective
arises from the realization that due to the similar atomic size
(r
Ta = 143.0 pm, and rNb = 142.9 pm) between BCC Nb and Ta [ 26],
the two elements form continuous solid solution in the phase diagram,
such that the equiatomic NbTa solid soluiton can be assumed to be a
psudo solvent. One can then contrast the e ﬀects of adding another
component, i.e., solute Ti (i.e., 33%Ti in the NbTa base solution) versus
Hf on the mechanical properties. This line of thoughts greatly simpli ﬁes
our route towards ternary RMEAs: Hf combined with NbTa to produce
HfNbTa, versus Ti onto NbTa to arrive at TiNbTa, as well as a com-
parison of the two scenarios.
In the experiments the follow, we use arc-melting to produce
equiatomic alloys, from the NbTa binary to the ternary TiNbTa,
HfNbTa. Both Ti and Hf are also added to form (TiHf)NbTa, and this
quaternary alloy will be mentioned only in passing and included in our
comparison. Our focus is on ternary RMEAs. First, the microstructures
will be studied to observe if single-phase BCC RMEAs can be obtained,
and compared with the phases predicted by thermodynamic phase
diagram calculations (CALPHD). Second, standard tensile tests of these
alloys will be used to gauge the tensile strength and ductility of the
RMEAs achieved, and compare the strengthening potency of Hf versus
Ti. Third, the potency of solution strengthening due to Ti or Hf will be
semi-quantitatively analyzed using an empirical parameter and a pre-
viously postulated model. Fourth and ﬁnally, the failure mechanism of
the eventual tensile fracture will be compared for the RMEAs.
2. Experimental
Commercial elemental bulk materials with purity higher than 99.9%
(in weight percentage) were used as starting materials for alloy ingot
fabrication. A series of alloys with equi-atomic composition of NbTa,
TiNbTa, HfNbTa and TiHfNbTa were processed using arc melting. The
arc-melted ingots in weight of about 50 g were fabricated under a Ti-
gettered argon atmosphere in a water-cooled copper hearth. They are
subjected to several remelting and ﬂipping runs to ensure composi-
tional homogeneity. The e ﬀective cooling rate during the solidi ﬁcation
of the ingot is on the order of 1 K/s.
Crystalline phases in the as-cast alloys were identi ﬁed using x-ray
diﬀraction (XRD), on a Rigaku D/max 2500 di ﬀractometer (Rigaku,
Tokyo, Japan) with monochromatic Cu K
α radiation. Step-scanning
mode was used for scanning within a range of relevant di ﬀraction angle
(2θ) to ensure the accuracy of determined position of di ﬀraction lines.
Lattice parameters of crystalline phases with BCC structure were de-
termined by ﬁtting the whole di ﬀraction line pro ﬁles using pseudo-
Voigt function. As-cast microstructures of the alloys were characterized
with a Quanta 600 scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI,
Eindhoven, the Netherlands) and electron backscatter di ﬀraction
(EBSD) attached on Supra 55 SEM (Zeiss, Germany). Chemical com-
position and elemental mapping on cross-section surfaces of the as-cast
alloys were analyzed using an EDX detector attached on SEM.
Dog-bone shaped tension samples were taken from the central
portion of the arc-melted button-like ingots, with a gauge dimension of
8 mm in length, 2.5 mm in width and 1 mm in thickness. Uniaxial
tensile tests were conducted at RT on a 2 kN Instron 5848 micro-
mechanical testing machine (Instron, Norwood, MA) using a laser ex-
tensometer. A constant strain rate of 5 × 10
−4 s−1 was used, and at
least four specimens were measured for each alloy. Fracture surfaces of
specimens after tensile failure were examined on Quanta 600 SEM.

3.2. Comparison with CALPHAD-predicted phases
To predict the equilibrium phases in the investigated alloys, phase
diagrams are calculated using CALPHAD (commercial software
Thermo-Calc and TTNI8 database, Version 8.3). Fig. 6 (a)-(d) display
the predicted relative mass fraction (wt.%) of phases as a function of
temperature for the four alloys. As shown in Fig. 6 (a), the NbTa binary
in solid state forms a single BCC phase at all temperatures; this is indeed
the only phase observed as shown in Section 3.1. In the TiNbTa ternary,
a single BCC phase is stable at temperature > 800 K, decomposing into
two solid phases (BCC and HCP) occurs at lower temperatures, as
shown in Fig. 6(b). The mass fraction of the two phases is approxi-
mately 85 wt% and 15 wt% at 500 K, respectively. Similarly, for the
HfNbTa ternary, the high-temperature BCC phase decomposes into
another BCC and HCP phases at ∼1400 K, as shown in Fig. 6 (c). The
mass fraction of the two co-existing phases is ∼60 wt% and ∼40 wt%
at 500 K, respectively. As for the TiHfNbTa quaternary, the decom-
position of solid phase occurs at ∼1100 K, as shown in Fig. 6(d), with
the BCC and the HCP accounting for 55 wt% and 45 wt% at 500 K, re-
spectively.
The predicted HCP phases have not been detected within XRD re-
solution limit (approximately less than 5 vol%). Only a single BCC
phase is found, as shown in Fig. 1. This is because the RMEAs obtained
via arc-melting are actually away from thermodynamic equilibrium due
to the fast cooling rate (the order of magnitude is ∼1 K/s) from the
melts. As such, the formation of HCP phase is suppressed, owing to the
lack of su ﬃcient time for atomic di ﬀusion required by the solid phase
transformation (i.e. phase separation). As such, the current single-phase
BCC structures are metastable. The phase formation in fully-annealed
state for these alloys is a subject for future work.

3.4. Fractography after failure in tension
Fig. 9(a) and (b) show SEM images of fracture surface after tensile
failure for the NbTa binary alloy. The fracture surface seen in Fig. 9(a)
presents parallel elongated ridgelines and valleys, ranging from 5 to
30 μm in size, rather than the characteristic of dimpled rupture in
conventional ductile metals. Fig. 9 (b) displays a zoom-in image of the
blocked area in Fig. 9(a), showing nearly-planar facets between ridges
and valleys typically 10 μm in width. This type of jagged peak-and-
valley morphology is typical in pure Ta after tensile failure at RT [ 29],
which is associated with a process of multistage coalescence. Under
successive non-uniform plastic deformation, microvoids occasionally
nucleate at speci ﬁc sites of sub-grains with high-angle boundaries, and
subsequently link up along the direction of applied tensile stressing to
create diamond-shaped multi-void cavities. These cavities grow and
interconnect, transverse to the direction of tensile loading, resulting in
ﬁnal rupture.
Fig. 9(c) shows an SEM image of the fracture surface after tensile
failure for the TiNbTa ternary RMEA. The fracture surface exhibits
ductile dimples with di ﬀerent sizes less than 5 μm, typical for micro-
voids coalescence transverse to the direction of tensile loading. On
further alloying the TiNbTa with Hf to form the TiHfNbTa quaternary,
the fracture surface remains a landscape of ductile dimples, as shown in

5. Conclusions
(i) Based on the NbTa ideal solution, via arc-melting we have
achieved ternary TiNbTa and HfNbTa that are single-phase BCC
RMEAs. Their lattice parameters are well in accord with the
Vegard's law. Chemical compositions of the NbTa and HfNbTa al-
loys are uniform, whereas the dendritic microstructure in the
TiNbTa RMEA shows the enrichment of lower- Tm Ti in inter-
dendritic regions and of higher- Tm Nb and Ta in dendrites.
(ii) HfNbTa and TiNbTa are the ﬁrst ternary RMEAs, the only other
one reported so far being NbTiZr [ 44]. HfNbTa and TiNbTa are
also the only two ternary RMEAs that exhibit tensile ductility
(NbTiZr merely showed compressive plasticity). Moreover,
HfNbTa has a combination of yield strength and tensile ductility
comparable with its RHEA counterpart TiZrHfNbTa, the Senkov
alloy. The TiNbTa RMEA has a tensile ductility in excess of 20%,
while its yield strength is < 500 MPa.
(iii) In terms of solution strengthening, alloying with Hf into the base
NbTa solution manifests a much higher strengthening potency,
compared with Ti. This potency di ﬀerence can be accounted for by
the eﬀects of atomic size and modulus mis ﬁts among constituents,
scaling linearly with the Θ parameter, = ∑Θε X() i i i
2 2/3 . Our pre-
vious empirical ﬁctive lattice model is also applicable to these
RMEAs, as they appear to ﬁt in with the general strengthening
trend for all the RHEAs.
