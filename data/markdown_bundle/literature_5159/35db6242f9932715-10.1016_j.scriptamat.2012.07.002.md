Enhancement of the strain hardening ability in ultraﬁne grained Mg
alloys with high strength
W.J. Kim, ⇑ S.I. Hong and Y.H. Kim
Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Hongik University, 72-1 Mapo-gu, Sangsu-dong, Seoul 121-791, Republic of Korea
Available online 10 July 2012
Two thermo-mechanical processing methods involving severe plastic deformation (SPD) are proposed for the development of a
novel microstructure having nanosized Mg 17Al12 (b) particles distributed within the interiors of ultraﬁne grains in Mg–Al based
alloys. The ﬁrst method involves deformation-induced break-up of the b phase during SPD, while the second method involves
low temperature dynamic precipitation during SPD followed by low temperature aging. The resulting processed materials exhibited
very high yield strengths of over 380 MPa and distinct strain hardening properties.

Keywords: Severe plastic deformation; Magnesium alloys; Mechanical properties; High ratio diﬀerential speed rolling
Many metals and metallic alloys processed by se-
vere plastic deformation (SPD) exhibit extraordinarily
high strengths as a result of signiﬁcant grain reﬁnement
at the submicrometer or nanometer level. However, they
often show little strain hardening ability, leading to
small uniform elongation. This lack of strain hardening
has been attributed to low dislocation activity within the
interiors of very small grains and/or pronounced dy-
namic recovery due to the presence of a high volume
fraction of grain boundaries [1]. Extensive studies have
been performed to simultaneously achieve high strength
and high ductility in SPD processed alloys [2–4].
According to the results, the enhanced strain hardening
in ultra ﬁne grained or nanostructured materials origi-
nate from the resistance to dislocation movement by
nanoscale second phase particles [2], nanotwins [3] or
Lomer–Cottrell locks [4] present in the grain interiors.
For Mg and Mg alloys with hcp structure possessing a
limited number of slip systems texture has a signiﬁcant
inﬂuence on strain hardening [5,6], because a change
in the orientation factor for basal slip can greatly aﬀect
the generation and storage of dislocations and dynamic
recovery [6]. When the developed texture favors basal
slip, higher strain hardening (ductility) is obtained.
However, this texture modiﬁcation, which has been
shown to be readily obtained by an equal channel angu-
lar pressing (ECAP) technique, often reduces the
strength below the level of the starting material due to
the texture softening eﬀect [5]. A high level of grain
reﬁnement can increase the strength by compensating
for and overcoming the texture softening eﬀect, but
the strain hardening ability sharply decreases when the
grain size is less than 2 lm [7–10]. For this reason it is
diﬃcult to simultaneously achieve high strength and
high strain hardening in Mg alloys by controlling the
texture and grain size only.
Mg–9Al–1Zn (AZ91) is a representative commercial
magnesium alloy that contains a large amount of coarse
b-Mg
17Al12 phase that is favorably located along the
grain boundaries of the matrix in a cast microstructure.
The most popular method of reﬁning the b phase in this
alloy is by artiﬁcially resolving the coarseb-Mg
17Al12 into
the matrix through a solution heat treatment before hot
deformation. When a SPD technique is applied to the so-
lid solutionized matrix, the matrix grains can be signiﬁ-
cantly reﬁned with a uniform size distribution of ﬁne
particles dynamically precipitated in the matrix [9–11].
The degree of grain and particle reﬁnement is closely re-
lated to the processing temperature because it directly af-
fects the dynamic recovery (recrystallization) and particle
growth kinetics. For Mg alloys a SPD processing temper-
ature below /C24 473 K often produces ultraﬁne grains.
Chen et al. [9] applied ECAP to solutionized AZ91 at
498–453 K and obtained a grain size of approximately
2 lm and b-Mg
17Al12 particles with sizes of /C24 0.5–1 lm.

at 448 K and obtained a grain size of 0.5 lm and a particle

at 423 K and obtained a grain size of 1.5 lm and a particle
size of 100–200 nm. These procedures produce very ﬁne
particles but they minimally contribute to strain harden-
ing because they are nucleated preferentially at grain
boundaries. As a result, the processed materials, which
have high yield strengths of over 350 MPa, exhibit low
strain hardening. For the second phase particles to play
an important role in enhancing strain hardening ability
they should be distributed in the grain interiors, because
particles in grain boundaries are not expected to impede
the movement of dislocations in grain interiors.
In this work we report techniques for improving the
strain hardening ability of ultraﬁne grained Mg alloys
with high strength through control of the size and distri-
bution of the second phase particles. We propose two
methods to obtain this microstructure. The ﬁrst method
involves dynamic precipitation of micron sized b-phase
particles during hot extrusion, followed by deforma-
tion-induced break-up of the particles down to the nano-
scale during SPD. This idea came from the observation
of the presence of very ﬁne b-particles within grain inte-
riors in the microstructure obtained after sequential
application of extrusion and ECAP to an as-cast micro-
structure of Mg–9Al alloy [12]. The second method in-
volves the application of SPD to the solid solution
treated matrix followed by low temperature aging. This
technique was originally proposed by one of the current
authors in a previous work on an age-hardenable alumi-
num alloy [13]. The microstructures obtained using these
two methods were examined, and the mechanical proper-
ties of the resultant materials were evaluated at room
temperature. Finally, the results were compared with
those of the ultraﬁne grained Mg–Al based alloys fabri-
cated by SPD with diﬀerent processing histories.
The AZ91 ingots (Mg–9.0 wt.% Al–0.13 wt.% Mn–
0.78 wt.%Zn) used in the present work were prepared
by gravity casting in an iron mold measuring 80 mm
in diameter. The ingots were machined to 70 mm diam-
eter and 180 mm long. The billets were subjected to hot
extrusion at 473 K to produce bars with a diameter of
12 mm (extrusion ratio of 40). Before the extrusion pro-
cess the ingots were solid solutionized at 693 K for 10 h
and cooled in cold water. The extruded material was
preheated at 473 K for 0.5 h and then deformed to
2 mm using hot rolling for a total of two passes, during
which the surface temperature was maintained at 473 K.
The obtained sheet will hereafter be referred to as the
rolled sheet. In the ﬁrst trial the rolled sheet was held
at 473 K for 10 min and then subjected to HRDSR at
a speed ratio of 2 through two-step rolling passes. In
HRDSR material in the form of a plate or sheet is sub-
jected to SPD by the large amount of redundant shear
deformation induced during rolling [14]. The sample
with a thickness of 0.3 mm obtained by HRDSR after
the second pass is hereafter referred to as the HRDSR
2SP sheet. In the second trial the rolled sheet was again
subjected to the solid solution treatment, and then
HRDSR was applied at a higher speed ratio of 3 and
a lower roll temperature of 423 K. A thickness reduction
of 70% was imposed through a single HRDSR pass.
After rolling the sample was annealed at 373 K for
10 h. The materials obtained before and after aging will
be hereafter referred to as the HRDSR 3SP and the aged
HRDSR 3SP sheets, respectively.

analyzed by optical microscopy (OM) and scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) (JSM-5600LV). The transverse
direction (TD) and rolling direction (RD) planes of
the HRDSR 2SP sheet were examined using ﬁeld emis-
sion transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM
2001F, 200 keV) equipped for energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS). The samples were jet polished with

3OH),
30% glycerin (C 3H8O3) and 10% nitric acid (HNO 3)

solution treatment revealed grains measuring 20–50 lm
in size (not shown here). Figure 1 a and b shows SEM
micrographs of the as-extruded material and the
HRDSR 2SP sheet taken from the middle layers of the
samples. Each microstructure was homogeneous over
the entire cross-sectional area. During extrusion many
ﬁne b phase particles with an irregular morphology were
dynamically precipitated at the boundaries of recrystal-
lized grains ( Fig. 1a). The volume fraction and the aver-
age size of the b phase particles were 0.13 and 0.91 lm
(distributed in the size range 0.5–1.5 lm), respectively.
The measured volume fraction is consistent with an
equilibrium amount of b phase in AZ91 ( /C24 0.12), indi-
cating that the b phase that dissolved during the solid
solution treatment was fully precipitated during the
extrusion process. During the subsequent conventional
rolling process, in which a large thickness reduction of
83% was imposed, the ﬁne b phase particles were only
slightly elongated along the RD (inset in Fig. 1a). Exten-

A high magniﬁcation SEM image of the HRDSR 2SP
sheet shows that the b phase particles were fragmented
into several pieces (indicated by arrows in the inset to
Fig. 1 c) and then plastically deformed to a thickness of
100–200 nm ( Fig. 1 c). Figure 1 d shows an SEM image
of the HRDSR 2SP sheet taken on the TD–RD plane,
revealing a ﬂat pancake-like b phase and the formation
of (sub) grains with sizes of/C24 50–100 nm within the phase.
Clusters of ﬁne particles with sizes similar to the (sub)-
grain sizes (inset in Fig. 1 d) were often found near the
elongated b phase particles, indicating that the (sub)grain
size and the size of the particles are correlated. The aver-
age grain sizes of the as-extruded material and the
HRDSR 2SP sheet, which were determined based on the
SEM images obtained, were 3.4 and 0.8 lm, respectively.
Figure 2 a–c shows TEM images of the HRDSR 2SP
sheet. Fringes and a network structure indicating the
presence of a high density of dislocations are observed
within some grains ( Fig. 2 a). Many spherical particles
measuring /C24 50–100 nm are dispersed in the grain interi-
ors (also shown in the inset to Fig. 2 a). The EDS and
TEM microdiﬀraction analyses conﬁrmed that the parti-
cles are Mg
17(Al,Zn)12 phase and are not crystallograph-
ically related to the matrix. Some dislocations are
attached to the particles (marked A, B and C), providing
evidence for interactions between dislocations and parti-
cles. The formation of subgrains measuring /C24 50–100 nm
in size is observed inside the deformed b phase (Fig. 2b),
which is consistent with the SEM image ( Fig. 1 d). b
phase grooves were often observed at those points in
which the transverse b/b boundaries met the Mg ma-
trix/b interface (indicated by arrows in Fig. 2 c). The
grooves appear to underlie a boundary splitting process
that leads to the formation of discrete b particles. It is
known that thin plate-shaped phases that contain inter-
nal boundaries are susceptible to shape instabilities as a
result of curvature-induced diﬀusional processes [16].
The diﬀusional processes occur as mass is transferred
from a groove to the nearby ﬂat surfaces of the plate,
which maintain an equilibrium dihedral angle at the
juncture of the plate surface and the internal boundary,
via recession of the boundary [16]. The current SEM
and TEM observations indicates that separation of the
nanosized b phase particles from the elongated b phase
ﬁrst requires the formation of nanosized (sub)grains
within it. Because a higher ﬂow stress produces smaller
subgrains [17], high shear stresses induced during
HRDSR should play an important role in forming the
nanosized subgrains.
Figure 3a and b shows the microstructure of the AZ91
alloy processed by the second method. The HRDSR 3SP
sheet (Fig. 3a) consists of spherical b particles measuring
/C24 50–100 nm, but most of these particles are located near
the grain boundaries. The average grain size measured by
SEM was 0.6 lm, which is smaller than that of the
HRDSR 2SP sheet. The microstructure of aged HRDSR
3SP shows very ﬁne particles additionally precipitated in
the grain interiors ( Fig. 3 b). To obtain this microstruc-
ture full precipitation of b phase at grain boundaries
should be prevented during dynamic precipitation, and
the presence of a high dislocation density in the grain
interiors, which provide nucleation sites for b particles
during aging, is important. This is the reason why a high-
er speed ratio and a lower processing temperature were
chosen in the second trial.

Texture diﬀerences among the investigated materials
may also result in diﬀerences in the work hardening
behavior. However, the HRDSR processed AZ31 and
AZ61 alloys with small H values have strong basal tex-
ture characteristics (maximum intensities of /C24 25–32
[11,18]), similar to those of the HRDSR 2SP and the
aged HRDSR 3SP sheets ( /C24 22–30) with much larger H
values. Furthermore, all of the ECAP processed Mg al-
loys with a basal slip favorable texture show small H val-
ues. This result may imply that the texture eﬀect on strain
hardening is insigniﬁcant, at least at ultraﬁne grain sizes.
In summary, we have demonstrated two strategies for
the formation and distribution of nanosized b particles
(/C24 50–100 nm) in the grain interiors of an ultraﬁne
grained Mg alloy. The current result is important be-
cause the obtained microstructure results in not only
high strength but also high strain hardening, which has
been diﬃcult to achieve in ultraﬁne grained Mg alloys.
