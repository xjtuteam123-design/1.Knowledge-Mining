ABSTRACT
Laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF) processed Nb-based alloy C103 (Nb-10Hf-1Ti wt.%) develops a complex, hier -
archical microstructure comprising a fine-scale solidification cell structure, overlaid with a dense dislocation- 
network outlining the cell boundaries, within the primary grains. Additionally, sub-grain boundaries and a 
fine-scale dispersion of nano-sized hafnium oxide precipitates, possibly forming during solidification, decorate 
the solidification cell boundaries as well as exist within the cells. This complex hierarchical microstructure re -
sults in impressive tensile mechanical properties. Post-build stress-relieving annealing and hot isostatic pressing 
(HIP) largely annihilates the solidification cell structure and associated dislocation network, lowering the 
strength but with substantial recovery of tensile ductility. Nevertheless, the resulting microstructure offers higher 
strengths as compared to their wrought counterparts.
1. Introduction
Fusion-based additive manufacturing (AM) has opened new oppor -
tunities for fabricating refractory alloys into complex parts [ 1 – 4 ]. Re -
fractory alloys are attractive because of their high creep strengths at 
high temperatures [ 1 , 5 , 6 ]. The recommended operating temperature 
range for commercially available refractory alloys as structural mate -
rials for space propulsion systems are significantly higher compared to 
superalloys, stainless steels, and ODS steels [ 7 ]. Niobium (Nb) is a 
desirable candidate from the refractory metal alloy family given its low 
ductile-to-brittle transition temperature of < 142 K [ 8 ] making it more 
amenable to various forming processes, and its low density of 8.57 g/cc 
compared to other refractory alloys [ 8 ]. A Nb-based refractory alloy of 
particular interest is Nb-10Hf-1Ti in wt%, commercially referred to as 
C103. The Nb-based alloy has a BCC single-phase solid solution micro -
structure and a bulk density of 8.85 g/cc [ 8 ]. Hf and Ti impart significant 
solid solution strengthening to Nb without compromising on ductility 
[ 9 , 10 ]. The strength-ductility combination in C103 makes it amenable 
to various fabrication routes (welding, forming, etc.) [ 11 ]. Since C-103 
has been used in aerospace applications, such as rocket nozzles, since the 
days of the Apollo missions, and is an important candidate alloy for 
future hypersonic applications [ 12 – 14 ], AM-based printing of near-net 
shape components is of great interest. However, there are only a 
limited number of studies on AM processed C103. Despite that, various 
AM techniques have been employed to print C103 such as directed en -
ergy deposition [ 15 ], electron beam powder bed fusion [ 16 ], and laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) [ 1 , 12 , 17 ].
LPBF is the technique of choice owing to the advantages it offers, e. 
g., better surface finish and precision, finer microstructural features, etc. 
[ 18 ]. Furthermore, the complex thermal history and high cooling rates 
in LPBF result in non-equilibrium, heterogeneous microstructures 
including high dislocation density, and cellular structures in some sys -
tems [ 19 – 27 ]. Such microstructures have a direct consequence on the 
structural properties of metallic alloys and are yet to be understood. 
Mireles et al . [ 12 , 28 ] was able to successfully fabricate C103 alloy using 
LPBF and observed tensile yield strengths of 540 MPa. Awasthi et al . [ 17 ] 
also printed C103 alloy using LPBF and stress-relieved it, and conse -
quently observed a room temperature tensile yield strength of 410 MPa. 
The increase in strength observed in the study, compared to wrought 
C103, was attributed to the presence of dislocation cell networks and

Also, while LPBF dramatically enhances the strength, the high re -
sidual stresses can result in early failure, necessitating a stress-relieving 
treatment. Additional defects are porosities (even in parts with > 99% 
relative density) and columnar grains [ 20 , 25 , 29 ]. In such a scenario, an 
additional step of hot isostatic pressing (HIP) appears promising in 
alleviating the properties making the printed part denser and more 
isotropic [ 15 , 16 , 28 ]. While the previous studies on AM processed C103 
have reported very good mechanical properties, they lack a detailed 
analysis of the underlying hierarchical microstructural features leading 
to these properties. Additionally, the influence of post-processing 
treatments, such as stress-relieving as well as HIP ’ ing, on the hierar -
chical microstructure and its consequent impact on the mechanical 
properties has not been addressed. These aspects form the basis of the 
present study on LPBF processed C103.

3. Results and discussion
The morphology of the pre-alloyed C103 powders is shown in Fig. 1

achieved at 180 W laser power, 200 mm/s scan speed, and 100 µ m hatch 
spacing. The as-built (AB) block was further analyzed along the build

sentative boundary marked in yellow), and elongated grains are also

It is interesting to note that the dislocation cell network seen here has 
been observed primarily in LPBF printed FCC-based alloys such as 
austenitic stainless steels and Ni-base superalloys [ 23 , 24 , 36 – 39 ]. How -
ever, reports of such a network in LPBF printed BCC-based alloys are 
quite limited. Recently, Liu et al . [ 40 ] observed similar features in an 
LPBF printed TiNbTaZrMo RCCA. Tekumalla et al . [ 41 ] have attributed 
such a dislocation cell network to the moduli/yield-strength differences 
arising from the heterogeneities resulting from LPBF printing, and the 
subsequent cyclic heating/cooling they undergo as the printing proceeds 
in a multi-layered fashion.
The representative engineering stress-strain curves for all the three 
conditions of LPBF printed C103 investigated in this study are shown in 
Fig. 4 (a) . The average values of yield stress (YS) and ultimate tensile 
stress (UTS) are included in Table 3 , revealing a distinct difference be -
tween the three conditions, with AB C103 demonstrating both high YS 
and UTS, while it is the lowest in AB + SR. The area fraction of Hf-oxide 
is highest in AB + SR, as seen in Table 1 , and the size scale of these 
precipitates is comparable in case of AB + SR and AB + HIP conditions. 
Therefore, despite the comparable size of the precipitates in these two 
conditions, the higher area fraction of the oxides in case of AB + SR, 
markedly decreases the YS presumably due to lower solid solution 
strengthening from Hf coupled with coarsening of the Hf-oxide pre -
cipitates. From Table 2 , it is evident that the fraction of LABs and MABs 
in the AB condition is comparable to the AB + HIP condition, however 
the fraction of LABs is significantly high in case of the AB + SR condi -
tion. As there is a higher fraction of boundaries with larger mis -
orientations in the first two conditions, the boundary strengthening can 
also be expected to be higher.
The comparable fraction of LABs, MABs, and HAGBs in AB and AB +
HIP conditions of C103 can be attributed to the fact that during both 
LPBF processing and subsequent HIPing, the material experiences 
stress/plastic deformation and competing relaxation effects such as re -
covery and recrystallization due to the nature of the two processing 
routes. In the case of additive manufacturing processes such as LPBF, the 
rapid heating and cooling cycles ~ 10
4 
– 10
5 
K/s [ 25 ] typically induce 
significant thermal gradients [ 44 – 47 ]. As a result of the thermal cycling 
effect, the material typically experiences thermal stresses sufficient for 
plastic deformation [ 44 – 47 ]. However, the very same thermal cycling 
effect also mimics in-situ localized annealing treatment due to the 
repeated heating cycles [ 44 ]. Therefore, the resulting substructure in an 
as-built component is a result of this competitive process between 
thermal stress induced deformation and relaxation processes during 
printing. Similarly, in the case of HIPing, a material may undergo plastic 
deformation due to the high pressures/isostatic stresses, along with 
competing relaxation processes resulting from the high temperatures 
involved [ 48 ]. Furthermore, because of inhomogeneous strain distri -
bution in AB C103 from printing and varying grain sizes, it is expected 
that recrystallization would not take place uniformly throughout the 
microstructure [ 49 ] in the HIP treatment of 1300

most importantly, it appears that the presence of the dense dislocation

sets it apart from AB + HIP condition despite having similar fraction of 
LABs, MABs, and HAGBs. Such an increase in YS due to the presence of 
dislocation cell network has been reported previously in LPBF printed 
FCC alloys [ 36 , 38 , 50 ].

4. Conclusion
In summary, we report a multi-scale heterogeneous microstructure 
resulting from LPBF printing of the Nb-based alloy, C103. Cellular so -
lidification leads to the formation of Hf-rich inter-cellular channels with 
Hf-oxide formation within these channels. A dense dislocation cell 
network was found overlapping with this cellular solidification structure 
which dramatically enhances the tensile yield strength. Its contribution
