ABSTRACT
Co-Al-W-based superalloys have a higher melting point than Ni-based superalloys, making them a promising new 
generation of superalloys for gas turbines. In this study, Co9Al9W — one of the most commonly used Co-Al-W- 
based superalloys — was investigated. Laser additive manufacturing technology was used to prepare the direc -
tionally solidified Co9Al9W. Single-track deposition experiments were conducted to explore the processing pa -
rameters that endow Co9Al9W with higher forming quality and better microstructure orientation. The geometry 
and dimensions of the single-track depositions and numerical simulations of the solidification conditions 
revealed that a high laser power and low scanning velocity were more conducive to the preparation of Co9Al9W 
with a directional microstructure. The microstructure of the Co9Al9W sample prepared using the selected pro -
cessing parameters exhibited good orientation. Directionally solidified Co9Al9W after heat treatments exhibits 
good tensile properties at room and high temperatures, demonstrably better than those of as-cast Co-Al-W-based 
superalloys.
1. Introduction
Superalloys are indispensable in aviation engines, aerospace pro -
pulsion systems, industrial gas turbines, and ship power plants [1 – 3] . 
Research on superalloys has long focused on Ni-based superalloys. The 
demand for more powerful aerospace engines is increasing [4 – 6] , but 
the lower melting points of Ni-based superalloys limit their application 
in high-temperature environments. In 2006, Ishida ’ s research team at 
Tohoku University in Japan [7] discovered a type of Co-Al-W-based 
superalloy with stable L1
2 
precipitation strengthening. Moreover, the 
melting points of Co-Al-W-based alloys is 100 – 150 ℃ higher than that of 
L1
2 
strengthened Ni-based alloys [8] , making them promising new 
candidates for gas-turbine applications.
For some time, Co-Al-W-based superalloys have been fabricated 
using traditional casting techniques [9 – 11] . However, owing to the 
distinct environment of gas-turbine components (such as turbine 
blades), the preparation of superalloys with directional microstructures 
is of enormous importance [12,13] . Additive manufacturing (AM) 
technology — which is capable of net-shape manufacturing of complex 
components — has developed rapidly in recent years [14 – 16] . Moreover, 
laser additive manufacturing (LAM) is a special type of AM used to 
prepare directional microstructure alloys owing to its special solidifi -
cation conditions with high-temperature gradients.
Consequently, LAM technology has been widely used in the prepa -
ration of Ni-based superalloys, and there have been many reports on its 
use to prepare microstructure-oriented Ni-based alloys with excellent 
performance [17 – 19] . Liang et al . [18] prepared a multilayer crack-free 
single-crystal Ni-based superalloy with fine dendrites by controlling the 
solidification conditions determined by the processing parameters. Paul 
et al . [19] demonstrated that the solidification conditions of LAM using a 
high-temperature gradient and fast cooling rate could not only obtain a 
directional solidification structure, but also reduce the composition 
segregation common in the liquid film during the final stage of 
solidification.
Generally, it can be difficult to prepare Co-Al-W-based superalloys 
using LAM because high-Al-content superalloys are generally considered 
non-weldable alloys [20 – 22] . In recent years, Co-Al-W-based alloys 
prepared by powder metallurgy and their properties have been reported 
[23,24] . The mechanical performance of the powder metallurgy 
Co-Al-W-based superalloys is better than that of commercial Co-based 
superalloys. Powder metallurgy Co-Al-W-based superalloys do not 
have directional microstructure and are not suitable for specific service

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Determination of the processing window
The specimens prepared using different forming process parameters 
exhibited evident differences in shape, size, and microstructure, 
particularly in the single-track deposition experiment. To determine the 
best processing window, single-track deposition experiments were 
conducted using a wide range of processing parameters.
3.1.1. Geometry of single-track depositions
The geometries of the additively manufactured specimens are most 
directly influenced by the laser processing parameters, such as the laser 
power and scanning speed, which affect the volumetric energy density 
and volumetric powder density entering the molten pool. Consequently, 
the geometry of the single-track deposition is the basis for determining 
the forming quality of block-printed materials — that is, a fuller and 
flatter single-track deposition guarantees better forming quality.
The transverse section scanning electron microscope secondary 
electron (SEM-SE) images of single-track Co9Al9W specimens are shown 
in Fig. 4 . Adequate laser energy input is a prerequisite for the alloy 
powder to melt sufficiently and establish effective metallurgical 
bonding. Increasing the laser power or decreasing the scanning speed 
increases the energy input. A laser power that is too low and a scanning 
speed that is too fast can make it difficult for the powder to melt to form 
a cladding layer. As shown in Fig. 4 (o) and (p) , the single-track depo -
sition does not exist.
The energy input not only determines whether the powder can be 
melted, but also deeply affects the geometry and size of the molten pool. 
Similarly, the powder mass input is an important factor affecting both. 
In this study, the powder feeding rate was constant; consequently, the 
powder mass input depended only on the scanning velocity.
The geometric dimensions of the single-track molten pool are shown 
in Fig. 5 . Fig. 5 (a) shows the geometrical characteristics of the single- 
track molten pool. The measured track height, depth, and width of the 
single-track depositions are shown in Figs. 5 (b) – 4 (g) . The track height 
increases with increasing laser power and decreasing scanning velocity. 
Similarly, the track depth also increases with increasing laser power and 
decreasing scanning velocity. With sufficient energy and powder mass 
input, track width is mainly determined by the laser processing head 
itself, but increases slightly with increasing laser power and decreasing 
scanning speed. With insufficient energy and powder mass input, track 
width decreases significantly. As the laser power increases, the energy 
input increases, so the volume of the substrate being melted becomes 
larger, and the depth of the molten pool will increase significantly. At 
the same time, the temperature of the entire molten pool increases and 
the size becomes larger, which allows more powder to be captured and 
melted by the molten pool. Therefore, the track height and the track 
width will also increase. When the laser power and powder feeding rate 
remain unchanged (the powder feeding rate has not changed in this 
article), the volumetric energy density and volumetric powder density in 
the molten pool will decrease as the scanning velocity increases. 
Therefore, the molten pool size decreases, and the height, width and 
depth of the track all decrease.
The geometric dimensions of single-track molten pools can signifi -
cantly affect the forming efficiency and quality of the LMD samples [30] . 
An extremely low energy input or powder mass input leads to an 
excessively low molten-pool height, which reduces the forming effi -
ciency and quality. Consequently, the forming process parameters that 
result in a single-track molten-pool height of less than 0.2 mm are 
considered unsuitable. Similarly, a high molten-pool height-to-width 
ratio can cause overlapping holes, and a large molten-pool height-to- -
depth ratio can cause overburning of the sample [31] . Consequently, the 
following process parameters were not suitable for the LMD of Co9Al9W 
in this study: 
1) Laser power of 900 W and scanning velocity of 5 – 20 mm/s;
2) Laser power of 1200 W and scanning velocity of 15 – 20 mm/s;
3) Laser power of 1500 W and scanning velocity of 20 mm/s; and
4) Laser power of 1800 W and scanning velocity of 20 mm/s.

During the early stages of solidification, fine Co9Al9W grains quickly 
appear at the bottom of the molten pool owing to the extremely fast 
cooling rate provided by the substrate. Subsequently, during solidifica -
tion, the epitaxial growth of dendrites (with these fine grains as seeds) 
begins. The dendrites grow along the deposition direction, and almost 
all parallel grains are oriented in the < 001 > direction. As solidification 
proceeds, the primary dendrite arms gradually thicken from tens of 
microns during the initial stages to hundreds of microns or even milli -
meters. Because the top of the molten pool is in direct contact with the 
gas in the equipment cavity, a large amount of heat is dissipated through 
the gas. Consequently, fine and partially equiaxed grains are formed on 
the molten-pool surface. During the preparation of the wall-like samples, 
these fine equiaxed grains are remelted by the next laser layer. The 
grains in the molten pool continue to grow epitaxially along the 
columnar crystals at the bottom of the molten pool, ensuring the 
orientation of the microstructure of the entire wall-like sample.

3.3.2. Fracture morphology of the heat-treated Co9Al9W
The tensile fracture morphologies of the HTed Co9Al9W at different 
are shown in Fig. 11 . The three groups of fracture morphologies show 
typical dimple fracture patterns, demonstrating that all the alloys fail in 
a trans-granular ductile manner. The dimple sizes in the fracture 
morphology are all relatively large, indicating that HTed Co9Al9W ex -
hibits good plastic deformation under these three temperature condi -
tions. The dimples in the fracture morphology at 700 ℃ are deeper, with 
deeper dimples indicating better plastic deformation, which is consistent 
with the tensile test results.
The fracture morphology at 800 ℃ exhibits a quasi-cleavage fracture 
mode, which proves that the sample still exhibits a degree of plastic 
deformation under this temperature condition, but the elongation of the 
sample has noticeably decreased. The fracture morphology at 900 ℃ 
exhibits a brittle fracture mode. The smooth fracture morphology in -

tensile load at 900 ℃ . Slip bands are evident at the edges of the frac -

4. Conclusions
This study explored the LAM processing window for Co9Al9W 
through numerical simulations and experiments. Processing parameters 
with high forming quality and good grain structure orientation were 
selected to prepare wall-like deposited samples. The microstructures and 
tensile properties of the wall-like samples before and after heat treat -
ment were characterized and tested. The major conclusions drawn are as 
follows: 
1) High laser power and low scanning velocity not only ensure forming 
quality but also provide solidification conditions with a large tem -
perature gradient and low solidification rate, which are more 
conducive to obtaining directional structures. The processing 
parameter combination of “ laser power, 1800 W; scanning velocity, 
5 mm/s; powder feeding rate, 10 g/min; and layer-lifting value, 
0.75 mm ” was selected to prepare the directionally solidified 
Co9Al9W.
2) The additively manufactured wall-like Co9Al9W consisting of FCC 
and L1
2 
phases exhibited a coarse columnar microstructure along the 
deposition direction. After heat treatment, the grain structure and 
phase composition of the directionally solidified Co9Al9W did not 
change, but the size of the L1
2 
precipitate increased to 200 – 400 nm 
and the distribution became more uniform.
3) The heat-treated Co9Al9W exhibited good room temperature tensile 
strength and ductility (yield strength of 600 MPa and elongation of 
32 %). The tensile yield strengths of the heat-treated Co9Al9W 
prepared in this work were better than those of the as-cast Co-Al-W- 
based superalloy from room temperature to 900 ℃ . Moreover, the 
temperature at which the yield strength decreased substantially was 
higher than that of the as-cast Co-Al-W-based superalloys.
