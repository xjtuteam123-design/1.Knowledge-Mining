Microstructures and mechanical properties of Ta–Nb–Zr–Ti–Al 
refractory high entropy alloys with varying Ta/Ti ratios
Ya‑Guang Dong1 · Shang Chen1 · Nan‑Nan Jia1 · Qiu‑Hong Zhang1 · Liang Wang1,2 · Yun‑Fei Xue1 · Ke Jin1,3

Abstract
The refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) containing disordered body-centered cubic (BCC) and ordered BCC (B2) 
structures often exhibit high strength but low ductility at room temperature, even in the compressive tests. In this study, 
 (Ta25-xNb25Zr25Ti25+x)95Al5 (x = 0, 5, 10) RHEAs are fabricated to investigate the compositional dependence of the micro-
structures and mechanical properties. All the three alloys exhibit a single BCC structure at the as-cast and solution-treated 
states, while the basket weave-like microstructures consisting of cuboidal and strip-like phases are formed after aging at 
600 °C. The microstructure of the aged alloys is sensitive to the compositions: only disordered BCC1 + BCC2 dual phases 
are observed in the (Ta25Nb25Zr25Ti25)95Al5 alloy; additional B2 phases are formed by replacing 5 at% Ta with Ti; a complex 
microstructure containing BCC1 + BCC2 + B2 + Omega phases are identified in the (Ta15Nb25Zr25Ti35)95Al5 alloy. Although 
replacing Ta with Ti results in a reduction of compressive yield strength from 1762 to 1243 MPa, the fracture strain is greatly 
enhanced from 7.3 to 44.3%, indicating that the strength-ductility balance can be tuned to a large extent in this RHEA system.
Keywords Refractory high entropy alloys · Microstructure · Mechanical properties · Strength-ductility balance
1 Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted increasing 
research attention due to their outstanding mechanical prop-
erties [1 –7] and unique physical properties [8 , 9]. Among 
this new family of alloys, refractory HEAs (RHEAs) have 
been considered as one of the promising candidates for 
future high-temperature structural materials, aiming at 
overcoming the service temperature limit of commercial 
Ni-based superalloys [10–16].
Extensive previous investigations have demonstrated 
that introducing secondary phases with coherent inter -
faces to the matrix is an important method to strengthen 
HEAs. For the HEAs with disordered face-centered cubic 
(FCC) structure, the ordered FCC (L12) precipitates in the 
Ni-Co-Fe–Cr-Ti–Al alloys enhance their strength without 
significant loss in ductility [17– 19]. For the RHEAs with 
body-centered cubic (BCC) structure, most reported alloys 
containing disordered BCC and ordered BCC (B2) phases 
exhibit a basket-weave-like microstructure consisting of 
cuboidal, spherical, and/or strip-like morphologies [20–23], 
which is similar to some FCC-L1 2 dual-phases Ni-based 
superalloys [24–26], and usually brings them high strength. 
For example, the equiatomic AlNbTiZr alloy, consisting 
of a B2 matrix and Zr5Al3 precipitates, has a compressive 
yield strength of 1579 MPa [27]. With the addition of Mo 
and Ta elements, the AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr alloy is composed 
of continuous channels of B2 matrix and periodic cuboidal 
and plate-like BCC precipitates, achieving an ultrahigh yield 
strength of 2197 MPa but a low fracture strain of 4.7% [21]. 
The morphology and mechanical properties of such coherent 
BCC/B2-structured RHEAs can be modified via tuning the 
composition and annealing process. For example, reducing 
the Al concentration in this alloy may result in the spherical 
BCC-structured precipitates uniformly distributed in the B2 
matrix, exhibiting a higher yield strength of 2350 MPa but 
lower fracture strain of 3.2% [21]. A special phase inver -
sion, from the B2 matrix and cuboidal BCC precipitates 
to the BCC matrix with discrete B2 precipitates, can be

A series of Ta25-x Nb25Zr25Ti25+x RHEAs have been 
designed to tune their Pugh’s ratio and Poisson’s ratio by 
the replacement of Ta with Ti, achieving a better strength-

that the addition of 5.88 at% Al in equiatomic TaNbZrTi

strategies, in this study, 5 at% Al element is added into Ta-
Nb-Zr-Ti alloys, i.e., (Ta25-xNb25Zr25Ti25+x)95Al5 (x = 0, 5, 
10). Their microstructures and mechanical properties are 
investigated, showing that the strength and ductility can be 
tuned to a large extent by controlling the Ta/Ti ratio and 
annealing treatments.
2 Experimental
The alloys with three designed compositions, 
 (Ta25-xNb25Zr25Ti25+x)95Al5 (x = 0, 5, 10), were fabricated, 
and are denoted in short as Ta25Ti25, Ta20Ti30, and Ta15Ti35 
in the following text, respectively. The Ta, Nb, Zr, Ti, and 
Al elemental metals with the purity of > 99.9% were used 
as raw materials, which were mixed by arc melting in the 
Ti-gettered high purity argon atmosphere. The as-cast ingots 
were flipped and re-melted at least six times with electro-
magnetic stirring, to enhance chemical homogeneity. The 
as-cast alloys were solution-treated (ST) at 1300 °C for 24 h, 
followed by water quenching, and denoted as ST-1300/24 in 
the following text. The alloys were then aged at 600 °C for 
6, 12, and 24 h, which were depicted as 600–6, 600–12, and 
600–24, respectively. During the heat treatment, all samples 
were wrapped in a Ta foil, and were sealed in the evacuated 
argon-backfilled quartz tubes.
The phases of the alloys were identified using X-ray dif-
fraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation 
in a 2θ range from 20° to 90°. The microstructures were 
characterized using a Zeiss Supra55 scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) coupled with backscatter electron (BSE) 
spectrometry. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) 
observations of the aged samples were performed with FEI 
Talos F200X microscopy operated at 200 kV with an energy 
dispersive spectrometer (EDS), and the TEM samples were 
prepared using ion milling after mechanical grounding down 
to the thickness of 30 μm. Room temperature compression 
tests were performed using an Instron machine under a strain 
rate of 1 × 10–3  s−1, and the test specimens are in the shape 
of a cylinder (Φ4 × 6 mm).
3 Results and discussion
3.1 Microstructure of the as‑cast 
and solution‑treated alloys
The XRD patterns of the as-cast Ta25Ti25, Ta20Ti30 and 
 Ta15Ti35 alloys are shown in Fig.  1a. All the peaks can 
be indexed with a BCC structure. The lattice constants 
(a) are determined as: Ta25Ti25 (a = 0.3344 ± 0.0001 nm), 
 Ta20 Ti 30 ( a = 0.3353 ± 0.0002  nm), and Ta15 Ti 35 
(a = 0.3363 ± 0.0002 nm). The increment of lattice constants 
with decreasing Ta/Ti ratio may be ascribed to the larger 
atomic radius of Ti (0.146 nm) than Ta (0.143 nm) [31].
Figure 1b–d show the representative SEM-BSE images of 
the three as-cast alloys, in which the dendrite and interden-
drite regions can be clearly identified due to constitutional 
segregation during solidification. The compositions deter -
mined with EDS analyses are listed in Table 1, according to 
which the dendrite regions are Nb- and Ta- enriched, due to 
their higher melting points, while the interdendrite regions 
are Zr- and Al- enriched. The distribution of Ti element 
seems to be relatively uniform in all the three as-cast alloys.
The XRD patterns and SEM-BSE images of the solution-
treated alloys are shown in Fig.  2. As shown in Fig.  2a, all 
the XRD patterns exhibit a single BCC structure, with no 
peaks observed corresponding to secondary phases. The 
microstructures consist of equiaxed grains without nota-
ble dendrite features and secondary phases, as shown in 
Fig. 2b–d. The average grain size ranges from 130 to 542 μm 
with decreasing Ta/Ti ratio.
3.2 Microstructure of the aged alloys
Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of the three alloys aged 
at 600 ℃ for different periods. After 6 h aging, the Ta25Ti25 
and Ta20Ti30 alloys still exhibit a single BCC structure, while 
two sets of BCC peaks can be observed in the Ta15Ti35 alloy. 
With increasing the aging time to 12 h, the peaks of sec-
ondary BCC phases can be observed in both the Ta25Ti25 
and Ta20Ti30 alloys, while a B2 phase can be identified in 
the Ta15Ti35 alloy. After 24 h aging, no further change is 
observed in the XRD patterns of the Ta25Ti25 and Ta20Ti30 
alloys, while an Omega phase can be identified in the 
 Ta15Ti35 alloy.

inside the grains in the Ta20Ti30 alloy; at least three phases, 
corresponding to the bright, grey, and dark regions, can be 
identified in the Ta15Ti35 alloy.

also been found in many BCC/B2-structured RHEAs, such 
as Al10Nb15Ta5Ti30Zr40 [42] and Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr 
[43]. Overall speaking, replacing Ta with Ti will diminish 
the stability of the BCC structure in this alloy system.
3.3 Mechanical properties
The compressive mechanical properties of the ST-1300/24 
and 600–24 samples are shown in Fig.  7. The ST-1300/24 
alloys exhibit good compressive ductility without fracture 
during loading. Their yield strength σ 0.2 is reduced from 
1304, 1112, to 973 MPa with the substitution of Ta with 
Ti. After aging at 600 °C for 24 h, the yield strength is 
enhanced up to 1762, 1509, and 1243 MPa of the Ta25Ti25, 
 Ta20Ti30, and Ta15Ti35 alloys, respectively. As mentioned 
above, although high strengths are commonly seen in the 
RHEAs containing basket-weave-like BCC/B2 microstruc-
tures, most previous alloys exhibited very low compressive 
ductility (< 10%) [21, 44]. Here, in contrast, the forming for 
B2 and Omega phases reduces the yield strength but greatly 
enhances the ductility ε , from 7.3% for Ta25Ti25, 21.6% for  
 Ta20Ti30 to 44.3% for Ta15Ti35 alloy.
This unusual microstructural dependence on the mechani-
cal properties may be attributed to the following factors. 
First, it has been reported that both the Pugh’s ratio and 
Poisson’s ratio increase with substitution of Ti for Ta in the 
 Ta25-xNb25Zr25Ti25+x base alloys, suggesting an enhanced 
ductility and stability of the solid against shear deforma -
tion [34]. Second, since the atomic size of Ti is larger than

47 to 103 nm with decreasing Ta/Ti ratio, indicating lower

may also result in better deformation capability. It needs to 
be noted that, although considerable compressive ductility 
has been achieved, the present alloys still hold near-zero 
tensile ductility according to our tensile tests. Recently, it 
has been proposed that RHEAs with a compressive ductility 
larger than 30% may also possess measureable tensile ductil-
ity [46]. However, our results show that the Ta15Ti35 alloy 
with a compressive ductility over 44% fractures before yield-
ing in the tensile tests, which may be due to complicated cor-
relation between yield strength and ductility of multi-phases 
RHEAs. Therefore, further efforts are desired to improve the 
tensile properties of this series of RHEAs to broaden their 
application fields.
4 Conclusion
In the present study, the microstructures and mechanical 
properties of three RHEAs, (Ta25-xNb25Zr25Ti25+x)95Al5 
(x = 0, 5, 10), have been investigated at their as-cast, 
solution-treated, and aged states. At the solution-treated 
state, single-phase BCC structures with equiaxed grain 
microstructures are observed in all the three alloys, but 
the average grain size increases from 130 to 542 μm with 
decreasing Ta/Ti ratio. After aging at 600 °C for 6 h and 
12 h, the three alloys exhibit a similar basket-weave-like 
microstructure. Extension of the aging time to 24 h results in 
considerable differences in the microstructures and mechani-
cal properties between the three alloys: the average size of 
cuboidal phases increases from 47 to 103 nm with decreas-
ing Ta/Ti ratio, while, more importantly, B2 and B2/Omega 
phases are formed when 5 and 10 at% of Ta is replaced with 
Ti, respectively. Although the mechanical properties are 
similar of these alloys at their solution-treated states, sig -
nificant differences are observed after aging. The Ta25Ti25 
alloy exhibits the highest strength of 1762 MPa but very 
limited ductility below 10%; on the contrary, the Ta20Ti30 
and Ta15Ti35 alloys exhibit lower yield strength (1509 and 
1243 MPa, respectively) but much higher fracture strain, 
achieving over 40% for Ta15Ti35. These results demonstrate 
a large space to tune the strength and ductility in this RHEA 
system, which may broaden the scope of their potential 
application fields.
