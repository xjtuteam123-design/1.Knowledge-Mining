ABSTRACT
Erosive wear is also known as impact wear. Several industrial components are degraded due
to solid particle erosion at high temperatures. Solid particle erosion of metallic materials at
high temperatures is in ﬂuenced by the nature of interaction between erosion and oxidation.
The main objective of the present work is to critically examine how strength and oxidation
behaviour can be tailored to enhanced resistance to solid particle erosion of Ni-base super-
alloys at high temperatures. It is noted that alloys which form Al 2O3 scale are likely to have
good erosion resistance. This study examines methods of improving the erosion resistance by
enhancing the elevated temperature mechanical properties. Methods for forming various
scales and improving their adhesion characteristics are also elaborated.

Introduction
Superalloys having excellent mechanical properties,
creep resistance, oxidation resistance and enhanced sur-
face stability at elevated temperature [ 1,2]a r ec o n s i d -
ered for high temperature applications. Superalloys can
be Ni-base, Co-base, Fe-base and Cr-base. Among these
superalloys, Ni-base superalloys are the most complex
and most widely used at high temperature. In fact, they
are used at the highest homologous temperatures. These
Ni-base superalloys are extensively used in aircraft,
marine, industrial and vehicular gas turbines [ 3]. They
are also used in rocket engines, nuclear reactors, sub-
marines, petrochemical equipment, steam power plants,
etc. However, the largest use of these superalloys is in
gas turbine industries [4]. Although Ni-base superalloys
have received attention several decades ago [ 5–7],
extensive research on these materials is still being pur-
sued [8–10].
One of the important degradation processes, to
which various components of gas turbines made of
superalloys or high temperature alloys are subjected,
is solid particle erosion, especially during operation
of the turbines in dusty terrain [ 11–13]. Although Ni-
base superalloy parts that are exposed to high tem-
peratures have protective coatings, there are many
locations where these parts are exposed without any
protective coatings [ 14]. Solid particle erosion of
metals and alloys at high temperatures is in ﬂuenced
by the synergy between erosion and oxidation. This,
in turn, is governed by pliability, toughness, adhesion
characteristics, morphology and thickness of the
oxide scale [ 15,16]. This leads to di ﬀerent interaction
mechanisms between oxidation and erosion [ 17,18].
In view of the above, the objective of the present
investigation is to critically examine the mechanical
properties and oxidation resistance parameters that
can be tailored and recommend strategies to enhance
erosion resistance of the Ni-base alloys based on data
available in the literature. The manuscript begins with
an introduction to various mechanisms of interaction
between erosion and oxidation followed by identi ﬁca-
tion of various important parameters that in ﬂuence
erosion properties. Approaches to improve erosion
resistance are dealt with in the subsequent section.
Finally, a discussion on the shortcomings of the present
approach and summary constitute the concluding part.
Mechanisms of erosion oxidation interaction
As mentioned earlier, the material loss during erosion
at elevated temperature is controlled by the synergy
of mechanical degradation known as erosion and
chemical degradation referred to as oxidation. Over
past several decades, researchers have carried out
extensive investigations on the eroded surfaces of
various materials using optical microscopy (OM),
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmis-
sion electron microscopy (TEM). They have identi-
ﬁed the presence of several erosion –oxidation
interaction mechanisms [ 18–23]. This author has
demonstrated four di ﬀerent interaction mechanisms
experimentally [16,18]. It is noted that no oxide scale
is formed when the test temperature is low and the
material is eroded at high impact velocities and high
particle feed rates. Even if oxide scale is formed, it
remains thin and deforms in a way similar to the

This can be achieved by increasing the strain hard-

strength and strain hardening coe ﬃcient at room and

increased by increasing its Co content. Interactions
between the dislocations in slip bands and a large
volume fraction of ﬁne secondary and tertiary γ′
precipitates are responsible for this behaviour at
lower temperatures. However, at higher tempera-
tures, formation of deformation microtwins causes
the above response. It was noted that as Co is added
to Ni-base superalloys, the volume fraction of pri-
mary γ′ precipitates tends to decrease and the volume
fraction of secondary and tertiary γ′ precipitates
increases. The addition of Co also results in a reduc-
tion in the stacking fault energy (SFE) of the alloy,
and this promotes the transition from isolated faults
shearing to microtwinning, especially at elevated tem-
peratures. The microtwin, thus formed due to defor-
mation, increases the yield strength and strain
hardening ability at elevated temperatures. Hence,
increased Co content can be responsible for improved
erosion resistance of Ni-base alloys as long as the
erosion regime is in the metal erosion regime.
Another important way of improving the strain
hardening exponent is dispersion strengthening.
During direct strengthening by second phase parti-
cles, dislocation interacts with second phase particles
by four di ﬀerent mechanisms. These are Orowan
bowing, cross slip around particles, dislocation bypass
by particle shearing and climb of dislocation over
particles. According to Ashby [ 39], initial ﬂow of
dispersion strengthened alloy is Orowan bypass
mechanism. Results of yielding of recrystallised TD
nickel were compared [ 40] using Orowan bowing and
particle shear theory. Agreements with experimental
results were closer with Orowan mechanisms.
Mechanism by dislocation climb can be seen at high
temperature above 0.5 T m. Normally at that tempera-
ture, oxidation-a ﬀected erosion prevails ahead of
metallic erosion. During operation of Orowan
mechanisms, each dislocation passes through the dis-
persoid by leaving a loop around the dispersoid.
Thus, increase in strain hardening exponent is sig-
niﬁcant for dispersion strengthened alloys where the
Orowan mechanism operates.
Even for a precipitation hardening system, the
strain hardening exponent can be increased. This
can be achieved if the Orowan mechanism is opera-
tive in the system. Prevalence of the Orowan
mechanism in many respects is dependent on the
size of the precipitates. Work due to Del Valle et al.
[41] revealed that when the γ′ size is greater than
about 50 nm, Orowan looping is the bypass
mechanism. On the other hand, when the γ′ size
is less than about 50 nm, precipitate shearing is the
controlling mechanism. T hus, the strain hardening
exponent can be increased and consequently ero-
sion rate can be reduced by having a γ′ precipitate
size above 50 nm.

Furthermore, chromia scales are not protective

that alumina scales usually formed below 900°C are
gamma phases. Although gamma alumina is also
protective, its rate of growth typically is similar to
that of chromia. However, adiabatic deformation
conditions under erosion may be responsible for the
formation of an alpha alumina scale even below 900°
C during erosion. At present, understanding in this
regard is far from satisfactory and certainly more
extensive study is desirable. To form alumina scales,
it is usual to have more than 15% Cr and more than
3% aluminium. Actually, oxidation-resistant protec-
tive ﬁlms are obtained by selective oxidation of Ni-
base superalloys [ 53]. Only Al, Cr and Si are the three
elements which can form protective scales by selective
oxidation. Selective oxidation depends on many fac-
tors such as alloy composition, alloy surface condi-
tion, and gas composition. Oxide maps indicating
types of scale as a function of composition for the
Ni–Cr–Al system are available. The presence of oxide
particles also promotes selective oxidation [ 54].
However, as the operating temperature is increasing,
Ni-base superalloys have been designed to have
improved mechanical properties at elevated tempera-
ture at the expense of oxidation resistance. Thus,
protection against the environment is primarily
achieved by applying coatings which form alumina
scales on exposure to elevated temperature.
Investigation by Chinnadurai and Bahadur [ 47] indi-
cates that pure Cr 2O3 scale forming Haynes 188 alloy
has higher erosion rate at elevated temperature than
Waspaloy which forms a scale containing Cr 2O3 and
Al2O3. It is possible that at the highest temperature of
testing, the presence of alumina scale keeps erosion
mechanism of Waspaloy in oxidation-a ﬀected ero-
sion regime whereas pure chromia scale is responsi-
ble for oxidation-controlled erosion for Haynes 188
alloy. As mentioned earlier, erosion rate in oxidation-
controlled erosion regime is higher than the erosion
rate in oxidation-a ﬀected erosion regime and hence
erosion rate of Haynes 188 alloy is higher than that of
Waspalloy.

Many times, the oxide layer that is formed can
actually consist of two or more layers [ 78]. An inner
porous layer and an outer columnar layer constitute
the scale. This outer columnar layer is quite resistant
to erosion particularly at glancing angle. In contrast,
an inner porous layer has a high erosion rate and is
not beneﬁcial at all. Alloys should be designed in such
a way that inner porous layers do not form. Even if
they are formed, they should be very thin and
beneath a thick columnar outer layer. Some oxide
scales may be segmented or compacted. During
elevated temperature erosion, where dynamic oxida-
tion conditions prevail, scales often relieve stresses by
cracking. Although the scales are cracked, they still
remain adherent to the substrate. The scale under
such conditions is called a segmented scale. For
example, the addition of Si to low Cr steel results in
the formation of segmented scale [ 79]. The erosion
rate of segmented scale is lower than thick continu-
ous dense scale. This is because of the fact that area of
degradation is con ﬁned to low crystallite sizes in the
segmented scale in contrast to large area degradation
of dense continuous compact scale.

An important shortcoming of present analysis is
the fact that all strength related parameters are taken
from uniaxial slow strain tensile testing. Actually,
erosion takes place under completely di ﬀerent defor-
mation conditions. During erosive wear, materials
deform under multiaxial compressive stress, at high
strain rate and high strain [ 91,92]. As the contact
time between the impacting particle and the eroding
material is only momentary, there is hardly any time
for the impact generated heat, to dissipate. Thus, the
deformation during erosive wear is completely adia-
batic. Mechanical properties of Ni-base alloys under
such conditions are not available. These data are
essential even for oxide scale and composite layer,
to predict the elevated temperature erosion behaviour
of Ni-base superalloys with higher reliability, repro-
ducibility and accuracy. Thus, systematic investiga-
tion of high strain rate deformation behaviour of Ni-
base alloys and oxide scales at elevated temperature
should be predominant in future work. An important
area which has long been neglected is assessment of
kinetics of oxidation during combined action of ero-
sion and oxidation. This area needs to be addressed
in a systematic way. Future research should invest
time and resource for characterising the oxidation
kinetics during high temperature erosion.
Another important issue is the non-availability of
a comprehensive database. In practice, carrying out
elevated temperature erosion testing is not only
highly time consuming but also extremely onerous.
This is further aggravated by di ﬃculty in keeping the
test equipment in working condition. This forces
investigators to minimise data generation and diverts
interest in other areas. The lack of an extensive data-
base has prevented more general and more accurate
predictions and alloy design. This is further com-
pounded by the fact that experimental conditions of
diﬀerent investigators vary widely, making it di ﬃcult
to carry out appropriate comparison.
Perusal of most literature and experimental
results with various coatings and materials sug-
gests that most prevalent mechanism of elevated
temperature erosion is oxidation-a ﬀected erosion.
However, the nature of the composite layer that
forms during erosion can really vary over a wide
range. Pauschitz et al. [ 93], with a series of sys-
tematic experiments, demonstrated the formation
of various types of layer during sliding wear at
elevated temperature. Similar variation in compo-
site layer formation is expected even in elevated
temperature erosion. When low melting materials
such as lead and aluminium are exposed at a
temperature slightly higher than the room
temperature, the materials become very soft.
Consequently, a composite layer is expected to
contain signi ﬁcantly higher amounts of erodent
particles than oxide scale. In such cases, the nat-
ure of the composite layer and erosion response
will be governed largely by the counter body
materials rather than oxi de scale. The possibility
of the formation of a similar layer is, however,
quite remote for Ni-base alloys.
Among the proposed mechanisms, oxide erosion is
the least possible mechanism. It is not only very
diﬃcult to simulate operating condition suitable for
oxide erosion during laboratory testing; it is also very
diﬃcult to come across an oxide erosion regime in
practice. This is despite the fact that an oxide erosion
regime can be bene ﬁcial especially at very high tem-
perature. Another possible regime which can be of
academic interest is the melt erosion regime. This
regime is not reported in the literature for similar
reasons. For the sake of brevity and for the sake of
simplicity such a regime is not considered here.
In this work, emphasis is given from the point of
view of the characteristics of eroding materials. It is
possible to alter the erosion condition and thus
change the prevailing mechanism considerably. The
design criteria in such a case will vary considerably.
For example, if the particle size is reasonably high, it
is possible to have oxidation-a ﬀected erosion ahead of
oxidation-controlled erosion for a wider range of
temperature and for a wider range of oxide scale. In
addition, if the particle feed rate can be increased, or
the system can be operated under a condition of
higher particle feed rate, it is possible to get a metal
erosion regime having the least erosion rate, for
higher range of temperature and impact velocity.
Oxidation-controlled erosion increases rapidly with
increase in temperature. In contrast, oxide erosion
is relatively independent of temperature or decreases
with temperature. Thus, if the operating condition
can be controlled in such a way that at high tempera-
ture oxide erosion prevails, erosion rate can be lower
than otherwise prevailing oxidation-controlled ero-
sion. Unfortunately, the possibilities of controlling
erosion conditions are low or almost nonexistent.
This area is not covered in the present manuscript
and is beyond the scope of this work. It will be
considered in a separate study.

Conclusions
(1) Both melting temperature and strain harden-
ing exponent can improve erosion resistance
as long as erosion occurs in the metal erosion
regime.
(2) Erosion rate decreases in the oxidation-
aﬀected erosion regime with the formation of
Al2O3 scale. This scale is better than even a
mixed oxide scale containing Al 2O3 and
Cr2O3. Improvement in adhesion characteris-
tics of the oxide scale also leads to increased
erosion resistance in this regime.
(3) In the oxidation-controlled erosion regime, the
erosion rate is low with formation of Al 2O3 or
Cr2O3 scale. Cr2O3 scale exhibits lower erosion
rate than Al 2O3 scale for a given adhesion
characteristic, that is achieved by dispersoids.
Improvement in adhesion characteristics of the
oxide scale also leads to increased erosion
resistance.
(4) In all erosion regimes, erosion rate increases
with increase in test temperature. However,
erosion rate decreases with temperature in
the oxide erosion regime.
(5) Erosion resistance in oxide erosion regime
improves with increase in hardness and frac-
ture toughness of the oxide scale. This indi-
cates that the formation of Al 2O3 scale is
beneﬁcial even in the oxide erosion regime.
