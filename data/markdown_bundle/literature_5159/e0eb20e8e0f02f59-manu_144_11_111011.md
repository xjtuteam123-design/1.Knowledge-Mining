Enhanced Strength and
High-Temperature Wear
Resistance of Ti6Al4V Alloy
Fabricated by Laser Solid
Forming
Strengthening titanium alloys and mitigating their wear degradation at various tempera-
tures is the key to industrial applications. In this study, high-performance Ti6Al4V alloy
(here, Ti with 6.25 wt% Al and 4.17 wt% V) was prepared by laser solid forming (LSF).
The phase and microstructure of the Ti6Al4V alloys were systematically examined, and
the overall improved mechanical properties were achieved. The coef ﬁcient of friction
(CoF) and wear of LSFed Ti6Al4V alloy against WC-6%Co cemented carbide using a
ball-on-disc tribometer at various temperatures revealed a temperature-dependent wear
mechanism. During the experiment, the CoF of LSFed Ti6Al4V would increase initially
and then decrease with the increased temperature. This trend is determined by the abrasive
wear dominating at a lower temperature, and it gradually changes to oxidative wear and
fatigue wear under higher temperatures. LSF provides a promising way to achieve
supreme mechanical and tribological properties in Ti6Al4V simultaneously.

1 Introduction
Titanium (Ti) and its alloys show higher speci ﬁc strength, lower
density, excellent corrosion resistance, and high-temperature dur-
ability, and are irreplaceable in aerospace and biological medicine
industries [ 1–3]. Currently, Ti6Al4V (Ti as a matrix with 5.5 –
6.75 wt% Al and 3.5 –4.5 wt% V) is the most widely used titanium
alloy, accounting for about half of the world ’s market share of tita-
nium products. However, owing to their high melting point, high
reactivity, and poor high-temperature creep resistance, it is dif ﬁcult
to machine titanium alloys traditionally [ 4]. Moreover, the exten-
sive need for complex geometries in aerospace industries has
further caused dif ﬁculties in alloy processing and forming, and
the machining accuracy cannot be guaranteed.
Additive manufacturing technology provides a feasible solution
for the preparation of hollow con ﬁguration of fan blade topology
optimization. Present commercial additive manufacturing tech-
niques could fabricate metals and alloys in a facile and economic
fashion with geometry design ﬂexibility, making the products of
novel lightweight fan blades readily possible.
Additive manufacturing technologies are now considered as the
most effective way to realize the processing and forming of Ti
alloy with overall balanced advantages [ 5]. At present, a variety
of additive manufacturing technology systems, such as selective
laser melting (SLM) [ 6], selective laser sintering [ 7], laser
melting deposition [ 8], and laser solid forming (LSF) [ 9,10] have
been developed. LSF was developed in the early 1990s and
achieved mold-free, fast, fully dense, and near-net-shape manufac-
turing of metal parts with high-performance and complex structures.
The basic principle of LSF technology consists of two aspects. First,
the three-dimensional shape of the part is converted into a series of
two-dimensional layer information. Then, the metal powder
material is melted layer by layer using synchronous powder
feeding laser cladding [ 11,12].
Since the mechanical properties of 3D printed alloys greatly
determine the application of additively manufactured parts, the
inﬂuence of preparation process parameters, element composition,
and post-treatment process [ 11,12] on the mechanical properties
of Ti6Al4V alloy by additive manufacturing has become the
focus. Lu et al. [ 13] investigated the in ﬂuence of laser power, scan-
ning speed, and feeding rate on the thermal and mechanical
responses of Ti6Al4V alloy during the LSF. Though the scanning
speed has a minor in ﬂuence on the thermal response, the increasing
scanning speed is conducive to the uniformity of the temperature
ﬁeld, the strain localization reduction, and the stress concentration
mitigation. Zhao et al. [14] investigated different section geometries
and found that the fast-cooling condition leads to the ﬁne basket-
weave microstructure, while the colony microstructure is obtained
under the slow cooling rate. Besides, different orientations of lath-
like α-phase in ﬁne basket-weave microstructure could effectively
reduce the length of dislocation slip and result in higher strength
and ductility. Shi et al. [ 15] investigated the effect of minor Fe
content on the mechanical properties of Ti6Al4V fabricated by
LSF technology and concluded that the tensile properties of the spe-
cimen along the deposition direction are enhanced with the increase
of Fe content. Zhao et al. [ 16] found that the post-treatment pro-
cesses could make Ti6Al4V alloys more ductile. After three post-
treatment processes, its total elongation and tensile strength can
reach 25.1% and 860 MPa, respectively. The gradual spheroidiza-
tion of the internal lath-like α-phase during the subcritical annealing
contributes signiﬁcantly. Lu et al. [ 17] systematically correlated the
annealing temperature and annealing time to the microstructure
evolution and microstructure characteristics of Ti6Al4V alloy by
LSF during the subcritical annealing. The results showed that the
size, volume fraction, and aspect ratio of the primary α-phase are
mainly affected by the annealing process. However, the width
and volume fraction of the secondary α-phase is mainly affected
by the aging process. In addition, when the annealing temperature

Except for these direct mechanical property analyses, wear
caused by friction is also one of the main reasons for Ti6Al4V ’s
failure. The poor wear resistance of Ti6Al4V alloy limits its appli-
cation in various ﬁelds [18,19]. Therefore, it is of great signi ﬁcance
to reveal the tribological properties of Ti6Al4V alloy under differ-
ent working conditions. Recently, scholars have carried out relevant
studies on the friction and wear properties to illustrate the tribolog-
ical mechanics of Ti6Al4V alloys prepared by different additive
manufacturing processes. Marquer et al. [ 20] investigated the tribo-
logical properties of Ti6Al4V alloy prepared SLM technology and
found that the average coefﬁcient of friction (CoF) was independent
of SLM orientation. Compared with machining parts, SLMed parts
had higher CoF and lower wear-rates. Chandramohan et al. [ 21]
investigated how heat treatment tunes wear resistance of Ti6Al4V
by direct metal laser sintering, and the test results showed that
grain re ﬁnement could reduce wear-rate. Li et al. [ 22] found that
the wear-rate of Ti6Al4V by laser powder bed fusion (PBF), elec-
tron beam PBF, and traditional processing has little variation
under the same test condition, even with different microstructures
and hardness values.
In addition to these phenomenal observations, the ultimate
reasons for wear and friction behavior change in Ti6Al4V have
also been studied. Li et al. [ 23] compared the tribological properties
of commercial Ti6Al4V and Ti6.5Al3.5Mo1.5Zr0.3Si alloys at
room temperature. They found that the wear regularity of alloys
increased with the increase of sliding velocity and contact pressure.
Notably, the author con ﬁrmed that the formation of tribo-oxide
layers led to mild wear at high-speed. Niu et al. [ 24] further
observed that the CoF and wear-rate of commercial Ti6Al4V
alloy under the water lubrication (i.e., with more oxygen) were
lower than under dry conditions. Philip et al.[ 25] found that under
the combined conditions of low load (50 –100 N) and low tempera-
ture (25 –200 °C), the effect of tribo-oxide layer of Ti6Al4V alloy
was negligible. Nevertheless, under high load (100 –200 N) and
high temperature (200–500 °C), the tribo-oxide layer as a protective
layer to wear came into being. Therefore, oxidative wear seems to
be the primary wear mechanism of Ti6Al4V alloy.
However, the generality of this conclusion is doubtful, particu-
larly when the severe working conditions of aerospace have
higher requirements on the tribological properties of Ti6Al4V
alloy. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanical
properties and tribological properties of the Ti6Al4V alloy prepared
by LSF is urgently needed for these industrial applications. In par-
ticular, given the lack of study into the LSFed Ti6Al4V alloy, the
precise description of tribological properties of Ti6Al4V alloy by
the LSF at different tested temperatures is desired. Thus, this
work aims to systematically study the tribological behaviors and
wear mechanism of Ti6Al4V alloy by LSF. To assess the advan-
tages of LSFed Ti6Al4V alloy more accurately, the performance
of Ti6Al4V alloy fabricated by the traditional process has also
been compared. Besides, the phase composition and microstructure
of the alloy were analyzed. Based on the results of the phase evolu-
tion, microstructural change, and enhanced mechanical properties,
the wear mechanism of Ti6Al4V at different temperatures was
made precise.
2 Material and Methods
In this study, commercial spherical Ti6Al4V powder produced
by the plasma rotating electrode process was used as the base mate-
rial. Table 1 presents the chemical composition of Ti6A4V powder.
To reduce the in ﬂuence of powder size on the performance of the
forming sample, the powder was sieved before preparation. The
powder size distribution was concentrated in the range of
74–165 µm after sieving, as listed in Table 2. At the same time,
the powder is dried for 2 h to avoid the problem of lower ﬂowability
due to moisture.
The forming Ti6Al4V alloy (hereafter referred to as sample A)
was produced by commercial LSF equipment (Xi ’ an Bright
Laser Technologies Corp., BLT-C1000, Xi ’ an, China), which
includes a Yb-Fiber laser with a maximum power of 4000 W and
a double-channel constant ﬂow powder feeder. The forming
process was protected by high-purity argon gas to prevent oxida-
tion. The forming strategy adopted a rotation angle of 90 deg
between the N +1 layer and the N layer scan line. According to
Table 3 forming parameters, the Ti6Al4V alloy with dimensions
of 140 mm × 25 mm × 110 mm was built. After forming, the
sample was treated with solution and aging heat treatments at
930 °C for 2 h, then air cooling, and 550 °C for 4 h and air
cooling [15]. We have to point out that this post-LSF heat treatment
temperature is high enough to relieve the residual stress and simul-
taneously produce equiaxial grains, which will both make the
LSF-induced heterogeneity negligible. So, our microstructural and
mechanical performance study focusing on the in-plane direction
is representative enough, and the building direction dependence is
also trivial [ 26,27]. Figure 1 illustrates the sequential procedures
of the preparation process for sample A. At the same time,
Ti6Al4V alloy with dimensions of φ50 mm was produced by a tra-
ditional forging process and in accordance with the National Stan-
dard (GB/T 2965-2007, China) as a comparative sample (hereafter
referred to as sample B), which is produced by Dongguan Zhenyu
Mould steel Co., Ltd., Huangshi, China.

Czech Republic) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS,

3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Microstructure. XRD diffraction patterns of samples A
and B are depicted in Fig. 3. Based on the corresponding relation-
ship of the phase peak intensity, samples A and B are mainly com-
posed of hexagonal α/α’-Ti phase and a small amount of cubic β-Ti
phase. Since the characteristic peaks of the α phase and the α’ phase
are similar, it is dif ﬁcult to distinguish them [ 20]. Hence, α-Ti and
α’-Ti are represented in the form of α/α’-Ti in Fig. 3. In general, the
growth of the α phase in the alloy is affected by the solidi ﬁcation
process and the thermal cycle process. When the temperature in
the molten pool is lower than the alloy phase transition temperature,
the β phase will transform into the α phase, and the α phase will ﬁrst
transform into the unstable martensite α’ phase at a higher cooling
rate. However, when the temperature rises, the α’ phase is prone to
decompose, forming ﬁne α and β phases. As shown in Fig. 3, the
lattice orientation of sample A consists of (101), (100), (102), and
a small amount (002), whereas the (102), (110), and (103) crystal
planes co-exist in the α-lattice planes of sample B. In addition,
there is a difference in the main orientation of their grains: (101)
and (002) crystal plane dominates in samples A and B, respectively.
Interestingly, the β phase of both alloys is dominated by the (110)
crystal plane. Comparing the diffraction peaks of Fig. 3 to the stan-
dard powder diffraction peaks, it can be found that the ratios of the
three strong peaks (100), (002), and (101) of samples A and B have
changed signiﬁcantly, indicating that the possibility of texture in the
sample has increased with the different temperature-dependent pro-
cessing routes.
The SEM morphology and main elements content of samples A
and B are illustrated in Fig. 4. Although Fig. 3 shows that both
samples A and B are composed of the α and β phases, the SEM mor-
phology reveals the differences in the microstructural morphology
of the two samples. The microstructure of sample A has lath-like
or short rod-like morphology (Fig. 4(a)), which is randomly distrib-
uted and has no ﬁxed direction. Moreover, the α phase is randomly
interlaced in the β phase. When the laser beam heats the titanium
alloy powder from the top, it is equivalent to reheating the previ-
ously deposited layers in the alloy structure. Therefore, each layer
of the alloy structure is subjected to multiple rapid heating and
cooling. This will result in the transformation of the β phase to
the α phase and the rapid growth of primary α phases into a lath-like
structure simultaneously. The short rod-like shape of the β phase is
formed due to its slow growth rate. As a result, the microstructure of
sample A is dense, and the volume fraction of the α phase is higher
than that of the β phase. The microstructural characterization also
conﬁrms that micro-defects such as cracks and voids are not
found inside the alloy. In comparison, the α phase and the β
phase of sample B exhibit equiaxial grains with a uniform distribu-
tion (Fig. 4(b)).

3.2 Mechanical Properties. The true stress-strain curves of
the tensile specimen of samples A and B are depicted in Fig. 5.
The mechanical properties of samples A and B are listed in
Table 4. The ultimate tensile strength ( σb) and yield strength (YS,
σ0.2) of sample A are 1126.0 ± 54.7 MPa and 1055.0 ± 55.3 MPa,
respectively, which are greater than the tensile strength (979 ±
62.6 MPa) and yield strength (908 ± 64.9 MPa) of sample
B. Meanwhile, though comparable, the ductility ( δ) of sample A
(8.0 ± 0.3%) is smaller than that of sample B (11.0 ± 0.9%). A3
and B3 samples show a larger ﬂuctuation in their average mechan-
ical property value, mainly because of the inevitable vertical pro-
cessing difference along building direction (since we chose the
horizontal specimen, as shown in Fig. 2) and the localized materi-
als’ response during LSF processing. Since the ﬂuctuation is
within ∼5%, this will not affect the validity of our mechanical
strength data, as compared with Refs. [ 28,29].
The tensile strength and yield strength in the sample are mainly
impacted by the width of the α martensite structure. Martensite
with a smaller width has many grain boundaries (as shown in
Fig. 4(a), the width of α martensite of sample A is about
2.25 µm), which prevents dislocation slip between grains and
improves the tensile strength and yield strength of the sample. On
the other hand, α’ martensite in titanium alloys is a soft phase,
and its strength is almost the same as the α phase. The β phase
becomes the strengthening phase after aging, which will enhance
the mechanical properties. Hence, the higher the content of the β
phase, the higher the tensile strength and yield strength [ 30]. Rele-
vant studies have shown that equiaxed crystals can effectively
inhibit the rapid formation of cracks and alleviate the rapid
growth of cracks to a certain extent [ 27]. Hence, the ductility of
sample B containing equiaxed grains during the tensile test is
higher than that of sample A. Table 4 also gives the microhardness
and Young’s modulus (E) of samples A and B for comparison.
3.3 Friction and Wear Properties. Figure 6 shows the time-
dependent curves of the CoF of samples A and B against the
WC-6%Co ball at the different test temperatures. The CoF of
samples A and B ﬁrst increases quickly, then decreases with
certain ﬂuctuations, and ﬁnally increases steadily (Figs. 6(a) and
6(b)) with the prolonged test time at different temperatures. The
average CoF of sample A is 0.403 and 0.405 under the test condi-
tions of 25 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Compared with other test
results (400 °C, 600 °C), the time to enter the stable period of
25 °C and 200 °C is shorter. As the test temperature is increased,
the average CoF of sample A changes to 0.315 (400 °C) and
0.239 (600 °C). Although the average CoF is smaller, the value
ﬂuctuates signi ﬁcantly during the stable period, indicating severe
physical and chemical changes on the friction surface, and a
stable transfer ﬁlm cannot be built. The average CoF of sample B
is 0.394 (25 °C), 0.405 (200 °C), 0.413 (400 °C), and 0.375
(600 °C), respectively. Compared with the CoF evolution trend at
the other test temperatures, the CoF of sample B always shows a
signiﬁcant upward trend during the stable period at 600 °C. As
shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), the stability of the CoF value of
sample B is better than that of sample A, proving that sample B
can form a stable friction lubrication system at different
temperatures.

with the increase of temperature. Besides, the alloy will gradually

wear debris enters into the pits or grooves of wear surfaces,
which lubricates the ball-disk system. Although, there are differ-

better than that of sample B at 600 °C test temperature.
4 Conclusions
This paper has systematically studied the mechanical properties
of LSF-fabricated Ti6Al4V alloy and emphasized its tuned high-
temperature wear resistance by considering the surface morphology
and compositional evolution in the matrix and near the surface. The
results cast light on the fundamental reasons to introduce the differ-
ent tribological performances in LSF-Ti6Al4V are summarized as
follows:
(1) The microstructure and mechanical properties of Ti6Al4V
prepared by laser solid forming technology (sample A) and
Ti6Al4V prepared by the forging process (sample B) were
examined. α/α’ and β phases were observed based on XRD
pattern analysis. Still, there was a difference in the orienta-
tion of the composition α/α’ alloy in samples A and
B. Sample A was composed of the lath-like or short
rod-like crystal structure with a grain width of 2.25 μm.
Sample B was composed of equiaxed crystals with a grain
diameter of 9.5 μm. The aforementioned difference in micro-
structure and phase gave a higher yield strength, tensile
strength, and Young ’s modulus in sample A than that of
sample B except for the ductility.
(2) The tribological properties of samples A and B under various
testing temperatures were thoroughly investigated. The
average CoF of samples A and B initially increased and
then decreased with the increased temperature. When the
temperature was below 200 °C, the CoF of samples A and
B were close, while the CoF of sample A was smaller than
sample B at 400 °C and 600 °C. Furthermore, the wear
volume of the two samples was the largest at 600 °C.
(3) The wear mechanism of samples A and B was clari ﬁed.
Groove and lip marks were found on the worn surface at
25 °C testing temperature, indicating an abrasive wear mech-
anism. According to SEM morphology and EDS results, the
fracture, crank marks, and oxygen elements increased, and
the groove marks decreased gradually with the increased
testing temperature. The failure forms were fatigue fracture
and oxidative damage. Therefore, with the increased testing
temperature, the wear mechanism of samples A and B was
dominated by abrasive wear and gradually changed to oxida-
tive wear and fatigue wear.
(4) By comparing the microstructure, mechanical properties, and
tribological properties of samples A and B, it showed that
part of the properties of Ti6Al4V alloy prepared by the
LSF technology could reach or even outcompete those of
Ti6Al4V alloy produced by the traditional forging processes.
