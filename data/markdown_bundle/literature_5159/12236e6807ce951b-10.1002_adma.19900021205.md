The Performance of Super alloys ** 
By Tom B. Gibbons* 
1. Introduction 
The term ‘superalloys’ has been used to describe alloys 
based on the Ni-Cr, Ni-Cr-Fe, or Co-Cr systems which are 
characterized by high strength and good resistance to envi- 
ronmental attack at elevated temperature. The strongest al- 
loys in this category, and the most widely used, are those 
based on the Ni-Cr system and this review will be limited to 
consideration of superalloys in this class. The earliest super- 
alloys viz the Nimonics, were developed by the Mond Nickel 
Company (now INCO) in Birmingham, England in response 
to the need for improved materials for the early jet engines, 
and Nimonic 80A was patented in 1940 for blade applica- 
tions in aero gas turbines. These relatively simple alloys rep- 
resented a major advance in high-temperature load-carrying 
capacity compared to the austenitic steels that had been used 
hitherto and, although it was not fully appreciated at the 
time, the key factor was the influence of the intermetallic 
compound, Ni3(Al,Ti), y’, in conferring high-temperature 
strength.[‘’The strengthening effect of additions of A1 and Ti 
had earlier been recognized by Chevenardl’] working on 
austenitic steels for high-temperature applications. The im- 
portant, and unusual property of this intermetallic com- 
pound is that the flow stress increases with temperature to a 
maximum at around 600 “C. Consequently the Ni-Cr-alloys 
exhibit no deterioration in tensile strength at temperatures 
up to 700°C and this behavior is illustrated in Figure 1, 
where the tensile properties of a wrought and a cast superal- 
loy are compared with those for two high-strength austenitic 
steels. The benefits in creep performance are clear from 
Table 1 where it can be seen that the superalloys can be used 
to a much higher proportion of the absolute melting temper- 
ature than other comparable materials. 
The volume fraction of this strengthening phase in these 
early alloys was - 20% and, as demand grew for stronger 
materials, alloy development led to the establishment of al- 
loys with a volume fraction of y‘ of up to 70 %. Fabrication 
difficulties have limited the maximum y‘ volume fraction in 
wrought alloys to - 45 % and components in the strongest

In the present paper the current status of superalloy tech- 
nology is outlined and the potential for further incremental 
improvement in critical areas is briefly considered. The im- 
portant topic of computer-aided engineering was judged too 
specialized an issue for detailed consideration here. Also, in 
reviewing developments and requirements in mechanical 
performance, the discussion has been limited to creep behav- 
ior, a topic which has increased in importance with the 
higher turbine-entry temperatures in both aero and industri- 
al engines. 
2. Processing 
2.1. Superclean Technology 
In developing techniques to produce materials of in- 
creased purity and cleanness the aim has been to minimize 
the effects of defects such as oxide inclusions in reducing the 
intrinsic strength of alloys used in critical applications. Cur- 
rently, novel melting techniques are being evaluated for the 
production of large quantities of material with low defect 
content and one of the most promising methods, namely 
electron-beam cold-hearth refining (EBCHR), has shown 
encouraging results for wrought alloys. Recent workL4] on 
IN 71 8 and on PK 50 (similar to Waspaloy) has confirmed 
that after EBCHR the gas content was reduced and the 
cleanness level enhanced. The results of low-cycle fatigue 
testing on IN 71 8 showed a considerable improvement in the 
cleaner material and there was no deleterious effect on the 
other mechanical properties. However, in earlier work[5] on 
the cast alloy IN738LC, EBCHR produced little benefit 
either in terms of casting performance or in mechanical 
properties although cleanness levels were enhanced some- 
what compared with material produced by conventional 
methods. In this case, the melting technology used was less 
advanced than that now available and it may in part account 
for the somewhat disappointing results obtained. 
Plasma melting is seen as a technique which is complemen- 
tary to electron-beam melting for the production of super- 
clean materials but hitherto has not been widely applied to 
superalloys. The presence of a gaseous environment (usually 
argon), which can be adjusted to be reactive, reduces vapour- 
ization losses so that control of alloy chemistry is inherently 
easier. The main characteristics of the two melting tech- 
niques have recently been reviewed.[61 
One consequence of the availability of alloys with in- 
creased levels of cleanness is that the number and size of 
inclusions routinely present in the product are below the 
levels that can be reliably detected and measured by normal 
methods of metallography or non-destructive evaluation 
(NDE). However, even at these very low levels, variations in 
defect characteristics can result in very significant differences 
in material or component performance. Thus, efforts have 
been made to develop new methods of cleanness assessment, 
and one of the most promising of these is electron-beam 
button melting. In this process, a sample (about 1 kg) of 
superalloy is melted under carefully controlled conditions 
and then allowed to solidify, again in a controlled fashion, so 
that a raft containing the inclusions is formed on the surface. 
Raft size is then used as a measure of cleanness and analyti- 
cal methods such as cathodo-luminescence can be used to 
identify individual particles. Preliminary findings indicate 
that, provided the processing conditions are controlled, the 
procedure will provide a ranking of alloy cleanness and has 
potential for development to give a more quantitative mea- 
sure of defect content.[71

2.2. Powder Fabrication 
The inherently fine grain size of powder products has been 
a major advantage in the main components produced from 
this type of material, viz high-pressure turbine discs. Howev- 
er, with increasing gas temperatures the creep resistance of 
turbine materials has become a more important parameter 
and a somewhat coarser grain size in discs produced from 
powder has become a desirable characteristic. In recent work 
on low-carbon Astroloy[81 the grain growth characteristics 
of powder produced by argon atomization (AA) and by the 
rotating electrode process (REP), and consolidated by hot 
isostatic pressing, have been investigated. The grain growth 
at higher temperatures evident in the REP powders (Fig. 3) 
occurs because the precipitate particles at the prior particle 
boundaries (ppb's) are larger in this material than in the AA 
version and are less effective in pinning the grain boundaries. 
Further grain growth would presumably be possible if the 
carbide decoration of the ppb's could be reduced. As expect- 
ed, the somewhat larger grain size of the REP gave a signif- 
icant improvement in creep rupture performance compared 
with the AA material.

3. Factors Affecting Creep Performance 
3.1. Microstructure 
Although modern superalloys have compositions which 
are complex and usually empirically defined, the microstruc- 
tural constitution in service is relatively simple and, in addi- 
tion to various types of carbide, consists mainly of a disper- 
sion of the ordered y' precipitate Ni,(Al,Ti) in a matrix of y, 
essentially a Ni-Cr-Co solid solution. With the early 
wrought alloys, great care was taken to develop heat treat- 
ments to optimize the y' size and distribution to give maxi- 
mum values of short-term properties. By contrast, the first 
cast alloys with a substantially increased content of precipi- 
tate were used in the as-cast condition. Modern cast alloys 
are heat-treated, and it is particularly important to establish 
the appropriate distribution of grain boundary carbides to 
ensure an adequate balance between strength and ductility. 
It is generally agreed that optimum creep resistance will be 
obtained with a large volume fraction of relatively large 
(- 1000 nm) y' particles. The influence of volume fraction of 
y' on creep performance is illustrated["] in Figure 4 for the 
wrought alloy Nimonic 101 and, for the coarser grain size, 
the creep rate is reduced by a factor of - 100 when the 
volume fraction of precipitate is increased from - 3% to

4. Advances in Corrosion Protection 
4.1. Conventional Coatings 
The relatively low content of chromium in the stronger 
superalloys gives insufficient corrosion resistance so that ad- 
ditional protection is necessary for most practical applica- 
tions. Aluminized coatings are widely used to protect blades 
and vanes against oxidation or sulphidation attack and when 
the pulsed vapor-phase process is used, internal cooling pas- 
sages can also be protected. Addition of chromium or silicon 
will improve the performance of the aluminides, and the 
introduction of noble metals, e.g. platinum, will improve 
coating stability. The platinum aluminides are more stable so 
that the rate of diffusion of aluminum into the substrate is 
reduced and coatings of this type are used in aero and indus- 
trial engines where long-term protection is required. Various 
additional modifications, including the incorporation of 
“active” elements, e.g. yttrium, lanthanum etc., to reduce 
spalling will bring further improvements. 
In recent laboratory experiments Rapp[301 has demon- 
strated the feasibility of producing diffusion coatings by 
simultaneous chromizing-aluminizing. The development is 
based on the thermodynamic principles involved and the key 
parameters are the choice of master-alloy composition and 
the use of a chloride carrier so that optimized amounts of 
chromium and aluminum can be co-deposited in a pack pro- 
cess. 
The more advanced overlay coatings (usually CoCrAlY) 
can in principle provide greater protection but problems as- 
sociated with cracking during thermal cycling and interac- 
tion with the substrate have limited the successful applica- 
tion of coatings of this type. Recent work has demonstrated 
the feasibility of controlling the through-thickness composi- 
tion of overlay coatings to provide the basis for the design of 
optimized coatings.[311 A satisfactory overlay must satisfy 
two potentially conflicting requirements, viz., good adhesion

resent a compromise and are not optimized to meet either 
surface or interface requirements. The development of 
tailored or graded coatings takes advantage of the flexibility 
of the processing routes for overlay coatings which allows 
changes in composition to be arranged such that optimized 
properties are obtained at both the surface and the substrate 
interface. The process involves the simultaneous deposition 
from three separate sources by electron-beam evaporation 
using microprocessor control based on evaporation rate to 
give compositional accuracy. A key factor is that the compo- 
sition can be adjusted to ensure an adequate level of coating 
ductility at the surface thereby reducing the tendency for 
cracking. The technique for producing graded coatings is at 
an early stage of development and further progress will in- 
volve the incorporation of active elements such as yttrium.

5. Conclusions 
1. The superalloys have reached an advanced stage of devel- 
opment but there remains some potential for incremental 
improvements in key areas. 
2. There is a current need for more sophisticated measures 
of performance to provide input data for advanced design 
methodologies and this is particularly true for complex 
stress situations. 
3 Exploitation of developments in coating technology will 
enhance performance in aggressive environments.
