The Possibility of Cr-base Alloys For High-temperature Applications

Keywords: Cr-base alloys, Ductility, Strength, High-temperature. 
Abstract: The mechanical properties of chromium (Cr) and Cr-base alloys are reviewed, with 
particular emphasis on the ductility at ambient temperature and t he strengths at high temperature. 
Analysis of rather scattered data and our recent results sugges t that Cr-base alloy can be ductilized 
greatly at ambient temperature and is quite capable of being str engthened to high levels at high 
temperature. New designs on composition would give high possibility to Cr-base alloys as structural 
materials used in high-temperature applications. 
Introduction 
The increased efficiency associated with higher operating temperature in gas turbines has 
prompted designers to search for new materials and techniques that can be used at temperature 
above the useful limits of Ni-base superalloys. The metal Cr has been considered as a possible base 
for alloy systems since the last forties due to its higher m elting point (1863 °C) than that of Ni-base 
superalloys. Its low density (20% less than most Ni-base alloys) and high thermal conductivity (two 
to four times higher than most superalloys) are also attractive ness to the benefit of the increasing 
efficiency [1-4]. Considerable effort has been made in order to expl ore the possibility of developing 
Cr-base alloys for high-temperature applications since the last of the fifties. However, the interest in 
Cr or Cr-base alloys was short-lived. Major disadvantages that h ave held back its commercial 
exploitation are the high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT), which for unalloyed 
recrystallize Cr with commercial purity is about 150 C in te nsion. Below the DBTT, Cr has 
complete a lack of ductility [5-7]. Therefore, Cr as a practica l alloy base has remained virtually 
unstudied since the late of last seventies. 
Recently, a new look has been taken at Cr-base alloy system s. Studies of the literature show that 
Cr, providing some of its disadvantages could be eliminated or minimized, actually has a rather 
attractive potential as an alloy-system base. This paper wil l review some research efforts directed 
toward improvement of the mechanical properties of Cr-base alloys. Among the major points 
emphasized here will be the considerations in improving the ambient duct ility and the strength at 
high temperatures.

Ductility at Room Temperature 
The brittle of Cr and Cr-base alloys at ambient temperature is a major barrier for their practical 
use as a high-temperature component, such as van or blade in gas-turbine engines. Some researchers 
believed that this brittleness is of intrinsic to Cr, which shows a sharp transition over a small range 
of temperature from ductility to brittleness. However, it has also been shown that electrolytic 
high-purity Cr exhibits adequate tensile and bend ductility at room temperature [8], which implies 
that Cr is certainly not inherently brittle at room temperature , and some external causes may 
influence the DBTT and the ductility at room temperature. These external factors mainly include 
impurities (nitrogen, carbon and oxygen et al.), surface condition and structure [9, 10]. 
Because the DBTT is lowered as purity increases, purification i s an obvious way to decrease the 
DBTT and thus increases the ductility at low temperature. Ac cording to theoretical and 
experimental observations, removal of nitrogen (and possible other simi lar elements) to extremely 
low levels (certainly < 0.0001%) should result in adequate ductility in all conditions [1]. Such 
purification is possible in principle, but it is doubtful whether it ca n be achieved and maintained in 
practice. 
Another obvious expedient for effectively improving the ductility at l ow temperature is either to 
stabilize or to remove the interstitial impurities (nitrogen, c arbon and oxygen et al.) by adding a 
scavenging element. Alloying addition may also decrease the DBTT and thus raise the ductility of 
Cr-base alloys. 
Some researchers investigated the elements (Ce, La, Hf, Th, Ti, U, Y , Zr…) that readily react 
with nitrogen in Cr during melting and casting, and found that Ce, Ti, Y and Zr additions promote 
room-temperature ductility in recrystallized Cr. Nb and Ta have also been shown to improve the 
ductility of fabricated and annealed Cr. Si is a relatively s luggish and probably weak nitride-former 
and Al has a small but definite improvement on the ductility of Cr . Boron addition can modify the 
nitride impurity in Cr after heat treatment and reduce the DBT T of impure cast Cr. Fe, Co, Re and 
Ru, which at large alloying concentrations promote ductility through the “Re duct ilizing effect”[11]. 
Recently, we found that adding Ag to the Cr with commercial purity (99.8 at%) can greatly improve 
its tensile ductility and Cr-2at%Ag is subjected to about 23% tensile elongation at room temperature 
that indicates that the production of ductile Cr-base alloys for us e at high temperatures is a definite 
possibility.

The Future of the Chromium Alloys 
Recently, the search for alloys capable of extended use at servi ce temperature in excess of 
Ni-base alloys, 1100 °C, has caused some interest in Cr-base alloys again. Ro et al. f ound that 
alloying with Re and W can make Cr-base alloy with enough duc tility at room temperature and 
superior high-temperature strength compared with existing Ni-bas e superalloy at 1200 °C [14]. Liu 
et al. [15] and Kumar et al. [16] suggested that the intermetall ic Cr 2X (X=Zr, Hf, Nb, or Ta) is more 
attractive than the carbide to strengthen Cr-base alloys since the available information indicates that 
it could form during ageing at a temperature near to the service temperature. The substantial 
progress in the development of high-temperature coating design and t ypes make it possible for 
protecting Cr from oxidation/nitrogen embrittlement during high-temper ature air exposure up to 
1300 °C. All these research light new ways to develop new Cr-base al loys which have good
