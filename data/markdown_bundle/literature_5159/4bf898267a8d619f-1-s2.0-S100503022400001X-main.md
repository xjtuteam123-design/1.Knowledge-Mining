The creep anisotropy of Ni-based single crystal superalloys was studied at 750 °C/750 MPa. Transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) observations showed that stacking faults and micro-twins were the typical 
dislocation conﬁgurations, which all originated from the activity of {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems. Then, the 
orientation rotation caused by the activity of the main {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip system would accelerate the spec- 
imen fracturing in shear fracture mode. Furthermore, the orientation rotation would dominate the creep 
anisotropy. The orientation rotation would occur during the entire creep process, the greater the degree 
of rotation, the more severe the asynchrony of rotation, which would result in nonuniform deformation 
and local stress concentration, and exacerbate the failure of the specimen.

1. Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys have been extensively used 
in advanced modern aero-engines, due to their excellent com- 
prehensive performance at elevated temperatures and inherent 
anisotropy [1] . Considering [001] is the preferred direction for 
crystal growth in Ni-based superalloys and its great mechanical 
properties, single crystal turbine blades usually are directionally 
solidiﬁed during the casting process with the crystallographic ori- 
entation [001] aligned with the blade stacking axis, and the stack- 
ing axis is usually denoted as the primary orientation [2–4] . Typi- 
cally, the predominant load experienced by the turbine blade sec- 
tion is centrifugal in nature (aligned along the stacking axis of the 
blade). Consequently, extensive research has been conducted on 
the mechanical properties of [001] oriented single crystals [ 5 , 6 ]. 
However, it is worth noting that the stacking axis of turbine blades 
does not always yield precisely [001] direction and deviations up 
to 15 ° are commonly accepted within the industry [7–9] . Further- 
more, the blade with complex geometries and intricate cooling 
channels routinely encounters extreme operating environmental 
conditions during service, resulting in localized multi-directional 
stresses and intricate thermal stresses [ 10 , 11 ]. Moreover, with the 
increasing thrust-to-weight ratio of aircraft engines, a thin-walled 
structure has been employed to reduce component weight and en- 
hance cooling eﬃciency, thereby exacerbating the complexity of 
blade service stress conditions [12] . Apart from centrifugal force,

turbine blades are also subjected to transverse stress. The orien- 
tation of the crystal under transverse stress is determined by the 
secondary orientation of the turbine blade. However, due to it be- 
ing neither speciﬁed nor controlled during the manufacturing pro- 
cess, the secondary orientation is randomly distributed with re- 
spect to the longitudinal direction of the turbine blade, resulting in 
a random crystal orientation under transverse stress [ 4 , 7 , 8 ]. There- 
fore, it is imperative to investigate the creep behavior of single 
crystals with other orientations to elucidate the damage mecha- 
nism of turbine blades during service. 
The inherent anisotropy of Ni-based single crystal superalloys 
has been manifested in all aspects of service performance, includ- 
ing creep, fatigue, tension, and corrosion behaviors [ 7 , 13-18 ]. The 
creep anisotropy is a signiﬁcant concern, especially when evaluat- 
ing the creep properties under conditions of low/intermediate tem- 
peratures and high stress [ 13 , 19-21 ]. The creep rupture life is intri- 
cately linked to the service temperature, external stress, γ’-phase 
size, and alloy composition. Mackay et al. [ 22 , 23 ] systematically in- 
vestigated the creep rupture properties of MAR-M247 with various 
crystal orientations at 774 °C/724 MPa, and the results showed that 
the creep rupture life ranked as [111] > [001] > [011]. Other alloys 
also observed similar characteristics, such as the second-generation 
superalloy CMSX-4 (750 °C/750 MPa, 850 °C/650 MPa) [24–26] , 
the third-generation superalloy DD33 (850 °C/650 MPa) [13] , and 
a fourth-generation superalloy (760 °C/810 MPa) [21] . However, 
the single crystal superalloy SRR99 showed a different trend at 
760 °C/790 MPa, where the [001]-oriented single crystal had the 
longest lifetimes, while the [111]-oriented had the poorest creep 
strength [ 19 , 27 ]. Therefore, the alloy composition has an important

inﬂuence on the creep anisotropy. More interestingly, in the vicin- 
ity of [001] orientation, the creep properties exhibit more obvious 
orientation sensitivity. The creep life of samples near the [001]- 
[011] boundary was much longer than those close to the [001]- 
[111] boundary [ 14 , 20 , 23 , 28 ]. Ghosh et al. [29] extended a general 
model of creep deformation to account for anisotropic creep in sin- 
gle crystals by a slip system approach and thought creep deforma- 
tion occurred by {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems. Caron et al. [30] found 
that the creep anisotropy was closely related to the size of γ’ 
phases. Mackay et al. [ 22 , 23 ] thought the creep anisotropy would 
be attributed to dislocation slip and orientation rotation. More re- 
sults also suggested that the activity of a single {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip 
system would lead to rotation, which resulted in creep anisotropy 
[ 31 , 32 ]. More recently, Heep et al. [28] proposed a new perspective 
that the creep anisotropy did not rely on dislocation networks or 
multiple planar fault slips, but was governed by dislocation reac- 
tions between γ-channel dislocation families with different Burg- 
ers vectors. In fact, the activity of different types of slip systems is 
interrelated and the activity of {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems is closely 
related to {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems [ 33 , 34 ]. Therefore, when study- 
ing the creep properties of different orientations, it is necessary to 
comprehensively consider the effect of different types of slip sys- 
tems. 
In order to get a better understanding of the relationship 
between the creep anisotropy and the activity of slip systems, 
a third-generation Ni-based single crystal superalloy is used in 
the present experiment [35] . The creep behaviors of four sin- 
gle crystals with different orientations at 750 °C/750 MPa were 
investigated. The microstructure evolution, deformation mecha- 
nism, and orientation rotation were notably emphasized. The fun- 
damental purpose of this work is to provide theoretical sup- 
port for revealing the damage mechanism of turbine blades in 
service. 
2. Experimental 
The nominal composition of the experimental Ni-based single 
crystal superalloy is as follows (wt.%): 3.5Cr, 9Co, 1.5Mo, 6 W, 8Ta, 
4Re, 6Al, and the balance Ni. For the present investigation, sin- 
gle crystal rods with four different orientations were produced 
with the seeding technique by using a modiﬁed Bridgman cast- 
ing furnace [36] . A stepwise solution heat treatment at 1290 °C/ 
1 h + 1300 °C/ 1 h + 1310 °C/ 3 h + 1320 °C/ 5 h + 1328 °C/ 
15 h, and then air cooling (A.C.), which was to diminish inter- 
dendritic eutectic microstructures and solidiﬁcation segregation. 
Subsequently, a two-stage aging treatment was adopted and it was 
1180 °C/ 4 h (A.C.) + 870 °C/ 24 h (A.C.). The primary aging process 
is to obtain uniformly dispersed γ′ phases, and the secondary ag- 
ing could adjust the cubic degree of γ′ phases. After the standard 
heat treatment, without affecting the processing of creep speci- 
mens, a small sample was cut from the middle edge of each single 
crystal rod to determine the initial orientation. Then, these single 
crystal rods were processed into creep samples with a gage length 
of 25 mm and a diameter of 5 mm. Then, the uniaxial constant 
load tensile creep testing was carried out in a CTM105-A1 creep- 
testing machine at 750 °C/750 MPa. After creep rupture, the area 
immediately adjacent to the fracture surface was cut off for analy- 
sis of orientation after creep rupture. 
An FEI Helios G4 CX focused ion beam microscope (FIB) was 
used to observe the γ/ γ ʹ microstructures and the fracture mor- 
phology. Specimens for observation for scanning electron mi- 
croscopy (SEM) were ground, polished using a diamond suspen- 
sion, and then chemically etched by a solution of 20 g CuSO4 , 
100 mL HCl, 80 mL H2 O, and 5 mL H2 SO4 . Samples, which were 
all perpendicular to the tensile axis, were cut for orientation anal- 
ysis. A ZEISS Sigma 300 SEM, equipped with an electron back- 
scattered diffraction (EBSD) detector (Oxford Symmetry Model), 
was used for the EBSD test. Finally, an FEI Talos F200X TEM, 
operating at 200 kV, was used for TEM investigations. Samples, 
for TEM observation, were mechanically ground to 60 μm ﬁrstly 
and then were electrochemically thinned using a twin-jet polisher 
with an electrolyte of 7% perchloric acid in ethanol at –25 °C, 
25 mA.

3.2. Orientation and microstructure after creep rupture 
3.2.1. Creep properties 
Fig. 2 compares the creep behavior of samples with different 
orientations at 750 °C/750 MPa. It could be seen that creep prop- 
erties are strongly inﬂuenced by crystal orientations. Sample A dis- 
plays the longest creep life of 620 h, while sample C exhibits the 
shortest creep rupture life of 5.4 h. For sample B, the creep rup- 
ture life is 127.6 h. Sample D also displays a good creep property 
and has a creep rupture life of 261.2 h. Except for sample D, all 
other samples show a good elongation, which is about 25%, while 
sample D has an elongation of less than 5%. 
Fig. 2 also shows the strain rate with respect to the time of dif- 
ferent samples at 750 °C/750 MPa. Except for sample C, all other 
samples display similar curves, where three creep stages could be 
clearly distinguished: primary, secondary, and tertiary creep stages 
[41] . Sample A with the most extended creep life has the low- 
est minimum strain rate at the secondary creep stage as shown 
in Fig. 2 (a). Comparing Fig. 2 (b, d), the minimum strain rates of 
samples B and D are more signiﬁcant than that of sample A, and 
the minimum strain rate of samples D is slightly greater than B. 
For sample C near [101] orientation, the strain rate–strain curve is 
entirely different, and it ﬂuctuates signiﬁcantly during the initial 
deformation stage and then continues to increase with increasing 
creep strain after the incubation period. 
3.2.2. Orientation 
During tension creep deformation, single crystals would rotate 
towards their slip direction. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze 
the crystal orientation after creep rupture. The orientation after 
creep rupture is calculated by all Euler angles as shown in Table 1 . 
After creep rupture, the orientation of sample A changes to [0.88 
0.27 9.96], which has a deviation of 5.28 ° away from [001]. Com- 
pared to the initial orientation, it is much closer to [001]. Similar to 
sample A, sample B also rotates towards the [001] orientation dur- 
ing the creep process, in which the crystal orientation rotates from 
[1.52 1.04 9.83] to [0.56 0.25 9.98] and the deviation from [001] 
also decreases to 3.52 °. After creep rupture, sample C with [6.45 
3.25 6.91] orientation is 1.93 ° off the precise [212] orientation and 
sample D is closed to [326] orientation. The rotation direction of 
different sam ples will be discussed in the following section.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Slip system activity and deformation mechanism 
As mentioned before, the main dislocation conﬁguration in γ’ 
phases is the planar fault, such as stacking faults and micro-twins. 
In the γ matrix, there are lots of dislocation tangles, especially 
in samples A and B. From a fundamental point of view, there 
is a close relationship between the orientation, Schmid factors, 
and the activity of slip systems. The dislocation in the γ matrix 
is a /2 < 110 > type dislocation, which originates from the activity 
of {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems [ 4 8 , 4 9 ]. These planar faults, including 
stacking faults and micro-twins, all originate from the activity of 
{111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems, and the prerequisite for the activity of 
these slip systems is that {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems are ﬁrst acti- 
vated [ 26 , 33 , 50 , 51 ]. Therefore, both the octahedral {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip 
systems and planar fault {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems should be con- 
sidered. 
Fig. 5 displays the Schmid factor contours for 12 octahedral 
{111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems. To more intuitively display the activation 
of various slip systems under different orientations, Schmid factors 
here all use absolute values. It is found that there are signiﬁcant 
differences in the Schmid factors of different slip systems for dif- 
ferent crystal orientations. The speciﬁc values of the Schmid factor 
of different samples for 12 octahedral slip systems are shown in 
Table 3 . For samples A and B, both before and after fracture, there 
are multiple {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems with signiﬁcant Schmid fac- 
tors, such as (111) [0 ¯1 1], ( ¯1 11) [101], and (1 ¯1 1) [011]. For sample 
C, there are two slip systems with a Schmid factor greater than 
0.4 before deformation. Sample D also has two slip systems with 
a Schmid factor greater than 0.4. The dislocation movement is in

dislocations [52] . The CRSS of {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ slip systems in the γ ma-

4.2. Creep anisotropy and orientation rotation 
Creep anisotropy of Ni-based single crystal superalloys is partic- 
ularly evident at intermediate temperatures and high stress. Based 
on extensive research on MAR-M247 and MAR-M200 with differ- 
ent orientations, MacKay et al. [ 22 , 23 ] established the relationship 
between crystal orientations and creep lives at about 760 °C, as 
shown in Fig. 9 (a). In general, the longest life is exhibited by crys- 
tals having orientation near the [111]; the crystals near the [001] 
also exhibit a very good creep life; and the shortest life is exhibited 
by crystals near the [101]. At the vicinity of [001] orientation, the 
creep properties exhibit signiﬁcant orientation sensitivity, in which 
the creep life of samples near the [001]-[101] boundary is much 
longer than that of samples close to the [001]-[111] boundary. The 
orientation of different sam ples before creep and after rupture is 
marked in Fig. 9 (a). Comparing Fig. 2 and Fig. 9 (a), our results 
also satisfy the relationship between crystal orientations and creep 
lives. 
During the creep process at 750 °C/750 MPa, the γ ʹ phases re- 
main cuboidal and there are no rafted structures at the creep rup- 
ture sample. Therefore, the creep anisotropy is mainly related to 
these activated slip systems. There are two types of slip systems 
that could be activated, {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems and {111} ⟨ 110 ⟩ 
slip systems. The orientation would rotate during the deformation 
process as shown in Fig. 9 (a). For samples A and B, the orienta- 
tion would rotate towards [001], while samples C and D would ro- 
tate towards [111]. The rotation path of these samples is consis- 
tent with the orientation rotation process of {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip sys- 
tems. The dislocation conﬁgurations also indicate that {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ 
slip systems dominate the creep process. Fig. 9 (b) is the illustra- 
tion of the orientation rotation process of {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ in a stan- 
dard stereographic triangle. For [001], [101], and [111] orientation, 
there are multiple activated {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems. The [001]- 
Y , [001]- X, Y-X, Y -[101], and X -[111] boundaries also represent re- 
gions of double slip, in which the Schmid factors are equivalent 
for two slip systems, and thus equal slip activity could occur on 
the two slip systems [23] . Although they are all double slip, the 
[001]- Y and the X -[111] boundaries belong to the conjugate double 
slip, while the [001]- X, Y-X, Y -[101] boundaries are the critical dou- 
ble slip. The other area would be divided into two parts, [001]- X- 
Y and X - Y -[101]-[111]. In [001]- X-Y region, (111) [ ¯1 ¯1 2] is the main 
active slip system, but for X-Y -[101]-[111], that is (1 ¯1 1) [121] slip

As mentioned before, the creep life of single crystal superal- 
loys is found signiﬁcantly sensitive to the crystallographic orien- 
tation. MacKey et al. [23] claimed that the creep anisotropy of sin- 
gle crystal superalloys could be attributed to dislocation slip and 
orientation rotation. Then, inspired by the function between creep 
rupture properties and orientation proposed by MacKey et al. [23] , 
Shi et al. [32] introduced the Schmid factor and the lattice rotation 
angle to modify the nominal stress and proposed the Orientation- 
Dependent (OD) method to evaluate the relationship between the 
orientation and the creep life. The OD method showed more ad- 
vantages at intermediate temperatures (760–850 °C), which meant 
the inﬂuence of orientation rotation was much more important. 
The research on Ti-6Al-4 V also indicated that the magnitude of 
the orientation rotation relative to the initial orientation exhib- 
ited a linear relationship with the plastic strain [75] . Therefore, 
the creep life may be related to the degree of orientation rota- 
tion. Compared to sample B, sample A is closer to the [001]- Y line, 
which means the degree of orientation rotation is smaller. Sample 
C is far away from the X -[111] line, hence a signiﬁcant rotation is 
required to reach the boundary. On the contrary, the degree of ori- 
entation rotation for sample D is smaller. 
Regarding the mechanism of the effect of orientation rotation 
on creep anisotropy, MacKay et al. [23] thought that orientation 
rotation was required to produce intersecting slip during the pri- 
mary creep stage, and the larger orientation rotation would result 
in large primary creep strains, which would accelerate specimen 
fracture. This explanation may seem reasonable, however, there 
were some single crystals with different orientations and lifetimes 
but similar primary strains in their research. Even in some studies, 
it has been found that some single crystals with a large orientation 
rotation trend exhibit smaller primary strain [ 14 , 28 ]. Therefore, it 
cannot be said that orientation rotation mainly occurs during the 
primary strain stage. In fact, {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems causing ori- 
entation rotation would dominate the entire creep process in cer- 
tain orientations, while in some orientations, there are few {111} 
⟨ 112 ⟩ slip systems that start during the primary creep stage [13] . 
As shown in Fig. 2 (d), the creep curve of sample D exhibits typical 
three-stage creep characteristics. However, the fracture morphol- 
ogy and dislocation conﬁgurations indicate that there is only one 
activated {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip system, and there is no occurrence of in- 
tersecting slip. This was different from the conclusion of MacKay 
et al. [23] , e.g. the lattice rotation was required to produce inter- 
secting slip, because second-stage creep did not begin until after 
the onset of intersecting slip. Qu et al. [31] thought the asynchrony 
of orientation rotation would cause nonuniform deformation and 
localized force inhomogeneity, leading to premature failure of the 
specimen. The research for Inconel 718 indicated that orientation 
rotation would result in strain localization [76] , while such hetero- 
geneity in local strain evolution led to a spatially heterogeneous 
damage distribution [77] . Theoretically, the crystal orientation of

5. Conclusions 
In the present work, the creep anisotropy of Ni-based single 
crystal superalloys at 750 °C/750 MPa was systematically investi- 
gated by analyzing the fracture morphology, dislocation conﬁgura- 
tions, and orientation rotation. The following conclusions could be 
given: 
(1) The creep properties are highly anisotropic in different orien- 
tation specimens at 750 °C/750 MPa. Sample C with [6.98 0.64 
7.13] orientation shows the shortest creep life of less than 5.4 h, 
and sample D with [3.73 2.44 8.95] orientation presents an 
intermediate life of 261.2 h. In the vicinity of [001] orienta- 
tion, the creep life exhibits signiﬁcant orientation sensitivity. 
Although they deviate [001] by a similar angle, sample A with 
[1.63 0.39 9.86] orientation displays the most extended creep 
rupture life of 620 h. In contrast, for sample B with [1.52 1.04 
9.83] orientation, the creep rupture life is only 127.6 h. 
(2) Stacking faults and Micro-twins are the typical dislocation con- 
ﬁgurations, which all originate the activity of {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip 
systems. The orientation rotation caused by the activity of the 
main {111} ⟨ 112 ⟩ slip system would accelerate the specimen 
fracturing in shear fracture mode. The (111) [ ¯1 ¯1 2] slip system 
in [001]-X-Y region (A and B) would cause the sample to turn 
towards the [001] pole. While in X-Y-[101]-[111] region (C and 
D), the dominant slip system is (1 ¯1 1) [121] slip system and the 
sample would rotate towards the [111] pole. 
(3) The orientation rotation would dominate the creep anisotropy. 
The orientation rotation would occur during the entire creep 
process, the greater the degree of rotation, the more severe 
the asynchrony of rotation, which would result in nonuniform 
deformation and local stress concentration, and exacerbate the 
failure of the specimen.
