ABSTRACT
Mo/Cu laminated metal matrix composites (LMMCs) have demonstrated remarkable potential in electronic 
packaging and aerospace applications, attributed to their superior comprehensive performance. Currently, Mo/ 
Cu LMMCs can be fabricated by inducing non-equilibrium conditions and employing electro-assisted processing 
techniques. However, the influence of Mo/Cu alloyed interfaces on mechanical properties remains insufficiently 
understood and requires further investigation. In this study, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were utilized 
to model the tensile and shear behaviors of the high-temperature and electric-field alloyed interface of the Mo/ 
Cu system. The simulation results reveal that the electric-field alloyed interface exhibits enhanced strength 
(tensile strength 10.33 GPa, shear strength 5.51 GPa) but reduced plasticity (fracture strain 0.278 in tension) 
compared to the high-temperature alloyed interface (tensile strength 9.7 GPa, shear strength 5.18 GPa, fracture 
strain 0.377 in tension), due to dislocation activity. This approach investigates the microstructure evolution and 
the fracture mechanisms of the alloyed interfaces at the atomic scale. Microstructure disparities lead to dislo -
cation pile-up, ultimately resulting in brittle fracture along grain boundaries. This research provides critical 
theoretical insights for the development of high-performance Mo/Cu LMMCs.
1. Introduction
Mo/Cu laminated metal matrix composites (LMMCs) are in high 
demand in the aerospace and electronic packaging industries because of 
their thermal expansion characteristics, which closely match those of 
semiconductor materials, as well as their superior electrical and thermal 
conductivity, and adequate mechanical strength [ 1 – 4 ]. However, due to 
the absence of a significant thermodynamic driving force between Mo 
and Cu, it is challenging to form a robust metallurgical bonding interface 
spontaneously. Given the outstanding comprehensive properties of 
Mo/Cu LMMCs, it is crucial to develop continuous, high-strength Mo/Cu 
alloyed interface without incorporating intermediate layer materials or 
impurity elements [ 5 – 8 ].
At present, the Mo/Cu system has successfully achieved alloying 
under non-equilibrium conditions. Zeng et al. [ 9 ] fabricated Mo/Cu 
laminated plates via powder sintering, rolling, and heat treatment pro -
cesses. EBSD and TEM analyses confirmed that the formation of a 
diffusion layer significantly enhanced the strength (reaching 624 MPa) 
and plastic deformation capability (17.7 %), while also altering its 
fracture mechanism from intergranular fracture to a combination of 
ductile and brittle fractures. Liu et al. [ 10 ] introduced amorphous in -
terlayers between the two phases of the Mo/Cu alloy using the 
high-temperature penetration method. The amorphous interface refers 
to a bonding interface where the interfacial region is composed of 
amorphous substances with long-range disordered atomic arrangement 
[ 11 , 12 ]. Liu et al. [10] found that the amorphous interface in Mo – Cu 
alloys originates from the mutual diffusion of Mo and Cu during the 
high-temperature penetration process. Specifically, Mo disrupts the 
long-range ordered arrangement of Cu, thereby forming the amorphous 
interface. Its function is to absorb dislocations, alleviate the stress

Therefore, in this study, the deformation process of the Mo/Cu 
alloyed interface was investigated at the atomic scale using molecular 
dynamics simulation. Initially, a Mo/Cu alloying model was established, 
and the alloying process was performed under high-temperature and 
electric-field conditions following relaxation. Subsequently, tensile 
loading perpendicular to the interface and shear loading parallel to the 
interface were applied to the two alloyed interface models to obtain the 
corresponding stress-strain curves. The microstructure of the interface 
was visualized and analyzed to elucidate the evolution and failure 
mechanism of the Mo/Cu alloyed interface. Furthermore, the micro -
scopic mechanism of the influence of electro-assisted processing tech -
nology on Mo/Cu alloyed interface properties were explained. This 
research can provide theoretical guidance for the preparation of high- 
quality immiscible Mo/Cu LMMCs and also offer a reference for 
revealing the microscopic mechanism of electro-assisted processing.
2. Simulation process
2.1. Potential function
Finnis and Sinclair et al. [ 24 ] developed the Finnis and Sinclair(F – S) 
potential function model based on the theory of tightly bound energy 
bands. Following expansions and enhancements by Tai et al. [ 25 ], this 
model is capable of simultaneously satisfying the requirements for both 
BCC and FCC metal systems. In this study, the Mo/Cu F – S potential 
function was constructed in previous research of our team [ 26 ]. Verifi -
cation results indicate that this potential function demonstrates high 
accuracy and reliability. All MD simulations conducted in this study 
were performed using the large-scale atomic/molecular parallel simu -
lator (LAMMPS) [ 27 ].

The relaxed model was subjected to the heat treatment process 
illustrated in Fig. 3 (a). The simulation was performed under NPT 
ensemble and periodic boundary conditions, with a holding temperature 
of 1200 K and a holding duration of 5 ns. Under these high-temperature 
conditions, complete atomic diffusion occurred, forming a high- 
temperature alloyed interface as shown in Fig. 3 (b). Similarly, the 
heat treatment process depicted in Fig. 3 (c) was adopted. During the 
heating and holding stages, an additional electric field was applied using 
LAMMPS. Through the synergistic promotion of atomic diffusion by 
both high temperature and the electric field, an electric-field-assisted 
alloying interface was formed, as shown in Fig. 3 (d). In previous 
studies [ 26 ], the accuracy of the constructed potential function was 
verified through comparative analyses from multiple dimensions 
(physical properties, surface energy, and Rose curve). By imposing pe -
riodic boundary conditions and employing the NPT ensemble (constant 
number of particles, constant pressure, constant temperature) in the 
model setup, the thermodynamic stability of the simulation system was 
maintained under the premise of keeping the number of atoms and 
pressure in the system constant, which is also more consistent with the 
actual situation. Furthermore, based on this model, the diffusion 
behavior of the immiscible Mo/Cu system under different temperatures, 
interface orientations, and electric fields was systematically investi -
gated, the vacancy diffusion mechanism was revealed, and the key pa -
rameters for electric field-assisted preparation were clarified. The 
detailed results were previously published in Ref. [ 26 ] by our team.
2.3. Deformation simulation
External loading of the alloyed interface model was performed. 
Firstly, atoms with a thickness of 10 Å at the top and bottom layers of the 
model were designated as the loading ends for fixation processing, and 
the removal speed of these atoms was determined. The model was 
relaxed at 300 K for a duration of 0.3 ns. Subsequently, under NVT 
ensemble conditions, a velocity was imparted to the fixed rigid atomic 
layers to enable uniform loading in the specified direction at a strain rate 
exceeding 1 × 10
9 
s

rate of interface deformation exceeds 1 × 10

s

, the influence of the 
strain rate becomes negligible, and the corresponding loading can be 
considered quasi-static or low-strain-rate loading. When loaded 
perpendicular to the interface, the fixed rigid atomic layers move along 
the positive z-axis direction, causing interface deformation and changes 
in the simulated box dimensions along the x, y, and z axes. Therefore, 
aperiodic boundary conditions were selected. When loaded parallel to 
the interface, the fixed rigid atomic layers move along the positive y-axis 
direction, resulting in deformation of the model box along the y and z 
axes. In this case, fixed boundary conditions were applied in these two 
directions. However, in the x-axis direction, since the simulated box 
does not undergo deformation but interactions between atoms and the 
interface still exist, periodic boundary conditions were retained.
2.4. Visualization method
In this study, the Open Visualization Tool (OVITO) [ 30 ] was 
employed to visualize the simulation results, and the Common Neighbor 
Analysis (CNA) [ 31 ] method was utilized to characterize the local 
atomic structure and identify the type of nuclear arrangement. Addi -
tionally, the Dislocation Analysis (DXA) [ 32 , 33 ] method was applied to 
determine the types and distributions of dislocations within the model 
and investigate the dislocation evolution process.

3.2. MD simulation loaded in the vertical interface direction
3.2.1. Expression of mechanical properties
The high-temperature alloyed interface model and the electric-field 
alloyed interfaces model were subjected to loading perpendicular to 
the interface direction, i.e., the tensile process, and the resulting stress- 
strain curves are presented in Fig. 5 .
From the curve characteristics, it can be observed that the interface 
initially undergoes an elastic deformation stage, during which the curve 
exhibits linear behavior. Subsequently, the interface transitions into the 
plastic deformation stage, where the curve displays pronounced 
nonlinear features. After reaching the peak stress, the curve begins to 
decline rapidly, indicating the onset of the softening stage. Specifically, 
the maximum stress achieved at the high-temperature alloyed interface 
was 9.7 GPa, whereas at the electric-field alloyed interface, it reached 
10.33 GPa, representing a 6.5 % increase compared to the high- 
temperature alloyed interface. Upon the stress decreasing to approxi -
mately 2.5 GPa, the rate of stress reduction starts to decelerate. Notably, 
the stress reduction rate is faster at the electric-field alloyed interface. 
When the strain reaches 0.25, the stress approaches 0 MPa, signifying 
complete interface fracture at this point. In contrast, at the high- 
temperature alloyed interface, the stress approaches 0 MPa when the 
strain reaches 0.35, leading to interface fracture. These results indicate 
that while the electric-field alloyed interface exhibits higher strength, its 
plasticity is relatively lower compared to the high-temperature alloyed 
interface.
3.2.2. The microstructure evolution of high-temperature alloyed interface
The tensile simulation process of the high-temperature alloyed 
interface was visualized and analyzed using OVITO, while the interface 
microstructure evolution was investigated through CNA and DXA. The 
results are presented in Fig. 6 . In the model, blue atoms represent Mo 
atoms, and red atoms represent Cu atoms. In CNA analysis, blue atoms 
indicate BCC crystals, red atoms indicate HCP crystals, green atoms 
indicate FCC crystals, and white atoms indicate amorphous states. DXA 
analysis removes atoms and retains only the dislocations within the 
interface. In the initial state ( ε = 0), the interface exhibited a layered 
structure consisting of crystalline-amorphous-crystalline regions. DXA 
analysis reveals that high-density dislocations are distributed along the 
amorphous interface, indicating stress concentration at the alloyed 
interface. When the strain increases to ε = 0.021, the model slightly 
elongates along the z-axis direction, with no significant changes 
observed. At this stage, Shockley dislocations and Stair-rod dislocations 
dominate in the Cu region. Notably, Shockley dislocations begin to slip, 
and the length of the Stair-rod dislocation line increases, suggesting that 
the interface has entered the plastic deformation stage.
As deformation progresses, the interface reaches its maximum stress 
at ε = 0.066. At this point, noticeable deformation occurs in the model, 
and necking begins to form in the transition zone between the amor -
phous diffusion layer on the Cu side and the FCC structure. DXA analysis 
shows a sharp increase in Shockley dislocation density on the Cu side, 
while the Stair-rod dislocation line continues to extend and generates 
numerous new Stair-rod dislocations. Specifically, due to the significant 
difference in atomic arrangement between the amorphous interface and 
the FCC structure of Cu, a potential barrier forms, preventing disloca -
tions from crossing the amorphous interface. This leads to dislocation 
pile-up at the interface, causing severe stress concentration. When the 
strain reaches ε = 0.157, corresponding to the position where the stress 
decline rate slows down in Fig. 5 , the interface undergoes significant 
deformation, and the amorphous interface begins to fracture. CNA 
analysis confirms that the fracture point is located in the connection area 
between the amorphous diffusion layer and the FCC matrix.
DXA analysis further reveals intensified dislocation pile-up at the 
amorphous interface, exacerbating stress concentration. At this stage, 
severe dislocation entanglement occurs within the Cu, forming a 
network of dislocation cells that severely restricts dislocation slip. 
Consequently, work hardening becomes evident, and deformation is 
significantly limited. Finally, when the strain reaches ε = 0.377, the 
interface fractures completely, leaving only a few atoms adhering to the 
interface. From the above analysis, it can be concluded that plastic 
deformation primarily occurs in the Cu region, indicating that the 
strength of the alloyed interface lies between that of the Cu matrix and 
the Mo matrix. Throughout the deformation process, the Cu matrix 
gradually forms dislocation cells through the proliferation and interac -
tion of internal dislocations, leading to work hardening. Meanwhile, the 
alloyed interface experiences severe dislocation pile-up due to micro -
structure differences, exhibiting brittle fracture characteristics along the 
grain boundaries.
To investigate the work hardening phenomenon of the interface 
during the tensile process, the stacking faults and dislocations in the 
main deformation area of Cu were extracted, and the results are pre -
sented in Fig. 7 . Fig. 7 (a) illustrates the initial dislocation and stacking 
faults (red atoms) extraction. The red spheres represent HCP crystals 
which has a direct and crucial connection with stacking faults. The HCP 
crystals configuration is a structural manifestation of stacking faults in 
the FCC structure of copper. Moreover, the HCP crystals reflect the dy -
namic growth of stacking faults and serve as microscopic markers for the 
involvement of stacking faults in strengthening mechanisms. The faults 
shown in the figure are all intrinsic stacking faults formed by the 
stacking of two layers of Cu atoms. Their formation is primarily attrib -
uted to the decomposition of all dislocations into two Shockley dislo -
cations during the model relaxation process to minimize the energy of 
the system. This manifests as two Shockley dislocations (the leading 
dislocation and the trailing dislocation) surrounding the HCP fault.

3.2.3. The microstructure evolution of the electric-field alloyed interface
The tensile process of the electric-field alloyed interface model was 
visually analyzed using the same method, and the results are presented 
in Fig. 8 . As shown in Fig. 8 , there are fewer dislocations within the 
model in the initial state ( ε = 0). The dislocations in Mo remain a / 
2 < 111 > and a < 100 > dislocations, while in Cu, only dislocation loops 
composed of Shockley dislocations, Stair-rod dislocations, and Hirth 
dislocations are observed. The dislocation distribution near the interface 
is sparse, indicating uniform stress distribution without significant stress 
concentration. At the onset of loading, when the strain reaches ε =
0.007, the interface undergoes slight deformation. The Shockley dislo -
cations in the dislocation loops begin to glide, causing the lengths of the 
Stair-rod dislocation lines and Hirth dislocation lines to increase, indi -
cating the initiation of plastic deformation. When the strain reaches ε =
0.054, the interface attains its maximum stress. At this stage, the 
interface experiences intensified deformation, with slight necking 
occurring in the transition zone between the amorphous interface and 
the FCC structure. Some dislocations in Mo are annihilated due to dy -
namic recovery. Under the synergistic effect of an electric field and high 
temperature, the processes of dislocation slip, reaction, and annihilation 
are accelerated. Meanwhile, when the deformation approaches the 
maximum stress, the dislocations inside Mo reduce in quantity through 
interactions (slip and mutual reaction), leading to a decrease in dislo -
cation density and ultimately achieving dislocation annihilation. This 
process helps to reduce local stress concentration, while the dislocation 
loops in Cu continue to expand along the {111} plane through the 
continuous gliding of Shockley dislocations. At this point, the micro -
structure potential barrier at the amorphous interface hinders disloca -
tion motion, leading to dislocation pile-up and localized stress 
concentration at the interface. As the strain continues to increase, when 
ε = 0.14, the necking phenomenon intensifies, and fracture occurs. At 
this stage, the dislocation loops in Cu become entangled with prolifer -
ating dislocations, forming dislocation cells. Severe dislocation pile-up 
at the interface also induces brittle fracture along the grain bound -
aries. Finally, when the strain reaches ε = 0.278, the interface fractures 
completely along the amorphous interface. In summary, plastic defor -
mation in the electric-field alloyed interface model primarily occurs in 
Cu. The continuous expansion of dislocation loops eventually leads to 
their entanglement with proliferating dislocations, forming a dislocation 
network that contributes to the work hardening effect. The interface 
exhibits the brittle fracture along crystal planes after reaching the ulti -
mate stress, attributed to the dislocation pile-up caused by microstruc -
ture potential barriers.
The dislocations and HCP structures in the Cu matrix were extracted, 
and the results are presented in Fig. 9 . Fig. 9 (a) shows that dislocation 
loops formed under the influence of an electric field surrounding the 
structure of faults, with a small number of HCP structure atoms observed 
near the alloyed interface. As the interface enters the plastic deformation 
stage, the Shockley dislocation within the dislocation loop glides along 
the {111} plane. After the leading dislocation glides, it promotes the 
expansion of the stacking faults, as indicated by the arrow direction in 
Fig. 9 (b). Fig. 9 (c) illustrates the microstructure extraction when the 
interface reaches its maximum stress. At this point, the continuous 
gliding of Shockley dislocations within the dislocation loop causes the 
lengths of the Stair-rod and Hirth dislocation lines to increase, resulting 
in further expansion of the stacking faults. During this stage, the shear 
stress induced by loading causes interface deformation, and scattered 
Shockley dislocations and HCP layer faults appear near the interface. 
The dislocation loop was analyzed, and its cross-section is shown in 
Fig. 9 (d). It can be observed that the Stair-rod dislocations and Hirth 
dislocations, respectively, form L-C lock and Hirth lock structures sur -
rounded by Shockley dislocations. The angles of these two locking 
structures complement each other to form a quadrilateral dislocation 
network. Due to their immobility, these structures effectively hinder 
dislocation movement and enhance the work hardening effect of the Cu 
matrix. Fig. 9 (e) shows the dislocation and layer fault extraction when 
the interface fractures. At this stage, the dislocations within the interface

dislocation reactions. As the strain continues to increase, Cu undergoes a

region shifts to the Cu side. Through dislocation proliferation, slip, and

gradual interface hardening. Interface failure occurs due to stress con -

along grain boundaries.

4. Conclusion
In this study, the Mo/Cu alloyed interfaces obtained by high- 
temperature and electric-field alloying process were subjected to 
loading in both vertical and parallel interface modes via MD simulation, 
and the microstructure evolution and failure mechanism of the interface 
during deformation were investigated. The main conclusions are as 
follows. 
(1) The tensile and shear deformation of Mo/Cu alloyed interface 
predominantly occurs on the Cu side of the interface. Shockley 
dislocations within this region continuously glide along the 
{111} interface. Upon encountering one another, they react by 
generating immobile Stair-rod and Hirth dislocations. At this 
stage, the interaction between dislocations and faults forms L-C 
locks and Hirth locks, which progressively expand as Shockley 
dislocations continue to glide. During the stress decline phase, 
dislocations within the Cu matrix become increasingly entangled, 
forming a reticular dislocation cell structure that restricts further 
dislocation slip.
(2) Owing to microstructure disparities between the alloyed interface 
and the Cu matrix, a potential energy barrier arises. This barrier 
inhibits dislocations from traversing the amorphous interface, 
thereby inducing a pronounced dislocation pile-up phenomenon. 
As strain increases, dislocation pile-up intensifies, leading to 
localized stress concentration at the interface. Ultimately, severe 
dislocation pile-up triggers brittle fractures along the grain 
boundaries.
(3) Both tensile and shear test results indicate that the strength of the 
electric field-treated alloy interface is approximately 6.5 % 
higher than that of the high-temperature interface. Analysis 
reveals that the electric field effectively reduces dislocation 
density within the interface, promoting a more uniform stress 
distribution at the interface. Furthermore, retained dislocation 
rings contribute to the direct expansion of locked structures 
during plastic deformation, thereby enhancing interface strength 
while concurrently diminishing its ductility.
