a b s t r a c t 
Nanocrystalline (nc) alloys, owing to their high strength, have been extensively studied over the past few years. 
High-temperature mechanical properties including tensile and fatigue were studied for commercially obtained nc 
nickel-cobalt-phosphorous (NiCoP) alloy through a novel mechanical testing setup. The Joule heating technique 
was used to enable high-temperature testing to 300 °C. The nc NiCoP was observed to maintain 90% of its strength 
to 130 °C and had > 1 GPa tensile strength up to 200 °C. Fatigue performance showed a minimum degradation 
between room temperature and 200 °C with an endurance limit of 750–825 MPa. Microstructure characterization 
revealed abnormal grain growth leading to fatigue fracture. 
1. Introduction 
Nanocrystalline (nc) materials have attracted signiﬁcant attention in 
the industry owing to their high strength [1–8] , hardness [ 9 , 10 ], mod- 
erate ductility [ 1 , 4 , 5 , 7 , 8 ], and improved fatigue performance [11–15] . 
Along with their outstanding mechanical properties, they have also been 
shown to possess excellent thermal stability [16–20] , and corrosion re- 
sistance [21–23] . The increase in the grain boundary (GB) volume can, 
however, negatively impact the electrical performance of the materi- 
als leading to an increase in electrical resistivity [24–26] . Nanocrys- 
talline materials can be synthesized using several diﬀerent techniques 
including mechanical milling [27–29] , electrodeposition [30–32] , and 
inert-gas condensation [ 33 , 34 ]. The processing technique can have a sig- 
niﬁcant impact on the mechanical properties of the materials [35] . Of 
these, the electrodeposition technique can be used to manufacture com- 
plex structures and microelectromechanical devices, commonly referred 
to as MEMS, at a relatively fast rate. Alloy synthesis using electrodepo- 
sition, however, comes with its own challenges including bath chem- 
istry and plating process optimization to achieve target compositions. 
These are greatly inﬂuenced by the thermodynamics (electrochemical 
reduction potentials) and kinetics (diﬀusion rates) of the elements be- 
ing plated. Among the electrodeposited systems, Ni, Cu, and Co based 
systems are widely popular due to their ease of plating in aqueous baths 
[ 1 , 2 , 30 , 32 , 36 , 37 ].

The plastic deformation behavior of metallic materials under an ex- 
ternal load is governed by dislocation movement. These dislocations 
usually get pinned at the GBs. For nc materials with grain sizes between 
10–100 nm, this GB pinning leads to a signiﬁcant hardening, resulting 
in an increase in the yield and tensile strengths and a lowered ductility, 
which ultimately impact the fatigue properties of the material which is 
critical for applications that require cyclic loading. Fatigue performance 
of the nc alloys and the failure mechanisms have been extensively stud- 
ied [11–15] . Some of the early studies, including Hanlon et al., reported 
~2X higher endurance limit for nc Ni over its microcrystalline counter- 
part in stress-controlled fatigue [38] . Cavaliere reported endurance lim- 
its of ~800 MPa and ~1200 MPa for nc Ni and nc Co respectively [13] . 
Several Ni-based nc alloys including NiFe, NiMn along with pure nc Ni 
have been shown to endure more than a million cycles at extremely 
high stress values during fatigue tests [15] . The superior fatigue perfor- 
mance of these nc alloys has been associated with their high strength 
and resistance to crack nucleation. It is postulated that typical fatigue 
loading leads to GB migration, grain rotation, and coalescence result- 
ing in an abnormal grain growth in the nc materials. The grains can 
grow from sub-100 nm to 1 micron or larger size in an extremely lo- 
calized region during fatigue. This region then loses the advantage of 
the nc grain structure viz. high yield and tensile strength. These large 
grains can support persistent slip bands at the sample surface forming 
surface extrusions and intrusions, which ultimately lead to crack nucle- 
ation during the fatigue process. This crack can then quickly propagate 
through the sample due to limited crack tip plasticity in the nc region 
followed by a typical tensile overload once the eﬀective stress exceeds 
the tensile strength of the material [15] . Sergueeva et al. [39] showed

The thermal stability of nc alloys has also been a topic of interest for 
high-temperature applications [42] . Usually, grains in nc alloys coarsen 
when subjected to elevated temperatures. This grain growth can lead to 
a loss of the superior mechanical properties that the material exhibits in 
its nc form. There have been several eﬀorts to ﬁnd nc materials resistant 
to this type of grain growth through increasing the thermal stability of 
the system [ 20 , 42 , 43 ]. Typically, nc alloys show better thermal stability 
than a pure metallic nc system, since the alloying elements, whether in 
solid solution form or precipitated at the GB, help resist any thermally 
assisted grain growth. Thermodynamic models have been used to pre- 
dict optimal compositions of diﬀerent nc systems to stabilize the grain 
structures at high temperatures [ 42 , 44 , 45 ]. 
Although there is a signiﬁcant amount of literature related to fatigue 
and mechanical properties of nc materials at room temperature along 
with the thermal stability of nc materials, there is very little information 
on the fatigue and mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. A 
MEMS application at Intel Corporation requires a material that has high 
mechanical strength, low electrical resistivity, high thermal stability, 
and robust fatigue life. Numerous material systems were investigated 
and nc NiCoP was selected for its ability to satisfy all these require- 
ments. In the current paper, we report mechanical properties in tension 
and fatigue performance of commercially obtained electrodeposited nc 
NiCoP alloy at diﬀerent temperatures ranging from 25 to 300 °C using 
a novel mechanical testing setup incorporating resistive Joule heating 
of the material. We also review diﬀerent possible failure mechanisms 
for fatigue and the correlation with the mechanical properties coupled 
with detailed microstructural analysis using x-ray diﬀraction and elec- 
tron microscopy techniques. To the best of our knowledge, this is the 
ﬁrst experimental study of mechanical and fatigue properties of a nc 
metal/alloy system at elevated temperatures. 
2. Experimental methods 
A commercially obtained (Integran Technologies Inc, Canada) nc 
NiCoP alloy was used for the fatigue and mechanical properties test- 
ing. The composition of the material as measured using a Thermo Niton 
XL3t 980 GOLDD + handheld X-ray ﬂuorescence tool was ~65–70% Ni, 
35–30% Co and 0.02–0.03% P by weight. 
Microtensile dogbone samples were produced using lithographically 
deﬁned molds built on a substrate with a peelable copper foil as shown 
in Fig. 1 . The substrates were laminated with a 120 μm thick photoresist 
(CX-A270 from Asahi Kasei) and then exposed. The molds were electro- 
plated at Integran Technologies Inc. using a proprietary bath chemistry 
and electroplating process for NiCoP. Once the molds were plated, the 
substrates were soaked in acetone to remove the photoresist.The dog- 
bone samples were released from the mold by sliding a thin carbon steel 
blade under the specimen. Finally, the peelable copper foil remaining on 
the backside of the dogbone was etched away using a copper etch solu- 
tion at 40 °C and any remnants of the photoresist attached to the edges 
of the dogbones were removed with acetone and a benchtop ultrasonic 
cleaner. 
Typically, nc materials are known to undergo a phase change, GB 
relaxation, and grain growth on exposure to heat which can ultimately 
impact their mechanical properties [46–49] . Since the aim of the study 
was to measure high-temperature mechanical properties, all samples 
were heat-treated (HT) at 300 °C in air for 1 h before testing to ensure a 
stable microstructure during the test. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
showed that the thickness of the oxide layer generated during the heat 
treatment was less than 10 nm. This thickness is not expected to impact 
the mechanical properties of the ~100 μm thick samples. 
2.1. Thermal stability 
TA Instruments model Q1000 diﬀerential scanning calorimeter 
(DSC) was used to measure the thermal stability of nc alloys. The sample 
(~7 –12 mg) was heated in a sealed aluminum pan to 485 °C at a rate 
of 7.5 °C/min. 
2.2. Resistivity 
Since the high-temperature mechanical testing (tensile and fatigue) 
was enabled through Joule heating, it was important to measure the 
resistivity of NiCoP in order to determine the amount of current required 
to achieve the target temperature. The resistivity was measured using 
the four-point probe technique as described elsewhere [50] . 
2.3. Microstructure characterization 
The crystallinity of the samples was examined by X-ray diﬀraction. 
The data was collected using a PANalytical Empyrean X-ray diﬀractome- 
ter (XRD) equipped with graphite-monochromated Cu Ka radiation at 𝜆
= 1.541 Å. XRD was also used to measure the residual stress of the HT 
NiCoP at room temperature. Fracture surface analysis post fatigue was 
performed using a focused ion beam (FIB) and a scanning electron mi- 
croscope (SEM). 
2.4. Mechanical properties 
The gauge section of the dogbone was a 4:1 scaled-down version of 
ASTM E345 standard geometry. In order to protect the fragile gauge 
section due to handling, the sample was embedded inside a protective 
frame which is cut prior to uniaxial loading as shown in Fig. 1 . A ded- 
icated custom microtension testing apparatus as shown in Fig. 2 a was 
built to accurately evaluate nc alloy samples for a thickness range of 
50–200 μm. A precision linear frictionless guide with dual carriages 
was used to mount the top and the bottom half of the ﬁxture. A high- 
resolution primary load cell Interface Force WMC-220N was mounted 
on the bottom half of the ﬁxture aligning with the sample uniaxial load- 
ing axis. In order to resolve the issue of sample misalignment causing 
non-uniform strain and oﬀ-axis loading in the gauge section, the top and 
the bottom part of the ﬁxture was ﬁrst assembled and then was precisely 
machined and refaced to tight tolerances. The ﬂatness/coplanarity of the 
top and bottom faces along with the pin alignment to mount the sam- 
ple was measured to be within 13 μm using a confocal microscope to 
further validate the dimensional accuracy of machining and the ﬁxture 
assembly. Once the sample was mounted on the ﬁxture using embedded 
alignment pins, face clamps were used to tighten and secure the sample 
in place as shown in Fig. 2 c. The top half of the ﬁxture was driven using 
a high precision linear actuator Instron 5948. 
A non-contact full-ﬁeld 2D digital image correlation (DIC) optical 
technique was used to directly measure strain in the gauge section.

speed camera Photron SA-X, as shown in Fig. 2 b, was used to charac-

2.5. High cycle fatigue testing

investigated under diﬀerent temperature conditions, an innovative fa- 
tigue testing setup was designed and developed. The primary objectives 
for the setup included clear traceability to the exact stress ( + /- 25 MPa 
error), high cycling frequency ( > = 10 Hz), and relatively precise temper- 
ature control of the sample (up to 350°C with + /-10°C accuracy). Due to 
the direct stress extraction of a tension-tension setup, this was chosen as 
the method of choice for the fatigue apparatus. The ﬁxture and samples 
used were directly replicated from those used for the microtensile testing

to gauge thickness and material used (typically ~7–13 m Ωfor materi-

were relatively high ( < = 14 A), resulting in actual power amplitudes in 
the range of 1.5–3 W. The forced current was measured directly via an 
inline and thermally constant shunt resistor to the sample. 
The entire setup was controlled by a LabVIEW PXIe chassis which 
handled all the mechanical and temperature control in addition to the 
data acquisition for each sample. The load cell used is designed for low- 
frequency applications, thus to accurately adjust to the target loads, the 
sample would be cycling and the software would periodically (user de- 
ﬁned) slow down the cycling to accurately measure the load at both 
the high-tension and low-tension cases and adjust the voice-coil force 
as necessary. Once corrected, the system would maintain the new high 
and low force conditions on the voice coil until the next periodic check 
was executed. Thus, in normal cycling conditions, the load-cell would 
show a value equal to the exact average of the low and high forces cho- 
sen. The low to high tensile stress ratio (R ratio) for fatigue testing was ~
0.1. A high-speed validation (supplementary material) showed that the 
physical-mechanical response limit for the system falls between 15 –20 
Hz. This limit is slightly subject to the maximum force targeted thus 
a safe cycling window was chosen to be 10 Hz resulting in > 850,000 
cycles per day. 
The temperature control section of the software utilized feedback- 
based inputs to accurately maintain a target power across the sample. 
The temperature of the sample was measured via a 5X magniﬁcation 
infrared (IR) camera system (Flir SC6100); power would be increased 
until the target temperature was reached as measured in the center of 
the gauge section. The power would then be maintained for the duration 
of the fatigue cycling experiment. The samples were cycled until either a 
low force (sample failure) or electrical open was detected. Testing crite- 
ria such as the number of cycles, measured load, electrical response, and 
control adjustments were all logged for the duration of the experiment 
and any failures were speciﬁcally ﬂagged. 
2.6. High-temperature microtensile testing 
The fatigue testing setup was utilized for high temperature microten- 
sile testing as well. Blue tempered steel dogbone samples with similar 
dimensions as the nc NiCoP samples were used to perform microten- 
sile testing on both setups (microtension setup and fatigue setup with a 
voice coil) to ensure that they yielded the same results. Temperatures 
used for the testing were 70, 130, 200, and 300 °C. The rest of the testing 
parameters and setup including the strain rate, sample dimensions, and 
the strain measurement system were the same as the room temperature 
microtensile testing described above. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Physical properties 
The DSC results ( Fig. 4 ) showed a peak at 450 °C indicating high 
thermal stability and resistance to grain growth for the nc NiCoP. This 
is well above the thermal stability of nc Ni (~290 °C) and close to that 
of nc NiP (~420°C) [51] . 
In nc materials, GB electron scattering increases the resistivity of 
metals owing to the large volume of the GBs. The electrical resistivity of 
NiCoP did not signiﬁcantly change between pre and post heat treatment 
and remained constant at ~20 μohm-cm. This resistivity is fairly similar 
to that reported by Aus et al. for nc Ni [24] . This served as a starting 
point for calculating the current needs and expected wattage for the 
samples during Joule heating for high-temperature testing.

XRD analysis showed mainly (111) and (002) peaks for both as- 
deposited (AD) and HT NiCoP corresponding to a typical single phase 
face centered cubic (FCC) structure as observed by Sharma et al. in elec- 
trodeposited nc Ni-30 at% Co system [52] . The very small amount of P 
is expected to segregate at the grain boundary and form Ni 3 P, CoP, and 
Co 2 P precipitates post heat treatment [ 53 , 54 ]. Table 1 summarizes the 
data which shows that the average crystallite size calculated using the 
Scherrer equation [55] was higher for (111) oriented grains compared 
to the (002) oriented grains. A small increase in the grain size was ob- 
served after the initial heat treatment at 300 °C for 1 h. This represents

temperature mechanical testing, including tensile and fatigue. This crys-

indicating a minimum change in the crystal orientation throughout the 
heat treatment. Figs. S7 and S8 (supplementary material) show the XRD

3.3. Mechanical testing results 
Room temperature tensile testing of the NiCoP samples showed an 
increase in the Young’s modulus from 153 GPa to 173 GPa and 0.2% 
yield strength from 1.25 GPa to 1.80 GPa post heat treatment (300 °C 
for 1 h). This shows that while the increase in the Young’s modulus 
is ~13%, the yield strength increases by 44%. This shows that a heat 
treatment step is critical in any applications of nc materials requiring 
large elastic deformation (high yield strength to modulus ratio). This, 
however, was accompanied by a reduction in the ductility of the alloy 
from 5.0 to 1.7%. Similar results have been reported by Wang et al. for 
electrodeposited nc Ni [47] . Schuh et al. showed that nc Ni exhibits in- 
verse Hall-Petch behavior below grain size ~14 nm [56] . The observed 
increase in the yield strength here matches the expected trend based on 
the grain size increase post heat treatment as seen in Table 1 . Typically, 
dislocations are generated at the GB for nc alloys during plastic defor- 
mation. These then glide through the grain and may again disappear at 
the GBs [ 57 , 58 ]. However, along with the grain size increase, the heat 
treatment also stabilizes GBs and reduces the number of non-equilibrium 
GBs in nc materials [57] . This would inhibit any dislocation generation 
as well as dislocation absorption at the GBs associated with plastic de- 
formation. All this ultimately results in an increase in the yield stress 
( 𝜎Y ) and a reduction in the ductility post heat treatment. The increase 
in the modulus post heat treatment can also be attributed to the over- 
all decrease in the GB volume and a reduction in the non-equilibrium, 
high energy GBs. For nc NiCoP, this increase in the strength and the 
reduced ductility can also be associated with precipitation hardening 
by nickel phosphide (Ni 3 P) and potentially cobalt phosphides (CoP and 
Co 2 P) post heat treatment [ 53 , 54 ]. 
As seen in Fig. 5 b, the HT nc NiCoP was able to hold its yield and 
ultimate tensile strengths within 90% of the room temperature values 
to 130 °C. At higher temperatures (200 and 300 °C), these properties 
degraded signiﬁcantly while the ductility was enhanced ( Fig. 5 c). Even 
with this observed degradation, the 0.2% yield strength at 200 °C was 
maintained over 1.1 GPa with around 5% ductility. Although the XRD 
( Table 1 ) showed that there is no signiﬁcant grain growth at 300 °C 
for an extended period, signiﬁcant degradation in the strength and an 
increase in the ductility occurred at a temperature as low as 200°C. 
These changes have been associated with GB sliding for the nc mate- 
rials at high temperatures which are diﬀerent from the typical inverse 
Hall-Petch eﬀects [39] . The enhanced ductility at high temperatures can 
also be associated with the higher ability of the GBs to emit and to ab- 
sorb the dislocations at elevated temperatures. The mechanical proper- 
ties are tabulated in Supplementary Table S2. It should be noted that 
the tensile test samples consistently failed in the middle of the gauge 
section. 
Any residual stress in the samples can also impact the yield and ten- 
sile strength of the material. The residual stress measured for the HT 
NiCoP samples using XRD was - 91 + /- 103 MPa (Fig. S9). The mea- 
surement mean value was signiﬁcantly lower ( < 10%) than the yield 
and tensile strength values ( Fig. 5 b and Table S2) and had a large er- 
ror associated with it, implying that the stress ﬁeld is weak and outside 
the limits of the XRD measurement capability. This value for Ni-(30–
35%)Co-(0.02–0.03%)P is, however, of the same order of magnitude 
as the residual stress measurements performed by Wang et al. [59] for 
a similar composition material. Their Ni-29%Co samples had residual 
stress of ~-10 MPa. A future study using a higher resolution XRD tool 
equipped with a temperature-controlled stage to measure residual stress 
as a function of temperature would improve our understanding of the 
mechanical testing results. 
3.4. Fatigue testing results 
For fatigue testing, achieving one million cycles is considered as the 
endurance limit. A preliminary assessment showed that a couple of non- 
HT nc NiCoP samples reached one million cycles at 750 and 825 MPa 
during 25 °C temperature fatigue. Further fatigue testing showed that 
the HT NiCoP (300 °C 1 h heat-treated) can withstand more than one 
million cycles from room temperature to 200°C for an upper-stress value 
of 750–825 MPa. Stress vs number of cycles (S-N) curves with actual 
maximum fatigue stress and maximum fatigue stress normalized with 
the tensile strength are shown in Fig. 6 a and Fig. 6 b, respectively. Fa- 
tigue life degradation was observed only at 300 °C where the endurance 
limit was ~580 MPa as seen in Fig. 6 c. 
The GB mediated processes including GB migration, grain rotation, 
and grain coalescence are typically involved in the abnormal grain 
growth preceding the fatigue crack initiation. This abnormal grain 
growth in typical fatigue fractured samples was conﬁrmed through FIB 
cross-section at the fracture surface as observed in Fig. 7 f. Typical frac- 
ture surface showed a triangular region as seen in Fig. 7 a. The crack 
initiation usually occurred at the base of the triangle. As observed in 
Fig. 7 b–f, the abnormal grain growth preceding the crack initiation oc- 
curred in an extremely localized region. Once the crack started propa- 
gating, the cross-section area reduced, and the samples failed through 
tensile overload. Here, the ion beam images shown in Fig. 7 c–f do not

All these processes can get impacted by high temperature resulting 
in an increase in GB related activities [ 39 , 41 , 60 ]. Here in the current 
study, however, we ﬁnd that the material held its fatigue endurance 
limit ( Fig. 6 c) from room temperature to 200 °C despite the tensile and 
yield strengths reducing by more than 20% and 60%, respectively, at 
the higher temperature. Heckman et al. [61] studied the NiFe system 
with diﬀerent grain sizes and found a fair correlation between the ten- 
sile strength and the fatigue endurance limit. A similar analysis using 
the fatigue amplitude normalized with the tensile strength, however, 
showed that the fatigue performance for the 300 °C samples was much 
higher than that at the rest of the temperatures ( Fig. 6 b). This indicates 
a diﬀerent failure mechanism for the samples tested at 300 °C. 
It was observed that the failure location for the samples fatigued at 
25 to 200 °C (here onwards referred to as “lower temperatures ”) was 
always at the end of the gauge section with cracks initiating in the cur- 
vature region. There was no correlation with the sample orientation 
in the testing setup (top or bottom of the gauge section), ruling out 
any setup related misalignment. For 300 °C fatigue, however, the sam- 
ples consistently failed in the middle of the gauge section. As shown in 
Fig. 8 , the target temperature was always achieved in the middle of the 
sample. The ends of the gauge section are always colder due to rapid 
heat dissipation through the rest of the sample. The crack location can 
be explained based on the actual temperatures at the crack initiation, 
mechanical properties of the material at diﬀerent temperatures, and a 
stress concentration due to the curvature at the end of the gauge section. 
Based on the sample geometry, the curvature leads to a stress concen- 
tration factor of 1.15–1.20 [62] (i.e. 15–20% higher stress at the end 
of the gauge section in a very localized region of the sample) which 
was not accounted for during the design phase of the dogbone molds. 
For the fatigue at lower temperatures, the yield strength of the material 
at the center of the gauge section is consistently higher than the maxi- 
mum fatigue stress ( Fig. 5 b). These samples operate in the elastic region 
throughout the fatigue cycle. In this case, the crack initiation occurs at 
the highest stress concentration region near the curvature at the end of 
the gauge section. However, since the yield strength drops below the 
maximum fatigue stress for the 300 °C fatigue testing ( Fig. 5 b), the plas- 
tic deformation in the center of the gauge section leads to cracking and 
moves the fracture location in the “hot zone ”o f the sample. It is still 
remarkable that the endurance limit (580 MPa) at 300 °C is equivalent 
to the yield strength of the material (555 MPa) at 300 °C. 
The current endurance limit values at the lower temperatures (~
750–825 MPa) can be considered lower bound values mainly driven by 
the stress concentration due to curvature in the samples. Furthermore, 
based on a sampling of a subset of failed unit, it was evident that the 
cracks initiated at the surface facing the substrate. Electroformed sam- 
ples are known to have a signiﬁcant amount of internal stress at the

4. Conclusions 
To conclude, we have developed a novel Joule-heating based tech- 
nique to measure high-temperature mechanical properties and fatigue 
performance of nc NiCoP. The material exhibited extremely high yield 
and tensile strength ( > 1.5 GPa and 2 GPa, respectively) which did not 
degrade signiﬁcantly ( > 10%) until 130 °C. An in-situ residual stress mea- 
surement as a function of temperature using XRD, along with a TEM 
investigation of the chemical structure at the nanoscale, will provide 
additional insight to more thoroughly interpret the mechanical proper- 
ties measured here. Fatigue testing revealed that the material held its 
endurance limit from room temperature to 200 °C. The fatigue life was 
reduced by an order of magnitude at 300 °C. Increased GB sliding as pos- 
tulated by Sergueeva et al. [39] and MD simulation results by Panzarino 
et al. showing faster GB migration and coalescence at high tempera- 
ture [41] may explain the reduced strength and fatigue performance at 
300 °C. Fatigue performance for normalized stress level was, however, 
signiﬁcantly higher for 300 °C samples than the other lower temperature 
samples. It is possible that lower temperature samples would perform at 
similar normalized stress levels if the geometrically induced stress con- 
centration was not present. Additional studies with a modiﬁed dogbone 
geometry are needed to test this hypothesis. High-temperature mechan- 
ical properties along with stress concentration resulting from the sample 
geometry have been used to explain the fatigue life and crack locations. 
It would be interesting to observe the temperature-dependent fatigue re- 
sistance of the high thermal stability alloys designed based on GB solute 
segregation as proposed by Darling et al. [44] . This type of experiment 
can test if the GB segregate atoms which suppress thermal grain growth 
can also help suppress the abnormal grain growth during and possible 
GB sliding at high temperature during the fatigue of the nc alloys. A 
similar guide for the mechanical stability of the nc grains can also be 
extremely helpful in designing alloys for diﬀerent applications. Here, 
we conclude by emphasizing a need to develop a deformation mecha- 
nism map for nc materials similar to the Yamakov et al. with an added 
variable of temperature along with stress and grain size [64] .
