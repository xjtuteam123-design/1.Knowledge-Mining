abstract
Anterior cervical decompression for two or more cervical spondylotic levels can be performed using
either multiple anterior cervical discectomies and fusion or anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion
(ACCF). A variety of options for ACCF implants exist but to our knowledge, there is no clinical data for
the use of tantalum trabecular metal implants (TTMI) for ACCF. A retrospective review was performed
of prospectively collected data for ten patients undergoing ACCF with TTMI between 2011 and 2012.
Radiological outcome was assessed by measuring the change in cervical (C) lordosis (fusion Cobb and
C2–C7 Cobb), graft subsidence (anterior/posterior, determined by the subsidence of anterior/posterior
body height of fused segments; cranial/caudal, determined by the cranial/caudal plate-to-disc distances)
and rate of fusion using lateral cervical X-rays of patients at 0, 6, 12 and 24 months post-operatively. The
Neck Disability Index (NDI) assessed clinical outcome pre-operatively and at 6, 12 and 24 months post-
operatively. Cervical lordosis (Cobb angle of fused segment) was 5.2 /C176(± 4.2/C176) at 0 months and 6.0 /C176(± 5.7/C176)
at 24 months post-operatively. Graft subsidence was observed to occur at 6 months post-operatively and
continued throughout follow-up. Anterior, posterior and caudal subsidence occurred more in the ﬁrst
12 months post-operatively than in the following 12 months (p < 0.05). Average pre-operative NDI was
45%. Average NDIs were 18%, 13% and 10% at 6, 12 and 24 months post-operatively, respectively. ACCF
patients treated with TTMI demonstrated stable cervical lordosis over 2 years of follow-up and 100%
fusion rates after 2 years. Measures of subsidence appeared to decrease with time. Patients experienced
improved clinical outcomes over the 2-year period.

1. Introduction
Anterior cervical decompression for two or more cervical
spondylotic levels can be performed either using multiple anterior
cervical discectomies and fusion (ACDF) or anterior cervical cor-
pectomy and fusion (ACCF). Little data exists in determining the
most appropriate choice of intervention, with results suggesting
comparable fusion, subsidence and kyphotic angulation rates
[1,2]. Variable options for ACCF implants exist, but to our knowl-
edge there is no clinical data for the use of tantalum trabecular
metal implants (TTMI) for ACCF. The purpose of this study was to
clinically and radiologically evaluate the use of the TTMIs for
ACCFs.
2. Methods
2.1. Patient population
Between 2011 and 2013, ten patients in our institution under-
went an ACCF using TTMI (Zimmer, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and
were included in the review. These were patients with spondylotic
pathology producing myelopathy or radiculomyelopathy and were
refractory to conservative treatment. A total of twelve patients
underwent a TTMI ACCF. Of these patients two were lost to
follow-up and were excluded from the series. A total of nine
patients had a single-level fusion and one patient had a multi-
level fusion. One had both ACCF and a posterior lateral mass fusion.
Patient demographic data is described in Table 1 .
2.2. Surgical technique
All patients were placed supine with a gel liner transversely
positioned underneath the shoulder blades. The head was

in situ. Following a lateral X-ray to conﬁrm positioning, a standard
right-sided ventral access approach was performed exposing the
anterior prevertebral space. Intra-operative level checks were per-
formed followed by a formal exposure and dissection of the longus
colli bilaterally. Cervical Koros retractors were placed underneath
the longus colli. Distraction pins were placed at the adjacent verte-
bral bodies and the distractors placed in situ . Discectomies were
then performed, followed by formal corpectomies exposing the
posterior longitudinal ligament. In all cases this was resected to
expose the dura. The corpectomy lateral margins were deemed
adequate when the pedicles could be palpated with a blunt hook.
The endplates were cleaned and the appropriate sized TTMI cho-
sen. Once implanted, anterior plates (Zimmer) were secured with
two variable-angle 15 mm screws in the cranial vertebra and two
ﬁxed-angle 15 mm screws in the caudal vertebra. Two variable-
angle screws were used for the middle vertebra in the multi-
level ACCF. A drain was used on one case only. No post-operative
orthosis was used.
2.3. Data collection
Lateral cervical radiographs were taken at four time points:
immediately post-operatively, and 3, 6 and 12 months post-
operatively. Three major characteristics were determined from
these ﬁlms: cervical lordosis, measured with the Cobb angle of
fused segment and the C2–C7 Cobb angle, graft subsidence by mea-
suring the anterior/posterior heights of fused segment and the
cranial/caudal disc-to-plate distances and assessment of fusion.
Figure 1 and 2 deﬁne these measurements.
Graft subsidence was determined by four kinds of subsidence
(anterior, posterior, cranial and caudal) and the difference between
anterior and posterior body height of the fused segment, modelling
the method used by Park [1]. Subsidence was measured at four
time points—post-operative, 6 months, 12 months and 24 months,
using lateral cervical X-rays. All X-rays were calibrated to the
known height of the TTMI used in each patient. Surgimap Spine
(Nemaris, NY, USA) was used for all measurements.
Fusion was assessed after 12 months and osteointegration was
inferred if there was no evidence of motion deﬁned by 63/C176trans-
lation on lateral ﬂexion/extension radiographs and 65/C176angular
motion on lateral ﬂexion and extension radiographs and no
evidence of radiolucency greater than 50% around the bone-TM
device interface (U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA]
criteria/guidance).
Pre-operative, 6-, 12- and 24-month Neck Disability Index (NDI)
scores were determined.
2.4. Statistical analyses
The amount of subsidence that occurred between time points
was compared using paired t-tests. Signiﬁcance was set at
p < 0.05 and all analyses were conducted with SAS v9.3
(SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
2.5. Ethical approval
The institutional Committee for Medical Ethics approved the
design of the study.

3. Results
The average age of our patients was 57.4 years (38–76 years). Of
the ten patients, two received a C4 corpectomy, four received a C5
corpectomy, two had a C4–6 corpectomy, one had a C4 corpectomy
with C4–6 lateral mass fusion, one had a C5 corpectomy with C4–6
lateral mass fusion. One patient had a C4–6 corpectomy. All
patients achieved osteointegration ( Table 1 ).
There was no statistical change in cervical lordosis ( Table 2 ).
The C2–C7 Cobb angles and Cobb angle of fused segment did not
change signiﬁcantly over the ﬁrst 6 months compared with the
second 6 months (p > 0.05) and no statistical difference was seen
comparing the ﬁrst year with the second year (p > 0.05).
There was signiﬁcant progression in anterior, posterior, cranial
and caudal subsidence. Subsidence occurred the most in the ﬁrst
6 months then slowed down. There was a statistically signiﬁcant
change between the different time points ( Table 3 ).
Before surgery, the average NDI score was 45%, 24 months after
surgery, the average NDI was 10%. There was a steady decrease in
their scores at each time point ( Table 4 ).
There were no surgical complications in our series with no inci-
dences of graft dislodgement, infection, haematoma, cord injury,
dysphagia or hoarseness.
4. Discussion
In our series we found that ACCF patients treated with TTMIs
demonstrate stable cervical lordosis over 2 years of follow-up.
Measures of subsidence appeared to decrease over time but contin-
ued throughout follow-up. Nevertheless, patients experienced
improved clinical outcomes over the follow-up period as shown
by the decrease in NDI scores.
Anterior cervical decompression for two or more cervical
spondylotic levels can be performed either using multiple ACDF
or ACCF [3]. Autograft and allograft implants, such as iliac crest
or ﬁbular strut grafts have been considered the biologic and
biomechanical standard in anterior cervical reconstruction yet
are associated with donor-site and immunogenicity morbidity. A
number of synthetic substitutes for both ACDF and ACCF, have
been developed aimed at facilitating fusion, whilst mitigating
operative site morbidity. The synthetic cervical implants for both
ACDF and ACCF aim to offer all three of the fundamental properties
of autografts: osteogenecity, osteoconductivity and osteoinductiv-
ity, yet mitigate the associated donor site morbidity of autografts.
Trabecular metal is one of the synthetic implants available. It is a
porous tantalum biomaterial with structure and mechanical prop-
erties similar to that of trabecular bone with proven osteoconduc-
tivity. It has shown to be more osteoconductive than other
commercially available biomaterials [4,5] and is already used in
a variety of applications in orthopaedic surgery. Clinical and radi-
ological evaluation of porous tantalum cervical interbody fusion
devices in ACDFs have been reported in a number of prospective
studies. To date, there is no clinical data to validate the use of por-
ous TTMI for ACCFs. However, studies exist looking at the use of
TTMI in ACDF as well as studies looking at the radiological out-
comes of autologous bone graft use in ACCF, which are useful to
contextualise our results.
Autologous bone graft is considered the gold standard implant
in ACCF and this can be used to contextualise our ﬁndings. In Park
et al.’s (2010) [1] comparison of ACCF and multilevel ACDF (using
ﬁbular strut graft) we see a graft subsidence and loss of cervical
lordosis that appeared to occur mainly during the ﬁrst 6 weeks
after surgery and continued to subside at the posterior portion of
the caudal endplate even after 6 weeks. The amount of subsidence
seen after 6 weeks was 5 mm (anterior), 3.5 mm (posterior),
1.7 mm (cranial) and 3.9 mm (caudal). Although direct comparison
with our data is not possible, it offers a reference as to the amount
of subsidence expected in ACCF when measuring the four kinds of
subsidence.
There are few studies that concern the use of TTMI in ACDF but
only Fernandez-Fairen et al. [6] measured subsidence. In their
prospective randomised controlled study of 61 patients ran-
domised to ACDF with tantalum metal implants and ACDF with
bone graft, they saw a 7.1% subsidence rate with 3 mm of subsi-
dence in the 28 patients who had a porous tantalum metal implant
ACDF. NDI scores in their study were 46.8% before surgery and 19%
after 24 months. Löfgren et al.’s [7] study comparing trabecular
metal and the Smith-Robinson technique in ACDF were able to
demonstrate clinical relevant improvement in NDI (set at least

This study has several weaknesses that may limit the interpre-
tation of results. Firstly, this series included a small sample with
some patients not returning for post-operative X-rays at all pre-
scribed time points. The post-operative X-rays were performed
by radiographers without a standardised protocol and as such a
true-lateral X-ray was not always obtained, affording the possibil-
ity of error during measurements. Nevertheless, the Park et al.
(2010) [1] method of measuring subsidence used in our study
addresses the various regions where graft settling may occur and
is likely to capture subsidence. Although there are limitations to
this study, the aim of this series was to provide some preliminary
data in the use of TTMI in ACCF as a pilot study.
5. Conclusion
ACCF patients treated with TTMI demonstrated stable cervical
lordosis and good fusion rates over 2 years of follow-up. Measures
of subsidence appeared to decrease over time but continued
throughout follow-up. Nevertheless, patients experienced
improved clinical outcomes over the follow-up period. While a
small sample size limits interpretation of the results of this pilot
study and comparison cannot be made with other studies, we
believe this study demonstrates that TTMI is a radiologically and
clinically sound alternative to conventional grafts in ACCF.
