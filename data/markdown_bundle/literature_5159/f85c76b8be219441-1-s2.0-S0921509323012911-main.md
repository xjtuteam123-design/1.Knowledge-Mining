ABSTRACT 
Oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) refractory metals, represented by tungsten and molybdenum alloy, pos -
sesses many excellent properties such as high-temperature strength, outstanding thermal microstructure stability 
and good resistances to oxidation and corrosion. However, in traditional preparation technologies, these ex-situ 
oxides usually form poor oxide/matrix interface relationships and keep coarse sizes at grain boundaries (GBs), 
which prominently limit the improvement of alloy mechanical properties until now. In this work, we skillfully 
employed novel phase interface engineering to successfully construct completely coherent oxide/matrix in -
terfaces in ODS Mo alloys through the newly formed ternary phase oxide nanoparticles or diffusion layers. 
Furthermore, these ODS Mo alloys are characterized by ultrafine grains, ultrafine subgrains, small and dispersed 
intragranular and intergranular particles. An amount of oxygen impurities can also be depleted via phase 
interface engineering, thus purifying Mo matrix. The phase interface engineering endows ODS Mo alloys with 
ultrahigh strength, excellent ductility and excellent hot machinability, which illuminates a new research di -
rection for those ex-situ oxide dispersion strengthened alloys with excellent mechanical properties. 
1. Introduction 
The strengthening of metals has always been an eternal topic for 
researchers, and is usually represented by four strengthening mecha -
nisms: solution strengthening, fine grain strengthening, second phase 
strengthening and work hardening [ 1 , 2 ]. Among them, introducing 
second phase particles into metal matrix possess universality and 
applicability, which has been widely used in almost all alloy systems [ 3 ]. 
The second phase particles within metal grain interior or at GBs have 
different division of labor. The intragranular particles mainly blocks and 
accumulates dislocations to improve alloy strength, and the intergran -
ular particles could refine matrix grain by blocking GBs movement [ 4 , 5 ]. 
According to the way of adding second phase particles, they are 
divided into in-situ dispersoids and ex-situ dispersoids [ 6 – 8 ]. In-situ 
particles usually are precipitated within the metal grains, which are 
common in copper [ 9 ], aluminum [ 10 ], nickel [ 11 ], cobalt [ 12 ] and 
other alloys. Because of self-precipitation, the in-situ particles tend to 
form stable coherent interfaces with metal matrix [ 2 , 7 ]. The atoms at 
coherent interfaces are one-to-one, and the lattices on both sides are 
connected with each other. The elastic strain field can interact with the 
dislocation stress field near the interface, which hinders dislocation 
movement and thus improve the hardness and strength of material [ 3 , 7 ]. 
For instance, Al – Cu alloys could precipitate in-situ θ particles during 
aging to improve mechanical property [ 13 ]. The γ ′ and γ ′ ′ phases in 
nickel-based or cobalt-based alloy systems are common in-situ 
strengthening particles, in order to control the high temperature me -
chanical properties and improve the microstructure stability [ 1 ]. Due to 
the restriction of robust coherent interface, the in-situ dispersoids are 
relatively difficult to migrate, fuse and grow, thus maintaining their 
ultrafine nanometer size [ 10 , 14 ]. Compared to large particles, dispersed 
in-situ nanoparticles could share the external force together and are not 
prone to stress concentration, thereby reducing the generation of crack 
sources. In turn, these nanoparticles carry more force to continue to 
elevate material strength [ 14 ]. More importantly, previous researchers

For tungsten [ 16 ], molybdenum [ 8 ] or some refractory high entropy 
alloys [ 17 ], the in-situ precipitation of second phase is not applicable. 
Ex-situ dispersoids such as rare earth oxides and carbides have aroused 
the interest of most researchers [ 5 ]. According to the change trend of 
powder grain size, the addition ways of ex-situ dispersoids are divided 
into two types: “ top-down ” ball milling method and various “ bottom-up ” 
chemical methods [ 3 , 18 ]. However, the lattice of above traditional 
ex-situ second phase is usually quite different from the one of matrix, 
resulting in the weak semi/non -coherent interfaces [ 19 ]. Generally 
speaking, the weak semi/non -coherent interfaces have relatively less 
contribution to the strength and ductility compared to robust coherent 
interface [ 16 ]. Moreover, the semi/non -coherent interfaces make the 
second phase easier to move, fuse and grow, which in turn weakens their 
integral pinning effect [ 20 ]. Normally, the smaller the intragranular 
particle, the stronger the ability of pinning dislocation, and the higher 
the alloy strength [ 3 ]. Thus the strength of traditional ex-situ particle 
strengthened alloy still has a great room for substantial improvement. 
On the other hand, ex-situ intergranular particles are also indispensable 
for some alloys (W, Mo, etc.). The intergranular particles can not only 
refine matrix grain, but also play a vital role in improving the structure 
thermal stability [ 18 ]. Unfortunately, the ODS alloys fabricated by 
traditional preparation processes usually possess large intergranular 
particles, no matter how fine or uniform the precursor powder is. For 
example, the intergranular oxide particles size of ODS W alloy is in the 
range of a few microns to several hundred microns, and their phase 
interface with W is semi/non -coherent relationship [ 19 , 21 ]. Due to the 
deformation incongruity between these large intergranular particles and 
metal matrix, their phase interface tends to become the stress concen -
tration region, and further lead to the microcrack initiation and final 
failure [ 18 ]. How to control intergranular particle size is another 
bottleneck of ex-situ particle dispersion strengthened alloys. As afore -
mentioned, although the traditional ex-situ second phase can improve 
strength, they themselves may not be conducive to improving ductility 
[ 3 , 16 ]. Achieving simultaneous improvement of strength and ductility is 
still a severe challenge for ex-situ particle dispersion strengthened 
alloys. 
We deviated from the traditional design strategy of the ex-situ dis -
persoids doped alloy, and turned our attention to the ex-situ particle/ 
matrix interface design at the nanoscale. Taking the traditional ODS Mo 
alloy system as the representative, through distinctive phase interface 
engineering, namely two-step freeze-drying, subsequent multi-stage 
low-temperature sintering and final multi-stage swaging, the stable ex- 
situ particle/matrix coherent phase interfaces were successfully con -
structed. This benefits from the newly formed strengthening phases or 
diffusion layers through the reaction of the ex-situ second phase and 
matrix component. Exhilaratingly, such diffusion layers form robust 
coherent interface with the ex-situ oxide particle inside and the metal 
matrix outside at the same time, which break the bottleneck of the 
interface mismatch between the traditional large oxide particles and 
metal matrix. The strong coherent interface endows the alloy with many 
refreshing features, such as ultrafine grains, subgrains and ex-situ 
nanoparticles, outstanding high temperature machinability, an excep -
tional match of the ultrahigh strength and excellent ductility, etc. At the 
same time, the potential refining, purifying, strengthening and tough -
ening mechanisms were also carefully discussed.

Generally speaking, the intragranular oxide particles can block the 
movement of dislocations, so the dislocations continue to accumulate 
near the intragranular oxide particles [ 3 , 5 ]. The dislocations with 
different Berger vectors interact at near phase interface, and some dis -
locations will be orderly to form dislocation networks (see Fig. 4 c, yel -
low dotted circle A) [ 39 ]. The dislocation network can also capture the 
moving dislocation and thus block the dislocation movement, which 
helps to improve the f-MoY alloy strength [ 39 ]. On the other hand, 
under the action of external forces, when the dislocations released from 
the same dislocation source are blocked by strong obstacles (i.e. intra -
granular particles), the dislocations are arranged to form dislocation 
pile-up groups (see Fig. 4 c, yellow dotted circle B). The intragranular 
particles only exert a force on the dislocation at the head of the dislo -
cation pile-up group, and the other dislocations interact with all other 
dislocations in the group. The appearance of dislocation pile-up group 
leads to stress concentration at phase interface, which also helps to 
improve the strength of f-MoY alloy. The strengthening and toughening 
mechanism will be discussed in detail later.

6.3. Strengthening and toughening mechanisms 
The strengthening and toughening mechanisms of matrix and second 
phase were discussed respectively. Firstly, the grains, subgrains, dislo -
cations, misorientation angle, and purity of Mo matrix all have impor -
tant effects on the performance of f-MoY alloys. According to the four 
strengthening mechanisms of metals, fine grain strengthening is the only 
strengthening way that simultaneously improves strength and ductility 
[ 3 , 5 ]. Unlike low melting point alloys such as Ni alloy and Al alloy, the 
Mo is one of representative refractory metal, and its melting point is as 
high as 2620 
◦
C. Therefore, when high-temperature deformation is 
performed below 1000 
◦
C ( » 0.5 Tm), grain boundaries can still effec -
tively block dislocation movement. It can be seen that the preparation of 
ultrafine Mo grains is of great significance regardless of the mechanical 
properties at room temperature or high temperature. Under the same 
stress, smaller grain (i.e., more GBs) accumulates fewer dislocations at 
GBs than larger grains, which is not conducive to activating the dislo -
cation source of adjacent grains [ 3 ]. It is necessary to continue to apply 
stress, which undoubtedly increases strength. On the other hand, 
compared to coarse grains, ultrafine grains have a smaller stress con -
centration and a slower rate of accumulated dislocations at GBs. This 
reduces the probability of crack initiation, thereby improving alloy 
ductility [ 3 , 5 ]. Therefore, the ductility of f-MoY alloy is higher than that 
of pure Mo or ODS Mo in literatures. More importantly, from the fracture 
morphology at room temperature (see Fig. 9 ), the f-MoY alloy exhibits 
an interesting hierarchical structure. The hierarchical structure achieves 
delamination toughening, and its related principle is to relax the triaxial 
stress condition and passivate the crack tip while the cracks and crack 
branching planes intersecting [ 47 , 48 ]. Rupp et al. explained that the 
cracks begin before the notch tip and only propagate through shear 
fracture of ligament [ 48 ], which helps improve ductility. The pure Mo 
exhibits an inconspicuous hierarchical structure phenomenon compared 
to f-MoY alloy, which is related to its low ductility. 
On the basis of ultrafine grains of f-MoY alloy, many ultrafine sub -
grains with low angle grain boundaries (see Fig. 4 c – d) have been formed 
through a gentle down-top dynamic recovery. When the deformation of 
f-MoY alloy becomes large, the crystal dislocations proliferate continu -
ously. The cellular structure formed by dislocation winding undergoes 
cell wall flattening through the sliding and climbing of dislocations, 
resulting in the formation of subgrains [ 16 , 47 ]. In other alloy systems, 
such as tungsten alloys [ 16 ], nickel alloys [ 42 ] and copper alloys [ 43 ], it 
has been fully demonstrated that subgrains can improve strength and 
ductility simultaneously. The strengthening mechanism of subgrain 
boundaries is similar to that of grain boundaries. Subgrain boundaries 
can capture and hinder dislocation movement, so that the subgrains 
could store high density dislocations and further improve the strength of 
f-MoY alloy [ 42 ]. Furthermore, the width of subgrain boundaries is 
smaller and its misorientation angle is lower than GBs. In this condition, 
the subgrain boundaries are less prone to stress concentration and 
microcracks. Moreover, subgrains possess some inherent effects such as 
passivating the crack tip and providing an ideal path for dislocation 
intersection, which also plays an important role in improving ductility 
[ 16 , 47 ]. From the perspective of purity, phase interface engineering in 
our work has reduced the oxygen impurity content as much as possible, 
as mentioned earlier. For other impurities that are inherently segregated 
at defects, more GBs or subgrain boundaries (i.e., finer grains) could 
dilute the concentration of impurities (C, P, S, etc.). This undoubtedly 
has a positive promoting effect on improving the strength and ductility 
simultaneously. Inversely, the subgrains of pure Mo are hardly distin -
guished, which explains its relatively low ductility.

f-MoY alloys are extremely smaller. Generally speaking, the growth of
