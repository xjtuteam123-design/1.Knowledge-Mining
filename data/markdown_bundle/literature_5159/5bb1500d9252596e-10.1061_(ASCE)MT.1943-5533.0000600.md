Effect of Temperature on Strength and Elastic
Modulus of High-Strength Steel
Wei-yong Wang, Ph.D. 1; Bing Liu 2; and Venkatesh Kodur, P.E., F.ASCE 3
Abstract: This paper presents the effect of temperature on the mechanical properties of high-strength alloy structural Q460 steel. The
strength and stiffness properties of steel degrade with temperature and this deterioration has to be properly accounted for in the fire resistant
design of steel structures. The strength and stiffness degradation is also influenced by the composition of steel. Because of a lack of data
specific to high-strength Q460 steel, design standards assume the high temperature strength variation of Q460 steel to be same as that of
conventional mild steel. To overcome this drawback, strength and stiffness properties of Q460 steel were measured at various temperatures in
the range of 20 –800°C. A relative comparison of measured data indicates that high-strength steel experiences a slower loss of strength and
stiffness with temperature than conventional steel. Data generated from the experiments, namely, load-displacement relationships and vi-
bration frequency, were utilized to develop relations for yield strength, tensile strength, and elastic modulus of Q460 steel as a function of
temperature. The proposed relations developed specifically for high-strength Q460 steel can lead to better fire resistance prediction of steel
structure systems. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)MT.1943-5533.0000600. © 2013 American Society of Civil Engineers.

Introduction
In recent years, high-strength steel (HSS) has found wide applica-
tions in modern construction due to the many advantages it offers
over conventional mild steel, such as high strength and strength to
weight ratio (Wang et al. 2010). The definition for classifying struc-
tural steel based on strength varies in different design standards.
ASTM specifies a nominal yield stress of 690 N=mm
2 (100 ksi)
for quenched and tempered steel, which is the equivalent to
HSS used in plate form ( Bjorhovde 2004 ). The Hong Kong stan-
dard defines high strength steel as having a nominal yield stress
between 460 and 690 N=mm
2 (Hong Kong Buildings Department
2005). The European standard Eurocode 3 (EC3) [ European
Committee for Standardization (CEN) 2005a ] has an implied
delineation point of 460 N=mm2 (67 ksi) to distinguish between
mild and high strength steel. In Australia, high-strength structural
steel is defined as steel that has a maximum yield stress exce-
eding 450 N=mm
2 [Standards Australia (SA) 1998 ]. In China,
steels with a yield strength higher than 420 N=mm2 and ultimate
strength greater than 590 N=mm2 are classified as HSS ( Li et al.
2006). The most common type of HSS in the Far East is Q460
[Standardization Administration of the People ’s Republic of China
(SAC) 2008 ] steel [equivalent to ASTM A572 Grade 65 steel
(2007)], which has a yield strength of 460 N=mm2.
One of the primary applications for high-strength Q460 steel is
in hollow structural sections that are widely used in the structural
members of high-rise buildings. Examples of recent buildings uti-
lizing Q460 steel include Beijing National Stadium (also known as
the Bird ’s Nest), and the China Central Television (CCTV)
building.
When used in buildings, steel structural systems have to meet
required fire resistance ratings specified in building codes, because
fire represents one of the extreme conditions encountered during
the lifetime of a building. For evaluating fire resistance, high-
temperature strength and stiffness properties of steel are required,
and these properties degrade with temperature.
A review of literature indicates that many experiments have
been conducted to evaluate the effect of temperature on strength
properties of conventional mild steel ( Kirby and Preston 1988 ;
Twilt 1988; Chen and Y oung 2004 ; Kodur and Harmathy 2008 ).
Most of these studies were conducted to assess the variation of
strength and stiffness properties of structural steel with yield
strength in the range of 250–350 N=mm
2 [representing ASTM
A36 or A992 steel in the United States ( 2008, 2011)]. V ery few
studies are reported in the literature on the temperature effects
on strength and stiffness properties of high-strength structural
steel. Li et al. ( 2003) conducted experiments to evaluate the proper-
ties of high-strength bolt steel, made of 20MnTiB ( SAC 1988), and
found that the chemical composition of steel has an effect on high-
temperature properties. Chen et al. ( 2006) studied the mechanical
properties of BISPLA TE 80 (SA 2004) steel and proposed relations
for yield and ultimate strength and elastic modulus as a function of
temperature. Ban et al. (2011) reviewed published test data on room
temperature mechanical properties of HSS. In addition, Qiang et al.
(2012a, b, c) recently conducted limited tests to evaluate high-
temperature mechanical properties of S460 steel and S690 steel
in addition to post-fire (residual strength) mechanical properties.

However, there are no specific studies on the effect of temperature
on mechanical properties of high-strength Q460 steel.
The primary chemical compositions in conventional carbon
(mild) steel are iron, carbon, manganese, sulfur, and phosphorous.
The alloy steels are made by adding alloys, such as nickel, titanium,
boron, and chromium, and these alloys influence the mechanical
properties of steel. For example, molybdenum, chromium, and
niobium can increase the fire resistance property of steel, whereas
chrome and nickel can enhance the corrosion resistance of steel ( Li
et al. 2006 ). The chemical composition of Q460 steel is different
from that of mild steel, and therefore, the high-temperature behav-
ior of Q460 steel is envisioned to be different. Current design rules
on fire resistance of steel structures [ CEN 2005b; British Standards
Institution (BSI) 1990 ; China Association for Engineering Con-
struction Standardization CECS200 2006 ] are primarily based
on the experimental results from mild steel and do not account
for specific property variations in Q460 steel.
This paper presents results from experimental studies on the
high-temperature properties of high-strength Q460 steel. A series
of high-temperature property tests is conducted to evaluate the
yield, ultimate strength, and elastic modulus of Q460 steel. The test
data are utilized to propose high-temperature property relations for
the strength and modulus of Q460 steel as a function of temperature.
Strength and Modulus at Elevated Temperature
Steel is often characterized based on yield strength and elastic
modulus property. Cottrell atmosphere is easily formed in low car-
bon (mild) steel, so the low carbon steel at room temperature has a
clear and well-defined yield plateau in tension. However, alloy
steels, such as Q460 steel, do not have a clearly demarked yield
plateau at room or high temperatures, as shown in Fig. 1. Generally,
the yield strength of mild steel at elevated temperature is evaluated
at strain levels of 0.2% offset, or 0.5, 1.5, or 2.0%. The 0.2% offset
yield strength ( f
0.2;T ) is the intersection point of the stress –strain
curve and the proportional line offset by 0.2% strain (Fig. 1). The
yield strengths of f0.5;T , f1.5;T , and f2.0;T are those corresponding to
the strain levels of 0.5, 1.5, and 2.0%.
Because there is no clearly demarked yield point, the definition
for determining the yield strength of steel at elevated temperatures
varies in different design codes of practice. The European Conven-
tion for Constructional Steelwork ( ECCS 2001 ) specifies that the
yield strength at room temperature corresponds to 0.2% offset
strain level, and for temperatures exceeding 400°C, yield strength
is assumed at a nominal strain level of 0.5%. When temperature is
in the range of 25∼400°C, yield strength is assumed to correspond
to the interpolation between the strain level of 0.2% offset and 0.5%
nominal strain. BS5950 ( BSI 1990) specifies that the yield strength
at elevated temperature can be taken at the strain level of nominal
strain 0.5, 1.5, or 2% for different types of structural members. The
yield strength at elevated temperature in EC3 ( CEN 2005b ) and
Eurocode 4 (EC4) ( CEN 2006 ) is based on the strain level of
0.2% offset. Kirby and Preston ( 1988) recommend the use of
1% nominal strain for evaluating the yield strength at high temper-
ature. AISC ( 2005) recommends that the properties for steel at
elevated temperatures are adopted from the European Conven-
tion for Constructional Steelworks (ECCS) Model Code on Fire
Engineering ( 2001).
The elastic modulus is the ratio of stress to strain of material in
the elastic stage. It is often measured as the tangent modulus to the
initial linear elastic portion of the stress-strain curve. At elevated
temperatures, it is difficult to accurately measure strains due to
the complexities with high-temperature strain gauges. This is often
overcome by utilizing the dynamic method ( SAC 1991 ; ASTM
2001), which can compute the elastic modulus of materials at
elevated temperatures. Steel specimens possess specific mechanical
resonant frequencies that are determined by the elastic modulus,
mass, and geometric property of test specimens. Therefore, the dy-
namic elastic properties of steel can be evaluated if the geometry,
mass, and mechanical resonant frequencies of a steel specimen are
measured. Dynamic elastic modulus is determined using the reso-
nant frequency in either the flexural or the longitudinal mode of
vibration. The accuracy and feasibility of this dynamic method
have been validated by Peng ( 2009).
In the current study, the yield strength at elevated temperature is
evaluated at a strain level of 1% nominal strain because Q460 ex-
hibits good ductility. The ultimate strength f
u;T is measured as the
maximum value of stress in the stress –strain curve, and the elastic
modulus ( ET ) is obtained through the dynamic method.
Experimental Studies
To develop high-temperature strength and stiffness properties of
high-strength Q460 steel, a comprehensive test program was de-
signed. The test program consisted of two types of tests, one for
strength and the other for elastic modulus. These tests were con-
ducted at various temperature points in the range of 20∼800°C.

test setups, one for tension strength and the other for elastic modu-
lus, were specially designed and fabricated. The test setup for ten-

a temperature control system. Three thermocouples are mounted on
the furnace to measure temperatures at the upper, middle, and lower
zones. The average value of these three thermocouples is assumed
as the representative furnace temperature. The furnace has an outer

Test Specimens
The specimens for tension tests are 760 mm long and their configu-
ration is shown in Fig. 4. For tension tests at room temperature, the
recommended configuration is as shown in Fig. 4(a). Preliminary
tests reveal that the failure of HSS specimens in the range of
200–450°C always occurs at the ends of the section (cooler region)
because the tension strength of the heated portion of steel increases
with temperature, resulting from the blue brittleness effect. This
effect forms a blue oxidation film on the surface of steels when
they are subjected to approximately 300°C. This phenomenon is
called blue brittleness ( Dolzhenkov 1971 ) and this effect leads
to a small increase in strength and decrease in the ductility property
of steel. To capture this effect, the test specimens used for temper-
atures ranging from 200 –450°C were reconfigured, as shown in
Fig. 4(b). For these specimens, the heating portion is 500 mm,
so it is difficult to calculate the strain through measured axial dis-
placements. For these specimens, only stress –axial displacement
curves are described in the test result section.
All test specimens are prepared by cutting samples from a struc-
tural steel sheet with a nominal plate thickness of 11 mm. A total of
22 specimens are used in this experiment. At each temperature, two
specimens (S-1 and S-2) are tested and the average of the two val-
ues is the result. The actual dimensions of the specimens are mea-
sured using V ernier calipers. The middle part of the specimen is laid
in the furnace and the two ends are connected by the grips of the
electro-hydraulic servo universal testing machine.
For the elastic modulus test, a total of six specimens (S-1 to S-6)
are prepared by cutting a Q460 steel plate. The specimens are of
cylindrical shape and the dimensions are shown in Fig. 5. Special
attention was paid in preparing the specimen to precise dimensions
because the dimensions can have a large influence on the results.

Test Procedure
In the tension tests, the specimen is placed in the furnace, which is
turned on to heat up to a predefined target temperature. The rate of
heating is 10°C= min (SAC 2006b). When the target temperature is
attained, the specimen is maintained at this temperature (in the fur-
nace) for 20 min to reach a steady-state condition throughout the
specimen. After that, a tensile load is applied in increments on the
specimen while keeping the temperature constant. A constant ten-
sile loading at a rate of 0.5 kN=s is applied on the specimen, as
specified by SAC ( 2002, 2006b). The tension tests are conducted
at target temperatures of 20, 100, 200, 300, 400, 450, 500, 550,
600, 700, and 800°C.
For the high-temperature elastic modulus tests, the specimen is
placed in the furnace and connected with two copper wires. The
furnace is then turned on and the specimen is heated to the prede-
fined target temperature. After attaining a stable temperature
throughout the specimen, a power signal is generated and adjusted
until the resonance arises on the computer screen. At this point, the
fundamental frequency of the specimen is noted. The schematic
diagram for the elastic modulus test is shown in Fig. 6.

points, namely, 20, 100, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500,
550, 600, 700, and 800°C. The room temperature elastic modulus

Yield Strength and Ultimate Strength
The recorded load and displacement at various temperatures
were used to calculate stress and strain values. These stress –
strain points are plotted in Fig. 8(a) for 20 and 100°C, and various
temperatures in the range of 500∼800°C. In the temperature
range of 200∼450°C, the evaluation of strain becomes complex
due to variations in section geometry along the heating part
[Fig. 4(b)]. Hence, stress –displacement curves are plotted at these
temperatures.
As shown in Fig. 8(a), with an increase in temperature, the
strength and ductility of Q460 steel generally decreases, ex-
cept at 800°C, when the ductility increases. Results plotted in
Fig. 8(b) show that in the temperature range of 200∼450°C, the
tensile strength is higher than at room temperature. This is due
to the deformation aging of carbon and nitrogen interstitial atoms
in the temperature range of 200 to 450°C. This phenomenon is
called blue brittleness and the temperature range at which this oc-
curs varies with the composition of steel.
From the data generated by the tension tests, the yield and ulti-
mate strengths were evaluated and are given in Table 2. The yield
strength at temperatures of 20 and 100°C, and in the range of 500 to
800°C, corresponds to a strain level of 1%, whereas the yield
strength at temperatures of 200, 300, 400, and 450°C was obtained
by identifying the inflexion of the stress –displacement curves.
The ultimate strength at all temperature points was obtained,
corresponding to maximum stress points on the curves. Also,
the yield (ultimate) strength reduction factor of Q460 steel was ob-
tained as the ratio of yield (ultimate) stress at temperature T to that
at room temperature and is given in Table 2.

At 600°C, Q460 steel retains 70% of its room temperature strength,

The yield strength of Q460 steel is compared with that reported
in literature for other HSSs, such as SA ( 2004) and BISPLA TE 80
(Chen et al. 2006), and mild steel, such as Japanese Industrial Stan-
dard (JIS) ( 1999) and SM41 ( Li and Zhang 2001 ), and SAC
(2006a) and Q235 ( Tan 2000). The comparison of strength ratios
plotted in Fig. 9 indicates that Q460 follows slightly different
trends than other steels. In the temperature range of 150 to 500°C,
Q460 steel has a higher yield strength reduction factor than
BISPLA TE 80 steel, although both are HSSs. The reduction factor
of the yield strength of Q460 is much higher than those of
SM41and Q235 at temperatures over 150°C. Additionally, Q460
steel has a higher strength retention than mild steel. These varia-
tions in strength trends and the better performance of Q460 steel
can be attributed to the chemical composition of steel. The chemi-
cal compositions of these different steels are tabulated in Table 3
and show that Q460 steel contains more alloys (such as chromium
and niobium) ( Chijiwa 1993 ) that are beneficial to fire resistance
properties.
The yield strength reduction factor from the tests for Q460 steel
is also compared with current provisions in EC3 ( CEN 2005b ),
CECS200 (2006), AISC (2005), and BS5950 (BSI 1990) in Fig. 10.
These code provisions are based on measured data for mild steel.
The predictions of CEN, CECS200, AISC, and BSI are similar and
lower than the obtained strength results for Q460 steel for temper-
atures greater than 150°C. Hence, the use of code provisions for the
fire resistance design of high-strength Q460 leads to conservative
predictions.
The ultimate strength of Q460 steel is compared with reported
experimental results on other high-strength and mild steels ( Chen
et al. 2006; Li and Zhang 2001; Tan 2000) in Fig. 11. Fig. 11 shows
that strength trends for Q460 steel and BISPLA TE 80 steel match
well, and the ultimate strength reduction factor of these two steels is
generally higher than SM41and Q235 steels. In the temperature
range of 20 –300°C, Q235 steel exhibits a more pronounced in-
crease in strength due to an acute blue brittleness effect that has
been observed in this particular grade of steel by other researchers
(Chen et al. 2006 ; Li and Zhang 2001 ; Tan 2000).

Conclusions
Based on the results presented in this paper, the following conclu-
sions are drawn:
1. High-strength Q460 steel exhibits slower loss of strength and
stiffness throughout the temperature range of 20 –800°C than
mild steel. This is primarily due to the presence of chromium
and niobium, which improves the fire resistance properties
of steel.
2. The strength and stiffness reduction factors for Q460 steel at
elevated temperatures are quite different, and thus, the prop-
erty relations specified in current codes and standards, which
are based on mild steel, do not apply for Q460 steel.
3. The proposed strength and elastic modulus relations for Q460
steel can be used for evaluating the fire resistance of structures
fabricated with Q460 steel.
