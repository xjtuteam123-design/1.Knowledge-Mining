1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), such as face-centered cubic (FCC) structural CoCrFeMnNi (at.%), exhibit better hydrogen embrit -
tlement (HE) resistance than the traditional 304 and 316 stainless steels [1] . This is due to the low hydrogen diffusion coefficient and 
high hydrogen solubility in HEAs, which results from the strong local chemical environment fluctuation and lattice distortion of HEAs 
[2] . However, the yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of CoCrFeMnNi (at.%) HEA are only ~ 250 and ~ 550 MPa, 
respectively. Such values cannot meet the high-strength requirement of engineering structural materials. To improve the strength and 
plasticity, developing HEAs with nonequal atomic ratios and various strengthening mechanisms through a multi-principal element 
design is considered a cost-effective method. For example, Fe

In recent years, the development of HE-resistant alloys by precipitating and dispersing irreversible hydrogen traps has emerged as a 
research hotspot. In this case, NbC carbide has attracted considerable attention because of its high hydrogen adsorption capacity, good 
thermal stability and superior precipitation strengthening capability [19 – 21] . Thermal desorption analysis (TDA) suggested that the

further revealed that the adsorption energy of hydrogen at a semi-coherent NbC/ α -Fe interface can reach 81.8 kJ/mol. In the FCC 
structural matrix, the coherence of the NbC/ γ interface depends on the size of NbC particles [25] . When the size of NbC is larger than

lattice misfit increases from 15 % to 20 % [27] . These misfit cores may act as potential strong adsorption traps for hydrogen.

2.2. Microstructural characterization
The microstructures before and after tensile testing under in-situ hydrogen charging were characterized using electron back- 
scattered diffraction (EBSD), electron channel contrast imaging (ECCI), scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), and X- 
ray energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS). Samples for EBSD and ECCI characterization were initially ground using 400# – 5,000# SiC 
sandpapers, followed by polishing with diamond (2.5 μ m) and silica (50 nm) suspensions, respectively [14,28] . The acceleration 
current and voltage during EBSD characterization were set at 7 nA and 20 kV, respectively. The optimum imaging conditions can be 
obtained at an acceleration voltage of 30 kV and a current of 3 nA for ECCI analysis. Samples for STEM characterization were initially 
ground using SiC sandpapers to a thickness of ~60 μ m, and then electrochemically polished to a thin film in 4 vol.% perchloric acid 
alcoholic solution under a voltage of 40 V at 30 ℃ . STEM observations and EDS were carried out via the Talos F200X transmission 
electron microscope operated at 200 kV. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were acquired via 300 kV FEI Titan G2 microscope 
with image-side aberration corrector. Geometric phase analysis (GPA) on the HRTEM images was performed using the open-source 
Strain ++ software.

3.2. Tensile properties with and without hydrogen charging
Fig. 4 a shows the typical engineering tensile stress – strain curves of the 0Nb and 0.05Nb iHEAs in air and under in-situ hydrogen 
charging. The equiatomic CoCrFeMnNi HEA (Cantor alloy) prepared by using the same process as the current iHEAs was also tested for 
comparison. As shown in Fig. 4 b – c, Nb microalloying remarkably increases the YS (695 MPa) and UTS (1,049 MPa) when tested in air. 
The YS and UTS of the 0.05Nb iHEA are about two times higher than those of the equiatomic CoCrFeMnNi HEA.
The in-situ electrochemical hydrogen charging remarkably degrades the UTS ( Fig. 4 b) and TE ( Fig. 4 c) of the three alloys, in which 
the negative effects on 0Nb iHEA is the most prominent. By contrast, hydrogen charging does not affect the YS of the three alloys, 
probably due to less hydrogen diffusion into the materials at this stage, and insufficient hydrogen damage or hydrogen strengthening is 
produced. In particular, the 0Nb iHEA shows the lowest TE value under in-situ hydrogen charging, whereas the TE of the 0.05Nb iHEA 
(16 %) is similar to that of the CoCrFeMnNi HEA (17 %). Fig. 4 d quantitatively compares the HE susceptibility of the three alloys using 
the strength loss and plasticity loss calculated by Eqs. (1) – (2) . The 0Nb iHEA shows the highest HE susceptibility with 36.5% strength 
loss and 83.8% plasticity loss, followed by the CoCrFeMnNi HEA with 28.7% strength loss and 70.7% plasticity loss. The 0.05Nb iHEA 
exhibits the lowest strength loss and plasticity loss of 17.4% and 58.4%, respectively. The tensile testing results indicate that the 
0.05Nb iHEA not only has good synergy of strong and ductility, but also excellent HE resistance.

3.3. Cracking paths and deformation microstructures
Fig. 5 shows the deformation microstructures of the 0Nb and 0.05Nb iHEAs after being strained in air with an elongation of ~50%. 
As seen from the EBSD-phase maps, the 0.05Nb iHEA ( Fig. 5 d) contains lower ε -martensite content than the 0Nb iHEA ( Fig. 6 a), 
indicating retarded martensite transformation. The EBSD-Kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps ( Fig. 5 b and e) suggest the 
uniform distribution of microstrain in the two iHEAs after straining in air. High-magnification ECC images in Fig. 5 c and f show a large 
amount of deformation-induced lamellar ε -martensites in the two iHEAs at a submicron scale. Moreover, the width of the ε -martensites 
in the 0.05Nb iHEA is lower than that in the 0Nb iHEA. The differences in the phase content, strain distribution and size of ε -mar -
tensites between the two iHEA indicate that Nb microalloying can retard the growth of ε -martensites.
Fig. 6 compares the local deformation microstructures in the sample ruptured under in situ hydrogen charging. Based on the ECC 
images of the deformation microstructures in Fig. 6 a-b, band structures, such as ε -martensites and SFs are observed in the 0Nb iHEA. 
However, only dislocation tangles and SFs are observed in the 0.05Nb iHEA ( Fig. 6 e-f). Fig. 6 c and g show the KAM maps in the area 
near the sample surface (hydrogen-affected zones). It is found that the local misorientation at GB in the 0.05Nb is much lower than the 
0Nb iHEA ( Fig. 6 g), indicating lower local stress concentration along the GB in the 0.05Nb iHEA. This can also be confirmed by the 
grain reference orientation deviation (GROD) map shown in Fig. 6 d and h, in where the GROD values show a peak at the GB area. 
Moreover, the GROD values in the 0Nb iHEA is higher than that of 0.05Nb iHEA, no matter in the grain interior or at GBs ( Fig. 6 j). In 
addition, the high value of KAM along the thickness direction in 0Nb iHEA is also more profound than the 0.05Nb iHEA ( Fig. 6 k).
Fig. 7 shows the HE crack propagation path and deformation microstructures of the 0Nb iHEA fractured under in-situ hydrogen 
charging. The EBSD-IPF map ( Fig. 7 a) shows almost completely intergranular (IG) cracking. The spiculate crack tip suggests that the 
crack propagated rapidly with low resistance. The EBSD-KAM map in Fig. 7 b reveals that the high local misorientation is primarily 
distributed at the GB triple junction region along the crack. This indicates that high density of dislocations is accumulated in these 
regions, and the deformation along the crack path is not uniform during crack propagation. The corresponding EBSD-GROD map in 
Fig. 7 c also shows that the degree of plastic deformation is different, with an evident hydrogen-enhanced localization of plastic 
deformation in the grains adjacent to the crack. Fig. 7 d–e illustrate the deformation microstructures at the crack tip via ECCI. A number 
of lamellar ε -martensites and deformation twins (DTs) vertically intersect the grains near the crack. In addition, SFs can be observed in 
the grain interior. Our previous study has indicated that these lamellar structures can induce micro-stress concentration at the GB, 
resulting in hydrogen concentration and IG cracking. [14] Consequently, the 0Nb iHEA shows high HE susceptibility and complete IG 
cracking in the presence of hydrogen.
Fig. 8 shows the deformation microstructure adjacent to and 20 μ m from the crack surface of 0Nb iHEA, representing the high- and 
low-hydrogen-concentration regions, respectively. Based on the ECC image shown in Fig. 8 b, deformation in the high-hydrogen- 
concentration region is dominated by dislocation slip, with features of dislocations, SFs, and ε -martensites. For the low-hydrogen- 
concentration region, the dislocation density, as well as the number of SFs and ε -martensites, are remarkably decreased ( Fig. 8 c). 
This change indicates that hydrogen plays a crucial role in enhancing dislocation propagation. Moreover, “Z” shaped cracking mor -
phologies are observed at the crack surface ( Fig. 8 d), which indicates isolated crack propagation caused by the decohesion of micro- 
voids in front of the crack tip. The microstructures reveal that the formation of the “Z” crack may be associated with the nearby shear 
band structures.
Compared with the 0Nb iHEA, the percentage of IG cracking parts in the 0.05Nb iHEA is remarkably decreased ( Fig. 9 a). Moreover, 
the local misorientation along the crack and in the nearby grain interior is higher than the 0Nb iHEA ( Fig. 9 b). The corresponding 
EBSD-GROD map in Fig. 9 c also shows higher grain distortion in the crack propagation area. The KAM and GROD indicate that the 
0.05Nb iHEA exhibits higher plastic deformation capability and growth resistance than the 0Nb iHEA during crack propagation in the 
presence of hydrogen. In addition, few ε -martensites and DTs can be observed near the crack tip ( Fig. 9 d–e). The high-magnification 
image in Fig. 8 e indicates that the deformation microstructures are mainly SFs. Most of the SFs end near the NbC particles. 
Furthermore, dislocation tangles are observed near the NbC particles. These deformation microstructures confirm that the NbC par -
ticles can hinder the growth of ε -martensites and DTs during tensile deformation in the presence of hydrogen.
4. Discussion
4.1. Microstructural evolution
Nb has a strong carbophile, which can be easily combined with C in the alloy to form NbC particles [33] . NbC has skyscraping 
thermal stability with a decomposition temperature of nearly 1,200℃, and it does not suffer from evident growth even after prolonged 
heating in the medium-temperature zone [34] . In the present study, 0.05 at.% Nb was alloyed into the 0Nb iHEA. Nb is preferentially 
combined with C in alloys to form NbC rather than Cr. By considering the thermal stability of NbC and the content of Nb, nano-sized 
NbC particles with an average size of 8.36 nm were formed in the FCC matrix through a thermomechanical process. Moreover, the 
formation of chromium carbides was inhibited, which is harmful to the HE resistance of HEAs [18] .
NbC has the same crystal structure of FCC as the iHEA matrix. However, the lattice parameter of NbC is ~0.42 nm, whereas that of 
the FCC matrix is ~0.36 nm. Therefore, the NbC and FCC matrix could form a semi-coherent interface, which is associated with the size 
of NbC particles [24,35] . Relevant literature suggests that the NbC/γ interface can form when the size of NbC is bigger than 4.1 nm 
[26] . Moreover, when the size of NbC increases from 7.5 nm to 21.1 nm, the misfit of the NbC/γ interface increases from 15% to 20% 
[27] . In the CoCrFeMnNi (at.%) iHEA, a high content of Nb and C can form coarse NbC (~42 nm) and a coherent interface [36] . In this 
study, the nano-sized NbC particles were precipitated in the 0.05Nb iHEA by a semi-coherent interface (misfit 15.38%) through a well-

4.2. Hydrogen-induced cracking
The tensile test under in-situ hydrogen charging suggests that the three HEAs exhibit serious HE susceptibility. This result can be 
attributed to the “ stress-assisted hydrogen diffusion ” [39] and “ dislocation – hydrogen codiffusion ” [40] . The premature failure induced 
by hydrogen results from the hydrogen-assisted IG crack nucleation, propagation and coalescence. This phenomenon has been 
associated with the hydrogen-enhanced decohesion (HEDE) mechanism, that is, the presence of hydrogen can reduce the cohesive 
strength of GBs [41,42] . Research documented in sources reveals that the self-diffusivity and interstitial diffusivity of atoms at GBs are 
significantly greater — by several orders of magnitude — than within the grains themselves, particularly at GB triple junctions. Notably, 
the diffusivity of hydrogen across GBs in nickel [43] and palladium [44] is recorded to be 100 to 1,000 times more than in the bulk 
lattice. This elevated GB diffusivity leads to pronounced hydrogen accumulation along GBs compared to the grain interiors. 
Furthermore, the microstructural stresses that concentrate at GBs affected by band structures enhance local hydrogen accumulation. 
This, in turn, may activate the hydrogen embrittlement-decohesion (HEDE) mechanism and accelerate intergranular (IG) cracking 
[14] .

in FCC structural materials [45,46] . For example, the SFE of CoCrFeMnNi HEA was reported to decrease by 30 % when the hydrogen

4.3. HE resistance improvement mechanism induced by NbC precipitates
4.3.1. Hydrogen adsorption behavior
The mechanical degradation of materials in the presence of hydrogen is strongly dependent on the content and distribution of 
diffusible hydrogen [51] . Suppressing the HE by using irreversible hydrogen traps dispersed in the grain interior (e.g., precipitates) has 
been the main method for developing anti-HE alloys [52] . Thus, MC-type carbides, such as VC, TiC and NbC, have been applied as 
irreversible hydrogen traps in high-strength steels [19 – 21] . The NbC has been widely considered because of its high hydrogen 
adsorption capacity, good thermal stability and precipitation strengthening. However, the hydrogen in the matrix is difficult to diffuse 
into NbC bulk because of its overtop hydrogen diffusion barrier energy [24] . The experimental and computational results have 
revealed that the cores of misfit dislocations at the interface of NbC and the matrix are the deep hydrogen trapping sites [21,53] . The 
adsorption energy of the semi-coherent interface can reach 81.8 kJ/mol [21] . Moreover, the hydrogen adsorption capacity of NbC is 
strongly dependent on the specific surface area, for example, the higher the specific surface area, the higher the hydrogen adsorption 
capacity [54] .
The hydrogen adsorption behavior of the interface can be attributed to the interfacial strain fields, defect density, electronic 
structure, and chemical environment [24,55 – 57] . These mismatches create pronounced strain fields within the interface region, 
altering the local electronic structure and, consequently, the adsorption behavior of hydrogen atoms. Specifically, the strain fields 
induce lattice distortions, potentially increasing the number of adsorption sites or modifying their associated energy levels [58] . 
Moreover, lattice mismatch typically results in a high density of defects at the interface, such as dislocations and vacancies, which act 
as trapping points for hydrogen atoms, thereby significantly enhancing adsorption efficiency [59] . The local strain fields and changes 
in electronic structure around these defects, notably altering the density of electronic states near the Fermi level [60] . Such changes 
can influence the electronic interactions between hydrogen atoms and the substrate, adjusting the hydrogen adsorption energy.

the precipitation of NbC particles and grain refinement restrain the dislocation slip, rather than the nucleation of dislocations.
4.4. Crack initiation mode
Previous works of literature have revealed that impinging ε -martensites and DTs at GBs can induce the hydrogen concentration in 
this region and result in IG cracking ( Fig. 12 a) [14,73] . Compared with the 0Nb iHEA, the deformation mechanism of the 0.05Nb iHEA 
can effectively decrease the local hydrogen concentration along GBs ( Fig. 12 b), thereby improving the HE resistance of the iHEA. 
Moreover, the strong hydrogen adsorption and pining effect on dislocation of NbC can trap the hydrogen atoms diffusing with the slip 
dislocation, which further increases the adsorption of hydrogen at the NbC/ γ interface and improves the HE resistance.
5. Conclusions
In the present study, the strength and HE resistance of iHEAs are simultaneously improved through well-controlled Nb micro -
alloying and a thermomechanical process to control the size of NbC and the NbC/ γ interfacial misfit. The work was conducted by 
microstructural characterization and analysis of the tensile cracking behavior under in-situ hydrogen charging of the 0Nb and 0.05Nb 
iHEAs. The following conclusions are drawn: 
(1) The grain size of the 0Nb iHEA decreases from 5.9 μ m to 2.1 μ m by micro alloyed with 0.05 at.% Nb and recrystallization at 
850 ℃ for 30 min. Nano-NbC particles with a size of 8.36 nm are semi-coherent to the FCC matrix.
(2) After Nb microalloying, the YS and UTS are improved to 695 MPa and 1,049 MPa, respectively. Meanwhile, the HE resistance is 
remarkably enhanced.
(3) The NbC particles effectively suppress the hydrogen diffusion in the matrix by working as deep hydrogen trapping sites. The 
NbC particles also hinder the growth of ε -martensites and DTs by suppressing dislocation slip, which delays the hydrogen 
concentration along the GBs and further improve the HE resistance.
