capturing orientation and temperature dependence of ﬂow stress
Satyapriya Gupta, Curt A. Bronkhorst∗
Department of Engineering Physics, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Madison, WI, 53706, USA
Abstract
We develop a dislocation density based crystal plasticity model to capture the microme-
chanical behavior ofγ′ strengthening nickel-based superalloys. The elasto-viscoplastic model
presented here accounts for hardening behavior of both γ and γ′ which are the two phases
considered in the present work. The model includes multiple strengthening mechanisms such
as Orowan stress, evolving slip resistance caused by dislocation interactions, andγ′ structural
contribution to initial slip resistance. Interaction between γ and γ′, a key feature of these
alloys has been modelled in terms of back stress induced by dislocation pileup or looping
around the large γ′ precipitates. Furthermore, anti-phase boundary (APB) shearing is con-
sidered as the dominant deformation mechanism for shearable γ′ precipitates. In addition
to octahedral{1 1 1}⟨1 1 0⟩ slip, the model also accounts for cube slip systems {1 0 0}⟨1 1 0⟩
which are known to be instrumental in γ′ shearing and introducing ﬂow stress orientation
dependence. Temperature dependence of the initial slip resistance is ﬁtted against exper-
imental observation of anomalous yielding for two diﬀerent single crystal orientations. An
optimization procedure based on the minimization of the error between simulated and exper-
imental stress strain curves, is adopted to evaluate a selected group of material parameters.
Subsequently, proper working of the model is tested against an independent set of single
crystal experimental data available for CMSX-4 around service temperature and MD2 at
room temperature. The model also represents a strong orientation dependence of yield stress
anomaly (YSA), a salient feature of Ni-based superalloys.
Keywords: Ni-based superalloys; γ−γ′ microstructure; Anti-phase boundary shearing;
back stress; crystal plasticity.
1. Introduction1
At present, Ni-based superalloys are the unanimous choice of structural material when2
it comes to high temperature advanced aerospace application, particularly in the hottest re-3
gions of the gas turbine (above 800◦C). Excellent combination of high temperature strength,4
toughness, fatigue, creep, and oxidation resistance exhibited by these alloys (McLean et al.,5

the precipitate. Morphological parameters of the alloy such as volume fraction (up to 0.712
in single crystal), average size (in sub-microns) of γ′ and the spacing between them signiﬁ-13
cantly inﬂuence the deformation behavior of the alloy (Sengupta et al., 1994, Keshavarz and14

2. Crystal plasticity model for Ni-based superalloy93
The single crystal constitutive model presented in this section is guided by work performed94
in the past (Rice, 1971, Hill and Rice, 1972, Asaro and Rice, 1977, Asaro, 1983b,a, Kocks95

assumed to be the predominant mechanism for inelastic deformation in γ channels whereas98
plastic deformation in γ′ precipitates mainly depends on the anti-phase boundary shearing.99

section.101

5. Discussion451
As discussed in Section 2, adoption of the model features speciﬁc to Ni-based superalloys,452
are based on experimental evidence. It is important to understand the physical signiﬁcance453
of these mechanisms to enhance the accuracy and eﬃciency of the model.454
5.1. Physical signiﬁcance of back stress455
Back stress capturing the interaction of dislocations in γ and γ′ is one of the most inter-456
esting features of the model. Back stress is required in the model to depict the mechanism457
of dislocations pileup and looping around the γ′ particles which has a strong inﬂuence on458
the deformation behavior of both γ and γ′. The magnitude and nature of the back stress459
is strongly inﬂuenced by the volume fraction and size of the γ′ along with the deformation460
magnitude. It is implemented in the model in such a way that it provides hardening for γ461
channel and help in shearing of γ′ precipitate, when loaded in forward direction and vice462
versa on the load reversal. However, deformation behavior of single crystal Ni-based super-463
alloys with large γ′ volume fraction such as CMSX-4 and MD2 considered in present study464
are dominated by the back stress inﬂuence on the γ′ precipitate.465
5.2. Rational behind dual ﬂow rule466
A signiﬁcant diﬀerence in the atomic arrangements of the γ andγ′ phases is the primary467
reason for the disparities in their active deformation mechanisms. Following are the under-468
lying diﬀerences in the plastic deformation of γ and γ′. Enhancing the ability of the model469
to capture these diﬀerences is the rational behind having separate ﬂow rules for γ and γ′.470
• Being a disordered fcc structure, γ channels exhibit an easy glide of 1
2⟨1 1 0⟩γ dislo-471
cations on octahedral planes, whereas shear of dislocation sparse ordered L12 γ′ pre-472
cipitates require a pair of 1
2⟨1 1 0⟩γ dislocations forming APB or stacking faults (Long473

5.3. APB shearing and its temperature dependence492
Ni-based superalloys rely heavily onγ′ precipitate shearing for their overall strength. Cre-493
ation of APB, when a pair of a
2⟨1 1 0⟩γ also known as superdislocations, cuts through γ′ is the494
well accepted mechanism of γ′ shearing (Raynor and Silcock, 1970, Milligan and Antolovich,495
1987, D´ ecamps et al., 1991, Lin et al., 2018). Formation of anti-phases withinγ′ disrupts the496
atomic ordering and thereby increases the energy of the crystal (Goodfellow, 2018). Since,497
more energy is required to create APB and thus for γ′ shearing, the strength of the alloy498
increases. In our model, this physical phenomenon is essentially captured by introducing a499
barrier APB resistance for the dislocation entry into γ′ ordered precipitate which is a linear500
function of APB energy. However, the absence of reliable temperature dependence of APB501
energy is still an open question which must be addressed for the accurate prediction of tem-502
perature dependent yielding. Experimentally measured and evaluated APB energy from the503
separation of 1

calculation (Manga et al., 2015) appear to be a promising approach to obtain temperature508
dependent APB111 and APB100.509
5.4. Temperature dependence of slip resistance510
As illustrated in Section 4.3, our model could aﬃrmatively capture temperature dependent511
yield stress anomaly (YSA) for CMSX-4 single crystals. This success of the model can512
be traced back to the adoption of temperature dependent slip resistance ﬁtted against the513
experimental observation of YSA as discussed in Section 2. However, for precise quantitative514
prediction of temperature dependent ﬂow stress, the physics behind the YSA must be added515
to the model in the future. The majority of past research suggest that the origin of the516
YSA is the formation of Kear-Wilsdorf (KW) locks during APB shearing of γ′ precipitates517
(Takeuchi and Kuramoto, 1971, 1973, Paidar et al., 1984, Veyssi` ere, 2001, Bonneville and518
Coupeau, 2008, Antolovich, 2015). KW locks are formed when a segment of 1/2⟨1 1 0⟩{1 1 1}519
screw dislocations preferentially (due to signiﬁcantly lower APB energy on cube planes) cross-520
slip from octahedral to cube planes and become immobile. Increased temperature triggers521
more cross-slip (due to drop in APB energy for cube slip) leading to a surge in KW locks522
and thus higher yield stress.523
