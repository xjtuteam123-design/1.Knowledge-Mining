ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Effect of the c¢ Precipitate Redissolution Rate
on the Microstructure and Creep Deformation Mechanism
of a Second-Generation Single-Crystal Superalloy
Yunling Du, Jianguo Du, Changen Xu, and Yongmei Li
Submitted: 7 July 2025 / Revised: 8 September 2025 / Accepted: 25 September 2025
A novel determination for optimal heat treatment for Ni-based single-crystal superalloys was proposed in
the present work to optimize the strengthening effects of refractory elements. The relationship between the
thermal stability of c¢ phase with the ﬁrst aging temperature and high-temperature creep life was estab-
lished. With the assistance of redissolution experiments, it was found that the thermal stability of c¢ phase
showed a parabolic relationship with the aging heat treatment temperature, and the same tendency ap-
peared for the creep life of specimens with different heat treatments. Generally, the stability of c¢ phase is
closely related to the contents of Al, Ti, and Ta. The higher aging temperature would increase the c¢-
forming elements content in c¢ phase; once the aging temperature is hyper-high, the enhanced solubility of c
matrix could trigger the redissolution of the primary c¢ phase, which decreases the c¢-forming elements
content in c¢ phase. This study veriﬁed that the redissolution experiments are productive for the deter-
mination of heat treatment by evaluating the c¢ stability, which could guide future application of Ni-based
single-crystal superalloys.
Keywords creep properties, heat treatment, microstructure
stability, redissolution, superalloys
1. Introduction
Single-crystal (SX) superalloys are widely used as the
primary materials for high-pressure turbine blades in modern
aviation engines due to their excellent high-temperature
strength, creep fatigue resistance, and outstanding corrosion
and oxidation resistance (Ref 1-3). The exceptional mechanical
properties of Ni-based superalloys at high temperatures
are derived from their unique c + c¢ two-phase microstructure
(Ref 4-6). Nickel (Ni)-based SX superalloys contain more
alloying elements, such as Ni, Re, Al, Mo, and W. The strategic
element Re plays the most vital role in enhancing the
temperature capability of Ni-based SX superalloys by increas-
ing the solid solution strength and suppressing elementary
diffusion during high-temperature deformation (Ref 7-9).
However, the high addition of Re inevitably poses a challenge
to the heat treatment of SX superalloy. Therefore, microstruc-
ture modiﬁcation via suitable heat treatment is necessary to take
full advantage of existing refractory elements to improve its
creep performance.
The most desirable mechanical properties were proposed
when the c¢ phase has a volume fraction between 65% and
75%, a size of 0.2–0.5 lm, and a nearly cubical shape, as
researchers reported (Ref 10, 11). However, the elemental
distribution of the c¢ phases and the mechanical properties
usually diverge from the ideal conditions mentioned above (Ref
12, 13). During the multiple heat treatment, there are two
models available for the formation of c¢ phase: One is the
precipitation during cooling after high-temperature solid solu-
tion treatment, and another is new nucleation during the ﬁrst
aging treatment, for example, when the content of c¢ phase-
forming elements such as Al and Ti in the matrix is too high.
The precipitation mode of the c¢ phases during the aging heat
treatment depends on the distance of the c¢ phase-forming
elements from the already precipitated c¢ phases and the effect
of the aging temperature on the degree of supersaturation of
solute elements in the matrix.
The c¢ phase-forming elements in Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys are composed primarily of Al, Ti, Ta, Hf, and W.
According to the Goldschmidt (Ref 14) atomic radius repre-
sentation, the radii of Ta, Hf, and W atoms are signiﬁcantly
larger than those of Al and Ti. As a result, the diffusion rates of
Ta, Hf, and W atoms are lower than those of Al and Ti atoms
during the same temperature and time aging process. Under this
chemical gradient, the solute atoms would expand from the
small c¢ phase to the large c¢ phase, which could be explained
by the Gibbs–Thomson effect (Ref 15). Besides, the c¢ phase of
heat-treated alloys would dissolve into the c matrix at the
elevated temperature. This indicated that greater creep proper-

developed low-density, second-generation nickel-based SX

The master alloy was ﬁrst melted in a vacuum induction

3.5 Creep Fracture Characteristics
The macroscopic fracture morphology and longitudinal
microstructure near the fracture surface of samples are shown in
Fig. 5.
No signiﬁcant differences were observed in the macroscopic
fracture surfaces of specimens under different heat treatment
conditions with all samples exhibiting a micro-pore aggrega-
tion-type fracture morphology. However, certain differences
appeared in the longitudinal microstructure near the fractures,
where more cracks could be observed in specimens C1080,
C1140, and C1160 (Fig. 5c, o, s) than in specimens C1100 and
C1120 (Fig. 5g, k). Meanwhile, the longitudinal fractures of
samples C1080, C1140, and C1160 all exhibited serious
necking phenomena, whereas the necking phenomena of
samples C1100 and C1120 were not particularly prominent. It
is believed that these discrepancies were attributed to their
different c¢ stability as heat treated at different temperatures.
That is, the ﬁrst aging treatment at 1100 /C176 C and 1120 /C176 C was
beneﬁcial to enhance the stability of c¢ phase to resist creep
deformation at 1100 /C176 C/120 MPa.

3.6 Dislocation Mechanisms
Figure 7 shows the TEM microstructure images of the
C1080-C1160 samples after creep fracture at 1100 /C176 C/
120 MPa. In general, the formation of a dislocation network
at the c and c¢ interfaces and some dislocations cutting into the
rafted c¢ phase could be observed in the deformed microstruc-
ture. The IPP software was used to count the spacing between
dislocation networks. In the C1080, C1100, C1120, C1140, and
C1160, the average spacing between dislocation networks is
0.867 nm, 0.816 nm, 0.772 nm, 0.743 nm, and 0.71 nm,
respectively. Under the inﬂuence of applied stress, the matrix
dislocations repeatedly cutting with these interfacial dislocation
networks, leading to various extents of damage to the interfacial
dislocation networks. Additionally, some superdislocations
cutting into the rafted c¢ phase can be observed. It is generally
recognized (Ref 2, 6, 23) that lattice misﬁt can signiﬁcantly
impact the density of dislocation networks. As the lattice misﬁt
increases, the spacing of the dislocations in the interfacial
dislocation networks decreases, and the density increases.
During the creep test, the interfacial dislocation networks
underwent a continuous process of formation and reorganiza-
tion until rupture of the sample occurred. It is commonly
acknowledged (Ref 9, 12, 24) that dense dislocation networks
can effectively hinder the movement of dislocations. Therefore,

dislocation movement. As the loading stress was applied, the
dislocations were activated. The driving force of dislocation
movement was the misﬁt stress and applied stress. Moreover,
the dislocations bow out into the c matrix when the Orowan
stress is overcome (Fig. 8a) (Ref 25, 26). Previous research
(Ref 27) investigated dislocation motion via in situ TEM and
reported that glide dislocations ‘‘bounce back’’ in another
direction by emitting subsequent dislocations from the phase
boundary or by cross-slipping of the original dislocation
(Fig. 8b). Once both loading stress and thermal exposure are
applied, the elements start to diffuse, with the c stability
element diffusing from the vertical channel to the parallel
channel and the c¢ stability elements diffusing from the parallel
channel to the vertical channel due to counter-diffusion ﬂuxes
(Ref 28), suggesting the gradual formation of rafted c¢.I n
addition, the dislocations are activated and accumulate at the c/
c¢ interface, subsequently interacting and forming a dislocation
network (Ref 29). The formation of a dislocation network could
effectively release the misﬁt stress, thus further facilitating the
formation of rafted c¢ (Ref 30). The rafted c¢ and homogeneous
dislocation network effectively impeded dislocation movement

the second stage of the creep test (Fig. 8c). In the intermediate
stage of the creep test, the dislocation moved by climbing over
the rafted c¢ (Ref 31, 32). This process was attributed to the fact
that the resolved part of the dislocation could climb over the
rafted c¢ by the reaction of the interfacial dislocation network
forming in the primary stage and the movable dislocation in the
c matrix (Fig. 8(d)). As the creep test continues, these two

and the Schmidt factor of this superdislocation is 0 (Ref 35).
Therefore, this type of superdislocation was difﬁcult to shear
through the c¢ phase in the steady state, thus decreasing the
strain rate. Moreover, the superdislocation with Burger vectors
of a <110> is distributed on the (111) plane. In the tertiary
stage of the creep test, these superdislocations sheared rafted c¢
and eventually resulted in the instant accumulation of strain
(Fig. 8e).
4. Discussion
4.1 Aging Temperatures on the Size of c¢ Phase
In this work, the change in the ﬁrst aging temperature
signiﬁcantly affected the c¢ phase size, and the impact factor
mainly depended on the driving force of the solid solution
atoms from c to c¢, which was necessary to change the size of
the c¢ phase. While the c phase was stable at higher
temperatures (solid solution at high temperatures), the c¢ phase
remained stable at relatively lower temperatures. This means
that the difference in the Gibbs free energy between the c and c¢
phases decreased with increasing ﬁrst aging temperature from
1080 /C176 C to 1160 /C176 C, and the corresponding decrease in the
driving force from c to c¢ is shown in Fig. 9(a) (Ref 36). For
specimens with smaller lattice misﬁts, the interfacial energy
predominated, with the smallest spherical surface area for the
same volume. Therefore, c¢ is spherical. For specimens with
larger lattice misﬁts, the elastic strain energy induced by the
lattice misﬁts predominated, resulting in subcubic c¢ phases due
to crystal anisotropy and the lowest elastic modulus ([001]-
orientation). Sluytman et al. (Ref 37) posited the observation
that the cupidity of the c’ phase in nickel-based single-crystal
superalloys exhibited an initial increase, subsequently followed
by a decrease, in conjunction with an augmentation in coherent
mismatch. Generally speaking, the higher the cube degree of
the c’ phase, the longer the high-temperature creep life of the
alloy. Consequently, from the perspective of the cubic degree of
the c’ phase, it can be conﬁrmed that the high-temperature
creep life of the alloy will ﬁrst increase and then decrease with
the increase in the coherent mismatch degree. Secondly, when
the degree of coherent mismatch between the two phases is
excessively high, the c/c’ phases will lose their coherent
relationship and become semi coherent/non coherent interfaces.
The coherent phase interface is a signiﬁcant high-temperature
strengthening structure of nickel-based single-crystal high-
temperature alloys. It has been demonstrated that this interface
can compromise the creep resistance of the alloy during its
transformation into a semi-coherent or non-coherent interface.
This is also an important reason why the high-temperature
creep life of the alloy will ﬁrst increase and then decrease with
the increase in coherent mismatch.

the morphology and phase relationships of the c and c¢ phases.

content of c¢-forming elements Al, Ti, Ta. The addition of Re,
Mo, and W also beneﬁts to improve the thermal stability of c¢

believed to be related to the varied c¢-forming elements content
in c¢ phase as the ﬁrst aging temperature changed. It is widely
believed (Ref 5, 7, 40) that during the ﬁrst aging treatment
process, four processes occur simultaneously, including the
nucleation, growth, and maturation of two types of c¢ particles,
as well as re-dissolution. Herein, the c phase is signiﬁcantly
supersaturated in Al, Ti, and Ta due to the quick cooling after
solid solution treatment. The aging treatment at elevated
temperatures would facilitate the diffusion of Al, Ti, and Ta
from the supersaturated c matrix to adjacent small c¢ particles.
Therefore, a higher aging temperature will increase the content
of c¢-forming elements in the c¢ phase, thereby enhancing
thermal stability. However, once the aging temperature is
hyper-high, the enhanced solubility of c matrix could trigger
the redissolution of the primary c¢ phase and even suppress the
growth of particles, which manifests that the typer high aging
temperature would decrease the c¢-forming elements content in
c¢ phase, which thereby improves the thermal stability.
4.3 Relationship Between c¢ Stability and Creep Properties
To further analyze the impact of the heat treatment regime at
different aging temperatures on the stability of the c¢ precip-

changes, Fig. 10 shows an integration of the creep curves with

dissolution at 1200 /C176 C. For the sample (C1120) subjected to
heat treatment at an aging temperature of 1120 /C176 C, the c¢
precipitates appeared more robust and stable. Correspondingly,
it exhibited the longest creep life. It can also be seen from the
above description that the stability of the precipitates during the

for the samples subjected to other aging temperature heat

were all approximately 65%, whereas the creep lives signiﬁ-
cantly differed. The primary reason for this difference lies in the
varying effects of aging temperature on the diffusion rates of
various elements in the alloy, particularly its impact on the
content of refractory elements in the c¢ phase.

4.4 Effects of Lattice Misﬁt on Creep Properties
Lattice misﬁt has a signiﬁcant effect on the microstructure of
the precipitates. The elastic strain energy caused by lattice
misﬁt and the increased interfacial energy resulting from
precipitation act as barriers to the nucleation of precipitates. As
the lattice misﬁt increased, the nucleation resistance of the
precipitates also increased, which affected the quantity and
volume fraction of the c¢ phases. However, with the increase in
the absolute value of the lattice misﬁt, the region where the c
and c¢ phases coexist decreases, making it more difﬁcult for the
c¢ phase to nucleate (Ref 41, 42). In addition, when the absolute
value of the lattice misﬁt was relatively large, the precipitates
interconnected to form rectangular bars, which was not
conducive to alloy microstructure stability. The changes in
the size of the lattice misﬁt affected the size, density, and
volume fraction of the precipitates, which further affected the
creep strength of the alloy.
Interfacial misﬁt has a signiﬁcant effect on the dislocation
motion during the creep process of the alloy. The motion of
dislocations plays a crucial role in the plastic deformation of a
material. When dislocations on different slip planes meet at the
c/c¢ interface, an interfacial dislocation network forms. During
the deformation process, the dislocation network also changes.
The driving force behind the formation and transformation of
dislocation networks is to effectively reduce the interfacial
mismatch between phases. Therefore, the magnitude of the
misﬁt affects the type and density of the dislocation network
(Ref 43-46). Figure 11 shows the relationship between the
creep life and lattice misﬁt of the alloy samples after heat
treatment and aging at different temperatures. Figure 11 shows
that under different aging conditions, the absolute values of the
misﬁt and the creep life did not exhibit similar trends. With the
change in the ﬁrst aging temperature, the creep life ﬁrst
increased with increasing lattice misﬁt, reaching its maximum
at 1120 /C176 C. However, it then begins to gradually decrease as
the lattice misﬁt continues to increase. In fact, the creep life of
the C1080-C1160 samples tended to match the trend of the
misﬁt. When the absolute value of the lattice misﬁt of the
sample (C1080 sample) was relatively low, although the c and
c¢ phases of the alloy sample had low rafting rates, the density
of the dislocation networks that formed at the c/c¢ interface was
not sufﬁcient. It was difﬁcult to effectively impede the
dislocations in the matrix from cutting the rafted c¢ precipitates;
hence, the creep life of specimen C1080 was less prominent.
With a further increase in the absolute value of the misﬁt, the
rafting process of the samples would be promoted, while the
interfacial dislocation lattice of the alloys became denser
(Fig. 6, 7 and 8). This resulted in better conﬁnement of the
matrix dislocations and a signiﬁcant increase in the creep life of
specimens C1100 and C1120. However, with further increases
in the c and c¢ two-phase lattice misﬁts, a signiﬁcant decrease
in the lifetime of the alloy was found. This is mainly due to the
signiﬁcant increase in the rafting rate of the alloy, which results
in the shorter lifetimes of both C1140 and C1160. Even if a
denser interfacial dislocation network can form in the early
stage of the creep steady state, it is soon destroyed because of
atomic migration and dislocation motion.
