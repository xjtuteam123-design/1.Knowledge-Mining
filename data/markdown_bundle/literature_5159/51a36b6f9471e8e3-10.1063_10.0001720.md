ABSTRACT
The mechanical properties of a high-entropy CrMnFeCoNi 2Cu alloy with an FCC lattice have been studied in a broad temperature range
(4.2– 350 K). The microhardness and Young ’s modulus were measured at 300 K for two structural states. The temperature dependences of
the yield strength, deforming stress, and strain rate sensitivity have been obtained. It was found that a high-strength state is realized in the
alloy while maintaining high plasticity, over the entire temperature range. Empirical estimates of microscopic plasticity parameters and
internal and effective stresses have been obtained using the thermally activated deformation model.

1. INTRODUCTION
A new promising class of metallic multicomponent materials,
known as high-entropy alloys (HEAs), has recently attracted signifi-
cant attention from researchers.
1– 8 A high entropy of mixing
reduces the free energy in HEA systems, a result of which is the sig-
nificant increase in the probability that substitutional solid solu-
tions with a simple crystal lattice will be realized in these alloys. As
such, the structure of multicomponent high-entropy alloys is
usually formed by single-phase solid solutions with an FCC or
BCC lattice. The crystal lattice of an HEA, which consists of atoms
of dissimilar elements with different electronic structures and sizes,
is significantly distorted.
5– 7 As a result, high-entropy alloys have a
number of advanced physical properties, including those that are
mechanical. Studies 9– 12 established that HEAs have unusually
strong solid-solution hardening. Single-phase TaNbHfZrTi and
AlTiVCrNbMo BCC alloys, and a number of FCC alloys, have
been used to demonstrate that HEAs exhibit an anomalously strong
athermal hardening, as well as (especially below room temperature)
an enhanced temperature dependence of the thermally activated
critical slip resistance component. Refs. 11 and 12 attribute these
features to crystal lattice distortions that make a significant addi-
tional contribution to the dislocation slip resistance in the HEA.
Ref. 11 explains the anomalously strong athermal solid-solution
hardening using the Burgers vector variation along the dislocation
line (both in length and direction), and by the presence of a com-
ponent perpendicular to the slip plane. A comparison of an HEA
to a pure FCC-lattice metal, carried out in Ref. 9, revealed that
these two cases have a large (several-fold) difference in their activa-
tion energies. This divergence points to an increase in the “friction”
forces generated by the crystal lattice during the motion of disloca-
tions in high-entropy alloys. These dislocations are caused by the
distortion of the crystal lattice, and a decrease in the distance
between the points where the dislocations are pinned by atoms of
elements that generate the greatest deformation. An increase in
solid-solution hardening in HEAs, by way of alloy structure regula-
tion, may be accomplished by introducing microadditives.
5,6,13 For
example, microadditives of aluminum to the Al xCo– Cr– Cu– Fe– Ni
system14,15 resulted in significant changes in the alloys ’ mechanical
properties. A thermal activation analysis of the processes that
control the deformation behavior of HEAs in a wide range of low
temperatures was performed in Refs. 15 and 16 to further analyze
the microscopic mechanisms of plastic deformation in high-
entropy alloys, which are characterized by both lattice type (BCC
or FCC) and microstructure (number of phases and grain size). It
turned out that the most probable barriers for controlling the

2. MATERIALS AND RESEARCH METHODS
The CrMnFeCoNi 2Cu alloy was obtained by arc melting of
elements in a MIFI-9 vacuum-arc furnace, by remelting a 200-gram
furnace charge in a purified argon atmosphere using a tungsten
electrode. The purity of the initial alloy components was higher
than 99.9%. To achieve chemical homogeneity, the ingots were
re-melted no less than 6 – 7 times, after which the melt was cooled
at a rate of 100 – 200 K/s (state I). Metallographic studies on thin
sample sections showed that the ingots did not contain macro-
scopic liquation, and that their density was 8.31 kg/m
3. The
obtained ingots were deformed by rolling in a DUO-500 rolling
mill at room temperature to 60% (state II). The degree of one-pass
deformation was 15%. After rolling, the alloy was annealed in
vacuum for 1 h at a temperature of 1370 K, and cooled together
with the furnace. Structural studies of the obtained ingots were
carried out by X-ray phase analysis using a DRON-UM1 diffrac-
tometer in monochromatic CuK
α-radiation, a scanning electron
microscope Super-probe-733 with a resolution of up to 7 nm, an
Auger microprobe JAMP-9500F with a resolution of (8 – 10 nm) in
the local Auger electron spectroscopy mode, and (2 – 4 μm) in the
electron probe X-ray microanalysis mode. Hardness H and contact
elastic modulus E were measured in accordance with the interna-
tional standard ISO 14577-1:2002 (E). The ingots deformed by
rolling to 60% were used to prepare rectangular samples with
dimensions of 3.8 × 2.2 × 1.8 mm for mechanical compression tests,
while blade-shaped samples for tensile tests had working section
dimensions of 15 × 1.5 × 0.6 mm.
The mechanical tests were carried out using a rigid deforma-
tion machine MRK-3 in the temperature range 300 – 4.2 K, with a
relative deformation rate of 4 /C2 10
−4 s−1 for compression strain,
and 10 −4 s−1 for tensile deformation. Liquid helium and liquid
nitrogen were used to attain cryogenic temperatures. Intermediate
temperatures in the 77 – 300 K range were obtained by blowing
nitrogen vapor through a shower enclosing the sample, and, in the
4.2– 77 K range, by cooling the sample with helium vapors. The
samples were deformed in the temperature range of 300 – 1150 K by
applying uniaxial tension with a relative strain rate of 4 /C2 10
−4 s−1
on a UTM-100 deformation machine.
Deforming stress σ was determined by the ratio of the load to
the initial cross-sectional area of the sample, and plastic strain, ε,
was defined as the ratio of the change in the sample length during
plastic deformation to its initial length. The obtained “load-time”
diagrams were re-plotted as strain curves in the “shear stresses
τ– strain ε” coordinates. Shear stresses τ were defined as τ = σ/3,
under the assumption that the {111}<110> slip systems of total dis-
locations, typical of FCC crystals, are active in the alloy. During
compression strain to ε ∼ 2%, the shear strain rate sensitivity
Δτ/Δ ln _ε
a was determined as the _εa strain rate increased from
4 /C2 10−4 s−1 to 1.8 /C2 10−3 s−1 (a factor of 4.4).

3.2. Mechanical characteristics of the alloy
3.2.1. Strain curves
Figure 3 shows the typical stress – strain curves under compres-
sion [Fig. 3(a)] and tension [ Fig. 3(b)] for the samples deformed by
rolling to 60% (state II). It can be seen that at all temperatures, up
to helium values, the plasticity of the alloy under compression is
high (> 15%), while the value of plasticity under tension is ∼ 5%.
Figure 3 shows that, as temperature decreases below ∼ 15 K,
the nature of the plastic flow changes from smooth to jump-like.
Regular jumps on the strain curves begin immediately after the
yield point and continue along the entire dependence. The depth
of the jumps Δτ under compression is ∼ 44 MPa at ε ∼ 1– 2%, and
increases with growing strain up to ∼ 115 MPa. The average value
of Δτ under tension is ∼ 200 MPa, and virtually does not depend
on ε. It should be noted that a jump-like behavior of plastic strain
at low temperatures has been observed previously for HEAs of
various compositions in a number of studies.
15– 17 It is assumed
that low temperature jump-like plastic strain in both HEAs and
conventional alloys is caused by slip localization during deforma-
tion, and may be explained by the effect of two related processes:
dislocation (avalanche dislocation motion
18– 20 and twins) and
thermal mechanisms.21– 23
The strain hardening rate Θ = dτ/dε as a function of shear
stress τ, shown in Fig. 4 , clearly demonstrates that in all cases, the
strain curves are stepwise in nature. At small τ, a strong decrease
in Θ is observed with increasing τ, which corresponds to the first
stage of the τ(ε) curve that has a parabolic character. At the initial
stage of deformation, a sharp decrease in Θ with increasing τ is
caused, as is the case in conventional polycrystalline materials, by
the grains ’ gradual involvement in the plastic strain, due to their
different orientation with respect to the direction of the external
force, and their uneven distribution of internal stress concentra-
tors, which act as sources of dislocation nucleation.
24,25 At the
second stage of linear hardening, the value of Θ changes insignifi-
cantly with increasing τ, and increases with decreasing
temperature.

3.2.4. Features of low temperature fracturing
A change in the shape of the samples is observed during com-
pression strain; it also turned out to be dependent on the tempera-
ture of the experiment. In the temperature range of 300 – 15 K,
under compression up to ∼ 15%, the samples acquired a “barrel”
shape and then bent, i.e., were losing stability. Therefore, the defor-
mation of the samples stopped in this temperature range, at strains
of ∼ 15%. In the temperature range of 15 – 4.2 K, at strains of ∼
15%, the samples fractured into two parts along a plane oriented at
an angle of ∼ 45° with respect to the compression axis, and an
incomplete shear was observed for some samples ( Fig. 11 ). This
method of fracturing observed at cryogenic temperatures is appar-
ently caused by the enhancement of the slip localization processes
in this temperature range. It should be noted that similar tempera-
ture features of the deformation method have been observed by us
earlier for the Al
0.5CoCrCuFeNi HEA.15
In case of tensile deformation, a “neck” is formed during the
strain process over the entire investigated temperature range, and at
∼ 5%, the sample fractures into two pieces along a plane oriented
at an angle of ∼ 45° with respect to the tensile axis, while the value
of plasticity is virtually independent of the test temperature.
Figures 12(a) – 12(f ) show typical patterns of the fracture
surface of the tension-deformed samples at various temperatures in
the range from 300 to 4.2 K. It can be seen that a typical ductile
fracture with numerous flat regions containing elongated protru-
sions, the width and depth of which is about 1 μm, is observed over
the entire temperature range under study, down to 4.2 K. At higher
magnification, Figs. 12(b) , 12(d), 12(f ), and 12(h), local inhomoge-
neities elongated in the shear direction with sizes ranging from
1 μm to 200 nm are visible on the fracture surface. This character of
the fracture, which persists down to helium temperatures, is due to
the rather high plasticity of the alloy in this temperature range.
4. CONCLUSION
Microhardness and Young ’s modulus have been measured for
the high-entropy two-phase FCC CrMnFeCoNi
2Cu alloy in two
structural states. The regularities of plastic strain and the fracture
features have been studied in a wide temperature range of 4.2 –
300 K, during the process of quasi-static uniaxial compression
strain and tension for state II (after preliminary rolling to 60%).
The temperature dependences of the yield strength have been
obtained, and the stepwise nature of the deformation curves has
been determined. It was found that a high-strength state is realized
in the alloy over the entire temperature range while high plasticity
persists. It has been found that as the deformation temperature
decreases to 4.2 K, the ductile nature of the fracture is retained. It
has been found that for the samples deformed by compression, the
absolute value of the shear yield stress τ
0.2 in structural state II is
higher, and its temperature dependence is stronger than in a
number of HEAs and conventional FCC metals. A thermal activa-
tion analysis of the processes that control deformation behavior has
been performed for this case, based on the experimentally deter-
mined temperature dependences of the yield strength, deforming
stress, and strain rate sensitivity (determined at ∼ 2% plastic defor-
mation). Empirical estimates of microscopic plasticity parameters
and values of internal and effective stresses have been obtained.
The performed thermal activation analysis has revealed the exis-
tence of a unified mechanism that controls the thermally activated
plasticity of the HEA, in the investigated temperature range. It has
been shown that most likely, the barriers that control the thermally
activated dislocation motion are local obstacles (clusters) with an
activation energy of H
0 = 0.82 eV, that consist of several atoms of
the same type. It has been found that a smooth plastic flow
becomes abrupt at temperatures below 30 K, due to the slip locali-
zation during low-temperature deformation, which is similar to
conventional alloys. It is assumed that the jump-like deformation is
caused by two interrelated processes: dislocation (avalanche dislo-
cation motion
18– 20 and twins) and thermal 21– 23 mechanisms.
