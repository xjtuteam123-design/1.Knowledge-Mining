abstract
In this paper, we report a signiﬁcant improvement in mechanical and oxidation properties of near
eutectic Nb–Si alloys by the addition of aluminum (Al) and control of microstructural length scale. A
comparative study of two alloys Nb-18.79at%Si and Nb-12.3at%Si-9at%Al were carried out. The
processing for microstructure reﬁnements were carried out by vacuum suction casting in water cooled
thick copper mould. It is shown that addition of Al suppresses Nb
3Si phase and promotes bNb5Si3 phase
under nonequilibrium solidiﬁcation condition. The microstructural length scale and in particular
eutectic spacing reduces signiﬁcantly to 50–100 nm in suction cast ternary alloy. A detailed TEM study
shows the presence of
d-Nb11Si4 phase in Nb matrix. The hardness of Nb solid solution can be increased
as a consequence to a level observed in Nb 3Si intermetallic due to the well oriented precipitates.
Compression test yields the ultimate strength of 1.8 70.1 GPa and engineering strain of 2.3 7 0.03%. In
comparison, the binary Nb-18.79 at% Si alloy possesses an ultimate strength of 1.35 7 0.1 GPa and
strain of 0.2 7 0.01% when processed under identical conditions. The latter exhibits coarser micro-
structural length scale (300–400 nm) and a brittle behavior. The indentation fracture toughness of Al
containing suction cast alloy shows a value of 20.2 70.5 MPaOm which represents a major improve-
ment over bulk Nb–Si eutectic alloy. The detailed thermal studies conﬁrm a multifold improvement in
oxidation resistance up to 1000 1C.

1. Introduction
There is an increasing demand for the development of ultra-
high-temperature structural materials which can replace the Ni
based superalloys for aeronautic and aerospace applications. The
intermetallic compounds based on the refractory metals —W, Mo,
Nb, Ti, V and Cr appear attractive for high temperature structural
applications [1,2]. Among all the intermetallics, alloys based on
niobium silicides with melting point temperature over 2000 1C
and excellent strength even at very high temperatures look
promising [3]. However, these intermetallics have high density
(density of Nb: 8.58 gm/cc) compared to other structural materi-
als such as those based on Al, Ti and Fe (densities 2.7, 4.5 and
7.6 g/cc respectively). Nb-based alloys also exhibit loss of strength
at high temperature and poor room temperature ductility. Apart
from this, the processing of these alloys also presents challenges
due to its high melting temperature. The conventional casting
practice therefore is not suitable and other processing routes
often need to be employed [4,5]. However, the major limitation of
these alloys is the fact that they oxidize very rapidly at tempera-
tures well below the melting point [6,7]. There are several studies
in recent time to address the above challenges [4–25].
The Nb rich end of Nb–Si phase diagram exhibits the presence of
different intermetallics phases such as Nb 3Si, bNb5Si3 and
aNb5Si3 [8]. The phases and their structures present in this system
are well reported [8]. In order to get low density alloy with high
temperature strength, it is important to get a composite of Nb and
Nb–silicides phases (low density). However the application of such
an alloy is limited by poor room temperature ductility and sudden
drop in yield strength at high temperature [3,9]. In order to over-
come the above problems, several alloying additions are attempted
in the literature [10–22]. The additions of Mo, W, Cr, Al, Hf, Ge and
Ga suppress the formation of Nb
3Si and promote the formation of
Nbss/bNb5Si3 eutectic composite. There exist conﬂicting reports on
the effect of the addition of Hf and Mo with the suggestion of
stabilization ofa-Nb5Si3 instead of b-Nb5Si3 phase [9–18]. In general
bNb5Si3 phase is preferred over a-Nb5Si3 due to better ductility at
room temperature [11]. Elements like V and Ta are reported to
promote the a-Nb5Si3 phase, while Zr, Fe and Ti on the other hand
stabilize Nb3Si phase [19–22].
In addition to stabilization of different phases, alloying addi-
tions also inﬂuence the mechanical behavior of the materials.

keeping all the above issues in mind. Among the non-transition
elements such as Al, Ga, Ge, Sn, and Al is most attractive from the
point of view of enhancing the oxidation resistance and room
temperature ductility due to substitution of Si with metallic Al
[24]. Al forms oxides which are even more thermodynamically
stable than Si and Nb based oxides. Al is also soluble in Nb to a
greater degree in conjunction with other refractory elements [25].
A series of papers were recently published by Tsakiropoulos
et. al. showing the effect of Al on phase stability and oxidation
behavior in ternary Nb–Ti–Si alloys [16,23]. In a recently pub-
lished work, these investigators also studied the role of Al on the
microstructure of another ternary alloy, Nb–18Si–5Hf [18]. How-
ever a detailed study of Nb–Si alloys with the only Al addition is
lacking. This paper aims at exploring the effect of alloying
addition of Al and a higher cooling rate that suction cast [26]
copper mould casting provides on the microstructure and
subsequent mechanical strength. We also probe the oxidation
behavior of such alloys and show a signiﬁcant improvement.
Admittedly, we do not aim at probing the consequence of ﬁne
microstructural length scale on the long-term stability and creep
properties in this paper. However, we note that there are short
term applications particularly related to space where fracture
toughness and oxidation resistance are of primary concern. The
results of the ternary suction cast alloy is compared wherever
possible with an Nb–Si binary near eutectic alloy (Nb-18.79 at%
Si) in terms of microstructure as well as mechanical and oxidation
properties.
2. Experimental procedure
We have prepared two sets of alloys, a binary alloy of Nb-
18.7 at%Si composition located at close to Nb rich side of eutectic
composition and a ternary alloy with composition of Nb-12 at% Si-
11at% Al using high purity elements (Nb (99.9%), Si (99.999%) and Al
(99.99%)). Samples were prepared by vacuum arc melting followed by
suction casting[26]. The detailed procedures are already described in
our earlier work [8]. The initial composition analysis was carried out
using an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS) attached with a ﬁeld
emission scanning electron microscope (SIRION-400, FEI make). For
bulk composition, analyses were carried out over at least ten large
regions in different samples. EDS measurements on binary and
ternary alloys show the compositions to be Nb-18.79 70.6 at%Si
and Nb-12.7 70.6 at%Si-970.5 at%Al respectively. The composi-
tions were further conﬁrmed by analyzing the samples using a
ﬁeld emission electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JEOL make,
JEOL JXA-8530F).
The composition analysis indicates a loss of 2 at% of Al during
melting process of Al added alloy. The ﬁnal average compositions
(from ten samples each of the two alloys) are given in Table 1. The
processed samples were characterized by SEM (SIRION-400, FEI
make), TEM (JEOL 2000FX and ﬁeld emission TECNAI F30, FEI
make), and X-ray diffraction (X’Pert PRO diffractometer- X’ pert
powder, Pan Analytical make,) to obtain compositional, morpho-
logical and structural informations. EPMA with nanometer probe
size is also used to determine the exact local composition of
primary as well as eutectic regions in the microstructure. Parti-
tioning of Al in different phases was determined by composition
mapping in STEM mode using the ﬁeld emission TEM (TECNAI
F-30) with the help of a high resolution EDS detector. Thin foils
for TEM were prepared using an ion mill equipped with a cryo
stage (Gatan, model 691).
In order to evaluate mechanical properties, hardness as well as
compression tests were carried out. The Vickers hardness was
measured under a load of 2 N and 0.2 N from load displacement
curves using a CSM make microhardness testing instrument (CSM
instrument make, CSM Micro-Hardness Tester). We have used a
diamond Vickers indenter and a loading time of 20 s. Additionally,
nanoindentation was also carried out to evaluate the effect of
each phase on the composite hardness. A Hysitron make nanoin-
denter (TI 900-Tribo Indenter) was used with 1000
mN load. The
alloys are subjected to indentation with higher loads (5 N, 10 N,
20 N and 50 N) to observe cracks at the corner of the indent and
to determine fracture toughness. The room temperature compres-
sion tests were carried out in a fast response hydraulic machine
(Dartec make Dartec Servo-hydraulic UTM with 100kN capacity).
The sample/die interfaces were lubricated with rosin grease to
minimize the friction effect. The compression tests were carried
out at room temperature as well as at elevated temperatures of
600 1C and 700 1C. The both ends of the cylindrical compression
samples were polished ﬂat prior to the experiments. The H: D
ratio of 1.5 and strain rate of 10 /C0 3/s was employed at room
temperature. The fracture surfaces of compressed samples were
studied using an SEM.
The density for 3 mm diameter and 4.5 mm height cylindrical
samples of binary and ternary alloys were measured according to
ASTM speciﬁcation D792-00 for composite material [27].

The oxidation behavior of these alloys has been studied using
thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) and differential thermal ana-

heated at a rate of 20 1C/min to reach temperatures of 600 1C,

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural and phase evaluation

Fig. 1 (b) and (c) show the typical SEM backscattered-electron
images of suction cast Nb-18.79 at%Si and Nb-12.7 at%Si-9 at%Al
alloys respectively. Both the micrographs reveal the presence of a

alloy obtained from the SEM micrographs ranges between

binary alloy (300–400 nm). The standard ASTM E562 method has

The wavelength dispersive ﬁeld emission EPMA is used to

For the ternary alloy, composition analysis of dendrite structure

The evolution of microstructure of the suction cast binary alloy
(Nb-18.79 at%Si) containing primary Nb 3Si phase and eutectic of
Nbss and Nb 3Si has been discussed elsewhere [8]. The aluminum
added ternary alloy microstructure reveals Nb ss and ﬁne lamellar
eutectic colony with cellular morphology. A longitudinal section
of the colony observed by us shows fan like structure of the
lamellae of the phases within colonies. Chadwick et. al. has
reported the evolution of colony structure in binary eutectic due
to the rejection of impurities of growing solid in the melt which
promotes constitutional supercooling [33]. The colony structure is
also reported in under cooled alloy melt [33]. We also note that
the addition of Al in the Nb–Si alloys have promoted lamellar
eutectic. This has been established by observing several sections
as well as fracture surfaces in SEM. Additions of Mo, V, Zr and Hf
are reported to promote similar lamellar eutectic [11,12,19,21]i n
place of rod eutectic that is observed in binary alloys.

density of the alloy. The reported densities of Nb, Nb 3Si and

experimental measurements show the density of binary alloy to
be 7.71 70.02 g/cc and the ternary alloy to be 7.13 70.02 g/cc.

summarize the role of Al in the oxidation process. Aluminum
prevents oxidation of suction cast alloy upto 700 1C in contrast to
that observed in the binary Nb-18.79 at%Si alloy. The high
solubility of oxygen in Nb rich phase containing Al (primary
phase) also resist oxidation. In addition, alloying with Al pro-
motes b-Nb5Si3 phase which is more resistant to oxidation [40].
However, at 1000 1C, oxidation of suction cast ternary alloy is
considerably enhanced, though still less than the alloy containing
no aluminum. On the basis of the fact that oxides in our samples
are signiﬁcantly enriched by Al and a much higher solubility of
oxygen in primary Nb phase can be observed. We argue that Al
must have played a major role for this twin effect which in turn
reduces the oxidation kinetics.
4. Conclusion
The current work reports the effect of addition of Al on Nb–Si
alloy (Nb-12.7 at%Si-9 at%Al) processed through non-equilibrium
solidiﬁcation route. The following are the major conclusions:
1. Al addition suppresses the formation of Nb 3Si phase and
stabilizes the bNb5Si3 phase. The ternary alloy (Nb-12.7 at%Si-
9 at%Al) on suction casting yield ultraﬁne eutectic (Nb ss/
bNb5Si3) composite with nanometer length scale containing
primary phase of Nb solid solution as ductile phase.
2. Al addition helps in formation of d phase ﬁne precipitate,
which leads to multifold improvement in the hardness of
Nbss and hence mechanical properties.
3. The relatively coarser primary phase and ultraﬁne eutectic in
the ternary alloy results in a high strength (ultimate strength
of /C24 1.8 GPa) with 2.3% plasticity. This alloy retains its
strength upto 700 1C (ultimate strength of 1.7 GPa at 2 h
exposure). The addition of Al improves the fracture toughness
of the alloy to 20.2 70.5 MPaOm which is higher than most of
the existing Nb rich Nb–Si alloys.
4. Kinetic studies indicate that the Al addition enhances oxida-
tion resistance signiﬁcantly upto 700 1C. The results indicate
enhancement of solid solubility of oxygen in primary Nb
phase containing Al. This phenomenon in addition to the
formation of stable oxides of Al and Si are responsible for the
good oxidation resistance behavior.
