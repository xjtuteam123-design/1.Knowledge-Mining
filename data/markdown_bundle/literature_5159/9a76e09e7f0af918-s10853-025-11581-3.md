ABSTRACT
Nickel-based superalloys are widely used in aerospace, energy, and chemical 
industries due to their excellent high-temperature strength, creep resistance, cor-
rosion resistance, and high-temperature stability. However, hot cracks are prone 
to occur during welding nickel-based superalloys, which can seriously damage 
the structural integrity, load-bearing capacity, fatigue life, and overall safety of 
welded components. Therefore, this article systematically reviews the composi-
tion classification system of nickel-based superalloys and the current application 
of welding techniques such as fusion welding, solid phase welding, and brazing 
in nickel-based superalloys. It also provides an in-depth analysis of common hot 
cracking issues during nickel-based superalloy welding. This analysis includes 
the formation mechanisms of solidification cracking, liquation cracking, and duc-
tility-dip cracking, as well as the influence of important factors such as element 
segregation, thermal stress concentration, and grain boundary weakening on hot 
cracking. In addition, this paper also sorts out the susceptibility test methods of 
hot cracking in nickel-based superalloys. This review provides a solid theoretical 
basis and technical support for the development of new nickel-based superalloy 
welding technology, hot cracking susceptibility test methods, and the exploration 
of more effective hot cracking suppression strategies.

and derives relevant parameters such as crack length 
and number, thereby quantitatively evaluating the hot 
crack susceptibility of the welded joint [35].

Nickel‑based superalloys
Nickel-based superalloys are a type of alloy formed 
by adding various alloying elements (such as Fe, 
Cr, W, Co, Al, and Mo) to nickel as a matrix, and 
strengthening through solid solution strengthen -
ing, precipitation strengthening, or oxide disper-
sion strengthening (ODS) mechanisms. This type of 
alloy can still maintain high strength, good oxidation 
resistance, fatigue resistance, and creep resistance at 
high temperatures (usually ≥ 600 ℃) and is one of the 
most widely used superalloys [36].
At present, nickel-based superalloys can be 
divided into four categories according to their com-
position: commercial pure nickel alloys, solid solu-
tion-strengthened nickel-based alloys, precipitation-
strengthened nickel-based alloys, and some special 
alloys (Ni–Al compound alloys and ODS alloys), as 
shown in Table 1.

Formation mechanism
Solidification cracking, generally appear between 
grains, that is, crack along the grain boundaries of 
the weld metal [66]. The surface morphology of the 
crack fracture presents a typical dendrite morphology 
[67–69]. As shown in Fig. 10, the structure at the solidi-
fication crack fracture of nickel-based superalloys is 
usually coarse columnar crystals, which are parallel 
to each other and have obvious directionality. Liquid 
film is also observed near the crack fracture. When 
the grains are relatively small and the liquid film is 
less, the fracture surface presents a smooth interface. 
When the grains are relatively large and the liquid film 
is more, the crack fracture surface may be relatively 
rough, accompanied by a large number of dents and 
particulate precipitates. Solidification cracking usually 
occur in the mushy zone during the solidification pro-
cess [70, 71], that is, the mixed zone of the solid phase 
and the liquid phase. The morphology of the mushy 
zone is shown in Fig. 11. The cracking phenomenon is

Regarding the formation mechanism of solidifi-

The tensile liquid film theory believes that liquid 
film and stress are the main causes of solidification 
crack [79]. As shown in Fig. 12a, the weld metal melts 
under a certain heat input and begins to solidify when 
cooled below the liquidus line. Figure 12b shows 
epitaxial nucleation. As the epitaxial growth contin-

center of the weld, as shown in Fig. 12c. As the cooling

the low-melting point eutectic is enriched at the junc-

linear expansion coefficients of the liquid film and the 
surrounding solid phase, large solidification shrinkage 
stress and strain will be generated at the solid–liquid 
interface [82, 83]. When the liquid film cannot adapt to 
the shrinkage strain, it will be torn to form a solidifica-

The intergranular bridging theory believes that the 
existence of intergranular bridging strengthens the 
bonding force between grains in the late stage of alloy 
solidification, making the alloy fracture stress value 
much higher than the result calculated by the liquid 
film theory. Solidification cracking are formed when 
intergranular shrinkage is hindered and intergranu-
lar bridging is destroyed [84, 85]. If a large number of 
intergranular bridges are formed, the bonding force 
between grains will be increased to a level equivalent 
to that within the grain. Then, the stress generated by 
the hindered solidification shrinkage may cause grain 
deformation without destroying the intergranular 
bridging.
The solidification shrinkage compensation theory 
believes that solidification cracking are mainly formed 
in the late stage of solidification because the solidifi-
cation shrinkage of the weld cannot be fully compen-
sated by the residual liquid phase [86, 87]. When the 
solidification process advances to the brittle tempera-
ture range with a small liquid phase fraction, the liq-
uid film cannot be interconnected between the closed 
dendrites and the contact between the solid phases 
is not enough to resist the propagation of cracks, the 
liquid film is torn under the action of solidification 
shrinkage stress, and the liquid phase remaining 
between the dendrites of the weld has a certain com-
pensation effect on the cracks. When the compensation 
capacity of the liquid phase is less than the solidifica-
tion shrinkage rate, solidification cracking will occur; 
otherwise, solidification cracking will not occur. If 
there are more residual liquid phases between the 
dendrites, the compensation effect will be more obvi-
ous [88].
The comprehensive action theory believes that 
solidification cracking is formed under the condition 
that the liquid film is very thin and the solidified den-
drites overlap a small amount [89– 91]. At this time, 
the intergranular bridging is the main part of stress 
concentration, which easily causes the fracture of 
the intergranular bridging between dendrites; when 
the solidification forms enough intergranular bridg-
ing and the solid phase overlap is strong enough, the 
intergranular bonding force reaches the intracrystal-
line level. The stress generated by the obstruction 
of solidification shrinkage only causes the deforma-
tion of the grains, and does not destroy the connec -
tion between the intergranular bridging to produce 
solidification cracking. In addition, the susceptibil-
ity of solidification cracking is closely related to the 
number and distribution of intergranular liquid films. 
The more liquid films there are between dendrites and 
the thin film distribution is formed, the greater the 
crack susceptibility. The comprehensive action theory 
emphasizes the relationship between the mechanical 
behavior of solid and liquid metals and factors such 
as liquid film and intergranular bridging.

tendency of the weld metal and the degree of restraint 
on the workpiece, which can affect the susceptibility 
of nickel-based superalloys welding solidification 
cracking, welding process parameters such as welding

solidification cracking. Welding speed is one of the key 
parameters that affect welding heat input, and heat 
input directly affects the cooling rate and tempera-
ture gradient of the weld, thereby affecting the sus-
ceptibility of solidification cracking. Slower a welding 
speed will increase the temperature gradient of the 
weld and the heat-affected zone, increase the risk of 
local overheating, and thus increase the susceptibil-
ity of cracks [126]. Faster welding speeds will acceler-
ate the cooling rate of the weld, which may cause the 
weld metal to shrink rapidly during the solidification 
process, increasing the susceptibility of solidifica-
tion cracking. Additionally, too fast a welding speed 
will reduce the shrinkage compensation capacity of 
the weld metal, resulting in insufficient liquid metal 
during the solidification process, thereby increasing 
the susceptibility of solidification cracking [127]. The 
greater the welding current, the higher the heat input 
and the greater the susceptibility to solidification 
cracking [128]. The arc voltage affects the arc length 
and power, which in turn affects the heat input. When 
the voltage increases, the arc length increases [129], 
resulting in an increase in the arc distribution radius, 
which increases the heat input of the workpiece. The 
weld area with a large depth-to-width ratio is prone 
to large thermal and mechanical stresses due to the 
rapid cooling rate, which increases the susceptibility 
to solidification cracking [28].
Suppression strategies
Solidification cracking in nickel-based superalloys 
can have many negative effects on the performance 
of welded joints, including reducing the strength and 
toughness of welded joints, shortening the service life 
of welded parts, and also reducing the corrosion resist-
ance and safety of use. Therefore, the generation and 
extension of solidification cracking must be strictly 
controlled during the welding process. In order to pre-
vent the occurrence of solidification cracking, metal-
lurgical measures mainly adjust the alloy composition 
of the weld metal, reduce the segregation of elements 
at the grain boundaries, refine the grain size, and form 
a stable and uniform phase structure in the weld dur-
ing solidification, thereby improving the plasticity of 
the material within the brittle temperature range. This 
can effectively reduce the crack susceptibility of the 
material during welding. Process measures focus on 
reducing welding stress and deformation by optimiz-
ing welding process parameters and controlling the 
restraint of weldments. Reasonable welding process 
parameters can avoid excessive heat input, thereby 
reducing the risk of crack generation. Controlling the 
restraint of weldments can reduce the residual stress

Formation mechanism
Liquation cracking refers to the intergranular cracking 
caused by the remelting of low-melting point eutectics 
on the grain boundaries of the heat-affected zones of 
adjacent welds in the near-seam area or multi-layer 
multi-pass welds due to the welding heat cycle during 
the welding process. Liquation cracking mainly occurs 
in materials containing more alloy elements, espe-
cially high-strength steels, such as austenitic stainless 
steel, aluminum alloys, and nickel-based superalloys.

is usually between the solidus and the liquidus. The 
characteristics of this area are that the parent material 
will be partially melted, the structure is non-uniform, 
the strength and plasticity are poor, and it is easy to 
become a crack source. As shown in Fig. 22, liquation

cracking. Obvious liquefaction traces can be observed

second phase particles, and the crack distribution is 
also relatively dense.
The formation mechanism of liquation cracking 
can be divided into two situations, grain boundary 
segregation, and component liquefaction. The grain 
boundary segregation mechanism believes that under 
the condition of high welding temperature, the low-
melting point eutectic formed by segregation of 
elements in the heat-affected zone or weld interlayer 
is remelted, and liquation cracking occurs along the 
austenite grain boundary under the action of welding 
stress [141, 142]. There are usually four reasons for the 
formation of segregation: 1. the interaction between 
the solid and liquid phases in the state of solid–liq-
uid coexistence (distribution coefficient k0 < 1) makes 
it easy for the solute to transfer into the liquid phase, 
thereby increasing the grain boundary segregation; 2. 
the grain boundaries migrate, so that the most diffu-
sive solutes (C, S, and P, etc.) are easily pushed and 
gathered at the grain boundaries; 3. local melting at 
the intersection with the grain boundaries causes sol-
ute segregation; 4. inhomogeneous new phases (such 
as sulfide phase, carbide phase, and nitride phase) are 
precipitated, etc.

Influencing factors
The liquation cracking susceptibility may be affected 
by the chemical composition (alloying elements and 
impurity elements), degree of liquefaction, grain 
structure, shrinkage tendency of the weld metal, 
degree of constraint of the workpiece, and welding 
process parameters. The action pathways of alloy-
ing elements (Nb, Mn, Ti, Al, Mo, W, Zr, B, etc.) and 
impurity elements (S, P, etc.) on liquation cracking 
susceptibility are generally: liquefaction mechanism 
(formation and composition of liquefaction factors), 
degree of liquefaction (amount of liquid formed), 
temperature range of liquid existence, and distribu-
tion of liquid (wetting characteristics).
Nb is a strong carbide-forming element that can 
combine with carbon to form stable NbC [147]. 
Moreover, Nb will segregate at the grain boundary 
and promote the precipitation of Laves phase [148]. 
The liquefaction of NbC and the melting of Laves 
phase will lead to the formation of liquid film [149, 
150], reduce the strength of the grain boundary, and 
form liquation cracking under the action of stress. 
The addition of the alloying element Mn will reduce 
the liquation cracking susceptibility of nickel-based 
superalloys, because Mn will diffuse into the fusion 
zone to form a high-melting point phase Mn2Nb 
[151], thereby inhibiting the segregation of Nb at the 
grain boundary and improving the comprehensive 
performance of the joint [109].

Ti can form γ′ phase (Ni₃ (Al, Ti)) with Al, and the

the liquefaction of the γ′ phase components and the

The W element will not only be enriched at the 
grain boundaries to form low-melting point carbides 
and borides, but will also extend the solidification 
range, intensify the segregation of Al and Ti in the 
intergranular region, promote the formation of eutec-

cracking [159]. Zr not only promotes the segregation 
of other elements at the grain boundary and increases 
the volume fraction of the γ/γ′ eutectic phase, but also 
forms a low-melting point eutectic with Ni, increas-
ing the susceptibility of cracks [160]. However, Zr can 
refine the grains and inhibit the formation of carbides, 
thereby improving the performance of the welded 
joint [161].
The effects of S, P, and B on the susceptibility of 
liquation cracking are similar to their effects on the 
susceptibility of solidification cracking in the melt-
ing zone. During the welding process, S, P, and B 
tend to segregate at the grain boundaries to form a 
low-melting eutectic phase [162], which significantly 
reduces the interfacial energy required for the liquid 
phase to spread along the grain boundaries, promotes 
the formation of liquid films at the grain boundaries, 
and increases the susceptibility of liquation cracking. 
Although the addition of B will generate low-melting 
point borides and increase the susceptibility of liqua-
tion cracking [162]. However, B is added to some 
nickel-based superalloys to improve creep proper-
ties [163], so the B content needs to be controlled to 
balance the creep strength and weldability of nickel-
based superalloys.
In conclusion, the chemical composition of the 
weld metal affects the formation of low-melting point 
phases, grain boundary segregation behavior, and 
the stability of the liquid film, thereby affecting the 
degree of liquefaction, the temperature range of liq-
uid existence, and the distribution of the liquid. In 
addition, grain size, weld metal shrinkage tendency, 
and degree of restraint also affect the liquation crack-
ing tendency of nickel-based superalloys. The finer 
the grains, the lower the crack susceptibility. This is 
because as the grain size decreases, the grain bound-
ary area increases, and reducing stress concentration 
[164]. Moreover, the size of the grains also determines 
the thickness of the grain boundary liquefaction film 
and the rate of liquid solidification. Small grain size 
will reduce the continuity of the liquid on the grain 
boundary and increase the area of the grain bound-
ary, thereby increasing the strength of the partially 
molten zone. Thinner liquefied films require less time 
to solidify during cooling, which will also reduce the 
accumulation of strain in the partially molten zone 
[165]. The greater the shrinkage tendency of the weld 
metal and the degree of constraint of the weldment, 
the greater the shrinkage stress and stress concentra-
tion generated by the weld metal during solidifica-
tion, and the greater the possibility of cracking in the 
partially molten zone [166].
Welding process parameters affect the liquation 
cracking susceptibility of nickel-based superalloys 
by affecting the welding heat input. Higher welding 
heat input will increase the temperature of the weld-
ing heat-affected zone, causing the low-melting point 
phase at the grain boundary to melt more easily and 
form a liquid film [167]. The presence of these liquid 
films will weaken the strength of the grain bound-
ary, making the material more susceptible to liqua-
tion cracking during welding. However, some studies 
have also pointed out that under high heat input con-
ditions [168], the temperature gradient during welding 
is low, the welding thermal stress is relatively small, 
and the thicker intergranular liquid film can withstand 
greater stress, so welding without liquation cracking 
can be achieved. Under lower heat input, although a 
small amount of liquation cracking will occur, weld-
ing defects such as coarse grains caused by high heat 
input and solidification cracking caused by element 
segregation can be avoided [169].

The intergranular bridging theory believes that the 
existence of intergranular bridging strengthens the 
bonding force between grains in the late stage of alloy 
solidification, making the alloy fracture stress value 
much higher than the result calculated by the liquid 
film theory. Solidification cracking are formed when 
intergranular shrinkage is hindered and intergranu-
lar bridging is destroyed [84, 85]. If a large number of 
intergranular bridges are formed, the bonding force 
between grains will be increased to a level equivalent 
to that within the grain. Then, the stress generated by 
the hindered solidification shrinkage may cause grain 
deformation without destroying the intergranular 
bridging.
The solidification shrinkage compensation theory 
believes that solidification cracking are mainly formed 
in the late stage of solidification because the solidifi-
cation shrinkage of the weld cannot be fully compen-
sated by the residual liquid phase [86, 87]. When the 
solidification process advances to the brittle tempera-
ture range with a small liquid phase fraction, the liq-
uid film cannot be interconnected between the closed 
dendrites and the contact between the solid phases 
is not enough to resist the propagation of cracks, the 
liquid film is torn under the action of solidification 
shrinkage stress, and the liquid phase remaining 
between the dendrites of the weld has a certain com-
pensation effect on the cracks. When the compensation 
capacity of the liquid phase is less than the solidifica-
tion shrinkage rate, solidification cracking will occur; 
otherwise, solidification cracking will not occur. If 
there are more residual liquid phases between the 
dendrites, the compensation effect will be more obvi-
ous [88] (Fig. 28).

refine the grain size and increase the area of the grain

the alloy. Rezaei et al. [178] evaluated the effect of pre-

Therefore, precooling treatment can significantly 
improve weldability.
Ductility‑dip cracking
Formation mechanism
DDC refers to the solid-state cracking phenomenon 
caused by the decrease in ductility of the material 
within the temperature range below the solidus line 
(0.5Ts ~ 1.0Ts). They are a common hot crack in multi-
pass welding of nickel-based superalloys. The mor-
phology of DDC is shown in Fig. 29. It can be observed 
that the crack has obvious intergranular characteris-
tics, the crack path is relatively tortuous, there is no 
liquid film feature at the grain boundary, the crack 
surface is relatively smooth, and the length and width 
of the cracks are relatively small, but the number is 
large and the distribution is relatively dense.
So far, there are three viewpoints on the formation 
mechanism of DDC, including grain boundary slid-
ing mechanism, grain boundary precipitation phase 
induction mechanism, and impurity element segre-
gation mechanism [182]. The effects of grain bound-
ary sliding and grain boundary precipitation on DDC 
mainly include the following three types: the first 
one is shown in Fig. 30a, which is a situation where 
there are only straight grain boundaries but no grain 
boundary precipitates or the precipitates are formed 
outside the DDC susceptible temperature range. Due 
to the existence of straight grain boundaries, under 
the action of stress, the grain boundaries are prone to 
slide and cause a large strain concentration at the tri-
ple grain boundaries. When this strain reaches above 
the critical strain of cracking, DDC will appear at the 
triple grain boundaries. The second type is shown 
in Fig. 30b, which is a situation where there are both 
straight grain boundaries and intergranular precipi-
tates. This situation is more complicated. The existence 
of straight grain boundaries makes it easy for the grain 
boundaries to slide and produce large stress concen-
tration, while the existence of intergranular precipi-
tates not only causes large strain concentration, but 
also reduces strain concentration, thereby inhibiting 
the generation of DDC. The third type is shown in 
Fig. 30c, where there are intergranular precipitates and 
curved grain boundaries. In this case, it is difficult to 
have a large strain concentration because the strain is 
distributed to the precipitates and grain boundaries 
to varying degrees, thus suppressing the generation 
of DDC cracks.

nism and the grain boundary precipitation phase

Influencing factors
The factors affecting the DDC susceptibility of nickel-
based superalloys include the chemical composition 
of the weld metal (impurity elements and alloying 
elements), the tortuosity of the grain boundaries, the 
grain size, the segregation and precipitation behavior 
at the grain boundaries, the grain boundary migration 
and pinning, the grain boundary orientation relative to 
the applied strain, and the dynamic recrystallization 
[185–188].
Most studies on the susceptibility of chemical com-
position to DDC mainly include the following ele-
ments: Nb, Ti, Mn, B, S, P, and H.
The addition of Nb can not only improve the 
intergranular corrosion resistance of the weld and 
induce the formation of fine columnar dendrites in 
the weld [189, 190], but also the formed Nb (C, N) 
and Ni3Nb provide grain boundary locking, changes 
grain boundary morphology, hinders grain bound-
ary sliding, and formation of porosity [180, 191, 192]. 
The effect of Ti on DDC susceptibility is similar to 
that of Nb. The Ti-rich nitrides and carbides formed 
have a grain boundary locking effect [180]. Moreover, 
the addition of Ti will also reduce the intergranular 
 M23C6 precipitates and reduce the susceptibility to 
DDC [ 193]. Mn can not only form MnS with S with 
a melting point of about 1600 ℃ [194], reducing the 
enrichment of S at the grain boundaries, but also form 
stable compounds with Nb or promote the uniform 
distribution of Nb without changing the subgrain 
structure, reducing the segregation of Nb at the grain 
boundaries, improving the ductility of the weld metal, 
and reducing the cracking tendency of DDC [195]. B 
can form borides (such as TiB₂ and AlB₂ ) with other 
elements (such as Ti and Al), which can pin the grain 
boundaries and hinder the sliding of grain boundaries. 
This strengthening effect helps to improve the strength 
and stability of grain boundaries, thereby reducing 
DDC caused by grain boundary sliding [196].
Due to multiple thermal cycles, elements such as S, 
P, and H will be concentrated at the grain boundaries, 
resulting in an imbalance between the intergranular 
strength and the intragranular strength, causing grain 
boundary embrittlement [197, 198], additionally, the 
aggregation of H will lead to local embrittlement in 
the weld metal, further reducing the ductility of the 
weld metal and increasing the risk of crack initiation 
and propagation [199].
The effects of higher heat input on DDC susceptibil-
ity are mainly reflected in three aspects [200– 203]: 1) 
grain coarsening, forming large angle grain bounda-
ries and reducing grain boundary strength; 2) dissolu-
tion of precipitated phases, reducing material strength 
and inducing grain boundary sliding; 3) increasing the 
temperature gradient, resulting in greater residual ten-
sile stress, and under the condition of plastic loss, the 
stress cannot be released, making DDC more likely 
to occur.

to reduce their content in the weld metal. Nishimoto 
et al. [205] studied the effect of filler metals with dif-
ferent P and S contents on microcracks in multi-pass 
welding of 690 nickel-based superalloy. As shown in 
Fig. 33, DDC appeared in three different weld metals, 
and the number and length of DDC increased with the 
increase in P and S content in the filler metal.
In multi-pass welding, excessive heat input is very 
detrimental to the DDC resistance of nickel-based 
superalloy weld metal. Therefore, reducing the heat 
input between layers can refine the grains, reduce 
the dissolution of precipitates and the generation of 
residual stress, increase grain boundary strength, and 
reduce the tendency of DDC. Lu et al. [206] studied 
the solidification cracking and DDC cracking of 690 
nickel-based superalloys during welding. As shown 
in Fig. 34, the length of the hot crack increases sig-
nificantly with the increase in heat input and applied 
strain. Larger heat input leads to greater dilution 
rate, large grain size, and large angle grain boundary, 
which in turn increases the susceptibility to solidifica-
tion cracking and DDC.
Hot crack susceptibility test method 
for nickel‑based superalloys
So far, many test methods have been developed to 
evaluate the susceptibility of hot cracks, aiming to 
determine safe welding conditions and quantify crack 
susceptibility. Hot crack susceptibility test methods 
can be divided into two categories according to the 
way of applying load, self-restraint, and external load 
[207], as shown in Table 4.
The self-restrained crack test is a test method for 
the tendency of welded joints to crack due to internal 
stress during the welding process [221, 222]. Com-
pared with the external load test, the advantages of 
the self-restraint test are low-cost setup and simple 
data. The specimen can be welded without external 
test fixtures, and the crack susceptibility between alloy 
compositions or welding process parameters can be 
compared by visual analysis and crack length meas -
urement, so as to qualitatively rank the materials or 
welding processes [217]. However, the disadvantage 
of this test is that it is difficult to quantitatively analyze 
the crack susceptibility. The specimens show cracks or 
no cracks, and no specific data can be obtained, such as 
solidification temperature range, critical stress value, 
and critical strain value, so it is difficult to compare 
different materials. It mainly includes fishbone crack 
test, circular-patch test (CPT), double filet weld test, 
cast pin tear test (CPTT), etc.
The fishbone crack test is one of the self-restrained 
solidification cracking tests, which is mainly used to 
evaluate the solidification crack susceptibility of thin-
walled structural materials. As shown in Fig. 35a, it is 
formed by machining transverse grooves of different 
lengths on the test plate to form different stress con-
centration areas [208]. The welding direction is usually 
from the side with the shortest slit length (the highest 
constraint on the weld) to the end with the longest 
slit length (the lowest constraint on the weld). When 
the welding heat source moves, the thermal stress 
generated by the solidification of the molten pool will 
induce longitudinal cracks in the center of the weld. 
The expansion of the crack is restricted by the groove, 
thus forming a fishbone-like crack distribution. There-
fore, the fishbone crack test uses the total length of 
the crack as the main indicator of crack susceptibility 
[223]. The longer the crack, the higher the solidifica-
tion crack susceptibility of the material. Zhou et al. 
[208] used the fishbone crack test to evaluate the effect 
of element Co on the crack susceptibility of NS1402 
nickel-based superalloy. The study showed that in 
the fishbone test, the crack tip propagates along the 
dendrite boundary, resulting in intergranular cracks. 
By adding Co, the cracking susceptibility can be effec-
tively reduced.
Circular-patch test has been used to evaluate the 
susceptibility to solidification cracking and heat-
affected zone liquation cracking [209]. As shown in 
Fig. 35b, the circular-patch test is a method of cutting 
a circular patch from the center of a square plate and 
welding it back to its original position. As welding 
proceeds, sufficient strain accumulates at a point 
around the weld to cause the sample to crack. The 
uncracked or cracked portion of the weld length, or the 
minimum weld diameter required to cause cracking, is 
used as the main indicator of crack susceptibility [224].

The cast pin tear test was originally developed by 
Hull to evaluate the solidification crack susceptibility 
of different alloys and determine the phase transfor-
mation characteristics [225]. The cast pin tear test is 
mainly used to detect the crack tendency caused by 
the casting during the solidification process, but it can 
provide a certain reference value for the analysis and 
prevention of welding hot cracks. The cast pin tear 
test is a solidification crack generated just below the 
pin head under the action of critical stress for a spe-
cific alloy. With the further increase in the length of 
the cast pin, the total surface crack length increases 
to 100% circumferential cracks, which eventually 
leads to complete separation of the pin. Therefore, 
the maximum die length without cracks is used as a 
quantitative indicator to evaluate crack susceptibility, 
and the maximum die length of 100% circumferential 
cracking and the maximum circumferential cracking 
response curve are used as additional indicators. The 
main advantages of the cast pin crack test are the small 
amount of material required for the test and the abil-
ity to test mixtures of different material combinations.
Researchers developed an improved cast pin crack 
test [ 226], as shown in Fig. 35d, which replaced the 
expensive and complex suspension melting technol-
ogy with arc melting technology, and has been used to 
study the susceptibility test of solidification cracking 
in nickel-based superalloys. Studies have shown that 
the improved cast pin crack test is very close to the 
solidification crack behavior trend in actual welds of 
nickel-based superalloys in terms of crack nucleation 
and propagation along solidifying grain boundaries 
and subgrain boundaries, solidification crack surface 
morphology, and crack backfilling of low-melting 
point eutectic components [221]. The effectiveness of 
the improved cast pin crack test has been verified by 
direct comparison with other solidification cracking 
tests. Orr et al. [212] evaluated the solidification crack 
susceptibility of ERNiCr-3 weld metals with different 
N contents using a cast pin crack test and found that 
the solidification crack susceptibility increased signifi-
cantly with the increase in N content.

Summary and outlook
(1) The main hot cracking problems faced by nickel-
based superalloys during welding—solidification 
cracking, liquation cracking, and DDC—have dif-
ferent formation mechanisms (respectively, tear-
ing of the liquid film at the grain boundaries at 
the end of solidification, localized liquation of 
grain boundaries caused by thermal cycling, and 
hindered grain boundary sliding at high tempera-
tures and low strain rates). However, they are all 
closely related to localized weakening or lack of 
plasticity at grain boundaries at high tempera-
tures. Element segregation is a key factor influenc-
ing hot cracking susceptibility: severe segregation 
of impurity elements such as S, P, and B, as well 
as alloying elements such as Si, Nb, and Ti, not 
only promotes the formation of low-melting point 
eutectic phases but also significantly reduces 
grain boundary strength, exacerbating cracking 
tendency. Appropriate amounts of elements such 
as Mn and Mo may enhance crack resistance by 
forming carbides that pin grain boundaries or 
alter segregation behavior. Suppression measures 
require a multi-pronged approach: composition 
design through strict control of harmful impuri-
ties, optimization of the content of elements prone 
to segregation, and addition of micro-alloying 
elements; use of process control measures such 
as low heat input welding, optimization of weld-
ing process parameters (such as welding speed, 
welding current, laser power), and application 
of mechanical constraints (such as ultrasonic 
vibration, and magnetron arc swing) to reduce 
thermal stress and strain; and implementation of 
appropriate heat treatment (such as preheating, 
stabilization treatment, and solution treatment) 
to reduce segregation, dissolve harmful phases 
and improve grain boundary conditions.
(2) Accurately assessing hot crack susceptibility relies 
on a variety of testing methods. Existing testing 
methods are primarily divided into two catego-
ries based on the restraint method: self-restraint 
tests and external load tests. Self-restraint tests 
(fishbone crack test, CPT, double filet weld test, 
CPTT) utilize internal stresses generated by the 
specimen’s inherent geometry or solidification 
shrinkage to induce cracks. These methods are 
relatively simple to perform, as the specimens can 
be welded without external test fixtures. They can 
also be used to compare the crack susceptibility 
of different alloy compositions or welding pro -

Although significant progress has been made in 
the study of hot cracks in welding of nickel-based 
superalloys, the following directions still need further 
exploration:
(1) Existing studies mostly focus on the influence of 
a single factor (such as chemical composition or 
heat input). In the future, it is necessary to com-
bine multi-factor simulation technology to reveal 
the multi-dimensional relationship between 
composition–process–structure–performance, 
especially the synergistic mechanism of thermal 
stress and micro-segregation, so as to more com-
prehensively understand the formation process 
of welding hot cracking.
(2) Develop new welding technology and filler mate-
rials: For single crystal or directionally solidified 
alloys, it is necessary to develop low thermal 
damage welding technology (such as ultrashort 
pulse laser welding and cold metal transition 
technology), and develop suitable high-temper-
ature brazing filler metals and intermediate layer 
materials (such as high entropy alloys and nano-
modified coatings) to improve welding quality 
and reliability.
(3) Combined with machine learning algorithms, 
establish a welding process parameter database 
and a hot crack prediction model to achieve inter-
disciplinary integration and intelligentization of 
welding processes. Through big data analysis 
and model prediction, optimize welding process 
parameters, prevent the occurrence of thermal 
cracks in advance, and improve welding effi-
ciency and quality control level.
Future research needs to build a bridge between 
theoretical depth and engineering practice, trans-
form basic research results into effective solutions 
in actual engineering applications, and promote the 
continuous development of nickel-based superalloy 
welding technology toward high precision and high 
reliability.
