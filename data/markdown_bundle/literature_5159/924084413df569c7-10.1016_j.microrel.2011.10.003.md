abstract
The Zn–4Al–3Mg based solder alloy is a promising candidate to replace the conventional Pb–5Sn alloy in
high-temperature electronic packaging. In this study, the tensile properties of Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn alloys
(x = 0, 6.8 and 13.2 wt.%) at high temperatures (e.g., 100 /C176C, and 200 /C176C) were investigated. It was found
that the uniaxial tensile strength (UTS) of Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn solder alloys all decrease monotonously with
the increment of temperature. The elongation ratio at 100 /C176C is superior to that at room temperature
whereas follows a signiﬁcant drop at 200 /C176C. The microstructure observations show that a typical brittle
fracture of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy occurs at room temperature and 200 /C176C under normal tension, whereas a
ductile fracture is found at 100 /C176C. The 6.8 wt.% Sn addition in Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy causes a dramatic
decrease of yield strength, and a slight deterioration of the ductility.

1. Introduction
Owing to their good wettability, high ductility and low shear
modulus, the Pb–5Sn and Pb–10Sn solders has been widely used
in die attaching of power devices, and still remain the exemptions
in RoHS directive [1]. So far there is no reliable high-temperature
solder alloy which can replace these high-Pb solders completely.
Thus it is crucial to seek for a suitable high-temperature solder
which possesses the similar solidus and liquidus as Pb–5Sn or
Pb–10Sn, as well as the material properties. Several candidates,
such as 80Au–20Sn [2,3], Bi based alloys [4,5], Sn–Sb based alloys
[6], and Zn based alloys have been reported [7–11].
The melting temperatures of Tl and Cd are very close to that of
Pb (i.e., 330 /C176C). Unfortunately, both Tl and Cd are toxic as Pb,
which excludes them from the potential element candidates. Bi is
another potential element, as its melting temperature is about
270 /C176C. However, it has been found that the presence of Bi will
enhance the brittleness of solder alloys dramatically. Due to its
low melting temperature (230 /C176C), Sn also cannot be used as matrix
material. It has been reported that Zn based alloy may be a prom-
ising candidate for the high-Pb solder replacement. In particular,
Shimizu et al. have studied the melting point of a ternary
Zn–4Al–3Mg eutectic alloy, and also found that 3% Ga addition in
this alloy can be used for die attaching at 320 /C176C [8]. However, gal-
lium is known to cause liquid metal embrittlement in aluminum
by signiﬁcantly reducing the cohesion between aluminum grains,
and thus lead to severe embrittlement failure [9,11]. Sn addition
in Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy may also be suitable as an alternative
replacement of high-Pb solders. However, only solidus and liquidus
temperatures were presented, and no other material properties
associated to the solder reliability were reported [8].
To this end, this paper is devoted to study the effect of Sn addi-
tion on the material properties of Zn–4Al–3Mg solder alloy, partic-
ularly at high temperatures. The microstructure observations are
then performed to reveal the plausible failure modes.
2. Materials and experimental procedures
The Zn–4Al–3Mg solder alloy was fabricated by using Zn, Al and
Mg ingots (purity >99.9%). The alloy was melted at 700 /C176C under
the CO
2 protective atmosphere and kept for 4 h to achieve the
homogeneous characteristics. A certain amount of ZnCl 2
(0.1–0.15% of total ingot weight) was then pressed into the molten
alloy for the oxide reduction. After 5–10 min stabilization, the mol-
ten alloy was poured into stainless steel mold to obtain the Zn–
4Al–3Mg alloy. Similarly, the Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn alloys were ob-
tained by adding a certain amount of pure Sn into the molten
Zn–4Al–3Mg and performing a subsequent solidiﬁcation.
The melting temperature of Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn alloys were mea-
sured by differential thermal analysis (DTA) technique under high-
purity nitrogen. The ramp rate and the cooling rate of temperature
are 20 /C176C/min and 5 /C176C/min, respectively. The specimen was pre-
pared by linear cutting process, as schematically shown in Fig. 1 .
The surface of the specimen was then polished carefully by using
ﬁne grind paper. The tensile tests were carried out by using an
Instron5848 tester at 20 /C176C, 100 /C176C and 200 /C176C, respectively. The
tensile loading rate is 0.001 mm/s.

After careful grinding and polishing, the cross-section of speci-

phases in the alloy matrix. The microstructures were then

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Melting characteristics
Fig. 2 illustrates the measured DTA curves of Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn
alloys during a typical heating/cooling cycle. Based on the ICTA
(International Confederation for Thermal Analysis) standards, it
can be found from Fig. 2 a that the melting point TM of Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy is 340.8 /C176C, which is consistent with the existing result
[8]. And the onset of nucleation point TN of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy is
330.1 /C176C, which leads to the undercooling of 10.7 /C176C for Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy according to Mueller’s approach [12]. Similarly, the
melting and nucleation points of Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn alloy are
determined as 327.6 /C176C and 324.5 /C176C, respectively, which leads to
an undercooling of 3.1 /C176C. The Zn–4Al–3Mg–13.2Sn shows an und-
ercooling of 4.8 /C176C, which is computed by the difference between
its melting point (324.7 /C176C) and nucleation point (319.9 /C176C).
Clearly the 6.8 wt.% Sn addition reduces the melting point of
Zn–4Al–3Mg to some extent. In the meantime, the undercooling
of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy is also reduced due to the Sn addition. How-
ever, a large Sn addition (e.g., 13.2 wt.% Sn) does not cause further
change of melting/nucleation points, as well as the corresponding
undercooling value.
3.2. Mechanical properties
In the high-temperature soldering for die attaching in power
devices, one crucial issue is to maintain the strength of solder alloy
at high temperature. In this study, the tensile properties of
Zn–4Al–3Mg–xSn alloys were examined at high temperatures,
say, 100 /C176C and 200 /C176C, respectively. The results at 20 /C176C were also
presented for comparison.
Shown in Fig. 3 a–c are the stress–strain curves of the Zn–4Al–
3Mg–xSn alloys at various temperatures, say, 20 /C176C, 100 /C176C and
200 /C176C, respectively. At room temperature, all of three alloys exhi-
bit a brittle fracture when they are subjected to the normal tension
due to the abrupt drop of stress. At high temperatures (e.g., 100 /C176C),
it is interesting that a typical ductile failure of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy is
observed. It can also be seen that the 6.8 wt.% Sn addition, how-
ever, results in a signiﬁcant deterioration of the plasticity of
Zn–4Al–3Mg at high temperatures (100 /C176C and 200 /C176C). A larger
amount of Sn addition (e.g., 13.2 wt.%) shows a much smaller strain
at full failure. Apparently, based on the initial elastic portion in
these stress–strain curves, the Sn addition reduces both the yield
strength and Young’s modulus of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy, particularly
at high temperature (e.g., 200 /C176C). The 6.8 wt.% Sn addition
decreases the ductility of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy to some extent; how-
ever, the strength is still acceptable at high temperature. The
Zn–4Al–3Mg–13.2Sn alloy may not be suitable for high tempera-
ture soldering, as its yield strength and strain at full failure drops
dramatically at 200 /C176C due to the thermal softening of the materi-
als. Such phenomenon is readily linked to the atomic energy of the
materials. Following with the increase of temperature, the ions
adsorb the thermal energy and reach a new equilibrium state with
wider vibration amplitude, which in turn softens the materials
[14].

Fig. 4 shows the uniaxial tensile strength (UTS) of the Zn–4Al–
3Mg–xSn alloys at different temperatures. For Zn–4Al–3Mg alloys,
the tensile strength can reach 216.44 MPa at room temperature.
Following the temperature increment, it can be seen that the
tensile strength decrease monotonously. It is interesting that the
tensile strength is still as high as 44.68 MPa at 200 /C176C, which is
comparable with the value of Pb–5Sn solder at room temperature
[13]. The Sn addition affects the tensile strength of Zn–4Al–3Mg
alloy signiﬁcantly. Basically, the larger Sn addition in Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy results in a greater drop of tensile strength, irrespective
of the temperature. In particular, the Zn–4Al–3Mg–13.2Sn alloy
exhibits an extremely weak strength at 200 /C176C. The Zn–4Al–3
Mg–6.8Sn alloy still maintains a remarkable strength at high
temperature (e.g., 200 /C176C), which is also comparable with the value
of Pb–5Sn alloy.
The evolution of elongation ratio of those alloys shown in Fig. 5
may be an issue to be concerned. At room temperature (i.e., 20 /C176C),
the elongation ratio of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy is only 3.4%, which is still
the best among three Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn alloys. Amazingly, the
elongation ratio of all the alloys is sharply improved at 100 /C176C,
for instance, 15.6% for Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy. However, at higher tem-
perature, say, 200 /C176C, this value drops signiﬁcantly but is still com-
parable with the one at room temperature. At the low temperature
(e.g., 100 /C176C), the elongation ratio of all the solders increase follow-
ing with the temperature is also due to the thermal softening
effect. However, at a higher testing temperature (e.g., 200 /C176C), the
coarsening of IMC particles in Zn–Al–Mg solders, which will be
presented in the next session, tends to be more profound. This is
anticipated to deteriorate the elongation ratio of these alloys sig-
niﬁcantly. The Zn–4Al–3Mg– xSn ( x = 6.8 and 13.2 wt.%) alloys
show the similar trend of elongation ratio against the temperature.
However, a larger Sn addition (13.2 wt.%) in Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy
leads to a signiﬁcant decrease of elongation ratio.
3.3. Microstructure observation
Shown in Fig. 6a is the microstructure of the Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy
at room temperature. It can be seen that the alloy is composed of
coarse Zn-phases and ﬁne structure phase [15]. A cascade interme-
tallic structure is also detected in Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy. The chemical
compositions of the phases are determined by EDX, and the results
are presented in Table 1 . It can be seen that the dark gray area of
the cascade structure is Zn 2Mg phase (positions 1 and 2), while
the light gray area attached with Zn 2Mg is identiﬁed as Zn 11Mg2
(position 3). Fig. 6 b shows a close-up view of the ﬁne structure
phase, in which the black Zn 2Al (position 5) and gray Zn 11Mg2
phases (position 6) were probed. The network structure is
Zn-based matrix (position 4) containing small amount of Al
solubility.

Fig. 7 shows the microstructure of the fracture surfaces of
Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy at different temperatures, namely, 20 /C176C,
100 /C176C, and 200 /C176C. A typical brittle fracture surface of Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy at 20 /C176C is observed, as illustrated in Fig. 7 a. In general,
the intermetallic compound (IMC) exhibits much larger Young’s
modulus and yield strength than the solder matrix. The presence
of the large-size cascade ZnMg 2/Zn11Mg2 structure in Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy may be responsible for such brittle fracture, which is
prone to cause the stress concentration due to the mismatch of
material properties. A sharp V-shape trace shown in Fig. 7 ai s
formed, where the cascade IMC structure is originally located in
the alloy matrix. The damage can easily initiate and propagate
along the interface between IMC and the surrounding phases.
At 100 /C176C, the failure mode of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy, however,
shows a typical ductile mode, as illustrated in Fig. 7b. The dimples
formed in the fracture surface provide a direct evidence of the duc-
tile failure feature. This indicates that the elongation ratio at this
temperature will be also improved, which is consistent with the
tensile experimental results. When the specimen was subjected
to normal tension loading at 200 /C176C, no dimple feature was found
at the fracture surface, see Fig. 7 c. As a result, the ductility of the
alloy is anticipated to be deteriorated, indicating a brittle
-dominant failure mode. At high temperature (e.g., 100 /C176C), the
yield strength and Young’s modulus of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloys are sig-
niﬁcantly reduced, as depicted in Fig. 5. Thus it is reasoned that the
stress generated at the interface between IMC and eutectic phases
is relaxed at 100 /C176C somehow and thus averts the brittle failure.
However, a much higher temperature, say, 200 /C176C, may cause the
coarsening of the IMC particles, as well as the grain growth of
the matrix [16], which in turn degrades the ductility of the alloy.
Fig. 8 illustrates the fracture surfaces of Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn and
Zn–4Al–3Mg–13.2Sn alloys subjected to the normal tension. Simi-
larly as the Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy, both Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn and
Zn–4Al–3Mg–13.2Sn alloys exhibit a brittle-dominant failure
mode at 20 /C176C and 200 /C176C. In particular, shown in Fig. 9a is a sand-
wich solder joint formed between Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn and Al sub-
strates. The solder joint was fabricated as follows. First of all, the
aluminum substrate with 2 mm thickness was deeply cleaned in
10% NaOH solution, followed with the emersion into 4%HNO 3 solu-
tion for the acid–base neutralization. Then the aluminum substrate
was cleaned again by using distilled water and dried by pressure
air. A Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn ﬁlm with the thickness of 0.5 mm was
then assembled between two Al substrates. Before soldering, the
soldering oven was vacuumed and cleaned subsequently by Ar
air. The specimen was then soldered at the temperature of 350 /C176C
under the high-purity Ar atmosphere. No ﬂux was applied for the
soldering. The ramp rate is about 30 /C176C/min, and the dwell time
at peak temperature is about 10 min. No visible defects are de-
tected at the interfaces of both sides. A small amount of particles
are found to disperse in the solder matrix, which is distinct from
the network structure in Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn alloy. Based on the
measured element mapping results shown in Fig. 9b–d, it is found
that these particles is mainly contributed from Sn. This may lead to

4. Conclusions
The Sn addition effect on the tensile property of Zn–4Al–3Mg
alloy at high temperatures was studied. The following conclusions
can be drawn:
(1) The Sn addition can reduce the solidus and undercooling of
Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy to some extent. Larger Sn addition (e.g.,
13.2 wt.%) does not result in further reduction of solidus and
undercooling.
(2) The Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy maintains the strength at 200 /C176C, which
is still comparable with Pb–5Sn high-temperature solder at

3Mg alloy at room temperatures may easily cause the stress
concentration in the solder joint. The Sn addition can result in
a signiﬁcant drop of yield strength of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy. The
strength of Zn–4Al–3Mg–6.8Sn alloy at high temperature (e.g.,

devices. Further addition of Sn (e.g., 13.2 wt.%) will lead to extre-

of elongation ratio.
(3) The typical brittle fracture of Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy is found at
room temperature, which may be attributed to the presence
of large size Zn–Mg IMCs. It is interesting that at 100 /C176C, the
Zn–4Al–3Mg alloy shows a typical ductile fracture. The Sn
addition, however, will deteriorate the ductility of Zn–4Al–
3Mg alloy. Thus the amount of Sn addition should be controlled
carefully.
