The nickel-based alloy Inconel 617 and Alloy 800H have been envisaged as the leading candidate for the 
higher-temperature Molten Salt Reactors (MSRs) due to their superior high-temperature properties. To 
evaluate their potential applications, both alloys were irradiated by He ion at 750 and 850 °C with cor- 
responding He ion ﬂuence. Same irradiation tests were performed on Hastelloy N alloy to study its mi- 
crostructure evolution at elevated irradiation temperature, as well as being the reference alloy to reveal 
candidate alloys irradiation tolerance. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals the depth distribu- 
tion of irradiation induced He bubbles and Frank loops. The TEM graphs and quantitative analysis shows 
that when irradiated at 850 °C, the formation of precipitated clusters lead to denser and boarder distribu- 
tion of He bubbles in Inconel 617, while the bubble-loop complexes form and contribute to larger volume 
fraction of He bubbles in Hastelloy N alloy. The nanoindentation technique is used to measure their me- 
chanical changes, and the results show that the Alloy 800H has the lowest degree of irradiation induced 
hardening when irradiated at 850 °C, whereas that of all alloys irradiated at 750 °C are basically similar. 
Further, the yield strength increment is calculated and deviations between experimental and calculated 
values are caused by the formation of precipitated clusters in Inconel 617 and the selection of smaller 
obstacle strength in Hastelloy N alloy. By comparison, the Alloy 800H demonstrates a greater potential 
to function as the structural material for higher-temperature MSRs due to smaller volume fraction and 
better resistance to irradiation induced hardening.

1. Introduction 
The molten salt reactors (MSRs), one of the advanced reactor 
designs of “Generation IV” nuclear power plants, offer many 
advantages in comparison with other current nuclear reactors, 
namely increased safety, reliability, and high eﬃciency [1] . Raising 
the operating temperature of MSRs is one of the most econom- 
ical methods to improve the eﬃciency in their comprehensive 
high-temperature applications, such as hydrogen production [2] . 
Since the most promising candidate structure material, Hastelloy 
N alloy with dissatisﬁed high-temperature mechanical proper- 
ties [ 1 , 3–5 ], is diﬃcult to fulﬁll the temperature requirements 
of higher-temperature MSRs operated over 700 °C. Therefore, 
potential improvements of higher-temperature MSRs require the 
development of new materials and increased understanding of 
how those materials behave in the reactor core with high neutron

Several alloys have been selected as promising candidates to be 
used as in-core structure materials. Among these alloys, Inconel 
617 and Alloy 800H alloy possess excellent high-temperature 
strength, which have been envisaged as the leading promising 
structural material candidates for the higher-temperature MSRs

true reactor conditions because of the easier control of irradia- 
tion parameters [10] . The previous studies on the microstructure 
evolution of those alloys mainly focus on the behavior of dis- 
placement damage induced by energetic heavy ions [ 2 , 11–15 ]. 
However, it is known that He embrittlement is a primary concern 
for the integrity of nickel-based nuclear structure materials at 
high temperature [16–20] . As a product of (n, α) nuclear transmu- 
tation reaction, He atoms easily precipitate as He bubbles, which 
further degrade the mechanical performance of alloys in nuclear 
reactors [21] . Till now, reports on the He-induced damage at high

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Material preparation 
The samples used in this study were Alloy 800H, 
Inconel 617 and Hastelloy N, with the dimensions of 
10 mm × 6.5 mm × 1 mm, which were cut from cold rolled 
and annealed bulk alloys. Their material compositions are shown 
in Table 1 . Prior to irradiation, the samples were mechanically 
polished down from 400-grit to 20 0 0-grit SiC paper and mechan- 
ically polished to get a mirror-like surface. Then, these samples 
were electro-polished at 36 V using an electrolyte (50% H 2 SO 4 , 
40% glycerol and 10% deionized water) at 0 °C for 10 s to remove 
the surface stress layer. 
2.2. He irradiation

pelletron accelerator at the Shanghai Institute of Applied Physics,

eters of the TMSR-LF1, the maximum total neutron irradiation dose 
of the core components in the service life is ∼15 - 30 dpa ( ∼ 1 - 
2 × 10 23 n/cm 2 ) [24] . Hence, corresponding average He concentra- 
tion in Hastelloy N can be calculated by the following equation:

2.3. TEM characterization 
The cross-sectional TEM samples were prepared using a fo- 
cused ion beam (FIB) to access their full depth distribution of 
damage. Microstructure examination was performed using the us- 
ing TEM (Tecnai G2 F20) with a maximum accelerating voltage of 
200 kV. He bubbles were characterized by through-focused bright 
ﬁeld imaging based on the Fresnel contrast mechanism. For faceted 
He bubbles, the longest distance was considered as the size while 
for spherical He bubbles, the size is represented by their diame- 
ter. Additionally, since the total number density of Frank loops is 
not simply four times that of Frank loops observed in one Rel-rods 
images, Rel-rods technology with two g vectors is used to charac- 
terize the size and number density of Frank loops. The measure- 
ment of He bubbles and Frank loops are used Nanomeasure soft- 
ware. Additionally, convergent beam electron diffraction was used 
to quantity the thickness of FIB thickness. 
2.4. Nanoindentation measurement 
Nanoindentation tests were carried out on the investigated al- 
loys before and after irradiation using a G200 nanoindentation in- 
strument with a diamond Berkovich tip. The continuous stiffness 
measurement (CSM) mode is made to a depth of 20 0 0 nm below 
the surface. To guarantee the statistical signiﬁcance and ensure the 
accuracy of the test results, at least 10 independent indents were 
made for each sample.

4. Discussion 
4.1. The effect of irradiation temperature on microstructure evolution 
In the Alloy 800H, a higher irradiation temperature leads to 
a bigger average size and lower number density of He bubbles, 
as well as dislocation loops. In the case of Inconel 617, the vari- 
ation of Frank loops is more signiﬁcant, while He bubbles aver- 
age size decreases, and number density increases with increasing 
irradiation temperature. Additionally, the He bubbles distribution 
stays at a signiﬁcant border in Inconel 617 sample irradiated at 
850 °C. The reasons are discussed as follows: According to pre- 
vious studies [ 31 , 32 ], when Inconel 617 alloy exposures at high 
temperature, the volume fraction of precipices, such as M 23 C 6 , γ’ 
phase, increases with increasing aging time and temperature from 
700 °C to 900 °C. Due to the small activation energy for M 6 C and 
M 23 C 6 carbides nucleation, the C atom accelerated by high tem- 
perature diffuse in matrix and also in the GBs, encounter Mo and 
Cr atoms to form precipitated through reactions: 6M + C → M 6 C 
and 23M + 6C → M 23 C 6 . Additionally, when there is an increase in 
aging time, the volume fraction of precipitated particles increases 
[32] . It is well known that precipitates can perform as the nu- 
cleation of He bubbles to increase their number density [ 33 , 34 ]. 
In order to verify these viewpoints, EDS mapping with the step 
length of 0.2 nm is performed at the depth of about 1800 nm in 
Inconel 617 sample irradiated at 850 °C, and the result is depicted 
in Fig. 12 . The EDS mapping shows that these clusters are enriched 
in C and Cr, while Co, Fe, Ni and Mo are depleted. Due to the short 
irradiation time (2.83 h for Inconel 617), these precipitated clus- 
ters may be in the nuclear stage of forementioned precipitates. In 
the case of He atom diffusion, bubbles are expected to result from 
multi-atomic nucleation limited by the thermal dissociation of He 
atoms form the He nuclei in the high temperature regime [ 35 , 36 ]. 
These dissociated He atoms will diffuse until trapped by sink or 
other bubbles. Therefore, these observed precipitated clusters, the 
embryo of precipitates, increase the number density of He bubbles. 
Due to higher volume fraction of nucleation sites and higher tem- 
perature, He atoms become easily thermally dissociated form He 
bubble at the peak damage and diffuse to deeper regions, followed 
by trapped to form new He bubbles. Moreover, the depleted atoms 
located at cluster initially, such as Ni and Mo atoms, lead to the 
signiﬁcant coarsening of Frank loops. 
In Hastelloy alloy, the bubble-loop complexes are observed and 
their size increases with the irradiation temperature, wherein the 
bubbles have faceted shape. At present, the formation progress of 
the bubble-loop complex has attracted extensive attention. Based 
on the Burton calculation [ 37 , 38 ], when a gas bubble contacted 
with a dislocation loop in metal, they could interact with each 
other to form bubble-loop complexes. Additionally, Li et al. [25] be- 
lieves that the bubble-complexes are formed through trapping of 
the dislocation loops or self interstitial atom (SIA) by He bubbles. 
During irradiation, the He-V complexes trap the SIAs and act as nu- 
cleation sites of I-Loops, which result in a lower probability of nu- 
cleation sites of SIAs and vacancies. At room temperature, the size 
of existing He-V-SIA complexes is small. With increasing temper- 
ature, the mobility of defects increases, and He-V-SIA complexes 
grow up into larger bubble-loop complexes by absorbing loops and 
He-V clusters [25] . Han et al. [26] found that obvious He bub- 
bles appeared inside regions of dislocation loops, when these loops 
grow to a certain size by in-situ TEM observations, which suggests 
that He bubbles are more likely to nucleate in the large-size dis- 
location loops. Bhattacharya et al. [ 39 , 40 ] found that during the 
irradiation, helium atoms are trapped near dislocation loop cores, 
the mobility of these trapped He atoms by “pipi-diffusion” can re- 
sult in the formation of He-V clusters at the loops core. The dislo- 
cation loops grow by preferential absorption of interstitials, while 
these He-V clusters aggerate into helium bubbles and then grow 
gradually by absorption of He atoms and vacancies. Recently, Bai

dislocation loops that attach to He-vacancy complexes, resulting 
in the formation of bubble-loop complexes. Compared with for-

the formation of faceted helium bubbles. As the He-V-SIA com- 
plexes or dislocation loops decorated by He bubbles grow, the dis- 
location loops tend to shrink by absorbing vacancies from the bub- 
bles [42] . To reach equilibrium conditions, the shrinkage stress for 
the dislocation loop needs to be balanced by the overpressure of 
the bubbles, thus leading to the formation of faceted He bubbles 
[41] . Based on above studies, the size of bubble-loop complexes 
increases with temperature and He ion ﬂuence. In the Hastelloy N 
sample, no complexes are not observed irradiated at 700 °C with 
ion ﬂuence from 5 to 13 × 10 16 ions/cm 2 [22] , while heteroge- 
neous nucleation of He bubble are observed at 750 °C with ion 
ﬂuence of 4.4 × 10 16 ions/cm 2 , which suggests that irradiation 
temperature ( ≥750 °C) plays the critical role in complex formation. 
Based on the Bhattacharya et al. reports [ 39 , 43 , 44 ], apart from high 
irradiation, large He concentration and purity of the materials (in 
terms of elements like C, N, O and Si) all play roles in formation 
of bubble-loop complexes, which may be the reasons why these 
complexes are not observed in Alloy 800H and Inconel 617 alloy. 
4.2. The relationship between microstructure changes and 
nanohardness 
It has been accepted that the ion irradiation induced defects 
can pin dislocation lines and inhabit their free motion, resulting 
in the increasing of yield stress and thus leading to the harden-

5. Conclusion 
In summary, the Alloy 800H, Inconel 617 and Hastelloy N al- 
loy have been irradiated by He ion at 750 and 850 °C with corre- 
sponding ion ﬂuence. Their microstructure evolution, including He 
bubble and Frank loops, is observed by TEM while the irradiation 
induced hardening is characterized by nanoindentation technique. 
The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) With irradiation temperature increasing, He bubble size in- 
creases, and number density decreases in Alloy 800H and 
Hastelloy N, whereas opposite evolution phenomenon occurs 
in Inconel 617 alloy. Additionally, coarsening evolution of Frank 
loops is observed in all investigated alloys. 
(2) A higher irradiation temperature leads to an increased vol- 
ume fraction of helium bubbles in Hastelloy N alloy caused by 
the formation of bubble-loop complexes, while decreased vol- 
ume fraction in other alloys. Among the investigated alloys, Al- 
loy 800H has the smallest volume fraction of He bubbles, fol- 
lowed by Inconel 617 and Hastelloy N alloy, which demon- 
strates that Alloy 800H possesses the best resistance to swelling 
over 750 °C. 
(3) Large volume fraction of precipitated clusters, which may be 
the embryo of precipitates, formed in Inconel 617 samples irra- 
diated at 850 °C. The combination effect of precipitated clusters 
and thermal dissociation of He atoms lead to the denser and

(4) Alloy 800H possesses the best resistance to irradiation harden-

sistances are almost similar. 
(5) The deviations in hardness between experimental values and 
calculated values in Inconel 617 and Hastelloy N alloy are 
caused by the formation of precipitated clusters and the selec- 
tion of smaller obstacle strength for over-pressured He bubbles 
and dislocation loops.
