Temperature-independent elastic modulus is termed as Elinvar effect, which is available by tuning the 
continuous spin transition of ferromagnetic alloys via composition optimization and the ﬁrst-order 
martensitic transformation of shape memory alloys via plastic deformation. However, these reversible 
mechanisms are restricted generally in a narrow temperature range of less than 300 K. Here reports, by 
tuning a spinodal decomposition in a Ti-Nb-based titanium alloy via aging treatment, both the Elinvar 
effect in a wide temperature range of about 500 K and a high strength-to-modulus ratio of about 1.5% 
can be obtained by a continuous and reversible crystal ordering mechanism. The results demonstrate that 
the alloy aged at 723 K for 4 h has a nanoscale plate-like modulated β+ α" two-phase microstructure and 
its elastic modulus keeps almost constant from 100 to 600 K. Synchrotron and in-situ X-ray diffraction 
measurements reveal that the crystal ordering parameter of the α" phase increases linearly with temper- 
ature from 0.88 at 133 K to 0.97 at 523 K but its volume fraction keeps a constant of about 33.8%. This 
suggests that the continuous ordering of the α" phase toward the high modulus α phase induces a posi- 
tive modulus-temperature relation to balance the negative relation of the elastically stable β phase. The 
aged alloy exhibits a high yield strength of 1200 MPa, good ductility of 16% and a high elastic admissible 
strain of 1.5%. Our results provide a novel strategy to extend the Elinvar temperature range and enhance 
the strength by tuning the crystal ordering of decomposition alloys.

1. Introduction 
Most solid materials expand in volume and soften in modulus 
upon heating due to anharmonic phonon vibrations [1–3] . How- 
ever, a few of them can maintain constant elastic modulus, be- 
ing temperature-independent. This novelty is termed as Elinvar ef- 
fect, which was ﬁrst discovered by Guillaume in FeNi alloys [4] . 
The Elinvar effect is essential in many ﬁelds such as precision in- 
struments, acoustic applications and aerospace industry [4–6] . So 
far, Elinvar effect has been observed mainly in ferromagnetic (FM),

antiferromagnetic (AFM), shape memory (SM) alloys and high- 
entropy (HE) alloys. They are brieﬂy summarized below. 
The ﬁrst group is ferromagnetic (FM) alloys, where the relation 
between Young’s modulus ( E ) and temperature ( T ) varies contin- 
uously with their compositions [4] . For example, the d E /d T value 
increases from normally negative, via zero, to abnormally positive 
as the Ni content decreases [4] . Hence, the alloys exhibit Elinvar 
effect (d E /d T ≈ 0) in a narrow composition range near Fe-45Ni in 
atomic percent [4] . Several models such as the continuous spin- 
state transition have been proposed to explain the underlying ori- 
gin [ 7 , 8 ]. Although there is no direct experimental evidence for 
these modeled details [9] , it is generally assumed that the volume 
fractions of two magnetic states with different modulus evolve 
continuously with T . This produces the Elinvar effect in a T win- 
dow of less than ∼300 K [5] . The E - T relations of FM alloys are also

sensitive to the applied magnetic ﬁelds, as evidenced by the fact 
that the Elinvar effect gradually deteriorates and eventually disap- 
pears with increasing magnetic ﬁelds [5] . Additionally, these alloys 
can be strengthened by thermal treatments, but their E - T relations 
change accordingly due to the redistribution of alloying elements 
[ 10 , 11 ]. This results in a complicated balance between the Elinvar 
effect and the mechanical properties. Hence, non-magnetic Elinvar 
alloys with balanced mechanical are highly desired. 
The second group is antiferromagnetic (AFM) alloys such as Mn 
and Cr alloys [12–16] . Similar with the FM alloys, their E - T rela- 
tions can be tuned by compositional optimization to get the Elin- 
var effect. The anomaly in AFM alloys is generally explained by 
magnetic transition between AFM and PM (paramagnetic) states 
or continuous magnetic ordering [12–18] . Both mechanisms are in- 
sensitive to magnetic ﬁelds, but generally operate in a T range of 
less than 100 K [12–16] , which is much narrower than that of FM 
Elinvar alloys. 
The third group is shape memory (SM) alloys whose E - T rela- 
tions can be tuned by reversible martensitic transformation (MT). 
Since the MT is of ﬁrst-order in nature [19] , the associated elastic 
softening/hardening is much more pronounced than that induced 
by the magnetic transitions in FM and AFM alloys. To achieve the 
Elinvar effect, the avalanche-like crystal structure change should 
be suppressed. This can be realized by severe plastic deformation 
in conventional SM alloys [20–23] . Previous studies have shown 
that the deformation-induced nanoscale martensite, including its 
domain and embryo, can be thermally sluggish, which results in 
the Elinvar-like effect in a T range of up to about 250 K [20–26] . In 
addition to plastic deformation, doping defects, similar to chem- 
ical optimization in FM and AFM alloys, can induce the Elinvar- 
like effect in some SM alloys in a T range of about 200 K [27–36] . 
This can be explained by a reversible mechanism called strain glass 
transition [37] , which is triggered prior to the MT. Such a mecha- 
nism has also been applied to explain the oxygen-doped titanium 
alloys termed as Gum Metals [35] , which show Elinvar effect after 
severe deformation [ 35 , 38 ]. It should be noted that, although the 
initial sharp E - T relation becomes much smoother after severe de- 
formation or doping, the T coeﬃcient ( e ) of elastic modulus used 
to characterize the Elinvar effect has rarely been reported for these 
SM alloys. Additionally, plastic deformation would not be a good 
strategy to obtain a reliable Elinvar effect for β-Ti alloys. For ex- 
ample, the swaged Gum Metals with identical stress-strain curves 
exhibit distinct E - T relations: it can be either T -independent with 
a completely unchanged average tensile modulus of ∼55 GPa be- 
tween 70 and 500 K [38] or T -dependent with a storage modulus 
of ∼70 GPa at 300 K and ∼60 GPa at 200 K [39] . Such a discrep- 
ancy is in contrast to the stable Elinvar effect observed in FM and 
AFM alloys. 
The last one is a recently developed Co-Ni-Hf-Ti-Zr high- 
entropy alloy with a highly distorted crystal lattice and a complex 
atomic scale chemical ordering [40] . This alloy exhibits an extraor- 
dinary Elinvar-like effect from room temperature up to 900 K and 
an elastic strain of 2%. The ab initio molecular dynamics simula- 
tion reveals that the crystal distortion becomes weaker at higher 
temperatures [40] . This atomic packing ordering from the initial 
highly-distorted crystal to the perfect crystal can compensate for 
the thermally-induced elastic softening. However, direct evidence 
from quantitative measurements for this theoretical model is lack- 
ing [40] . Moreover, this high-entropy alloy appears to be brittle 
since its compressive strain at room temperature is less than 10% 
[40] . 
The above summary indicates that the Elinvar effect is avail- 
able via reversible mechanisms. However, these reversible transi- 
tions in FM, AFM and SM alloys are limited typically to a nar- 
row T range of less than about 300 K [ 5 , 12–18 , 20–39 ], which hin- 
ders their applications in a wider T range. This limitation could be 
solved by a multifunctional titanium alloy Ti2448, abbreviated by 
its alloy composition Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn in weight percent [ 41 , 42 ], 
since a continuous and reversible atomic rearrangement mecha- 
nism can be triggered to obtain both superelasticity and tunable 
thermal expansion in a wide T range over 500 K [43–45] . The nov- 
elty is related to a nanoscale spongy-like Nb modulation produced 
by spinodal decomposition [43–47] . As a result, the β- α" MT in the 
Nb-lean domains is conﬁned elastically by the surrounding Nb-rich 
domains [46] . Hence, the ﬁrst-order MT changes into a continu- 
ous crystal ordering transition operated by a coupled mechanism 
of atomic shear and shuﬄe [47] . The crystal ordering can be mea- 
sured experimentally, as evidenced by in-situ synchrotron X-ray 
diffraction (SXRD) results that both shear and shuﬄe components 
increase linearly from the bcc crystal of the β phase to the hcp 
crystal of the α phase along the atomic pathway by keeping the or- 
thorhombic (orth) symmetry of the α" phase [48] . In addition, this 
crystal transition is coupled with a microstructural transition from 
the spongy-like to the plate-like [49] . Both transitions can be char- 
acterized by a crystal ordering parameter deﬁned in Ref. [49] . The 
continuous crystal ordering of the α" phase (orth) toward the α
phase (hcp) would result in a modulus hardening (d E /d T > 0), since 
it is well-known that the α phase has a higher modulus than both 
β and α" phases [50–52] . That is, the positive E-T relation of the 
Nb-lean α" phase would balance the negative E-T relation of the 
elastically stable Nb-rich β phase. This inspires us to explore the 
Elinvar effect in a wide T range by tuning the crystal ordering pa- 
rameter via isothermal aging treatment. 
In this study, we demonstrate that the Ti2448 alloy aged at 
723 K for 4 h exhibits Elinvar effect and Elinvar-like effect in a 
wide T range of 500 and 600 K, respectively. In-situ X-ray diffrac- 
tion analyses will demonstrate that the crystal ordering parameter 
of the α" phase varies linearly with T but its volume fraction re- 
mains almost constant. Some thermal and anelastic measurements 
indicate that the Elinvar effect is reversible and free of hystere- 
sis. The Elinvar effect is explained by the ordering-induced elastic 
hardening of the α" phase to compensate for the elastic softening 
of the β matrix upon heating. The nanoscale plate-like microstruc- 
ture induced by the spinodal decomposition also leads to a high 
yield strength of about 1200 MPa and a good tensile elongation of 
about 16% at room temperature. Furthermore, we will reveal that 
both Elinvar and Invar effects cannot be obtained simultaneously in 
Ti2448 alloy, since the Elinvar effect is caused by the ordering to- 
wards the α phase but the Invar effect by the disordering towards 
the β phase.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Mechanism of Elinvar effect in Ti2448 
The Elinvar effect in most alloys can be explained by the T - 
dependent change in the volume fraction of the high-modulus 
phase or state due to these magnetic transitions in ferromag- 
netic (FM) and antiferromagnetic (AFM) alloys [ 8 , 17 , 18 ], and by the 
MT and strain glass transition in shape memory (SM) alloys [20–
36] . However, Ti2448 alloy contains only non-magnetic elements. 
Besides, its thermal and dynamic properties ( Figs. 5 and 9 ) ex- 
clude the ﬁrst-order transitions such as the MT. The frequency- 
independent modulus and internal friction curves above ∼100 K 
in Fig. 6 (b) are also unexpected from the strain glass transition 
mechanism, which is generally characterized by the frequency- 
dependent modulus dip and internal friction peak [37] . Finally, our 
in-situ heating XRD measurements reveal that the volume frac- 
tion of the α" phase with higher modulus maintains almost in- 
variant ( Fig. 10 (a)), which further indicates that the Elinvar effect 
in Ti2448 alloy is in sharp contrast to these Elinvar mechanisms 
by T -dependent volume fraction changes. Hence, these previously- 
proposed mechanisms are invalid for the Elinvar-state Ti2448 alloy. 
It is well-known that the α phase has a higher modulus than 
both the β and α" phases in titanium alloys [50–52] . Additionally, 
the aged Ti2448 alloy with α" + β dual-phase microstructure has a 
dynamic Young’s modulus of ∼79 GPa, which is much higher than

at this stage using only one sample with the Elinvar effect. This 
would be estimated in the future by adjusting the aging time to 
get the samples with different ordering parameters at room T . Sec- 
ondly, the E-T relation of the elastic β phase is simpliﬁed as a 
linear relation. Actually, it is slightly curved in a wide T range 
of 600 K. Lastly, the upper limit T of Elinvar-like effect would be 
about 750 K, which is the starting point where E decreases signiﬁ- 
cantly ( Fig. 7 ). This can be explained in Fig. 14 (b) that the ordering 
parameter η of the α" phase reaches unity at higher temperatures, 
leading to a transition from the novel positive E-T relation of the 
α" phase to the normal negative E-T relation of the α phase at the 
T point of about 750 K. This will deteriorate the Elinvar effect. 
4.2. Ordering and disordering of the α" phase 
The data in Fig. 11 reveal that the α" phase exhibits a contrary 
thermal response being dependent on the initial value of the or- 
dering parameter η. As to the Invar state alloy with an initial η of 
about 0.30 at room T , the T increase results in lattice contraction 
along some crystal orientations ( Fig. 11 (a)), which is the origin of 
the negative thermal expansion (NTE) of the α" phase to produce 
the Invar effect of the α" + β dual-phase alloy [ 43 , 66–68 ]. However, 
as to the Elinvar-state alloy with an initial η of about 0.89 at room 
T , the T increase results in an opposite effect of lattice expansion 
( Fig. 11 (a)), which is the origin of positive thermal expansion of 
the α" + β dual-phase alloy ( Fig. 12 (a)). 
The above converse thermal response of the α" phase can be 
quantitatively characterized by its ordering parameter change in 
Fig. 11 (b). As to the zero expansion Invar state alloy with an initial 
η of about 0.30, the T increase results in a continuous crystal dis- 
ordering of the α" phase governed by a negative linear η-T relation 
[ 43 , 44 ], a continuous decrease of η toward the direction of the β
phase with bcc crystal structure (noted by the circles in Fig. 11 (b)). 
This is similar to the ﬁrst-order β- α" MT in shape memory tita- 
nium alloys except for the linear η-T relation of the studied alloy. 
However, the α" phase with a high initial η value of about 0.89 
exhibits an opposite thermal response: the T increase results in a 
continuous crystal ordering of the α" phase governed by a posi- 
tive linear η-T relation ( Fig. 10 (d)), showing a continuous increase 
of η toward the direction of the α phase with hcp crystal structure 
(noted by the squares in Fig. 11 (b)). This is totally different with 
the deﬁnition of thermoelastic MT, including both reversible and 
irreversible MTs. The linear η-T relations found from both β- α" and 
α"- α phase transitions are in sharp contrast with the well-known 
exponential relation of these conventional ﬁrst-order phase trans- 
formations. This provides a new opportunity to pioneer the con- 
tinuous and reversible phase transitions for revealing the origin of 
linear temperature relation and exploring the functional properties 
over a giant temperature range. 
Additionally, the contrary thermal response of Invar and Elinvar 
effects in Fig. 11 (b) demonstrates that both effects cannot be ob- 
tained simultaneously in the studied Ti2448 alloy, as evidenced by 
the normal thermal expansion behavior of the Elinvar-state alloy 
and the positive E-T relation of the Invar state alloy [43] . This is 
different with the cold-swaged Gum Metals, which were claimed 
to couple with both Invar and Elinvar effects [38] .

5. Conclusion 
Aided by spinodal decomposition and the associated mi- 
crostructural transition from the nanoscale spongy-like to the 
plate-like in the Ti2448 alloy via aging treatment, the Elinvar ef- 
fect is obtained over a wide temperature range of ∼500 K, which 
is much wider than that of the previously-reported Elinvar alloys. 
A continuous crystal ordering mechanism has been proposed to 
explain the elastic hardening of the α" phase in the Nb-lean do- 
mains and the compensation on elastic softening of the β phase 
in the Nb-rich domains. The proposed mechanism is consistent 
with the experimental ﬁndings that the volume fraction of the α" 
phase is independent of temperature but its ordering parameter 
increases linearly with temperature along the atomic pathway to 
form the high modulus α phase. This continuous ordering mech- 
anism is reversible thermally but its atomic pathway is contrary 
with the well-known β- α" MT mechanism, in which heating re- 
sults in a converse transformation towards the direction to form 
β phase. Additionally, the nanoscale microstructure transition via 
the spinodal decomposition mechanism has a great contribution to 
strength but weak damage to ductility. This is in sharp contrast 
to the well-known decomposition-induced brittle in these conven- 
tional spinodal decomposition alloys with either spongy-like or 
plate-like microstructure. Therefore, the novel spinodal decompo-
