1. Introduction 
Increasing demands in high temperature structural applications 
bring new challenges for metallic alloys, which should have superior 
mechanical property, tribological property and high oxidation resis -
tance at elevated temperature [ 1 , 2 ]. Though, the Ni-based superalloys in 
use currently have excellent properties at high temperatures until to 
1050 
◦
C, they are subjected to catastrophic failure above the operating 
temperature of 1300 
◦
C because of their limited melting temperature 
[ 3 ]. As mechanical and tribological properties generally degrade at 
temperatures above about 60% melting temperature [ 4 ], the exploration 
of alloys consist of refractory elements with high-melting points may be 
expected to increase the maximum working temperature of metallic 
materials. 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) represent a new class of emerging 
advanced materials, which have attracted wide interest because of their 
unique structure and combination of properties [ 5 – 7 ]. HEAs are defined 
as multi-principal element alloys composed of five or more than five 
main elements with contents between 5 at.% and 35 at.% [ 8 , 9 ]. Inspired 
by the novel design concept of HEAs and the desire to exploit new 
elevated temperature structural materials, the concept of refractory high 
entropy alloys (RHEAs) consisting of refractory elements Hf, Nb, Mo, Ta, 
W and V has been developed as a candidate elevated temperature 
structural material [ 10 – 12 ]. Researches shows that some RHEAs have 
more excellent high temperature properties compared with the Ni-based 
superalloys [ 6 , 9 , 13 , 14 ]. Especially, RHEAs like NbMoWTa and 
NbMoWTaV exhibit impressive strength up to 1600 
◦
C [ 9 ]. Besides, 
wear resistance of MoTaWNbV RHEA is three times of a commercial 
Inconel 718 Ni-based superalloy under the same experimental condition 
[ 15 ]. Thus, the superior properties of RHEAs at high temperature make 
them have broad potential applications in gas turbines, nuclear reactors, 
and rocket engine nozzles [ 16 , 17 ]. Unfortunately, for most RHEAs, high 
density and poor oxidation resistance due to the addition of heavy metal 
elements (e.g., Nb, Mo and Hf) could result in poor wear resistance at 
elevated temperatures, which seriously limit their practical applications 
[ 18 ]. 
Recently, developments in HEAs begin to use minor addition of Al as 
an alloying element [ 19 – 21 ]. Researches show that Al addition in HEAs 
could not only reduce the density, but also enhance the wear and 
oxidation resistance at elevated temperature [ 22 ]. For example, with Al

Although numerous efforts have been developed to explore the effect of 
Al addition on the mechanical and oxidation properties of RHEAs, 
limited studies have been done on the tribological behaviors at different 
temperatures. As a candidate high temperature structural material, 
evidently, the evaluation about the influence of Al addition on the 
properties of RHEAs must involve the tribological behaviors over a wide 
temperature range. 
In summary, RHEAs are effective substitutes for traditional nickel-

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructural characterization and mechanical properties 
The XRD patterns of the annealed Al0 – Al0.75 samples are shown in 
Fig. 1 . The XRD curve of Al0 alloy reveals the BCC peaks of a single solid 
solution. The peaks of precipitate phase that represent the Laves phase 
verified by the following TEM are found in the XRD curve of Al0.25 
alloy. With the increase of Al addition, both intensity and quantity 
corresponding to the Laves phase peaks increase in Al0.5 and Al0.75 
alloys ( Fig. 1 ), indicating that the volume fraction of Laves phase in -
creases with the increase of Al addition. 
The SEM images ( Fig. 2 ) and corresponding EDX chemical 
compositions ( Table 1 ) for the annealed Al0 – Al0.75 samples are in line 
with the XRD results. Specifically, the microstructure of Al0 alloy con -
tains a single-phase with an average grain size of millimeter level and 
the constituent elements distribute uniform, as shown in Fig. 2 a and 
Table 1 . It is apparent that with Al addition, the chain-like Laves phase 
consisting of Zr, V and Al begins to precipitate at the boundary of the 
equiaxed grain and inside grains in the Al0.25 alloy, and the volume 
fraction of precipitate phase further increases as the Al addition in -
creases for Al0.5 and Al0.75 alloys ( Fig. 2 b – d). Using software to 
calculate the volume fraction of the second phase of the three aluminum- 
containing alloys, it was found that their second phase volume fractions 
were 17.9% ± 0.33% (Al0.25 alloy), 36.4% ± 1.1% (Al0.5 alloy) and 
40.2% ± 1.2% (Al0. 75 alloy). The crystallographic property and 
chemical elemental partitioning in sub-micron and nanometer scale of 
these alloys were further studied based on the TEM techniques. Exam -
ples of the obtained bright-field TEM images, select area electron 
diffraction (SAED) analysis and EDX element mappings from Al0.5 alloy 
are presented in Fig. 3 a. The results are very similar to the SEM results, 
in which a relatively bright area with a Laves structure enriched in V, Zr 
and Al is found to be embeded in the BCC matrix phase enriched with Ti 
and Nb.

4. Conclusions 
In this study, low density TiZrV
0.5
Nb
0.5
Al
x 
series RHEAs were pre -
pared by different Al element addition. The variation of microstructure 
and mechanical property with Al addition were investigated. Especially, 
the tribological behavior at different wear testing temperature and the 
corresponding wear mechanism were investigated. The TiZrV
0.5
Nb
0.5 
RHEA has a single BCC-phase solid solution structure. With Al addition, 
the microstructure begins to precipitate Laves phase, and the volume 
fraction of precipitated phase increases as the increase of Al content. A 
unique microstructure that the acicular Laves phase distributed uni -
formly in the BCC phase matrix is obtained for the Al0.25 alloy. The 
hardness of the alloys increases form 329.3 HV for the Al0 alloy to 473.5 
HV for the Al0.5 alloy, then, the hardness decreases slightly to 462.6 HV 
for the Al0.75 alloy. 
The dry sliding wear tests at different wear testing temperatures 
show that under different temperature conditions, the effect of Al 
addition on the wear resistance of the alloy is distinguishing. Under a 
testing temperature below 400 
◦
C, the wear resistance of Al0 – Al0.75 
alloys are dominated by mechanical wear rather than oxidation wear. In 
this condition, the Al0 alloy with the lowest hardness or the Al0.5- 
Al0.75 alloys with poor crack resistance presents an undesirable wear 
resistance. When the testing temperature is larger than 600 
◦
C, the 
dominant wear mechanisms change into oxidation wear, and the worn 
surface are covered by the oxide tribological layers. Because of Al 
addition, a stable dense oxidation layer that could protect the alloy 
matrix by completely isolating the contact between the alumina ball and 
the alloy matrix is generated, resulting in very low wear rates. 
The present results suggest that Al addition has a complex effect on 
wear resistance of the TiZrV
0.5
Nb
0.5
Al
x

Al addition in the alloys can both reduce the alloy density and increase 
wear resistance in a wide temperature range.
