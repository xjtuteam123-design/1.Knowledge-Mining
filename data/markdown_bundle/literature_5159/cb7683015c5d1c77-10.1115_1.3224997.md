Development of a Procedure for 
Estimating the High Cycle Fatigue 
Strength of Some High 
Temperature Structural Alloys1 
The generation of strain controlled fatigue data, for the standard strain rate of 4 x 
10~3 s~', presents a problem when the cycles to failure exceed 105 because of the 
prohibitively long test times involved. In an attempt to circumvent this difficulty an 
evaluation has been made of a test procedure involving a fast cycling rate (40 Hz) 
and load contolled conditions. The validity of this procedure for extending current 
fatigue curves from 105 to 10s cycles and beyond, hinges upon the selection of an 
appropriate strain value, since the strain range can change rapidly during the early 
stages of fatigue. Results from annealed 2 1/4 Cr-1 Mo, Type 304 stainless steel, 
Incoloy 800 H and Hastelloy X, tested over a wide range of temperatures, show that 
the strain measured at Nf/2 is a reasonable estimate since it gives an excellent 
correlation between the strain and load controlled tests in the 105 range where the 
data overlap. It seems clear that the differences in cycling rate and early stress-strain 
history for the two tests do not significantly affect the correlation. It may, 
therefore, be concluded that such load control test procedures may be used as a 
valid fast way for extending currently available fatigue curves from 105 to 108 
cycles, and beyond. 
1. Introduction 
Most fatigue work currently being carried out in the power 
industry is focused on the low cycle fatigue (LCF) regime 
where the cycles to failure (Nf) are typically less than 105. 
Such information is used to estimate the degree of fatigue 
damage accumulation in components which are subjected to 
large cyclic strain changes brought about by thermal tran­
sients. 
Most experimental work is carried out at a recommended 
strain rate of 4 x 10~3 s_1 in order to standardize the test 
procedures. Although this strain rate represents an ac­
celerated straining condition compared to that which is 
normally experienced in most components [1] it is, never­
theless, insufficiently fast to obtain data, in the range 
Nf> >105 for practical (short) testing times. In order to 
circumvent this problem accelerated testing procedures are 
currently being developed to obtain meaningful high cycle 
fatigue (HCF) data which could be used to extend currently 
available fatigue curves from about 105 to the 108-109 cycle 
range. Jaske suggests that 3 accelerated test procedures may 
be use to obtain valid HCF data [1]: 
(a) Perform the entire test under strain control while 
measuring the load.

(b) Perform the initial portion of the test, where large 
transient stress-strain responses often occur, in strain control. 
Once a stable response is achieved, switch to load control for 
the remainder of the test. 
(c) Perform the entire test under load control while 
monitoring the strain response to assure that transient stress-
strain effects are minimal. 
A good motivation for adopting the 2 latter procedures is that 
load control is inherently more simple [1] and that faster rates 
of cycling may be used to reach high Nf values. 
Over a period of years the current authors have ac­
cumulated a large quantity of HCF data (104-108 cycle range) 
for a variety of power reactor materials [2-4]. An attempt is 
made here to evaluate their usefulness in extending current 
LCF curves up to the 108-109 cycle range. The data were 
produced primarily to evalute the effects of fully reversible 
stresses on the fatigue life of vibrating heat exchanger 
components, large rotating apparatus and piping tee joint 
locations where the mixing of fluids at different temperatures 
may lead to cyclic stresses. In these situations the loading 
sequence involves a ramping of the applied stress (strain) 
range from zero to some predetermined steady state value. 
The cycling rate employed was fast (40 Hz) in order to 
simulate anticipated frequencies. Although the test precedure 
does not exactly conform to any of Jaske's recommendations 
[1], because of the initial stress ramping sequence, the current 
work is intended to evaluate the effect of such ramping and to

2. Experimental Procedures 
All of the HCF results were obtained for an air en­
vironment on Materials Test Systems (MTS) closed loop 
electrohydraulic fatigue units operating in the push-pull mode 
with a sinusoidal load controlled waveform at a frequency of 
40 Hz. Specimens were heated with a resistance type furnace 
controlledto±2°C(4°F). 
Above are listed the materials examined together with their 
chemical compositions. In some cases two heats of the same 
material were used. No obvious systematic differences in 
fatigue life could be attributed to heat-to-heat variations and 
the heats were used interchangeably. 
The 2 l/4Cr-lMo steel was austenitized at 916°C (1700°F) 
for 1 hour, then held at 704°C (BOOT) for 2 hours and 
slowly cooled; the Type 304 stainless steel, Incoloy 800H and 
Hastelloy X were solution treated at 1066°C (1950°F), 
1149°C (2100°F) and 1177°C (2150°F), respectively, and all 
were then water quenched. After heat treatment the specimens 
were surface ground to an hour-glass configuration. The 
Incoloy 800H heat HH 7427A and the Hastelloy X specimens 
had a 0.64 cm (0.25 in.) minimum diameter whereas all others 
had a 0.32 cm (0.125 in.) diameter. 
At the initiation of each test the load range was ramped 
from zero to the predetermined value in approximately 10 
seconds (400 cycles). Testing continued at this load level until 
either failure occurred or it was decided that failure was 
highly unlikely and the test was discontinued.

or absent [5,6]. Hastelloy X and 2 l/4Cr-lMo are not ther­

fatigue limits are not achieved even after 108 accumulated 
cycles.

To compare LCF and HCF strain controlled fatigue curves 
a means must be devised to convert the HCF stresses to 
strains. A simple conversion using Young's moduli has been 
used in the past and is based on the assumption that HCF 
stresses are purely elastic. The data given in Fig. 10 for In­
coloy 800 tested at room temperature show that this results in 
a very poor correlation with the LCF curve and indicates that 
in the 104-105 cycle range there is a considerable inelastic 
strain which is not accounted for in a simple Young's modulus 
conversion. The same result was obtained for other test 
temperatures and materials [3]. To overcome this problem 
"cyclic stress-strain curves" were constructed from the data 
in references [8 through 13]. These curves, an example of 
which is given in Fig. 11, relate the steady state stress and 
strain levels at 0.5 Nf and account for both elastic and plastic 
strain components. Using these curves to convert the HCF 
stresses to strains it may be shown that a good correlation is 
obtained (Figs. 12 through 15). The use of cyclic stress-strain 
curve data thus appears to be an acceptable procedure for 
correlating HCF and LCF data. 
4. Discussion 
The analysis above clearly indicates the usefulness of load 
controlled HCF tests in extending LCF curves to the 108-109 
range. An extremely important factor which has to be ad­
dressed, however, is the effect of strain rate since HCF cycling 
rates are typically 1-2 orders of magnitude higher than LCF 
rates in the 104-105 cycle regime where the low and high cycle 
curves overlap. Although the good correlations obtained 
between low cycle and high cycle data are only an indication 
that cycling rates are not of primary importance in this range, 
data to support this are available in the open literature [9].

it would take only 0.7 hour. In the case of Type 304 stainless 
steel age hardening is small at elevated temperatures [5] in­
dicating that major thermally induced changes in the 
metallurgical structure do not occur during test. Hence, the 
correlation of LCF and HCF curves for such materials will 
probably not be influenced by this effect. However, in the 
case of materials such as Inconel 718, which undergo rapid 
strengthening at elevated temperatures [15], considerable in-
test strenghtening would result during long term LCF testing. 
One would, therefore, expect that for a given endurance limit 
(say at 105 cycles) the fatigue strength measured by LCF 
techniques would be much greater than that obtained by HCF 
testing. This has been confirmed by Korth and Smolik [16]. 
For age softening materials the reverse behavior would be 
anticipated and fatigue strength measured by LCF procedures 
would give lower values. A possible procedure for in­
vestigating such aging effects would be to carry out some 
additional work on material which has been thermally aged 
prior to testing. Any potential losses in fatigue strength 
brought about by long term changes in metallurgical structure 
or corrosion could thus be identified and appropriate 
allowances made when applying the data to component 
design. 
Another important problem which needs to be considered is 
how differences in the initial stress-strain response could 
influence subsequent steady state deformation and the fatigue 
life. Figure 5 shows that the attainment of stable hysteresis 
loops is different in strain controlled and load controlled 
tests. For load control there could be large strains associated 
with early deformation when the material is relatively soft, 
whereas for strain controlled procedures the stress amplitude 
continuously increases until stable conditions are reached. In 
the HCF tests used in the current study the ramping of the 
load permits significant work hardening to occur before the

Results from the literature are not definitive in assessing the 
effects of transient deformation on fatigue life. However, 
there is strong evidence to show that for some face-centered 
cubic metals and alloys early deformation history is erased by 
subsequent long term steady state cycling. Coffin, for 
example, has demonstrated that for aluminum alloy 1100 and 
Type 347 stainless steel the cyclic stress-strain behavior is 
initially quite different for the annealed and cold worked 
states [17]. However, deformation for each state rapidly 
towards a unique hysteresis loop as cycling continues at a 
given strain amplitude. A similar results was obtained recently 
for Type 304 stainless steel [18]. It, therefore, appears that 
fatigue life is principally controlled by the magnitude of the 
steady state stress (strain); transient deformation behavior, 
for low stress (strain) conditions, only exists for a very small 
fraction of the overall fatigue life [10] and is quickly at­
tenuated. There is no firm evidence that it can exert any 
noticeable influence on fatigue behavior. On this basis it may 
be concluded that the current HCF test procedure may be used 
as an optional alternative to those recommended by Jaske [1].
