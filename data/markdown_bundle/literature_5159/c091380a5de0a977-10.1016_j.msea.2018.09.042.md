Abstract 
Compared with arc melting, ultrahigh strength and good elongation of Ti-25Zr-10Al alloy via laser 
melting and s ubsequent low temperature annealing are investigated. The results show a strong 
dependency of the microstructure on the methods of melting and subsequent low temperature annealing 
process. Compared with orthorhombic α'' martensitic phase after arc melting, at the condition of 
relatively high cooling rate by laser melting, hexagonal close-packed α' martensitic phase with {101̅1} 
twinning is observed, and recrystallization with the average grain size of 0.3μm occurs after subsequent 
low temperature annealing . In addition, ultrahigh strength of 1352MPa and good elongation of 10.9% 
are obtained. 
 
Keywords: titanium alloy; selective laser melting; mechanical properties; microstructure 
 
1. Introduction 
Titanium (Ti) based alloys exhibit an excellent combination of mechanical and physical properties 
for key applications in aerospace and medical apparatus [1-3]. Recently, a series of Ti-Zr based alloys, 
such as Ti-Zr [4-6], Ti -Zr-Mo-Cr [7], Ti-Zr-Mo-Sn [8], Ti-Zr-Ta [ 9], Ti-Zr-Nb [10, 11], and TiZrAl 
[12, 13 ] alloys , are reported to have good corrosion resistance and excellent mechanical properties , 
compared with t raditional Ti alloys. H owever, extensive application of Ti alloys has been still

## Page 3

somewhat hampered by the relatively poor processing performance and high cost . To achieve 
tremendous potential of Ti alloys, selective laser melting (SLM), as one of the most promising additive 
manufacturing technologies, has gained increasing attention recently since it enables cost-effective 
fabrication of near-full-dense components with almost any complicated shapes , thus, some unnecessary 
processes are also eliminated. In addition, the selective laser melted alloy can directly achieve excellent 
mechanical properties as forged alloy. Nowadays, many Ti alloys have been manufactured by SLM, 
such as pure Ti [ 14], TC4 [15], TC11 [16], Ti2AlNb [17], etc. On the other hand, SLM has a universal 
problem, i.e. residual stress. M. Junaid [18] has reported that maximum residual stress can reach 280 
MPa (compressive) and 320MPa (tensile) in selective laser melt ed Ti-5Al-2.5Sn alloy . J. Ahn [19] 
calculated that the residual stress of selective laser melt ed TC4 alloy was approximately in the range 
between 750 and 780 MPa , comparable to the yield strength of around 900 MPa at room temperature. 
So, the residual stress in selective laser melt ed Ti alloys contributes to a large extent to the size 
instability of mechanical parts , and it is also a big risk of failure in application due to a significant 
reduction in plasticity. 
To reduce and eliminate residual stress during SLM , subsequent high temperature annealing is 
carried out . Phase transition and recrystallization , which can effectively reduce stress , occur during 
annealing. Zhao [20] reported that superior ductility and high tensile strength were achieved through 
equiaxial alpha microstructure fabricated by high temperature annealing in laser solid formed TC4 
alloy. Liu [21] indicated that beta heat treatment of laser melting deposited 
Ti-5Al-5Mo-5V-1Cr-1Fe alloy lead to the formation of herringbone Widmanstätten grain 
boundary α (α WGB), the α WGB effectively inhibited the crack propagation along grain boundary, 
resulting in the predominantly transcrystalline fracture and improved ductility. Tian [22] showed 
that both the strength and ductility of the laser deposited Ti-4Al-1.5Mn alloy were improved by 
water quenched from the α+β duplex phase region. In addition, according to the study by He [23], 
nano-scaled α lamellas formed by decomposition of metastable ω phase during low temperature 
isothermal heat treatment of laser surface melted Ti-55511 alloy. In this paper, combined with 
decomposition of metastable martensitic phase and recrystallization, grain refinement and excellent 
mechanical properties of a Ti -based alloy via laser melting and subsequent low temperature annealing 
are investigated. 
2. Experiment

## Page 4

For the present experiment, the Ti-25Zr-10Al (at.%) alloy ingots were prepared by melting a 
mixture of pure Ti (99.9 wt.%), Al (99.9 wt.%) and sponge Zr (97 wt.%) in argon atmosphere vacuum 
arc melting furnace (SKY Technology Delelopment DHL-400). To ensure compositional homogeneity, 
the ingots were flipped and re -melted thrice. And the laser melted ingots were prepared by using An 
IPG fiber laser system (IPG Photonics YLS -400-S2-TR). High-purity argon gas was used as shielding 
gas and passed through a coaxial nozzle at 3 L/min to protect the melted pool from heavy oxidation. 
Powder delivery was used to produce laser melted ingots. Spherical Ti-25Zr-10Al powders (Zr: 41, Al: 
7.3, O: 0.18, C: 0.09, N: 0.03, H: 0.008, Ti: balance, wt .%) were chosen as the starting material , and 
these powders were sieved using a 200 -mesh sieve to improve flowability and particle distribution . A 
Ti-25Zr-10Al plate was used as the substrate . The laser beam diameter, laser power, scanning speed, 
and powder-feeding rate in this investigation were 1.44mm, 800W, 5mm/s, and 4.3g/min, respectively. 
Specimens with sizes approaching 1mm×2mm×25mm were machined directly from laser melted ingots. 
Phase and microstructure tests were all sampled at the center of ingots. Differential scanning 
calorimetry (DSC) was used to determine the transition temperature at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. 
According to the result of DSC, as shown in Fig. 1, the arc melt ed and laser melted ingots were 
annealed at 500 °C lower than the β transus temperature (830±30 °C) of approximately 300 °C for 2 h. 
The phas es of the specimens were confirmed by conventional X -ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα 
radiation (D/max-2500/PC). The microstructure of the heat treated plated samples was examined using 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which specimens were prepared via twin -jet electrochemical 
polishing in a solution containing 10% perchloric acid and 90% methanol at 1 4 V and −35 °C. 
Bone-shaped plate specimens with an original gauge length of 21 mm and a cross-sectional dimension 
of 1 mm×2 mm were prepared by using wire cutting and sandpaper grinding for the tensile tests . To 
ensure accuracy, five identical samples were used for each proces s. Uniaxial tensile tests were 
performed on an Instron 5982 testing machine at a strain rate of 5×10−4 s−1. 
3. Results and discussion 
The XRD patterns of Ti-25Zr-10Al alloy after melting and annealing are shown in Fig. 2. It is 
clear that the phases are distinctly different between arc melted and laser melted ingots, i.e., the cooling 
rate can significantly influence the phase transformation of solidification process. The XRD pattern of 
arc melt ed alloy is mainly α′′ martensite phase peaks . When ingot is cooling from molten state , t he 
liquid phase solidifies into β phase, and subsequently transforms into α′′ martensite on further cooling

## Page 5

below β→α transus temperature . Due to t he relatively quick cooling rate of arc melt ing, the α′′ 
martensite phase forms instead of the equilibrium phase transition from β phase to α phase. When 
raising the cooling rate through laser remelting, something interesting happen s. The α/α′ phase along 
with β phase is observed. Raising cooling rate can restrain equilibrium phase transition and martensite 
phase transition, then more β phase can be stabilized to room temperature and a certain amount of α/α′ 
phase, instead of α′′ phase, forms in laser melted alloy. On the other hand, a ll diffraction peaks match 
with those of α phase, no β phase and/or other martensitic phase peaks is observed in the XRD patterns 
of both arc melted and laser melted alloys after annealing at 500 °C. This indicates that β phase and 
martensitic phases completely transform to α phase. 
The TEM micrographs of Ti-25Zr-10Al alloy after melting and annealing are shown in Fig. 3. It's 
obvious to observe that arc melted alloy is mainly composed of α′′ martensite phase with dislocation, 
but α/α′ phase with {101̅1} twinning is observed in laser melted alloy clearly. {101̅1} twinning is a 
common mechanism of plastic deformation at temperature above 673 K in hcp titanium with limited 
slip systems [24, 25]. And Zhou [26] had reported that high residual stress formed during β- hot-rolled 
and water quenching could induce {101̅1} deforming twinning in α/α′ phase of TiZr binary alloy. Due 
to the high residual stress occurs by rapid cooling rate of laser melting in this study, {101̅1} deforming 
twinning forms in α/α′ phase of current TiZrAl alloy. This rapid cooling induced deforming twinning 
has been also reported i n laser melted pure Ti and TC4 alloys [ 14, 15 ]. In addition, after s ubsequent 
500 °C annealing, only α phase exists in both arc melted and laser melted alloys after annealing. 
However, the m icrostructure morphologies are quite different , n anoscale lath and equiaxial α phase 
with the average size of 0.3μm form in arc melted and laser melted alloys, separately. Normally, the 
phase transformation in Ti alloys during cooling ( β/martensite→α) is governed by the so -called 
Burgers orientation relationship {0002 }α || {110}β and <11-20>α || <111>β with 12 possible 
α-orientations that can transform from a single parent β grain [27]. Thus, the lamellar α phase, 
transformed from β phase, with basketweave or widmanst ̈tten morphology appears in arc melted alloy. 
But in laser melted alloy, the high residual stress and a large number of defects promote the occurrence 
of recrystallization , t herefore, the morphology of α phase is not lath but equiaxial. The nucleation 
process of α phase after different processes of melting and annealing is observed clearly in Fig. 4. More 
nucleation point forms in laser melted alloy, compared with limited in arc melted alloy, as shown in Fig. 
4b and d. For current TiZrAl alloy, the schematic illustration of microstructure evolution during

## Page 6

different processes of melting and annealing is shown in Fig. 5 . Compared with arc melted alloy, rapid 
cooling rate of laser melting process leads to high residual stress and a large number of defects, 
including dislocation and twinning, which are potential nucleation points of equiaxial α phase. 
Fig. 6 and Table 1 demonstrate the mechanical properties of Ti-25Zr-10Al alloys after d ifferent 
processes of melting and annealing. The mechanical properties of Ti -25Zr-10Al alloy are evidently 
dependent on solidification conditions. The yield strength and the tensile strength of samples increase 
from 1087Mpa and 1201MPa to 1205MPa and 1257MPa, respectively, for laser melted alloy instead of 
arc melted alloy. And after 2h 500 °C annealing, the yield strength and the tensile strength of arc 
melted alloy decrease to 1039 MPa and 1162Mpa, but the elongation increases from 5% to 7.7%. This 
is a common phenomenon, which is caused by recovery, in alloys after annealing. On the other hand, 
the yield strength, the tensile strength and the elongation of laser melted alloy are all improved greatly 
after 2h 500 °C annealing. Due to the equiaxial α phase with the average size of 0.3μm forms during 
annealing, ultrahigh strength of 1352MPa and good elongation of 10.9% are obtained. 
 
Conclusions 
In conclusion, compared with arc melting, ultrahigh strength and good elongation of Ti-25Zr-10Al 
alloy via laser melting and s ubsequent low temperature annealing are investigated. The microstructure 
is strongly dependent on thermal history. Compared with orthorhombic α'' martensitic phase in arc 
melted alloy, at the condition of r elatively high cooling rate by laser melting, Body-centered-cubic β 
phase and hexagonal close-packed α' martensitic phase with {101̅1} twinning is observed, and due to a 
large number of nucleation point caused by high residual stress and complex defects, recrystallization 
with the average grain size of 0.3μm occurs after subsequent 2h 500 °C annealing, and then, ultrahigh 
strength of 1352MPa and good elongation of 10.9% are obtained.
