Abstract 
This paper describes the role of solidification processing of high-temperature eutectic alloys in the development of high- 
temperature structural materials. Particular emphasis is placed on directional solidification of eutectic-based alloys using cold crucible 
Czochralski crystal growth. Alloys with melting temperatures up to 2250 “C were directionally solidified by this technique for study of 
the microstructure, phase equilibria and mechanical properties. The attributes and limitations of the Czochralski technique are 
compared with those of other methods for solidification of high-temperature alloys. The microstructures and fracture toughness data 
of directionally solidified in situ composites generated from Nb-Si and Cr-Nb alloy systems are presented. Property enhancements 
that can be achieved by directional solidification of high-temperature materials are discussed. 
Keywords: Intermetallics; Eutectics; Alloys; Solidification 
1. Introduction 
Melt processing is a critical step in the development 
of high-temperature materials for structural applica- 
tions. Two techniques have been developed recently 
for directional solidification of high-melting tempera- 
ture alloys. The first of these techniques is cold crucible 
Czochralski directional solidification [ 1,2], and the 
second is the optical imaging floating zone (OIFZ) 
technique [3]. The present paper discusses the former 
of these two techniques and its application to Cr-Nb 
and Nb-Si in situ composites. 
Intermetallics such as Nb,Si, Nb,Si, and Cr,Nb 
possess high melting temperatures, high moduli and 
good strength retention at elevated temperatures, but 
poor damage tolerance, or fracture toughness, at low 
temperatures [4,5]. One approach by which the high- 
temperature properties of the intermetallic can be 
exploited and its low temperature toughness accommo- 
dated is to generate an in situ composite consisting of a 
strong ductile phase and a high-strength, stable inter- 
metallic phase. This extrinsic approach to increasing 
the fracture toughness and damage tolerance is likely to 
be more fruitful than alternative attempts at increasing 
the intermetallic fracture toughness by increasing the 
intrinsic ductility [4-91. Recent studies of two-phase 
Nb,Si,-(Nb) alloys have demonstrated that ductile- 
phase toughening can be employed to enhance the low- 
temperature toughness and retain the high-temperature 
strength [7-lo]. However, for the toughening phase to 
be effective, the composite morphology, volume frac- 
tion, and dispersion must be carefully controlled. 
Directional solidification can provide an important 
role in control of the toughening phases in these in situ 
composites. These high-temperature in situ micro- 
composites are an important class of materials since 
intrinsic thermodynamic stability exists between the 
toughening and strengthening phases of the composite. 
In addition, the composite microstructure can be 
engineered by modification of the solidification condi- 
tions and alloy composition. 
The present paper describes directional solidifica- 
tion of intemetallic-based composites and focuses on 
the results of studies of Cr-Nb and Nb-Si in situ 
composites. The Cr-Nb composites consist of Cr,Nb 
and a refractory metal (Cr, Nb) solid solution. The 
Nb-Si composites consist of Nb,Si, Nb,Si,, or both, 
and are toughened by an Nb-Si solid solution (abbre- 
viated (Nb) in this paper). Nb-Si alloys with composi- 
tions of lO%-25% Si (all compositions are given in

2. Directional solidification 
The method used for directional solidification of 
high-temperature materials in the present study 
involves Czochralski crystal growth from an induction 
levitated melt which is retained in a segmented water- 
cooled copper crucible. This process is shown 
schematically in Fig. 1. Typically DS samples approxi- 
mately 1 cm in diameter and approximately 10 cm long 
are prepared using this technique. 
Generally a charge of 50-100 g is used for direc- 
tional solidification. The alloy is prepared from high 
purity (more than 99.99% pure) elements by induction 
levitation melting in the same segmented water-cooled 
copper crucible [ 1,2] that is used for directional solidi- 
fication. The alloys are melted at least twice, and in 
most cases three times or more, in order to ensure 
homogeneity prior to directional solidification. The 
melt is partially levitated by the electromagnetic field 
generated by the induction coil, and it is also partially 
supported by the skull of the alloy which is formed 
against the hearth of the crucible. Between each melt- 
ing operation the charge is lifted off the hearth to 
ensure complete melting of the initial elemental charge. 
Fig. 1. Schematic diagram showing Czochralski directional solid- 
ification from the segmented water-cooled copper crucible. 
The alloy is then directionally solidified by lowering a 
previously prepared seed crystal of the same alloy into 
the melt and withdrawing the seed at a constant and 
controlled rate. Typically growth rates in the range 
OS-35 mm min-’ are employed, depending on the 
alloy being directionally solidified and the preferred 
structure. The seed is attached to a water-cooled 
microprocessor-controlled pulling rod, whose rate of 
withdrawal and speed of rotation are controlled using 
programmable logic controllers. During directional 
solidification, the temperature gradient in the crystal 
and the temperature distribution in the melt are 
monitored using an IR camera. 
Directional solidification using this technique can be 
performed under vacuum or inert gas such as argon. 
Generally, because of the high volatility of some alloy 
species, directional solidification is performed under 
an atmosphere of ultrahigh purity argon (total impurity 
content less than 0.75 ppm). When an argon atmos- 
phere is to be used, before melting the charge the 
chamber containing the cold crucible is evacuated to a 
vacuum of better than 10e5 Torr in order to minimize 
contamination of the DS sample with interstitial 
elements such as oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen.

4. In situ composites of Nb-Si alloys 
4. I. Background on Nb-Si in situ composites 
In situ (Nb)-Nb,Si, composites have shown a 
promising combination of room temperature fracture 
toughness, strength and creep resistance [4,7-lo), but 
poor environmental resistance [9,11]. Single-phase 
Nb,Si, has promising high-temperature creep behavior 
[4] and high-temperature strength, but a room 
temperature fracture toughness of less than 3 MPa & 
[4]. It is therefore an attractive system for toughness 
enhancement by an extrinsic approach. Mendiratta et 
al. [7,8] adopted such an approach and reported frac- 
ture toughness values of greater than 20 MPa Jfrl in 
some hot-extruded Nb-Si alloy in situ composites. 
Directional solidification of Nb-Si alloys may also be 
used to generate aligned (Nb)-Nb,Si and (Nb)-Nb$i, 
in situ composites with a fracture toughness substan- 
tially greater than that of Nb,Si,. In this section the 
microstructures of these DS in situ composites is 
described together with fracture toughness measure- 
ments for a range of (Nb) volume fractions. The 
volume fraction was adjusted by the use of Si concentr- 
ations from 10% to 25%. The environmental limit- 
ations of this system can be improved by alloying with 
Ti and Al [9,11]. Examples of DS Nb-Ti-Si ternary 
alloy in situ composites are also described in this sec- 
tion. 
The basis for the in situ composites presented here 
is the Nb-rich side of the Nb-Si phase diagram, where 
there are the eutectic and eutectoid phase transfor- 
mations, as shown in Fig. 6. The eutectic between (Nb) 
and Nb,Si occurs at 1880 “C, and the eutectoid 
between (Nb) and Nb,Si, occurs at 1670 “C [16]. The 
eutectoid transformation of Nb,Si to (Nb) and Nb,Si, 
is very slow 141; extended exposure of the order of 
100 h at around 1500 “C is required to generate 
Nb,Si,. In as-cast alloys with Si concentrations less 
than the liquid composition at the peritectic tempera- 
ture, only Nb,Si and no primary Nb,Si, is observed. 
However, increasing the Si concentration beyond 
19.5% Si can provide Nb,Si, as the primary solidifica- 
tion phase [16].

5. Summary 
Cold crucible Czochralski crystal growth can be 
used to generate aligned in situ composites consisting 
of a strong metallic phase together with a high-strength, 
stable intermetallic phase. Aligned microcomposite 
structures can be generated from both eutectic and off- 
eutectic compositions in simple binary, as well as multi- 
component alloys. Examples of in situ composites 
generated from Cr-Nb and Nb-Si binary and 
Nb-Ti-Si ternary alloys have been described in the 
present paper. Toughness values of up to 14 MPa Jf;l 
have been achieved in intermetallic-based composites 
through the controlled introduction of a ductile phase. 
This effect has been observed in Nb-Si and Nb-Ti-Si 
in situ composites, but it is less effective in Cr-Nb 
composites.
