Ferritic Fe–Al–Ni–Cr alloys with coherent precipitates for
high-temperature applications
C. Stallybrass∗, G. Sauthoff
Max-Planck-Institut für Eisenforschung GmbH, D-40074 Düsseldorf, Germany
Received 25 August 2003; received in revised form 25 January 2004
Abstract
Strengthening by a homogeneous distribution of a second phase is a concept that is widely employed in high-temperature materials. It is
shown that suitable microstructures can be obtained in the Fe–Al–Ni–Cr system with ordered (Ni, Fe)Al precipitates in a ferritic matrix. These
precipitates lead to higher levels of yield strength at elevated temperatures than conventional iron-base high-temperature alloys. The results
show that the investigated materials exhibit the highest yield strength after solution heat treatment and lower values after aging, indicating
precipitation of ﬁne particles during air cooling. The decrease of yield strength in the latter case was more pronounced for alloys with a high
precipitate content than for leaner alloys.

1. Introduction
Heat-resistant ferritic steels have superior thermal con-
ductivity and lower thermal expansion than nickel-base su-
peralloys. Both properties are desirable for application in
energy conversion systems. However, their high-temperature
mechanical properties limit the operational temperature to
about 600
◦C. Considerable effort has therefore been made
to increase the high-temperature strength of ferritic materi-
als in order to achieve higher operational temperatures and
thereby a higher level of efﬁciency. The approaches that have
been followed include strengthening by a dispersion of ox-
ides or precipitates. While the former process has yielded
good results, its feasibility is limited owing to high produc-
tion costs. In this respect, precipitate strengthening is more
attractive. In the Fe–Ni–Al ternary system there is a mis-
cibility gap between the disordered A2 and the ordered B2
phases [1–3]. Both phases have very similar lattice param-
eters and are coherent and coplanar, a characteristic the al-
loys with compositions within the miscibility gap share with
nickel-base superalloys. Therefore, these alloys could be in-
teresting for high-temperature applications as an alternative
to nickel-base superalloys, and several studies have been car-

The present work aims at combining the excellent ox-
idation resistance of ferritic Fe–Cr–Al alloys with the
high-temperature strength of Fe–Ni–Al alloys with a high
volume fraction of the B2 phase. The focus of the study lies
on mechanical behavior in view of a possible application as
a high-temperature structural material.
2. Experimental
Eight Fe–Al–Ni–Cr alloys were produced by vacuum in-
duction melting with compositions based on the pseudobi-
nary section Fe + 2.5 at.% Al–NiAl + 1.25 at.% Al[1] with
the aim to obtain two-phase materials consisting of (Ni,
Fe)Al precipitates in a ferritic matrix. 10 wt.% of Cr was
added and substituted for iron and nickel in order to reduce
the aluminum content necessary to form alumina scales[12].
According to Ref. [13], Cr is mostly distributed in the fer-
ritic phase. The nominal chemical compositions of the al-
loys are listed in Table 1. The melts were cast into a copper
mold, yielding rods with a length of approximately 160 mm

These factors may account for the absence of the hyperﬁne
precipitate. The shape of the precipitates varied with the pre-

fractions, isolated cubes dominated while, with increasing
volume content, the precipitates ﬁrst formed plates and then
networks. In all cases, they were aligned along certain crys-
tallographic directions. The volume fractions of the larger
precipitates did not change signiﬁcantly with aging time.

4. Conclusions
It was shown that a homogeneous distribution of strength-
ening particles can be achieved in the Fe–Al–Ni–Cr qua-
ternary system. The precipitate morphology was found to
depend on the alloy composition, ranging from isolated par-
ticles at low Al and Ni levels to a maze-like morphology.
After aging at 900
◦C, the investigated materials contained
hyperﬁne precipitates besides the precipitates formed at
900
◦C, while no hyperﬁne precipitates could be found after
aging at 750 ◦C. These hyperﬁne precipitates seem to be re-
sponsible for the high yield strength at lower temperatures
and for the sharp decrease between 600 and 700
◦C. The
coarsening kinetics of the precipitates compared well with
values found for ternary Fe–Ni–Al alloys, which is impor-
tant in view of a possible application as high-temperature
material. Compression tests showed attractive yield stress
levels at high temperatures.
