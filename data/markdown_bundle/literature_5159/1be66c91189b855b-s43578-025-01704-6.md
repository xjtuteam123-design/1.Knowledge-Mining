and Ni‑Fe alloys

Nanotwins (NTs) significantly enhance strength and thermal stability of face ‑centered cubic (FCC) alloys 
compared to coarse‑grained counterparts. However, alloying elements can affect NT stability at elevated 
temperatures. We combined molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with a recently developed anisotropic 
multi‑phase phase field model to investigate NT stability in two Ni‑based binary alloys: Ni–Cr and Ni–Fe. 
MD simulations reveal Ni–Fe alloys have higher intrinsic stacking fault energies compared to Ni–Cr alloys 
across similar solute concentrations. Moreover, solute effects on incoherent twin boundary (ITB) energies 
differ notably. ITB mobility exhibits Arrhenius behavior within the studied temperature range. Phase field 
simulations, incorporating MD‑derived mobility laws, demonstrate that NT/grain boundary detachment 
and subsequent detwinning strongly depend on annealing temperature and NT thickness, particularly 
above 0.4 homologous temperature. These insights aid in designing nanotwinned Ni‑based alloys with 
improved thermal stability and mechanical properties; in particular, additional Cr or Fe content can 
significantly elevate the onset temperature of NT motion.

Introduction
Face center cubic (FCC) Ni–Cr–Fe alloys are frequently used 
in the nuclear industry as a high temperature alloy exhibiting 
excellent radiation resistance [1 , 2]. For many FCC metals, 
nanotwinned structuring, by the introduction of an unusually 
dense amount of coherent twin boundaries (CTBs), can provide 
even further strengthening [ 3–5] and thermal stability [ 4] and 
possibly radiation resistance [6–8] beyond those of their coarse-
grain counterparts. Each nanotwin (NT) is a domain bounded 
by /Sigma13{111} CTBs and has thicknesses of nanoscale dimen -
sions. Nanotwinned materials usually contain micron-sized 
grains comprising numerous stacks of NTs, most of which are 
bounded by grain boundaries (GBs). Nanotwinned structures 
were first extensively studied in low stacking fault energy (SFE 
< 45 mJ/m 2 ) metals such as Cu, where they notably improve 
mechanical properties while maintaining other properties such 
as conductivity [5 ]. In contrast, metals with higher SFEs, such 
as pure Ni (about 125 mJ/m 2 ), typically require extreme condi-
tions such as electrodeposition for the formation of NT [3 , 4]. 
Later, metals were alloyed, lowering their SFE and allowing for 
nanotwinned materials to be made in a wide range of materials 
and in a variety of ways [ 6, 9–11]. For instance, Guo et al. [ 12] 
fabricated a nanotwinned Ni20 Cr alloy by ultrasonic shot peen-
ing. Alternatively, Liang et al. [13] fabricated a nanotwinned 
Ni20 Cr alloy by the deformation mechanisms operating under 
tribological shear.
While NT materials provide advantageous mechanical prop-
erties, they are also known to be susceptible to loss of their NT 
structure when subjected to heating and annealing processes, 
leading to the removal of the NT s, microstructural coarsening 
or abnormal grain growth [14–16]. However, thermally induced 
detwinning, when properly managed, can yield a heterogene-
ous nanostructured material, one that may potentially be both 
strong and ductile [ 17–19]. Nevertheless, before consider -
ing improvements in Ni-based alloys by nanostructuring, it is 
important to understand the NT material response to elevated 
temperature annealing treatments and possible detwinning.
Detwinning typically occurs via the nucleation and 
migration of incoherent twin boundaries (ITBs), which 
are the /Sigma13{112} boundaries of the twin domain [3 , 20, 21]. 
ITBs can form when NTs detach from GBs at the NT/GB 
junction. The newly formed ITB then proceeds to migrate 
under thermal driving forces removing the NT domain. In 
this regard, both atomistic and mesoscale simulations have 
helped in understanding the dynamics of NT s or GBs in pure 
FCC metals at finite temperatures [22– 26]. Molecular dynam-
ics (MD) simulations have been widely used to resolve the 
mechanisms underlying thermally induced glide of GBs [ 22, 
23] and /Sigma13{112} ITBs [24 , 27] in pure Cu and Ni. Arrhenius-
type temperature-dependent migration of /Sigma13{112} ITBs is 
observed in pure Cu. Additionally, MD simulations have also 
linked migrating /Sigma13{112} ITBs with the radiation tolerance 
of nanotwinned Cu [ 25] and Ni [ 27]. Mesoscale simulations, 
like phase field (PF) models, can simulate the evolution of 
interfaces and boundaries. One challenge PF models need to 
overcome in modeling twins is that the energy between the 
CTB and ITB can be substantial. With advanced formulations 
that can treat such significant boundary energy anisotropy, PF 
models have been able to reproduce twin thickening, coupled 
grain/twin growth, and detwinning kinetics in FCC metals 
[24, 26].
Despite extensive research on NT s in pure metals, the 
influence of alloying elements such as Cr and Fe on the 
mobility and detwinning behavior of NT s in Ni-based alloys 
remains largely unexplored. Recent density-functional the-
ory (DFT) and CALPHAD studies show that SFE in Ni–Cr 
binaries decreases almost linearly with Cr content, while 
in Ni–Fe binaries it first decreases and then increases as Fe 
content continues to increase [ 28]. Additionally, solute inter -
actions can further influence boundary stability by either 
promoting or hindering migration. A recent MC/MD study 
[29] demonstrated that how Cr segregation can affect GB 
migration in Ni-Cr alloys. Cr concentrations can cause the 
�5(310)[010] GB structure to migrate in multiple stages, while 
the �101(200)[100] GB migrates more smoothly, showing lit-
tle to no such fluctuations in migration behavior. Although 
these simulations did not directly address /Sigma13{112} ITBs, they 
highlight how Cr can alter boundary kinetics. Recent work by 
the authors explored the energy and mobility of ITBs in FCC 
Ni–Cr alloys, finding that they can be significantly influence 
by Cr concentration. Increasing the Cr content to 30% reduces 
the ITB energy by approximately 15% compared to pure Ni. 
Similar solute/boundary analyses have not been performed 
for the Ni–Fe binary much less Ni–Cr–Fe alloys. Understand-
ing these effects is crucial for the design and application of 
thermally stable NT-strengthened Ni alloys. While the general 
Ni–Cr–Fe alloy may be of interest, it is important to begin 
with the constituent binaries, namely Ni–Cr and Ni–Fe.
The objective of this work is to quantify the mobility and 
evolution of NT s in Ni-based alloys, with particular interest 
in the Ni–Cr and Ni–Fe systems. MD simulations are car -
ried out to compare ITB kinetics between Ni–Cr and Ni–Fe. 
Using atomistic results, a PF model is developed to simulate 
detwinning in Ni–Cr, the binary suggested to have the lower 
thermal stability.
This paper is structured as follows. First, we employ MD 
simulations to determine fundamental alloy-specific proper -
ties, including the intrinsic SFE ( γiSFE ), ITB energy ( σITB ), 
and CTB ( σCTB ) for Ni-based alloys. MD simulations are then 
used to quantify the temperature-dependent mobility of ITBs.

Discussion
/Sigma13{112} ITB migration kinetics
The present work provides insight into the migration behavior 
of nanotwins in Ni-based binary alloys using MD simulations 
and PF simulations. First, MD simulations reveal that Fe and 
Cr additions exert distinct effects on the ITB energy and SFEs 
in Ni-based binary alloys. Fe tends to increase the ITB energy 
while exerting only minor influence on SFE, leading to high 
SFE values in NiFe alloys. By contrast, Cr generally lowers ITB 
energy and substantially reduces the SFE in NiCr alloys. These 
different effects may arise from their atomic size. Fe has a similar 
atomic size as Ni, which could reduce its effect on the SFEs in 
the NiFe alloys. In contrast, Cr has a larger atomic size than Ni, 
which results in an atomic mismatch in the NiCr alloys. This 
mismatch can cause local distortion in the bulk but may also 
allow strain relaxation near the boundary, which could reduce 
ITB energy. The presence of high SFE and elevated ITB energy 
in the NiFe system implies a lower propensity for nanotwin for-
mation compared to NiCr. To date, unlike in NiCr, there are no 
reported nanotwin studies in NiFe.
In NiCr alloys, the overall trend is that decreasing Cr con-
centration increases ITB energy, consistent with the higher 
SFE at lower Cr concentration (like Ni90Cr) and lower SFE 
at higher Cr concentrations ( Ni70Cr). However, this trend 
is not strictly monotonic. An unusually high ITB energy is 
observed in Ni80Cr, deviating from the expected composi-
tion dependence. Such deviations may due to factors like Cr 
distribution around the boundary, which can influence the 
ITB structure and energy. While direct experimental measure-
ments of /Sigma13{112} ITB energy in NiCr alloys are lacking, recent 
experiments show a complex and non-linear dependence of 
Cr segregation along Ni GBs [ 32]. Such effects could result in 
the unusually high ITB energy for Ni80Cr.
MD simulations are also used to calculate the temperature 
dependent ITB migration in both Ni70 Fe and Ni70Cr, as shown 
in Figure  2. Compared with migration behavior for the same 
type of /Sigma13{112} in pure Cu, both Ni70 Fe and Ni70 Cr show 
higher threshold temperatures for measurable ITB migration. 
This elevated threshold indicates greater nanotwin stability 
in the Ni-based binaries, especially for Ni70Fe, where the ITB 
remains immobile up to about 1300 K. Given the low propen-
sity for nanotwin formation in Ni70 Fe and the high thermal 
stability of those that do form, a detailed investigation of NT 
behavior in the Ni–Fe system would likely require higher and 
far-from-equilibrium conditions. On the other hand, for Ni70
Cr, we identify a clear threshold temperature of 600 K above 
which measurable ITB motion exists. Below this threshold, the

One point worth noting is that the NiCrFe interatomic 
potential [33] employed in this work does not account for the 
magnetic effect associated with neither Fe or Cr, which could 
affect NT formation and migration behavior, especially below 
the Curie temperature (about 1043 K for Fe and 311 K for Cr) 
[34]. However, this omission likely has minimal impact on the 
current findings since the Fe concentration considered here is 
limited to 30%, and NT in Ni70 Fe remains stationary up to about 
1300 K which is well above the Curie temperature for Fe. Simi-
larly, the NT in Ni70 Cr remains stationary up to about 600 K 
which is also well above the Curie temperature. A recent work 
comparing the SFEs calculated via DFT (magnetic effect con-
sidered) and MD simulations with various potentials in Ni-Fe 
binary indicates that although absolute SFE value vary, the 
general trend of decreasing SFE with increasing Fe concentra-
tion holds consistently up to around 30% Fe [28]. This provides 
additional validation for selecting 30% Fe as an appropriate and 
meaningful concentration limit. Thus, neglecting the magnetic 
effects of Fe and Cr is not expected to critically affect the conclu-
sions of MD simulations here.
Detwinning behavior in Ni‑Cr binary
With temperature-dependent mobility informed by MD simula-
tion, PF modeling is employed here to study the roles of anneal-
ing temperature and NT thickness on migration of NTs. In 
previous work [24], this PF model was validated by comparing 
NT migration velocities in pure Cu calculated by MD and the 
PF model. Considering that NTs in Ni–Cr alloys exhibit lower 
mobility than those in pure Cu, MD validation for NTs in NiCr 
alloys will be less feasible. Therefore, we did not conduct direc-
tion MD simulations in this study.
As shown in Sec. 2.2.1, a narrower NT migrates faster than 
a thicker NT in Ni70 Cr binary alloy, which is similar to what 
was reported in pure Cu [24]. Additionally, PF simulations pre-
sented in this work show an increase in NT migration velocity 
with rising temperatures in Ni70Cr. This trend is consistent with 
expectations, given that the mobility follows an Arrhenius-type 
temperature dependence.
A notable difference between NT migration in pure Cu and 
Ni70 Cr lies in their respective onset temperature and velocities. 
For NT s with the same thickness (like 5 nm), NT migration in 
pure Cu occurs at temperatures below 400 K, with velocities 
exceeding 6 m/s at 400 K [24], whereas NTs in Ni70 Cr remain 
stationary until 600 K and exhibit a migration velocity of only 
1.7 m/s. For the 5 nm-thick NT in Cu (with a melting tempera-
ture Tm = 1357K ), the homologous temperature at 400 K is 
0.295. At the same absolute temperature for Ni70Cr, which is 
around 485 K, the calculated NT migration velocity is only 1.18 
m/s, which is less than 20% of the NTs migration velocity in pure 
Cu. This significant difference in homologous temperatures, 
along with the significantly lower NT migration velocities in 
Ni70 Cr at comparable homologous temperatures, highlights the 
reduced mobility and enhanced thermal stability of the Ni–Cr 
system.
Experimental studies also support the finding that Ni-
based alloys exhibit greater thermal stability than NT Cu. 
Nanotwinned Cu has an onset temperature for detwinning and 
grain growth that lies below 600 K [35], while Ni-based alloys 
(Ni–Mo, Ni–W) exhibit significantly enhanced thermal stabil-
ity, with twin boundary migration and grain growth suppressed 
up to 900-1000 K [4 ]. While these studies do not compare the 
detwinning behavior of pure Cu and Ni–Cr alloys, they nonethe-
less provide valuable benchmarks for validating our simulation 
results and support the conclusion that Ni-based alloys exhibit 
superior thermal stability compared to NT Cu.
Finally, PF simulations reveal that narrower NT s and lower 
GB energies both facilitate the detwinning process, indicating 
reduced thermal stability for narrower NT s associated with 
lower GB energies. A recent experimental study observed a simi-
lar trend in nanotwinned pure Ni, where the onset temperature 
for NT coarsening decreased with decreasing NT thickness, 
confirming lower thermal stability for narrower NT structures 
[4]. The size dependence reported here is primarily governed 
by curvature-driven migration. In general, the driving force 
for boundary motion is proportional to the product of bound -
ary energy and boundary curvature, F ∼ σκ , where κ is the 
curvature of the boundary. Boundaries with higher curvature 
migrate faster due to the reduced total interfacial energy [ 36].

force, and thus higher detwinning velocity. This is consistent

The findings of this work suggest that there might be a 
trade-off between achieving thermal stability and enhancing 
strength or radiation resistance in nanotwinned alloys. Finer 
NTs increase strength and radiation resistance by introducing 
a higher density of CTBs [ 5, 9, 11], yet this refinement reduces 
thermal stability because narrower NTs migrate more readily 
[20, 27]. Alloying can serve as a powerful factor to minimize 
this trade-off. Effective strategies could focus on finding ways 
to produce fine NTs in alloys with high SFE and to reduce the 
GB density. The solute selection must first and foremost lower 
the SFE to permit NT formation, while simultaneously reduc-
ing ITB migration mobility. Our results show that FCC Ni–Fe 
exhibits substantially lower ITB mobility than Ni–Cr at the same 
temperature and concentration, suggesting that, if NTs could be 
synthesized in Ni–Fe, they would be much more thermally stable 
than in Ni–Cr. Finally, because NT stability is ultimately limited 
by ITB/GB junctions, reducing the density of GBs would lower 
the number of vulnerable sites and thereby enhance overall ther-
mal resistance [4, 14, 35].
While this study employed a state-of-the-art multi-scale 
combination of MD and PF simulations to investigate NT behav-
ior during high temperature annealing, a few other features 
could be added. First, the current PF model does not include 
elastic energy contributions, which could influence NT stability 
and detwinning dynamics, especially in the presence of stress 
fields. Second, the potential segregation of Cr, particularly near 
incoherent twin boundaries (ITBs), has not been comprehen-
sively explored. Such segregation can be simulated using MC/
MD and may alter local boundary properties and affect both 
mobility and thermal stability of NTs. Additionally, the ternary 
Ni-Fe-Cr alloys are not considered in this work but our find-
ings on the binaries suggest ternaries would be worthy of future 
work. Their study would introduce interesting cross effects 
between Cr and Fe. The combined influence of Cr and Fe on 
SFE and ITB energy could be non-linear as Cr tends to lower the 
SFE and ITB energy, whereas Fe to increase them. The atomic 
size mismatch (Cr is larger and Fe is smaller relative to Ni) and 
possible magnetic effects at lower temperature could further add 
to the complexity. Future studies incorporating elastic effects, 
solute segregation and cross effects in ternary could provide a 
more complete understanding of the underlying mechanisms 
governing NT evolution in Ni-based alloys.
Conclusions
In summary, in this work, we present a study integrating atomic-
scale molecular dynamics and mesoscale phase field simulations 
of nanotwin (NT) mobility and interactions with grain bounda-
ries (GBs) under elevated temperatures. The molecular dynamics 
(MD) results show distinctly contrasting roles for alloying ele -
ments Cr and Fe in Ni: Cr significantly reduces the stacking 
fault and incoherent twin boundary (ITB) energies, promoting 
NT formation, whereas Fe has an opposite effect, potentially 
limiting NT formation and mobility. Subsequent phase field (PF) 
simulations employed a recently developed anisotropic multi-
phase phase field formulation, important for representing twins 
in FCC materials. The PF simulations were informed directly by 
MD-derived mobility laws and focused on Ni–Cr. They demon-
strated that NT migration velocity increases substantially with 
decreasing NT thickness and increasing annealing temperature. 
Nonetheless, NTs in Ni–Cr require a much higher homologous 
temperature than those in pure Cu to achieve the same migra -
tion velocity, highlighting the superior thermal stability of NT 
architectures in Ni–Cr alloys. Additionally, PF simulations 
reveal that narrower NTs and lower GB energies both facilitate 
the detwinning process. These insights facilitate the informed 
design of Ni-based alloys tailored for applications that require 
stable nanotwinned microstructures at elevated temperatures.
Simulation model and methodology
We employ a multi-scale approach that combines atomistic 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and mesoscale phase 
field (PF) modeling to investigate detwinning in two Ni-based 
alloys, Ni–Cr and Ni–Fe. The solute composition range spans 
from 0 to 30% of either Cr or Fe, ensuring that the alloy is single 
phase FCC. First, MD calculations are used to quantify three 
key defect properties: the 0 K stacking fault energy (SFE), the 
energy of /Sigma13[112] incoherent twin boundaries (ITBs), and the 
temperature-dependent ITB mobility as a function of alloy type 
and its concentration. The resulting mobility curves are then 
coarse-grained and imported as constitutive inputs into the PF 
model. With these atomistic parameters, the PF framework sys-
tematically explores how nanotwin thickness, temperature, and 
the neighboring grain boundary (GB) energy influence detwin-
ning kinetics and mechanisms.

properties, many of which are obtained from atomistic simula-

simulations are used to obtain static, alloy-specific properties, 
such as stacking fault energy (SFE) and ITB energy. A series of 
atomistic simulations are subsequently performed to estimate 
the temperature-dependent mobility of the ITB.

Two atomistic simulation cells are employed in this study. 
A smaller simulation cell, approximately 100 Å in the x-direc-
tion, 150 Å in the y-direction, and 170 Å in the z-direction, 
is used to calculate the SFE of Ni-based alloys. The lattice of 
this smaller cell is oriented such that the x-, y-, and z-directions 
correspond to [111] , [¯12¯1] , and [¯101] , respectively. A larger cell, 
shown in Figure  7, is used for calculating the ITB energy and 
temperature-dependent ITB mobility. Periodic boundary condi-
tions are applied in all directions, with two ITBs placed in the 
center of the simulation cell. The distance between these two 
ITBs is approximately 350 Å. The cell dimensions in the y- and 
x-directions are about 520 Å and 100 Å, respectively. The dimen-

study /Sigma13[111]60◦{1 185 } GB migration [23, 27] in pure Ni and 
/Sigma13{112} ITB in pure Cu [24]. Given that /Sigma13[111]60◦{1 185 } GB 
has a much longer periodic length along the boundary plane, the 
100 Å z-direction dimension should be sufficient to capture the 
migration behavior of the /Sigma13{112} ITB.
T o investigate ITB migration, the energy-conserving orien-
tational (ECO) driving force (DF) method [42, 43] is utilized. 
The same method was previously employed to address migration 
behavior in various GBs [22, 23] and ITBs [24]. By evaluating 
how closely the atomic environment aligns with the perfect ori-
entation of crystal, the ECO-DF method calculates symmetric 
orientational order parameters χ , which allow energy to be 
added to or subtracted from atoms near the twin boundaries. 
This energy difference gives rise to a net driving force for twin 
boundary migration, which lowers the overall energy of the 
system. The ECO force threshold order parameter φ is taken 
to be 0.25, and a cutoff distance 1.1 times the lattice constant at 
temperatures [23, 42].
Here, a driving force of 0.025 eV/atom is applied to study the 
migration of the ITB in both Ni-based alloys. T o investigate the 
temperature dependence of ITB migration, we consider mod-
erate temperatures ranging from 800 to 1400 K in increments 
of 100 K, corresponding to approximately 0.5 to 0.85 of the 
homologous temperature in the Ni-based binary alloys consid-
ered here. While it is acknowledged that twin boundary motion 
may not be the dominant deformation mechanisms above 0.6 of 
the homologous temperature, Ni-based alloys are well known for 
their exceptional thermal stability. Therefore, exploring higher 
homologous temperatures in our simulations allows us to evalu-
ate potential NT migration behavior under elevated temperature 
conditions.
For each temperature, three simulations with different initial 
velocities of atoms are performed to obtain the average veloc-
ity and ensure that the results are not biased by specific initial 
conditions. Prior to applying ECO-DF , each simulation system 
is brought to the desired temperature in 5 K increments, with 
equilibration for 5 picoseconds at each step. Subsequently, the 
simulation cell is equilibrated for an additional 20 picoseconds 
to ensure that the temperature has stabilized, and the system 
has relaxed to a stress-free state. The isothermal-isobaric (NPT) 
ensemble is applied to maintain the desired temperature and 
pressure throughout the process. The ITB migration velocity is 
calculated from the ITB position vs. time curve. The ITB posi -
tion at any given time step is defined as the mean y-position of 
all atoms associated with the ITB. These ITB atoms are those 
not belonging to either grain in the ECO-DF calculations

The phase field model methodology
The PF model in the current work builds upon the multi-phase 
field model (MPF) developed by Moelans [46]. A similar PF 
model was used to study He bubble behavior in Fe [47, 48]. The 
formulation was later advanced to treat the significant anisot-
ropy in interface energy characteristic of NTs and applied to 
NTs in Cu [24].
The microstructure is represented by a set of non-
conserved order parameters, also called PF variable,
