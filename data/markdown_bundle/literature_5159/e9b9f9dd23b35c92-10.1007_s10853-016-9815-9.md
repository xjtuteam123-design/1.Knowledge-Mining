ABSTRACT
The application of grain boundary engineering (GBE) techniques to enhance the
physical and mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys could potentially
increase the efﬁciency of turbine engines. Compared to traditional GBE pro-
cesses that require multiple iterations of room temperature deformation fol-
lowed by annealing, novel techniques for GBE based on the optimization of the
thermal–mechanical processing parameters exhibit more potential for produc-
ing complex-shaped Ni-based superalloys components. To date, the formation
and microstructural evolution of R3 boundaries during thermal–mechanical
processing have yet to be fully understood. In this investigation, the effects of
deformation texture and strain were systematically investigated in an advanced
Ni-based superalloy, RR1000. Using various strains and annealing tempera-
tures, the effects of recrystallization and texturing were quantiﬁed. Although
texturing was often associated with recrystallization that caused the length
fraction of R3 boundaries to decrease, the formation of Goss type texture during
deformation was found to promote the formation of R3 boundaries upon
annealing when compared to deformation texturing h111i parallel to the rolling
direction.
Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys are the leading materials for
use in critical sections of advanced gas turbine engines
where the applied stresses and temperatures are the
highest. Each of the hot section structures in a turbine
engine adheres to a different set of design require-
ments and as a result, various types and grades of Ni-
based superalloys differing in composition, processing
route, and resulting microstructure are utilized. Single-
crystal superalloys are commonly used for nozzle
guide vanes and turbine blades where resistance to
creep deformation and thermal fatigue are the princi-
pal life-limiting mechanisms [ 1–6]. Conversely, poly-
crystalline Ni-based superalloys are used for a wide
range of structures that operate at lower temperatures
and higher stresses where resistance to cyclic loading
conditions is the primary design requirement [ 7–13].
As advanced turbine engines are being designed to
operate at higher temperatures to improve the

RR1000 is a commercially available c–c
0 Ni-based
superalloy used in turbine disk rotor structures that
possesses high strength and fatigue crack propagation
resistance at elevated temperatures. The typical pro-
cessing route for RR1000 involves gas atomization and
powder consolidation to form a billet. The resulting
ﬁne-grained microstructure present in the billet makes
the material amenable for isothermal forging into near
net shape structures. Process parameters for isother-
mal forging of RR1000 are typically selected to pro-
mote superplastic ﬂow of the material during
deformation such that the large majority of the
macroscopic strains are accommodated via grain
boundary sliding and rotations. Following forging,
RR1000 can be thermally processed using either a sub-
solvus or super-solvus heat treatment to yield a ﬁne or
coarse-grained microstructure, respectively.
Grain boundary engineering (GBE) has been
demonstrated to enhance resistance to corrosion [ 16],
creep deformation [17, 18], and fatigue crack propaga-
tion/initiation [19, 20] for a wide range of polycrys-
talline materials [ 21]. The introduction of high
proportions of special grain boundaries that possess a
high degree of coherency into the random grain
boundary network [ 22] disrupts all processes that are
directly or indirectly dependent on grain boundary
diffusion. Despite signiﬁcant improvements, commer-
cial exploitation of GBE has been limited [ 23], as con-
ventional processing routes require multiple iterations
of cold rolling to 5–20 % strain at room temperature
followed by short time annealing of a few minutes in
order to obtain desirable fractions of ‘ ‘special grain
boundaries’ ’ (*50–60 %) [24, 25]. This limits the appli-
cability of GBE for Ni-based superalloys since defor-
mation at room temperature is impractical as the high
ﬂow stresses required to deform Ni-based superalloys
limit the size and complexity of potential component
conﬁguration. Moreover, the short annealing times are
not ideal when applied to physically large components
due to the large thermal inertia involved. Consequently,
the current approaches used for GBE are not ideally
suited for the fabrication of complex components for
propulsion and power generation applications.
For turbine engines applications where resistance to
creep deformation is not the life-limiting design
requirement, polycrystalline materials are ideal since
the elevated densities of grain boundaries that interact
with dislocations confer strength and enhance the
resistance to fatigue crack propagation. At elevated
temperatures, however, the properties of grain
boundaries vary signiﬁcantly depending on their
characteristic misorientation or degree of coherency
[26–28]. The coherency or character of each grain
boundary is deﬁned by the degree of misorientation
associated with the crystal lattices of the two neigh-
boring grains. The resulting coincident site lattice
(CSL) at the boundary may then vary signiﬁcantly
depending on the relative orientations of the grains
[28]. Low CSL boundaries, characterized by a R value
greater than 29, incorporate crystalline defects and
vacancies which, in large concentrations, promote
diffusion mechanisms at elevated temperatures. This
further leads to environmental degradation and poor
resistance to creep deformation. Conversely, high CSL
boundaries, or R \ 29, are coherent interfaces that
contain few crystalline defects which minimize the
occurrence of the mechanisms by which diffusion and
mass transport develop along interfaces. Conse-
quently, polycrystalline materials that contain high
proportions of coherent R3 twin boundaries are the
most desirable for use at elevated temperatures.
Recent studies have investigated the feasibility of
utilizing hot or warm deformation processes for GBE of
Ni-based superalloys [ 29–32]. To promote strain-in-
duced boundary migration (SIBM), a sufﬁciently large
amount of strain energy needs to be stored within the
microstructure in order to obtain high fractions of R3
boundaries upon annealing from the release of strain
energy [ 29, 33–35]. By controlling the deformation
parameters, the fraction of twin boundaries can be
optimized [29]; however, the values obtained are typi-
cally lower than those reported in investigations where
iterative cold roll—anneal methods were used for GBE.
To assess and quantify the effects of recrystallization
and texture on the grain boundary character of RR1000,
samples were cold rolled to induce various degrees of
strain and texture prior to annealing. Characterization
of both the as-deformed and annealed microstructures
provided insight into mechanisms responsible for the
formation of R3b o u n d a r i e s .

Discussion
Samples of Ni-based superalloy RR1000 were cold
rolled using two different processes (UDCR and
2DCR) in order to investigate the effects of post
deformation texture and recrystallization on the
development and stability of special R3
n boundaries.
Using two different cold rolling routes allowed for the
comparison of the effect of two types of deformation
(texture) intrinsic to conventional cold rolling and
uniaxial compression independently of the deforma-
tion temperature. Acquiring a better understanding of
the effect of deformation may potentially facilitate the
design of an optimized processing route for the forging
of Ni-based superalloys that can take advantage of the
property improvements associated with GBE.
On the effects of recrystallization
For each group of UDCR and 2DCR samples, speci-
mens that were subject to the smallest amount of
reduction, respectively, CR 5 Y and CR 5 Y–5Z,
showed a signiﬁcant improvement of the grain
boundary character of the material after annealing
when compared to the undeformed and heat treated
material, Figs. 9 and 11. However, subsequent
increases in the strain resulted in a decrease in the
density and fraction of special boundaries for a given
grain size, Figs. 9 and 11. This can be attributed to
grain reﬁnement and recrystallization in the samples
deformed at higher strains: CR 10 Y,C R1 5 Y,C R2 0 Y,
CR 10 Y–10Z where smaller grains were obtained
after deformation when compared to the starting
conditions, and recrystallization occurred following
annealing, Fig. 4. One of the reasons responsible for
this phenomenon is that increasing the degree of
deformation or strain in the sample tends to reduce
the fraction and density of the pre-existing twin
boundaries that were present in the billet material
prior to deformation, as shown in Fig. 3 and Table 2.
As the microstructure accommodates plastic defor-
mation, the grains store strain energy in the form of
geometrically necessary dislocations GNDs that can
be characterized by the high levels of intragranular
misorientations [ 43], Fig. 5c. Additionally, as dislo-
cation activity occurs during deformation, transmis-
sion of dislocation slip across R3 boundaries tends to
be extremely difﬁcult due to low static interfacial
energies [ 29, 44, 45]. This results in dislocation pile-
up along the R3 boundaries and the accumulation of
signiﬁcantly high dislocation densities at these
interfaces can introduce defects that affect the
coherency of the R3 boundary and subsequently
annihilate the characteristic attributes associated with
coherent twin boundaries. Furthermore, as the mag-
nitude of intragranular misorientations increases due

In addition to the effect on twin boundaries, the
samples that exhibited the onset of recrystallization
were found to contain particularly low fractions of R9
and R27 boundaries, as shown in Fig. 11, which
indicates little to no grain boundary interactions
during SIBM. A ratio R3/(R9 ? R27) was derived by
Randle et al. [ 46] and used in several investigations to
quantify the connectivity of the grain boundary net-
work [ 47, 48]. This ratio was calculated and the
results are plotted against the grain size in Fig. 12.
With little to no recrystallization occurring in samples
CR 5 Y and CR 5 Y–5Z after annealing, they exhibited
the lowest ratios indicating that grain boundary
interactions occurred during SIBM and led to a
highly interconnected grain boundary network with
the presence of R9 and R27 boundaries as well as the
incorporation of convoluted R3 boundaries in the
random grain boundary network, Fig. 10b. In order
to form a greater number of R3
n boundaries, grain
boundary interactions resulting from the migration of
random boundaries are necessary [ 42]. This complex
grain boundary structure formed due to twin regen-
eration as well as the migration of the grain boundary
network in a microstructure containing pre-existing
twins. Conversely, with high R3/(R9 ? R27) ratios,
the samples that exhibited partial recrystallization CR
15Y,C R2 0 Y, and CR 10 Y–10Z revealed little to no
interactions occurred during annealing. Since most of
the pre-existing twin boundaries were annihilated
during deformation, as shown in Table 2, new twins
were formed as strain energy was released during
growth of both recrystallized and pre-existing grains.
Compared to the convoluted twins that formed in the
samples where no recrystallization occurred, the
majority of twin boundaries in these samples were
distinctive as they possessed elongated and straight
boundaries that tended to bisect the grains, especially
recrystallized grains, Fig. 10c. Therefore, no interac-
tions were possible to create triple junctions R3–R3–
R9o r R3–R9–R27. Finally, it was observed that the
R3/(R9 ? R27) ratio was constant in the absence of
recrystallization meaning that the R9 and R27
boundaries were stable in the microstructure whereas
in samples CR 15 Y,C R2 0 Y,C R1 0 Y–10Z, the ratio
was found to increase indicating that the R9 and R27
boundaries disappeared with recrystallization and
grain growth in the recrystallized samples.

investigations [ 33, 50, 51]. In order to introduce a
stacking fault, the grain boundary needs to move into
a deformed region [ 50] where strain induced

expected to have a higher number of triple junctions

recent investigation showing higher values of special
grain boundaries in cross rolled samples compared to
unidirectional rolling [ 47], which shows the beneﬁt of
multiple rolling directions.
The effects of texturing and recrystallization in Ni-
based superalloy RR1000 were investigated in sam-
ples subjected to UDCR and 2DCR. The predominant
Goss texture that formed in the material following
2DCR promoted the formation of an improved grain
boundary character distribution upon annealing of
the samples when compared to that found in the
UDCR samples. Consistent with other investigations,
recrystallization had a negative effect on the forma-
tion of a grain boundary network comprised of R3
n
boundaries. The overall Goss texture of the 2DCR,
Fig. 8, was found to be similar to the texture observed
in RR1000 following high-temperature uniaxial
compression tests [ 29]. Thus, this would appear to
suggest that a modiﬁed forging operation where the
induced strains are due to high-temperature uniaxial
compression may also possess a crystallographic
texture that is amenable for GBE.
Conclusions
Based on the results and observations presented in
this study, the following conclusions can be reported:
1. After annealing, the two directions cold rolled
(2DCR) samples exhibit higher densities and
fractions of R3
n boundaries for a given grain size
and speciﬁc strain level when compared to the
unidirectional cold rolled samples (UDCR).
Therefore, a predominant Goss texture was found
favorable for the formation of R3
n boundaries.
2. The effect of varying strain or cold rolling reduc-
tions on the formation of R3 boundaries in RR1000
was assessed. A critical strain level exists above
which grain reﬁnement and recrystallization will
occur during annealing. Below this critical strain,
annealing results in SIBM with a higher degree of
interaction occurring between R3 and HABs in the
2DCR samples which resulted in a higher number
of grain boundary triple junctions. Rolling reduc-
tions above the critical strain level were not
beneﬁcial for GBE as pre-existing twin boundaries
were annihilated during deformation followed by
recrystallization and the formation of random
HABs during annealing.
3. A highly interconnected grain boundary network
containing R9 and R27 boundaries formed during
annealing of samples that did not show strong
evidence of recrystallization. These convoluted
twin structures formed due to the migration of
the grain boundary network in a microstructure
containing pre-existing twins and regenerated
twins. Whereas, only new twins were formed
during annealing of partially recrystallized sam-
ples and no interactions between twin boundaries
were possible.
