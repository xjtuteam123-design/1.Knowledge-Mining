The present study deals with the high temperature characterization of the
unconventional, lightweight AA2099 and AA2055 Al-Cu-Li alloys (den-
sity = 2.63 g/cm 3 and 2.71 g/cm3 respectively), which are widely employed for
aerospace structural components thanks to their high speciﬁc strength at
room temperature. The alloys have been characterized through tensile tests at
200/C176 C and 250 /C176 C, after different overaging heat treatments, with the aim to
simulate the variation of mechanical properties occurring in a component
operating at high temperature. At 200 /C176 C, AA2099 alloy shows equivalent or
superior performance compared with AA2055; therefore, it exhibits advan-
tages in terms of speciﬁc strength due to its lower density; T1 precipitates,
dominating AA2099 after overaging, are considered to provide effective
strengthening. The reverse occurs at 250 /C176 C operating temperature, at which
considerable improvements are offered by the combination of both X and J¢
precipitates, which are present in AA2055 matrix in all overaged conditions.
INTRODUCTION
AA2099 and AA2055 belong to the third genera-
tion of Al-Li alloys and were patented by Alcoa
respectively in 2003 and 2012. 1 Thanks to Li
addition, which is known to signiﬁcantly reduce
density while increasing Young’s elastic modulus,1-3
these alloys exhibit low density (2.63 g/cm 3 and
2.71 g/cm3, respectively4,5) combined with excellent
mechanical properties, superior to any conventional
Al alloy. This characteristic, together with the
possibility to use standard production processes,
makes them widely used for structural aerospace
applications and very attractive in any ﬁeld where
resistance and light weight are requirements, such
as in the automotive ﬁeld, where the race to boost
fuel economy is getting extremely demanding. 6
Regarding Al-Cu-Li alloys in general, Li is fun-
damental to provide a high speciﬁc strength. After
solution, quenching and artiﬁcial aging, Al alloys
with high Li content have a microstructure domi-
nated by coherent d¢ precipitates (Al 3Li), which
however have been considered mainly responsible
for the poor thermal stability of Al-Cu-Li in terms of
fracture toughness.1,7,8
Like Li, Cu additions provide both solid solution
and precipitation strengthening, since Cu is
involved in the formation of T1 (Al2CuLi) and J¢
(Al2Cu) precipitates. T1 precipitates (Al 2CuLi), in
particular, offer the major contribution to the alloy
strengthening,1,7,9-11 and recent STEM investiga-
tions conﬁrmed these precipitates are among the
most thermally stable in Al-Cu-Li alloys when
exposed at temperatures higher than 200 /C176 C.12 The
formation of T1 at the expense of d¢ precipitates (and
J¢ due to the lower Cu available) is known to be
favored by a strain ﬁeld 1,7,9,10,13-16 and high Cu/Li
ratio,13,14 making both cold stretching prior to aging
(typical T8 heat treatment) and the balance of
alloying elements two key issues to enhance Al-Cu-
Li alloy performance. Moreover, speciﬁc amounts of
alloying elements such as Zn, Mn, Zr, Mg and Ag
can be added to tailor the alloy properties: Zn
enhances corrosion resistance; Mn and Zr are added
to control recrystallization during thermo-mechan-
ical processing; both Mg and Ag provide solid
solution and precipitation strengthening, and they
are also known to increase T1 precipitation kinetics,
segregating to the precipitate interface. 1,9,17-21

Several papers investigating Al-Cu-Li microstruc-
tural features after speciﬁc heat treatments or
minor element additions are available in the liter-
ature.
17,18,22-27 Focusing on speciﬁc on AA2099 and
AA2055 alloys, characterized respectively by a low
and high Cu/Li ratio, recent investigations by the
authors
12,28 highlighted a few differences in the T83
state, namely the higher amount and density of both
T1 and J¢ precipitates and the lack of d¢ precipitates
in AA2055 compared with AA2099. Both results are
in agreement with Decreus et al. 13 who studied the
effects of different Cu/Li ratios by comparing
AA2198 and AA2196 alloys, chemically similar to
AA2055 and AA2099, respectively: after artiﬁcial
aging, no presence of d¢ was found in the higher Cu/
Li ratio alloy (AA2198); moreover, T
1 nucleation has
a lower incubation time in the AA2198 alloy,
providing a larger amount of T1 precipitates com-
pared with AA2196. This characteristic also yields a
slower coarsening rate and dissolution time of T
1
phases in AA2196 compared with AA2198 alloy
during overaging (as highlighted in Ref. 28 for
AA2099 and AA2055, respectively).
The data are further conﬁrmed by Khan and
Robinson25 who focused attention on two Al-Cu-Li
alloys plus Mg additions and Mg + Ag additions.
The addition of solely Mg (similarly to 2099) was
found to retard the formation of GP zones and
consequently J¢ precipitate formation in the silver-
free alloy, providing the presence of d¢, T
1 and
curved-shaped S¢ precipitates (Al 2CuMg) in the
peak-aged condition. In the Al-Cu-Li alloy with
Mg + Ag additions (similarly to 2055), however, the
formation of Ag-Mg and Ag-Li co-clusters was
favored, reducing the amount of Li and Mg available
to trap vacancies, therefore favoring J¢ formation
with respect to T
1. Besides J¢, T1 and S¢ precipitates,
hardly any d¢ phases were visible because of their
dissolution in favor of T1. The same is supported by
Araullo-Peters et al. 21 whose atom probe analyses
highlighted the presence of T1, J¢ and S¢ precipi-
tates, nucleating in proximity of dislocation loops, in
the peak aged AA2198 alloy, chemically similar to
AA2055; in this case, due to the high Cu/Li ratio, no
d¢ phases were observed, even in the early stage of
the aging process.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, however,
very few studies deal with the effects of overaging
on both microstructural evolution and degradation
of mechanical properties of Al-Cu-Li, and most of
them investigated the effects of thermal exposure
below 200 /C176 C, which means that their results are
hardly applicable to the study of automotive com-
ponents operating at higher temperatures. A few of
these researches are focused on the study/optimiza-
tion of aging treatments, therefore often incur in
overaging;
10,27,29,30 most of them, however, are
focused on the effects of long-term aging at low
temperature,
8,27,31-34 which occurs during the life-
cycle of aerospace components because of aerody-
namic heating. All of these studies agree on the poor
thermal stability of d¢ precipitates. In particular,
Katsikis et al. 8 investigated thermal exposure at
70/C176 C up to 1000 h; Deschamps et al. 31 analyzed the
effects of long-term aging at 85 /C176 C up to 3000 h;
Davydov et al.33 focused on the effects of soaking at
85/C176 C and 95/C176 C up to 1000 h and 300 h, respectively;
Mou et al. 34 analyzed the effects of prolonged
(7000 h) thermal exposure up to 163 /C176 C; ﬁnally,
Ortiz et al. 32 examined the effect of prolonged
thermal exposure (1000 h) up to 177/C176 C, highlighting
the signiﬁcant reduction of tensile properties, but no
attention has been given to microstructural
features.
In addition, Jabra et al.
35 showed the conse-
quences of overaging on Al-Cu-Li alloys at temper-
atures as high as 290 /C176 C, revealing a consistent
decay of tensile properties, coupled with an increase
of the elongation to failure. A brief inset on overag-
ing of AA2198 alloy is also offered by Ovri et al. 36
who reported a substantial decay of properties and
microstructural evolution after 10 h at 370 /C176 C.
Only recently, however, the effects of overaging
on both AA2099 and AA2055 in the range 200–
300/C176 C were systematically studied by the
authors12,28 through both SEM and STEM
microstructural investigations and room tempera-
ture tensile tests after thermal exposure. The
results of overaging tests seem promising; however,
mechanical testing at the operating temperature of
interest is required to completely evaluate the
possibility of expanding the range of application of
AA2099 and AA2055 at high temperature or to
determine if one of these alloys should be preferen-
tially adopted at high temperature. The present
article aims to ﬁll this gap and deals therefore with
tensile tests at 200/C176 C and 250/C176 C of both AA2099 and
AA2055 alloys. The results have been compared and
discussed, with the aim to link the difference in
mechanical properties to the difference in chemical
compositions or better to the presence of diverse
precipitates. This is also considered an essential
step to tailor the alloys’ chemical composition and
heat treatment for speciﬁc applications at high
temperature.
EXPERIMENTAL
Both AA2099 and AA2055 Al-Cu-Li alloys were
provided by Alcoa in the T83 condition (solution
treatment, quenching, 3% stretching at room tem-
perature, followed by artiﬁcial aging) in the form of
extruded bars with 85 mm and 120 mm diameter,
respectively. The chemical composition limits, pro-
vided by the supplier, are reported in Table I; it can
be noticed that the main difference between the
alloys is the dissimilar Cu/Li ratio and the relevant
presence of Ag in the solely AA2055.
Aiming to evaluate the suitability of these alloys
for high-temperature applications, tensile tests
were carried out at 200 /C176 C and 250 /C176 C in slightly,
middle and extremely overaged conditions. It is in

fact widely known that heat-treated Al alloys, when
exposed to temperatures above 200 /C176 C or more,
undergo overaging. The phenomenon consists of a
diffusion-controlled coarsening of strengthening
precipitates, which ﬁnally lose their coherency with
Al matrix, resulting in loss of strength at both room
and high temperature. The variation of hardness
during overaging treatments is able to keep track of
these microstructural changes and to quantify their
effects in terms of degradation of mechanical prop-
erties. In previous studies by the authors
12,28 a
relationship between residual hardness and resid-
ual tensile properties at room temperature was
established for both AA2099 and AA2055. In addi-
tion to overaging, however, high temperature itself
is responsible for lowered mechanical properties,
since it induces a higher mobility of disloca-
tions.
37,38 Testing the alloy at high temperature
under different overaging conditions therefore
allows fully simulating the variation of mechanical
properties in a component operating at high
temperature.
For this purpose, before tensile tests, AA2099 and
AA2055 samples were subjected to the following
overaging heat treatments, which are considered
suitable to study the mechanical properties of a
component operating up to 300 /C176 C: (1) 6 h at 215 /C176 C,
(2) 24 h at 215 /C176 C, (3) 24 h at 245 /C176 C, (4) 72 h at
245/C176 C and (5) 24 h at 305/C176 C. Previous studies by the
authors
12,28 conﬁrm that these overaging conditions
are able to produce classes of residual hardness
evenly spaced between the typical T83 hardness and
the minimum residual hardness detected at 305 /C176 C
and report the related variation of RT tensile
properties.
In particular, at least two specimens were tested
for each combination of alloy (2099–2055), test
temperature (200 /C0250/C176 C) and overaging condition
(from 6 h at 215 /C176 C up to 24 h at 305 /C176 C). The tensile
samples geometry is shown in Fig. 1. During the
overaging treatments, the sample temperature was
controlled by a K-type thermocouple, allowing
± 2/C176 C variation of the targeted temperature. As
for the tensile tests, a tensile testing machine with
threaded grips, equipped with a resistance-heated
furnace, was adopted. To make sure that a homo-
geneous temperature ﬁeld was reached in the
sample, at least 2 h of holding was required for
each test; holding time was however adjusted for
each test to reach the required test temperature at
the specimen center, where a K-type thermocouple
was installed (as indicated in Fig. 1). Finally,
tensile test parameters were deﬁned according to
the EN ISO 6892-2: 2009 standard, with a strain
rate set equal to 3.3 9 10
/C03 s/C01; proof strength
(Rp0.2) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) were
evaluated according to the EN ISO 6892-1: 2009
standard.
For both alloys, data were processed with the aim
to accurately establish the relationship between the
average values of UTS, Rp
0.2 and residual hardness
at the speciﬁc test temperature. Residual hardness
was measured by micro-Vickers hardness tests,
with 1-kg load and 10-s dwell time. For each tensile
sample, four micro-Vickers indentations were per-
formed on a section of the gauge length after
testing, providing an average of eight measure-
ments for each combination of alloy, overaging
condition and test temperature. The measured
hardness values are the result of both overaging
treatment and soaking at the speciﬁed temperature
during tensile tests; therefore, they are not directly
comparable with the hardness values reported
in
12,28 after the solely overaging treatment.
Finally, tensile test data were considered together
with the STEM and SEM microstructural investi-
gations presented in Refs. 12 and 28 to assess how
the density, size and type of precipitates affect the
alloys’ mechanical properties at high temperatures.
