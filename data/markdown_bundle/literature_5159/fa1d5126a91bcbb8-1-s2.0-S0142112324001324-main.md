1. Introduction 
Because of high strength, pearlite wires are indispensable in a lot of 
important fields, such as aerospace, marine engineering, automobile 
industry, bridge construction, etc., and have been paid much attention 
by researchers [1,2] . Embury et al. [3] and Langford et al. [4] discussed 
the effect of microstructural evolution in cold-drawn pearlite wires on its 
mechanical properties, and pointed out that the strength and the 
lamellar spacing of pearlite follows the Hall-Petch relationship. It has 
been reported that Cr element tend to reduce the lamellar spacing of 
pearlite during the lead-bath quenching treatment [5] . Joung et al. [6] 
pointed out that hot-dip galvanizing can improve the corrosion resis -
tance of pearlite wire. In addition, the effect of temperature on me -
chanical properties of pearlite wire is of interest to the scientific 
community, and many studies have covered the effect of annealing on 
mechanical properties of pearlite wire [7,8] . Zhou et al. [9] found that 
low tempering treatment at 210 
◦
C could make the cementite in as- 
drawn state pearlite wire ( ε = 2.0) transform from amorphous state to 
nanocrystalline state, thus improved the tensile strength and plasticity. 
Takahashi et al. [10] studied the mechanical properties of pearlite wire 
( ε = 4.61) after tempered at 100 – 400 
◦
C. The results showed that the 
increase of tensile strength in the first stage (100 – 150 
◦
C) was attributed 
to the pinning effect of C solute atoms to dislocation motion, and the 
decrease of tensile strength in the second stage (150 – 400 
◦
C) was due to 
the recovery occurred in ferrite. However, these studies mainly focus on 
the tempering environment below 400 
◦
C. 
The concept of multicomponent alloys or high-entropy alloys (HEAs) 
was proposed in 2004 by Cantor et al. [11] and Ye et al. [12] , respec -
tively. This was a great advancement in alloy design, completely 
different from the traditional design concept of alloy that uses one or 
two elements as major component. In the design of HEAs, multi elements 
are used as the basic main component, and a breakthrough in alloy 
properties has been achieved by completely changing the crystal struc -
ture and phase constituents in the alloy [13,14] . This unique alloy 
design concept makes high entropy alloys possess excellent mechanical 
properties, such as high strength, excellent fracture toughness and good 
phase stability [15] . The hysteresis diffusion effect in high-entropy al -
loys (component atoms need to diffuse synergistically to achieve phase 
transformation) plays an important role in phase stability [16] . In 
addition, it was thought the large lattice distortion in HEAs hinders atom

a broad development prospect in industrial manufacture. At present, the 
studies on high entropy alloy wires mainly focus on the improvement of 
drawing process [18] , analysis of deformation mechanisms [19] and 
evaluation of deformation capacity [20] . Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA 
possesses excellent plastic deformation ability, corrosion resistance and 
high-temperature phase stability [21] , which can be continuously cold-

room temperature [22] . However, there are no studies on the stability of 
microstructure and mechanical property of Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA 
wire in thermal fatigue environment at high/medium temperature. 
In extremely deformed pearlite wire, the existence of high density of 
crystal defects, decomposition of cementite and thinning of pearlite 
lamellae make it very sensitive to temperature and a simple thermal 
history will decrease the strength greatly [23] . Therefore, the stability of 
mechanical property of pearlite wire at high temperature becomes a 
huge challenge, which also limits the further application. However, the 
hysteretic diffusion effect of component atoms in HEA is expected to 
inhibit the change of microstructure of HEA wire effectively and thus to 
stabilize the mechanical properties, so that HEA wires have a certain 
application prospect in high temperature working environment. 
Metal rope is consisted of metal wires by entwisting, as few as tens, as 
many as thousands. Usually, a few wires are first entwisted into a strand, 
and then many strands are further entwisted around a core strand to 
form a rope. The fatigue behaviors of metal wires between adjacent 
wires or strands caused by fretting wear have a very important influence 
on the service life, which have been paid more attention. For examples, 
Urbi et al. [24] analyzed the failure of a wire rope (6 × 36 construction 
of high carbon steel) of ladle crane in steel making shop after nine 
months of service, and thought that improper lubrication led to fretting 
wear between the internal wires that resulted in failure of individual 
strands while the rest of them were unable to carry the tensile load in 
service. Additionally, bending fatigue was predominant when it passed 
through the sheave during which inter-strand nicking occurred. Rou et 
al [25] carried out fatigue tests of stress-corroded steel wires, and re -
ported that the fatigue life of steel wires decreased gradually with the 
increase of corrosion degree. They thought that the influence of corro -
sion degree on the fatigue life of steel wire was first sensitive and then 
insensitive. In addition, stress corrosion affected the crack initiation 
location and shortened the crack propagation depth. Hu et al. [26] 
utilized a custom fatigue test apparatus to explore the effect of addi -
tional torsional load on the fatigue characteristics and damage behavior 
of SWR (regularly replacing steel wire rope), and reported that both 
twisting and untwisting boosted the elongation of SWR but reduced the 
endurance of SWR almost linearly. They thought that the twisting had a 
greater impact on the endurance of SWR than the untwisting. However, 
because the microstructure in the extremely deformed steel wires is 
highly sensitive to thermal history at high temperature, no studies on 
thermal fatigue stability of microstructure and mechanical property of 
pearlite wire at high/medium temperature are reported. 
In some cases, the metal ropes not only bear the actions of tensile, 
bending and torsional load [27,28] , but also face the thermal fatigue 
environment during the continuous cyclic reciprocation between high 
and low temperatures. For example, in steel ladle hanging operation 
system and aircraft carrier restraining system, the ropes also need to 
withstand the frequent cycling service of low temperature to medium/ 
high temperature (400 – 700 
◦
C) to low temperature with short time. It is 
equivalent to a low-cycle thermal fatigue. Thermal cycling from high 
temperature to low temperature repeatedly belongs to a special fatigue 
behavior, because it causes the variations of microstructure and hence 
mechanical property with cycling number, and even leads to the crack 
initiation and final failure. These are just the characteristics of fatigue. 
The above service cases require the metal wires to maintain high me -
chanical stability. Thermomechanical fatigue test is the best method to 
simulate the above service cases. However, to understand the effect of 
low-cycle thermal fatigue on microstructure and hence mechanical 
property of metal wire is also important and necessary for material se -
lection and structure design of metal rope. 
In this study, the effect of thermal fatigue cycling at different tem -
peratures of 400 – 700 
◦
C on the microstructure and mechanical property 
of Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire and pearlite wire were studied. The 
mechanism of thermal fatigue behavior was discussed to provide sci -
entific basis for the safe service of Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wires in the 
thermal fatigue environment. 
2. Experiment details 
In this study, Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire with a diameter 0.45 
mm (obtained by continuously cold-drawing in lab) and pearlite wire 
with the same diameter (provided by Jiangsu falsheng R & D Center Co., 
Ltd.) were selected for thermal fatigue test. The pearlite wire was cold- 
drawn from a bar with a diameter 5.5 mm to a wire with a diameter of 
1.6 mm at room temperature, then annealed, and finally drawn into a 
wire with a diameter of 0.45 mm in the water tank (with a strain of 
2.54). Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire was continuously cold-drawn 
from a bar with a diameter of 6.34 mm to a wire with a diameter of 
0.45 mm at room temperature (with a strain of 5.29). Four samples with 
a length of 45 cm were cut from Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire and 
pearlite wire, and experienced the thermal fatigue treatment described 
as follows. Three of them were used for tensile testing and the other one 
for microstructure observation. The chemical composition of 
Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire was determined using MAX-LMF15 
spectrometer, calibrated by a GSB03-2028 – 2006-4 standard sample of 
stainless steel. The actual chemical composition (in wt. %) is as follows: 
Ni, 37.82; Cr, 18.84; Fe, 32.46; Mn 10.36; and other trace elements. The 
chemical composition of the pearlite wire is as follows: C, 0.59 – 0.66; Si, 
0.15 – 0.35; Mn, 0.30 – 0.60; S, 0.00 – 0.03; P, 0.00 – 0.03 and Fe is the 
matrix element (provided by Jiangsu Falsheng R & D Center Co., Ltd.). 
In the service cases of steel ladle hanging operation system and 
aircraft carrier restraining system, the temperature that ropes need to 
withstand is roughly estimated in the range of 400 – 700 
◦

thermal cycling belongs to low-cycle. Because the wires adopted in this 
study are very small in diameter (0.45 mm), they can be heated to the set 
test temperature and cooled to the room temperature very quickly. So, 
both the heating time and cooling time in one cycle of thermal fatigue 
was designed as 1 min, which is thought to be enough for temperature- 
driven microstructure evolution. During the loading process of ropes, 
there are no strict constrained-ends as a component with enough stiff -
ness. The rope is constrained in use and not loose during the loading 
process. However, the constrained ends are not fixed. At the same time, 
the contacts between wires and between strands provide a large number 
of micro-constrains that significantly increase the complexity of 
constraint condition. In this study, the thermal fatigue stability of 
microstructure and mechanical properties of wire from high/medium

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 1 shows the microstructure of pearlite wire and 
Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire in as-drawn state. Fig. 1 a and 1b show 
the bright-field images and dark-field images of the pearlite wire, 
respectively. In Fig. 1 a, the wider bands with bright contrast are ferrite 
and the narrower ones with grey contrast are cementite, demonstrated 
by SAED pattern inserted. The pearlite wire exhibits multi-layer struc -
ture parallel to the drawing direction, where the average lamellar 
spacing of pearlite is about 25 nm and the average lamellar thickness of 
ferrite is about 20 nm. As denoted by the white arrows in Fig. 1 a, there 
are a large number of crystal defects (simple dislocations and entangled 
dislocations) in the ferrite. Dislocations can extend in the ferrite 
lamellae but the ends of them are locked at the ferrite/cementite 
boundary [33] . In the bright-field image, the cementite lamellae have a 
thickness of about 5 nm. They seem to be continuous without cracks or 
fragmentation. However, in the dark-field image, there are many small 
bright particles (cementite nanocrystals) in cementite lamellae, as 
shown in Fig. 1 b. The lamellar structure of cementite is no longer 
continuous, indicating that the lamellar structure of cementite has been 
changed significantly during the extremely plastic deformation occurred 
in pearlite wire. This process is called nano-crystallization of cementite 
[34] . This is the foundation for the subsequent spheroidization of 
cementite in high temperature environment. The inserted SAED pattern 
in the upper right corner of Fig. 1 a illustrates that the plane spacing of 
(0 1 1), (0 0 2) and (1 1 2) of the ferrite is 0.203 nm, 0.143 nm and 0.117 
nm, respectively. In addition, the plane spacing (1 2 1) of the cementite is 
0.209 nm. The diffraction spots of cementite are very weak, which 
means that the cementite may decompose to a certain extent during 
cold-drawing deformation. At the same time, it is seen that some 
diffraction spots of ferrite show obvious tail fins and drag phenomena, 
which may be caused by the segregation of C atoms in the ferrite (due to 
the decomposition of cementite) [35] . 
Fig. 1 c and 1d show the TEM images and selected area electron 
diffraction patterns of Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire in as-drawn state. 
As shown in Fig. 1 c, HEA wire exhibits multi-layer structure parallel to 
the drawing direction. The inserted SAED pattern in the upper right 
corner suggests that the crystal plane spacing of (1 1 1), (0 0 2), (0 2 2) 
and (1 1 3) of FCC matrix phase is 0.207 nm, 0.180 nm, 0.127 nm and 
0.108 nm, respectively. In addition, fine nanoscale deformation twins 
are observed between layers, as shown in Fig. 1 d. The formation of these 
twins is to coordinate the deformation between layers.

4. Discussion 
The mechanical behavior of the pearlite wire and 
Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire is closely related to the evolution of 
microstructure. In as-drawn state, the pearlite wire exhibits a multilayer 
structure with alternating ferrite and cementite lamellae, and the exis -

dislocations from one ferrite lamella into another, unless sufficiently 
large external force drives the dislocations to pass through the cementite 
lamella. Thus, the dislocations are increasingly entangling and prolif -

[42] , so Fe35Ni35Cr20Mn10 HEA wire in as-drawn state also has the
