High temperature shape memory alloys (HTSMAs), with the 
martensitic transformation temperatures above 100 
◦
C, have a wide 
range of engineering applications particularly in aerospace, automotive 
and energy exploration industries. Ternary alloying of NiTi with Pd, Pt, 
Au and Hf has proven effective in increasing the transformation tem -
peratures [1] . Among these alloys, NiTiHf alloy is regarded as the most 
potential candidate because of its relative lower cost [ 2 , 3 ]. 
Wire is the most practical form of shape memory alloys (SMAs) when 
they are used in actuators. However, drawing NiTiHf wires is chal -
lenging due to its brittleness and poor workability [4 – 7] . Till now, there 
has been limited study on NiTiHf wires [ 8 , 9 ]. Kim et al. [10] proposed 
that adding Nb can increase the ductility of NiTiHf alloys by forming 
eutectic structure containing soft β -Nb phase in the matrix. Inspired by 
their work, subsequent relevant studies mostly focused on the me -
chanical properties and the microstructure of bulk NiTiHfNb alloys after 
different heat treatments [ 11 , 12 ]. Recently, Lemke et al. [13] reported 
the functional properties of NiTiHfNb wires, especially with 8 at.% Nb 
content. However, the evolution of the microstructure, especially the 
morphology of Nb during drawing and its effect on the functional 
properties was not elucidated.

mm diameter (Ni

by adding a fourth element Nb. Unique Nb nanowires embedded in the 
NiTiHf matrix form during the drawing process, which increases the 
yield strength of the material. This work indicates the promising prac -
tical applications of NiTiHfNb wires and provides an opportunity to 
realize the optimization of functional properties through effective 
composite structure design.

In order to assess the mechanical properties, tensile tests were per -
formed for the bulk alloy and the 0.3 mm diameter wire in martensite 
state at room temperature ( Fig. 4 a). The bulk material fails at the strain 
of 8.7% and the ultimate tensile stress (UTS) is 900 MPa, showing a 
characteristic of continuous yielding. This is similar to the tensile 
behavior of the ternary NiTiHf alloys. Continuous yielding can be clearly 
attributed to the high critical stress for the reorientation/detwinning of 
martensite and high work hardening of NiTiHf alloys [18] . Besides, the 
morphology of eutectic Nb network structure in bulk samples may also 
hinder the reorientation/detwinning process, leading to the absence of a 
stress plateau. The 0.3 mm diameter wire fractures at 954 MPa and an 
elongation of 13.4%. In comparison with bulk materials, the yield 
strength is improved and the tensile curve shows a characteristic of an 
ascending stress plateau. Prior to 2% strain, the wire shows a signifi -
cantly higher slope and a characteristic of elastic deformation. This is 
completely different from the successive yielding of the bulk material. 
As a typical deformation texture of body-centered cubic (BCC) metal 
material, Nb nanowire usually exhibits a strong 〈 110 〉 fiber texture after 
drawing [19] . The morphology changes from eutectic network to 
nanowires with preferred orientation strengthens the material and im -
proves its yield strength. Another distinctive feature is that when 
deformation is greater than 2%, the tensile curve slope of the wire is 
obviously lower than that of the bulk material and an ascending stress 
plateau can be observed. The relative high stress plateau can be attrib -
uted to a synergetic effect of the stress plateau of Nb nanowires after 
yielding and a lower martensite reorientation stress. Since the inter -
planar spacing of Nb remains unchanged after yielding [ 20 , 21 ], there 
should exist large internal stress near the Nb/NiTiHf interface, which 
could assist the martensite reorientation/detwinning process, causing 
the critical stress to drop. Basic issues like detailed load transfer process 
and the exact value of the critical reorientation stress need further 
investigation both in experiments and simulations. It should be noted 
that as opposed to the strain over-matching between B2-B19 ’ trans -
formation strain of TiNi (~7%) and the elastic strain limit of the Nb 
nanowires (4.2 – 6.5%) [ 20 , 22 , 23 ], Nb/NiTiHf is an under-mismatching 
system. Both the elastic limit of martensite and the martensitic trans -
formation strain is below the elastic limit of Nb nanowires. In addition, 
at room temperature, the lattice of BCC Nb could not match well with 
the lattice of monoclinic B19 ’ as compared with the relatively low lattice 
mismatch between Nb and cubic B2 phase in NiTi. Thus, the intrinsic 
low elastic limit of martensite (NiTiHf) and the poorly matched in -
terfaces together result in the relatively poor mechanical properties of 
NiTiHfNb wires in comparison with NiTiNb wires [20] at room 
temperature.

III (after 5200 cycles), the actuation strain increases gradually until the 
failure at 7754 cycles, which is uncommon in fatigue curves. Fracture 
surfaces from the fatigue failed samples were examined by SEM 
(Fig. 4d–f) and we consider that stage III is highly related to the pull-out 
effect of Nb nanowires. The fracture surface of bulk NiTiHf alloys is 
partly or mostly intergranular in nature and consists of large crack path 
[25]. Typical rock-candy morphology and faceted appearances suggest 
the brittle fracture mechanism. Fig. 4d shows the relatively smooth 
fracture surface in the near-surface regions, indicating a brittle fracture 
behavior. In the core region, the fracture surface shows ductile charac -
teristics indicated by dimples with different sizes (Fig. 4e,f). Damage 
accumulates at the interface during cycling (e.g. dislocations accumu -
lation at the interfaces caused by lattice mismatch). If the applied load is 
greater than the interfacial bonding force, the Nb nanowires would 
debond and be pulled out eventually. At this point, the stress at the 
interface disappears and the load is mainly carried by the nanowires. 
Another possible reason for the rise in stage III is the preferred orien -
tation in martensite. Since the intrinsic slip resistance of NiTiHf is higher 
than other SMAs [27], detwinning/reorientation is particularly difficult 
in these alloys. After numerous cycles, the actuation strain does not 
saturate even at high stress levels. On the contrary, preferentially ori -
ented martensite gradually replaces the unfavorably oriented ones. The 
increase in volume fraction of detwinned/reorientated martensite can 
also contribute to a higher strain. By effectively regulating the volume 
fraction and morphology (length and diameter) of Nb nanowires and 
improving the microwire surface quality, a better fatigue life can be 
realized in the future. The fatigue and fracture mechanism need to be 
further explored for NiTiHf/Nb composite HTSMAs, which is completely 
different from that of NiTiHf alloys.
