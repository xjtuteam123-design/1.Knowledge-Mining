Abstract
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) offers advantages such as in-situ alloying, flexible alloy design, and cost-effective 
production. Alloying refractory metals, including tantalum (Ta) and tungsten (W), is particularly promising for high-
temperature applications due to their excellent performance under extreme conditions. In this study, TaW alloys were 
in-situ alloyed via LPBF, and their microstructural, mechanical, and acoustic properties were investigated. The addition of 
W refined the grain structure from columnar to equiaxed, reducing the average grain width from approximately 140 μm 
(Ta) to 39 μm (Ta10W), and increased the compressive yield strength from 500 MPa to over 1080 MPa. These improve-
ments were confirmed through various metallurgical and ultrasonic analyses. Both longitudinal and shear-wave velocities 
increased with W content, while the anisotropy index decreased from 2.05 to 0.83%. The ultrasonic nonlinearity increased 
by 22%, consistent with the broadening of the rocking curve observed in XRD. These findings can accelerate the adoption 
of TaW alloys in high-performance industrial applications.

2.3.3 Scanning Acoustic Microscopy
SAM is a high-resolution ultrasonic imaging technique 
widely employed for characterizing internal microstruc -
tures and detecting defects in materials [19]. SAM provides 
spatially resolved acoustic images by scanning a focused 
ultrasonic beam across a sample surface. This technique is 
particularly useful for investigating high-resolution inter -
nal features, such as LOF and cracks in samples. Because 
the commonly used frequency range is tens to hundreds of 
megahertz, SAM enables precise imaging of microscale 
internal defects, although not as detailed as OM. SAM gen-
erally operates based on water immersion.
The SAM system used in this study was equipped with a 
100-MHz focused transducer (Mi-Scope, Hitachi, Japan), as 
shown in Fig. 5. Ultrasonic waves were transmitted through 
deionized water and focused on the central region of the 
sample thickness parallel to the YZ plane, specifically at 
a depth of 1.5 mm below the sample surface. The imag -
ing window was set to cover the entire region between the 
surface and bottom signals, excluding surface and bottom 
reflections, and C-mode imaging was performed. Therefore, 
the defects appearing in the C-mode images originated from 
internal defects rather than the external surface or bottom 
features. C-mode imaging generates a high-resolution pla -
nar map of the sample by laterally scanning the transducer 
while maintaining a constant focal depth.

indicates that the misorientation of the crystallites or lattice 
strains increased as the W content increased. While both 
factors contribute, the increase in FWHM is interpreted as 
a qualitative indicator of higher dislocation density, as fol -
lows: (1) the formation of a solid solution with W atoms 
introduces lattice strain, which generates dislocations; and 
(2) the strain fields surrounding these dislocations are a pri-
mary source of the non-uniform lattice strain that leads to 
XRD peak broadening [ 28]. Therefore, the observed trend 
in FWHM suggests that dislocation density increases with 
W content. These findings are consistent with the observed 
increase in β ′ with higher W content (Table 4), and the 
related explanations are discussed in subsection 3.2.2.

the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have reported 
the sound speed of TaW alloys, the relationship between 
sound speed and grain size has been widely investigated 
for various AM metals, such as titanium and 316L stainless 
steel alloys [33]. Generally, when the ultrasonic wavelength 
is significantly larger than the grain size, scattering becomes 
negligible, and thus a reduction in grain size typically leads 
to an increase in wave velocity. Consistent with the shear 
wave velocity measurements, the longitudinal wave veloc -
ity increased with increasing W content, as summarized in 
Table 4. In addition, adding W also modifies other micro -
structural features, such as dislocation density, which may 
influence sound speeds. Although an increase in dislocation 
density can reduce the effective elastic modulus through 
damping, this effect is considered minor. In this study, the 
ultrasonic wavelength is in the sub-millimeter range, which 
is comparable to the overall sample size and grain size but 
significantly larger than the nanometer-scale spacing of dis-
locations. Therefore, the influence of dislocation-related 
damping on sound speed is expected to be negligibly small.

3.1.3 Mechanical Properties
The compressive stress-strain curves of pure Ta (#1), 
Ta2.5W (#2), and Ta10W (#3) are shown in Fig. 9. The 
compressive yield strengths averaged from three indepen -
dent tests were approximately 500, 690, and 1080 MPa for 
Ta, Ta2.5W, and Ta10W, respectively, with measurement 
deviations below 3%. This increase in strength is attributed 
to the combined effects of (1) solid-solution strengthening 
from dissolved W atoms, as evidenced by XRD peak shifts; 
(2) grain-boundary strengthening (Hall–Petch effect) due to 
grain refinement with W addition, confirmed by EBSD and 
SEM observations; and (3) increased dislocation density, as 
indicated by both the broadening of XRD peaks (rocking 
curve FWHM) and the increase in the nonlinearity param -
eter β ′ . These results are comparable to the strengths of the 
TaW alloys reported in previous studies [ 12, 31]. Although 
pure Ta is well known for its high ductility, the Ta2.5W and 
Ta10W alloys also exhibited significant ductility, as no frac-
tures occurred even when the tester reached its operational 
limit (approximately 90% strain).
