ABSTRACT 
Traditional high-strength heat-resistant Mg-RE-based wrought alloys often contain a high amount of RE solutes, 
but their yield strengths at high temperatures, such as 250 
◦
C, are still less than 300 MPa. In this work, we 
developed a low-RE-alloyed Mg – 4Er-2Nd-0.6Zn-0.2Zr (wt.%) alloy with excellent high-temperature strength. 
The yield strength of this alloy at 250 
◦
C is ~335 MPa, which is higher than most Mg-RE-based wrought alloys 
with more RE solutes. 
1. Introduction 
Magnesium (Mg) alloys are promising materials for lightweight 
structures in automobile, aircraft, rail, and other industries. Commercial 
Mg alloys such as AZ and AM systems possess acceptable mechanical 
properties, but their inadequate high-temperature strength constrains 
their operating temperature below 120 
◦
C [ 1 ]. In recent years, light -
weight Mg alloy components have been expected to apply in small in -
ternal combustion engines of small machinery, such as drones, powered 
tools, and unmanned aerial vehicles, with a working temperature of 
200 – 300 
◦
C, while their heat resistance has been a matter of concern [ 2 ]. 
Rare-earth (RE) addition to Mg alloys is widely recognized as one of 
the most feasible strategies to enhance mechanical properties, notably 
elevated-temperature strength and creep resistance [ 2 – 4 ]. As expected, 
a large number of high-strength Mg-RE-based alloys have been suc -
cessfully developed by thermomechanical processing and suitable heat 
treatments, such as Mg-10Gd-5.7Y-1.6Zn-0.7Zr alloy with the yield 
strength (YS) of 473 MPa [ 5 ], Mg-11.7Gd-4.9Y-0.3Zr alloy with the YS 
of 500 MPa [ 6 ], and Mg-4Sm-0.6Zn-0.4Zr alloy with the YS of 458 MPa 
[ 3 ]. These high-strength Mg-RE-based alloys have made great progress 
significant progress and are now comparable to several 
medium-strength Al alloys. Unfortunately, there has been very little 
research on the high-strength Mg-RE-based alloys ’ high-temperature 
mechanical properties. Recently, Zhang et al. [ 7 ] reported that the 
creep resistance and high-temperature strength of Mg – Yb based alloy 
are greatly enhanced by introducing nano-spaced stacking faults (SFs). 
They found that SFs exhibited exceptional thermal stability, effectively 
preventing dislocation motions at high temperature. In addition, Zhang 
et al. [ 8 , 9 ] developed heat-resistant Mg – Er – Y – Zn – Mn series alloys 
strengthened mainly by SFs, e.g. , YS of ~320 MPa at room temperature, 
and of more than ~285 MPa at 250 
◦
C. It can be found that such 
heat-resistant Mg alloys are typically characterized by the presence of 
profuse basal plane planar defects in the grain interiors [ 7 – 9 ]. Such 
microstructure design was originally based on the consideration that 
non-basal dislocation slips are more active at high temperature, while 
basal plane planar defects enable to inhibit non-basal slips more effec -
tively. The fact can be evidenced by Jian et al. work that nano-spaced 
SFs effectively hindering non-basal dislocation slips contribute to the 
ultrahigh strength in a hot-rolled Mg-RE-based alloy [ 10 ]. It must be 
pointed out that the strengthening of SFs only functions in the grain 
interior. But in the process of high temperature deformation, with the 
exception of non-basal dislocation slips, the intergranular deformation

study aims to unveil the mechanisms behind the increased high-

designing heat-resistant Mg alloys with high strength. 
2. Experiments 
The Mg – 4Er-2Nd-0.6Zn-0.2Zr (wt.%) alloy ingot was fabricated by 
the semi-continuous casting method. The actual chemical composition 
of the alloy was examined to be Mg-4.18Er-2.34Nd-0.72Zn-0.37Zr (wt. 
%) by an inductivity coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. The 
alloy was solid-solution treated at 500 
◦
C for 10 h, then quenched in 
warm water. Before extrusion, the alloy was preheated at 300 
◦
C for 30 
min, and then was extruded at 300 
◦
C with an extrusion speed of ~0.1 
mm s
 1 
and an extrusion ratio of ~6. After extrusion, the extruded bar 
was instantly quenched in the cold water. Finally, a cylindrical bar with 
a diameter of 20 mm and a length of 500 mm was obtained. Tensile 
specimens with a gauge length of 15 mm, a width of 4 mm and, a 
thickness of 2 mm, were tested at room and high (200, 250, and 300 
◦
C) 
temperatures along the extrusion direction (ED) using an Instron 5869 
machine with an initial strain rate of 10
 3 
s
 1

tested at least for each test. The microstructure characterizations were 
mainly made by optical microscopy (OM, Olympus GX71) and trans -
mission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F). The texture of 
the extruded alloy was examined by a scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM, TESCAN VEGA 3 XMU) equipped with an Oxford HKL Channel 5 
electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) detector operating at 40 kV 
along with the measured step of 0.2 μ m. The associated EBSD data 
analysis was done in the Oxford HKL-Channel 5 software. 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloy before 
extrusion 
Fig. 1 shows the microstructure and mechanical properties of the 
alloy before extrusion. Since the alloy has been solid-solution treated for 
10 h at 500 
◦
C before extrusion, there is almost no coarse intermetallic 
compounds in the alloy. The average grain size of the alloy is ~55 μ m. 
Fig. 2 b presents the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the solid- 
solution treated alloy, revealing that the ultimate tensile strength (UTS), 
yield strength (YS), and elongation (EL) are 194 MPa, 98 MPa, and 
11.3%, respectively. 
3.2. Mechanical properties of the extruded alloy 
Fig. 2 a shows tensile stress-strain curves of the extruded sample 
under varying temperatures. The variations of mechanical properties 
with temperature are plotted in Fig. 2 b. As the tensile temperature rises, 
the strength of the alloy decreases but the elongation increases. At room 
temperature (25 
◦
C), the UTS and YS are ~418 and ~382 MPa, along 
with a linear strain hardening. The YSs are ~350 and ~335 MPa at 200 
and 250 
◦
C, respectively, both which are higher than 300 MPa, and the 
linear strain hardening also occurred with just a little decrease in slope. 
At 300 
◦
C, the strain softening is noticeable, and the YS is only ~242 
MPa. In fact, even in the majority of Mg-RE-based alloys with high RE 
addition, high-temperature strength above 300 MPa at 250 
◦
C is scarce 
[ 7 – 9 ]. As a result, this alloy exhibits exceptional high-temperature 
strength. 
3.3. Microstructure characterization of the extruded alloy

presents a typical bimodal microstructure composed of fine dynamically

elongated unDRXed grains with a high proportion of ~70%. Such a high 
proportion of unDRXed grains should be induced due to the insufficient 
deformation by a small extrusion ratio and low extrusion temperature 
[ 11 , 12 ]. Fig. 3 b – f gives EBSD analysis results. The average DRXed grains 
size is ~1.2 μ m. The inverse pole figure map referring to ED ( Fig. 3 b)

< 10 1 0 > //ED, while DRXed grains show exhibit random orientations

4. Discussion 
It is commonly accepted that the heat resistance of Mg-RE-based 
alloys is highly dependent on its RE content, such as more RE solutes 
generate a larger number of thermostable Mg-RE phase to enhance heat 
resistance [ 17 , 18 ]. Thus, to compare the high-temperature strength 
between this alloy and traditional Mg-RE-based wrought alloys reported 
previously [ 7 – 9 , 17 – 19 ], the YS at 250 
◦
C as function of the RE content 
was plotted in Fig. 6 . The majority of Mg-RE-based wrought alloys had a 
YS at 250 
◦
C of lower than 300 MPa, while only a small part of 
Mg-RE-based wrought alloys with RE addition over 12 wt% have a YS at 
250 
◦
C of more than 300 MPa, as indicated by the black background in 
Fig. 6 . For comparison, T6-treated WE54 cast alloy [ 20 ] as the classical 
heat-resistant Mg alloy and typical Al alloys including cast Al – Si alloy 
and T8-treated Al – Cu alloy [ 21 ] was also included in Fig. 6 . Cast WE54 
and Al – Si alloys cannot compare to this alloy in any way, while the YS of 
T8-treated Al – Cu alloy at 250 
◦
C is also somewhat higher than this alloy. 
As a consequence, this alloy owns satisfactory high-temperature 
strength, despite the lower RE addition. Meanwhile, it also demon -
strates that the heat resistance of Mg-RE-based alloys is not only decided 
by RE content, but the most might be microstructure characteristics. The 
following section will discuss the underlying reasons for the remarkable 
high-temperature strength in this alloy. 
First of all, fine DRXed grains contribute to a high yield strength via 
refinement strengthening according to the Hall-Petch equation [ 5 , 6 ]. 
Nevertheless, nonequilibrium DRXed grain boundaries feature high 
energy and become extremely active at high temperatures [ 16 , 20 ]. 
Thus, grain boundary sliding is one of the important deformation modes 
for polycrystalline materials at high temperatures, especially for 
ultrafine-grained alloys [ 16 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that 
solute segregation or precipitation at grain boundaries diminishes grain 
boundary energy, enabling intergranular deformation to be suppressed 
[ 4 , 13 ]. Thus, amounts of Mg
3
RE particles at DRXed grain boundaries 
may be critical in limiting grain boundary sliding during 
high-temperature tension. 
Then, this alloy characterizes a high proportion of unDRXed grains 
with a strong basal texture. Therefore, their basal slip is a hard orien -
tation during tension, implying a considerable texture hardening effect 
formed in unDRXed grains [ 4 , 11 ]. For example, due to texture 
strengthening, a high YS of over 470 MPa was achieved in the extruded 
Mg-13Gd alloy with a high fraction (~85%) of unDRXed regions [ 11 ]. In 
addition, many LAGBs, which are low-energy interfaces comparable to 
twin boundaries that act as effective barriers to dislocation motions, 
were developed in unDRXed grains. According to Chen et al. [ 22 ], 
dislocations with similar orientations to the constitutive dislocations of 
LAGBs can transmit, whilst other with higher misorientation are pro -
hibited. This suggests that LAGBs can promote the strength-ductility 
synergy. Furthermore, unDRXed grains contain a high density of resid -
ual dislocations, which interacts or tangles with newly activated dislo -
cations during deformation, resulting in dislocation hardening [ 16 ]. In 
this alloy, residual dislocations are mostly constituted by a number of 
basal-associated < c + a > dislocations. Wu et al. [ 15 ] reported that such 
basal-associated < c + a > dislocations act as substantial barriers to the 
movement of all other mobile dislocations to contribute to c-axis strain, 
resulting in a high strain hardening. In prior research, we found that 
basal-associated < c + a > dislocation is thermally stable during creep of 
200 
◦
C, favoring increased heat resistance [ 23 ]. 
Lastly but most importantly, a high density of nano-spaced SFs was 
introduced in unDRXed and DRXed grains, which contributes to high- 
temperature strength by blocking non-basal dislocations [ 7 , 8 , 13 ]. The 
high-temperature plastic deformation of Mg alloys not only triggers 
basal dislocation slip, but also non-basal dislocation slips due to a 
decrease in critical resolved shear stress [ 16 ]. The basal plane SFs in this 
case are more effective at preventing non-basal dislocation slips, which 
is so important for high-temperature strength. Jian et al. [ 10 ] reported 
that a highly textural Mg-RE-based wrought alloy exhibited a superhigh 
YS of ~600 MPa mostly owing to the fact that high density of basal plane 
SFs inhibits non-basal dislocation slips. Moreover, SFs are always 
enriched by RE solutes as mentioned before, and thus solute-segregated 
SFs similar to basal γ’ phase should have a higher elastic module than 
Mg matrix [ 7 ]. For this, we proposed a hypothesis that the grain con -
taining SFs is a hierarchical structure, with SFs severing as the hard 
girder embedded in the soft α -Mg matrix. Such heterostructure stabilizes 
intragranular deformation and resists lattice rotation except for pre -
venting dislocation slips. During deformation, the interaction between 
SFs and mobile dislocations causes a high number of dislocations to 
accumulate around SFs, greatly increasing strain hardening [ 10 ]. As 
expected, this alloy maintains a well-defined strain hardening when 
deformed at 200 and 250 
◦
C ( Fig. 2 a). Our former work indicated that 
the solute-segregated SFs possess high thermostability, dramatically 
enhancing the heat resistance of Mg – Yb based alloy [ 7 ]. As a result, 
nano-spaced SFs play a crucial role in enhancing high-temperature 
strength of this alloy. 
By the way, RE solutes have a stronger tendency than non-RE solutes 
to form local clusters that provide order strengthening, resulting in a 
stable solid solution strengthening effect even at high temperatures [ 4 ]. 
Since RE atoms are larger than Mg in atoms size, they can increase local 
order by stiffening atomic bonds and influencing local electron density, 
which is why they have a better high-temperature strengthening effect 
[ 24 ]. Mo et al. [ 25 ] asserted that the increased elevated-temperature 
strength in cast Mg-Gd-Ca alloys is due to an additional local order 
strengthening induced by the local clusters of Gd and Ca solutes. In this 
alloy, both Nd and Er solutes have a bigger atomic radius than Mg atom 
and Er has high solid solubility in Mg, so the stable solutes-hardening is 
expected to be also responsible for enhanced high strength at elevated 
temperature. 
Based on the points discussed above, we concluded that nano-spaced 
SFs in grain interiors and abundant Mg
3
RE particles at DRXed grain 
boundaries majorly account for the excellent high-temperature strength 
of our alloy. Comparing with the congeneric Mg – Er – Y – Zn – Mn alloys 
majorly strengthened by nano-spaced SFs [ 8 , 9 ], the main reasons 
responsible for the improved high-temperature strength of our alloy 
follows. (i) Reasonable alloy design: co-addition of Er and Zn is 
conducive to creating SFs during extrusion, while Nd has a low solubility 
in Mg so that enables to develop Mg-RE particles at grain boundaries by 
dynamical precipitation. During high-temperature deformation, 
non-basal dislocations are activated expect for basal dislocations, while 
basal SFs hinder non-basal dislocations more effectively. Meanwhile, 
grain boundary movement is active in the fine-grained microstructure, 
but Mg
3
RE particles at grain boundaries suppressed this behavior. In

5. Conclusion 
In this work, a low-RE-alloyed Mg – 4Er-2Nd-0.6Zn-0.2Zr (wt.%) 
alloy with excellent high-temperature strength was fabricated, with YS 
of ~382, ~350 and ~335 MPa at 25, 200 and 250 
◦
C, respectively. The 
high-temperature strength at 250 
◦
C is dramatically higher than most 
Mg-Gd-Y-based wrought alloys with at least 12 wt% RE addition. 
Microstructure characterizations show that the alloy features a bimodal 
microstructure consisting of a low proportion of submicron DRXed 
grains with random orientations and a high proportion of elongated 
unDRXed grains with a strong basal texture. The bimodal microstructure 
provides substantial refinement strengthening and texture hardening, 
contributing to the strength of this alloy. Thin SFs lamellae were 
generated within both DRXed and unDRXed grains, and a higher density 
of SFs in unDRXed grains is possibly due to dynamical solute parti -
tioning during extrusion. Nano-spaced SFs can effectively prevent non- 
basal dislocation slip, stabilize intragranular deformation and resists 
lattice rotation during high-temperature tension, which is the most 
essential factor in enhancing high-temperature strength. In addition, 
profuse Mg
3
RE particles were dynamically precipitated at DRXed grain 
boundaries during extrusion, which improves high-temperature 
strength by suppressing grain boundary migration. Meanwhile, 
unDRXed grains were divided by lots of LAGBs and saturated by residual 
< c + a > dislocations, which are favorable to increasing the alloy ’ s 
strength.
