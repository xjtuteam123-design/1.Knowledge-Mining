1. Introduction 
Since the inception of the concept of high entropy alloys (HEA) in the 
year 2004 [ 1 , 2 ], there has been increasing interest in pursuing this 
approach as a viable means for designing engineering alloys with 
potentially useful combination of properties as summarized in compre -
hensive reviews [ 3 – 9 ]. The concept is based on the following two gen -
eral relationships which describe the changes in thermodynamic 
properties when several substances are mixed together:

Evidently, as Δ S

intermetallic compounds. Therefore, the initial idea of HEA is to be able 
to design an alloy which consists of thermodynamically stable single 
phase solid-solution and not susceptible to precipitating detrimental 
intermetallic compounds such as sigma, mu and Laves phases. Based 
upon the chemistry and entropy of mixing, two definitions of HEA are 
distinguished [ 5 ]: i) an alloy consisting of five or more principal ele -
ments with concentrations between 5 and 35 at.% and may also contains 
minor elements with concentrations less than 5 at. % and ii) an alloy 
with entropy of mixing Δ S
mix 
> 1.5 R. 
In addition to the high microstructural stability of HEA, other ben -
efits include: i) increased lattice distortion because atoms with different 
sizes can compete for the same lattice site which improves the me -
chanical strength, ii) sluggish diffusion kinetics and iii) the synergistic 
effects of elemental combinations or what is referred to as the “ cocktail ” 
effect [ 7 ]. However, it is noted here that some alloys classified as HEA 
can form secondary phases other than the solid-solution one [ 10 – 13 ]. 
More recently, there has been notable interest in medium entropy alloys 
(MEA) with 2 – 4 major elements and entropy of mixing larger than R and 
smaller than 1.5 R [ 14 – 18 ]. Experiment shows that the properties of 
some MEA are either comparable to or better than those of the HEA [ 14 ].

pared from high purity feedstock (99.999%) using vacuum induction 
melting and electro-slag re-melting to produce high quality ingot.

the elevated temperature tensile strengths of annealed and aged speci -

solid-solution strengthened alloys, the changes in 0.2% yield strength 
and ultimate tensile strength ( Fig. 4 a and b respectively) are divided into 
three distinct stages [ 18 , 20 ]. At lower and higher temperatures the 
strength is observed to rapidly decrease with increasing temperature 
indicative of thermally-activated deformation mechanisms. In other 
words, barriers to dislocation motion can be overcome by thermal en -
ergy with relative ease. The two stages are separated by an intermediate 
stage where the strength becomes less dependent on temperature. It has 
been argued that elements in solid-solution provide more effective 
barrier to dislocation motion which can not be overcome by thermal 
energy alone [ 18 ]. As shown in Fig. 4 c, a one-to-one correspondence 
exists between the strength and ductility in that the ductility tends to 
increase during the lower and higher temperature regimes with insig -
nificant change during the intermediate stage. However, an in-depth 
understanding of the relevant deformation mechanisms requires sepa -
rate studies which are beyond the scope of this letter.
