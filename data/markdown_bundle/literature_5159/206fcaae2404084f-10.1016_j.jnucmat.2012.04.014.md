abstract
The Nb–1Zr–0.1C (wt.%) alloy is being considered for structural components in the proposed Compact
High-Temperature-Reactors (HTR). The present work reports on the development of 30–50 kg ingots of
the alloy in correct composition as well as technology for forming the material in various shapes. The
work deals with the deformation behavior of as-cast material at different temperatures and strain rates,
recrystallization behavior at different temperature and time and evolution of microstructures at different
processing conditions (as-cast, deformed and recrystallized). The as-cast Nb alloys were deformed up to
35% at different temperatures. The deformation results showed that the ﬂow stress of the as-cast Nb alloy
increases with increasing temperature from 800 /C176C to 1000 /C176C. Beyond 1200 /C176C, substantial decrease in
the strength of the alloy was noticed. To determine the optimum recrystallization temperature and time
for the alloy, several heat treatments were conducted by systematically varying temperature and time. It
was found that the deformed Nb alloy could be recrystallized by annealing at 1300 /C176C for 3 h. The micro-
structures of the as-cast, deformed and recrystallized samples of Nb–1%Zr–0.1%C alloy were systemati-
cally characterized by optical, electron back scattered diffraction (EBSD) and transmission electron
microscopy techniques. The Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy showed signiﬁcant differences in the microstructure after
different thermo-mechanical treatments. Microstructures of the Nb alloy showed two phases: the matrix
(bcc) phase and the carbide phase. Electron Microscopy and energy dispersive spectroscopic analyses
revealed that the carbide precipitation undergoes various phase transformations. The as-cast structure
of Nb alloy had hexagonal Nb
2C precipitates in the Nb matrix and after extrusion, the deformed micro-
structure had two types of carbide precipitates: needle and rectangular morphology precipitates. The
needle shape precipitates were of (Nb, Zr)
2C with orthorhombic structure and rectangular shape precip-
itates were of (Nb, Zr) 3C2 with hexagonal structure. After annealing, the recrystallized microstructures
showed equilibrium structure i.e. (Nb, Zr)C precipitates (fcc structure) in the Nb matrix.

1. Introduction
High temperature reactors (HTR) have been envisaged to operate
at temperature higher than 1000 /C176C for several years in unmanned
conditions. For such applications, structural materials of the reactor
must able to withstand aggressive environment like high tempera-
ture, irradiation and possible exposure of air at high temperature,
etc. Therefore, refractory metals based alloys have been proposed
for structural application in HTR. In this regard, refractory metal
Nb and its alloys due to a good combination of properties like high
temperature strength, chemical compatibility with most liquid met-
als, easy fabricability, lower density in comparison to other refrac-
tory metals, stability in nuclear environments and availability, are
potential candidates for structural application in HTR. Due to these
unique properties, Nb alloys have found many applications ranging
from structural components – in space nuclear reactors, – in high
temperature reactors, – in aero-space engine to several biologic
applications [1–3]. The strength of pure Nb is known to decrease
substantially above 1000 /C176C. Various approaches have been used to
improve the strength of Nb at elevated temperature [4–7]. The
strength of Nb alloys can be increased by adding Zr and C to Nb by
solid solution strengthening and precipitation hardening [4–7]. Ti-
tran et al. [8,9] have carried out detailed study on the creep behavior
and microstructural changes in Nb–Zr–C alloys under various condi-
tions of samples (annealed, warm worked and welded). Their studies
have shown that upon annealing, the initial carbides, Nb

Existing literature on Nb alloys shows that deformation temper-
atures for the alloys are higher than 1600 /C176C [10,11]. Detailed
microstructural analysis of thermo-mechanical treatment of Nb–
Zr–C alloys has shown that upon high temperature deformation
(/C241625 /C176C) the carbide phase undergoes dissolution and reprecip-
itation and the degree of recrystallization and average grain size
increased with increased number of extrusions [12]. The recrystal-
lization temperatures reported for the alloy are in the range of
1450–1500 /C176C. It is also a known fact that Nb alloys are susceptible
to oxidation and decarburization above 1400 /C176C [1,10]. Any heat
treatment above 1400 /C176C needs special arrangements to keep oxy-
gen concentration extremely low to avoid oxidation. In connection
with Nb and its dilute alloys, two points are worth mentioning: (i)
Nb alloys are amenable to deformation at lower temperatures.
Such an attempt will bring down the fabrication cost of the mate-
rial low as facilities already existing for other materials can be uti-
lized. (ii) Low temperature deformation in warm deform region
would store high strain energy into the material which would
bring down the temperature of recrystallization below 1400 /C176C;
making again existing facilities available for the material. This two-
fold beneﬁt – in lowering the cost of fabrication – makes this alloy
competitive with respect to other commercial alloys and provides
a cost beneﬁt edge over other refractory metals. Therefore, the
present work focuses on the establishment of a new route for
deformation as well as recrystallization of the Nb alloy at lower
temperatures which would make the fabricability of the alloy suit-
able with the existing facilities.
The present study reports on preparation of the alloy, extrusion
of Nb alloy at low temperatures, structural changes observed sub-
sequent to deformation as well as recrystallization of the Nb alloy.
Determination of optimized temperature for annealing subsequent
to warm working, effects of temperature on deformation and
chemical analysis of various phases has also been reported.
2. Experimental
2.1. Melting of the Nb–1%Zr–0.1%C alloy
Nb is a refractory metal having high melting point and high
reactivity with oxygen and nitrogen. Therefore, melting of Nb
was carried out using electron beam (EB) melting facility under
high vacuum conditions. Fig. 1 shows the ﬂow sheet for the prep-
aration of the Nb alloy with desired composition. In order to obtain
low level of impurities electron beam reﬁned pure Zr and Nb met-
als and spectra pure graphite powder were used in the charge. In
order to ensure chemical homogeneity, in particular with respect
to carbon, throughout the length of ingot, several experiments
were carried by placing charge by different methods; e.g., (i) plac-
ing of high purity C powder in folded niobium sheets, (ii) placing
high purity C powder in a drilled Nb bar or (iii) placing the pure
Zr strips below the Nb charge. Several experimental trials were car-
ried out to ensure that the composition of the alloy is within the
desired range. For this purpose samples for chemical analysis were
collected at least from six different places of the ingot along its
length. Based on the variation in the chemical composition, exper-
imental parameters like; extraction rate, current density and vac-
uum level, etc.
A seed ingot for retraction head was developed by carefully pre-
paring the charge speciﬁcally for this purpose. The EB melted alloy
was hydride, dehydrated and remelted to homogenize the compo-
sition of the alloy. The composition of the alloy was veriﬁed by
combustion extraction method for carbon and inductively coupled
plasma method for zirconium and niobium analysis. Typical chem-
ical composition of the alloy is shown in Table 1 .
2.2. High temperature deformation of Nb alloy
2.2.1. Plastodilatometery
Deformation behavior of the alloy was assessed by carrying out
compression testing of as-cast Nb specimen cylinders (6 £ and
7.5 mm l) in plasto-dilatometer. Deformation was carried out at
different temperatures ranging from 800 /C176C to 1300 /C176C at 0.1 strain
rate (1 s
/C01) up to 35% deformation. During experiment vacuum
was maintained at 10 /C04 torr.
2.2.2. Hot hardness
In addition some of the samples were subjected to hot hardness
tests. Before starting the hot hardness experiments, the instrument
was calibrated using a standard (Cu: SRM: National Bureau of Stan-
dards, USA) sample. Five indentations were taken on the standard
sample using 1 N load. The diagonals were measured on the T.V.
screen using the telecomparator. The hardness obtained was found
to be within ±0.5% of the actual value.

2.2.3. Extrusion experiments
Extrusion experiments were carried out on hollow billets. For
this purpose solid ingots were machined and drilled to make an in-
ner diameter (ID). These hollow billets were mechanically jacketed
with seamless copper tubes of thickness 1.6 mm both on OD (outer
diameter) and ID side. Copper jacket on the billet has served three
purposes (i) prevention of the billet from excessive oxidation, (ii)
action as lubricant during extrusion and (iii) prevention any direct
contact of Nb-alloy with tooling surface. Billets were preheated
and soaked at a temperature of 810 /C176C for 2.5 to 3 h before extru-
sion. The selection of temperature was based on the input obtained
from the compressive testing. This issue will be discussed in the la-
ter part of the paper. A conical die with semi die angle of 60 /C176was
used to reduce the formation of dead metal zone inside the con-
tainer. Suitable shrinkage allowances were taken into account
while deciding the dimensions of the mandrel and the die with re-
spect to the cold dimension of the tubular product. Before the
transfer of the preheated billet into the extrusion press container,
the container surface, the die and mandrel were lubricated with
oil based graphite lubricant. Extrusions of the Nb-alloy billets were
carried out with extrusion ratio nearly at 6:1–5:1 and mean strain-
rate of 3.0–5.25 s
/C01. Subsequent to extrusion copper jacket was re-
moved. As-extruded tubular blank is shown in Fig. 2 . The tubular
blanks were ultrasonically inspected using pulse echo immersion
technique in a spiral seam with a suitable pitch of 20% overlap cov-
ering 100% of the surface. Defect indications not exceeding those
obtained from V-notches having a depth of 5% of the wall thickness
was taken as standard for qualiﬁcation.
In order to determine the optimum temperature of recrystalli-
zation, extruded samples were subjected to various heat treat-
ments starting from 1000 /C176C to 1500 /C176C for different time periods
under argon atmosphere followed by furnace cooling. Temperature
of the furnace was maintained within ±3 /C176C. Microstructural inves-
tigations and composition of phases in the alloy were carried out
using optical, scanning and transmission electron microscopy
(JEOL 2000FX, Technai 20) techniques. For optical metallography,
the samples were mirror polished and subsequently etched by an
acid solution containing HNO 3, HF and H 2SO4 in the ratio of
1:1:3. TEM samples were prepared by the ion milling technique.
Prior to TEM investigation, samples were plasma cleaned to avoid
any contamination. For determining the structure of the carbide
phases, selected area electron diffraction patterns (SAEDP) were re-
corded at different zone axes. Energy dispersive spectrometer
(EDS) attached with TEM has been used to get the composition
of different phases. Orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) was
also used to evaluate the extent of recrystallization in the heat
treated samples.
3. Results
Typical as-cast microstructure of the Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy is
shown in Fig. 3 . Large grains ( /C24mm) in the alloy were due to pro-
longed high temperature exposure during electron beam melting.
A magniﬁed image of a grain showed uniform distribution of nee-
dle shape precipitates in the matrix phase ( Fig. 3 b). Analysis of
SAED patterns from the needle phase showed that the precipitate
phase has the hexagonal structure with lattice parameters;
a = 3.1 ± 0.2 Å and c = 5.0 ± 0.2 Å. EDS has been carried out on more
than ten carbide precipitates. Detailed EDS analysis of the phase
showed that it contains Nb-93.06 ± 1.2 wt.%, C-6.916 ± 1.8 wt.%
and small unquantiﬁable percentage of Zr. Based on this informa-
tion the precipitate phase was identiﬁed as the Nb
2C phase.
Stress–strain plots and ﬂow curve of as cast samples are shown
in Fig. 4 a and b. As may be noticed from Fig. 4 b the ﬂow stress of
the alloy remains above 200 MPa up to 1300 /C176C. In the temperature
range from 600 to 800 /C176C the ﬂow stress dropped from 268 MPa to
241 MPa but between 800 and 1000 /C176C it increased up to 290 MPa
and subsequently exhibited a sharp drop to 200 MPa for testing
temperature higher than 1000 /C176C. Hot hardness tests conﬁrmed
this pattern of sharp rise in hardness for temperatures higher than
800 /C176C( Fig. 5). Reason for such increase in the strength of the alloy
was later established as the precipitation of additional carbides at
high temperatures.

3.2. Optimization of recrystallization parameters
In order to recrystallize the deformed samples, several heat
treatments were carried out systematically by varying tempera-
ture and time of annealing treatments. DSC experiments to estab-
lish recovery temperatures turn out futile as above 1000 /C176C, as the
surface oxidation of Nb becomes signiﬁcant even under pure Ar
atmosphere and exothermic peak shown by the sample could be
due to surface oxidation. Under such condition, recrystallization
temperature of the deformed alloy was determined by detailed
heat treatments and microstructural examination. Temperatures
of heat treatment were selected in the range of 0.5–0.6 T m of pure
Nb (1100–1400 /C176C). The idea behind the selection of this tempera-
ture range was to produce strain free equiaxed grains with uniform
grain size distribution and at the same time avoid substantial grain
growth during annealing. These heat treated samples were subse-
quently characterized by OIM and TEM. Fig. 13 shows few of the
OIM micrographs of the heat treated samples at various time and
temperature. From this study, it was observed that for tempera-
tures below 1200 /C176recrystallization of the alloy was not possible
even after annealing up to 24 h. The annealing treatment of 3 h
at 1300 /C176C, on the other hand, produced nearly a fully annealed
microstructure. In order to quantitate the percentage of recrystal-
lization, GOS (grain average misorientation spread) has been calcu-
lated from the OIM images. A large value of GOS indicates that
grains are under strain and they are not fully recrystallized. A fully
recrystallized grain will have low GOS (<2 /C176). Based on this criterion
percentage of recrystallization has been estimated and shown in
the last column of Table 2. This shows that 96% of the grains pres-
ent in the samples annealed at 1300 /C176C for 3 h has low GOS,
whereas other samples heat treated at 1200 /C176C have higher GOS.

4. Discussion
Carbides were considered the only suitable interstitial com-
pounds capable of not only providing precipitation strengthening
but also of satisfying the other alloy properties criteria, in particu-
lar minimal susceptibility to liquid–metal corrosion [10,13]. The
thermodynamic stability of various carbides to reduction by nio-
bium was reviewed and it was concluded that ZrC, HfC, ThCa,
TaC, Ta 2C and TiC should be relatively stable in a niobium matrix
at 1315 /C176C. Of this group, zirconium and hafnium carbides were
the most suitable [10,13]. From the view point of nuclear proper-
ties Hf carbides are not suitable. Therefore development of the al-
loy was centered on the formation of Zr carbides. It has been
shown that the Zr/C atomic ratio of the alloys containing approxi-
mately 3 at.% zirconium exhibits optimum mechanical properties
[10,13,14].
In the Nb–1%Zr–0.1%C alloy, during melting due to prolong
heating homogeneous distribution of alloying additions was
achieved. The formation of the Nb 2C carbide phase occurred during
cooling and probably at low temperatures. It has been shown that
the formation of Nb 2C can be suppressed by rapid quenching such
as quenching in liquid tin at around 327 /C176C [15]. Therefore, upon
heating above 800 /C176CN b 2C will become unstable with respect to
other phases. This argument is based on the fact that in the Nb–
1Zr–0.1C alloy heat treatments lead to the decomposition of the
Nb2C carbides into a phase mixture of carbides [16]. One signiﬁ-
cant aspect of this transformation is that the zirconium distribu-
tion does not remain homogeneous. Electron probe microanalysis
has shown that zirconium concentration increases signiﬁcantly in
the vicinity of Nb 2C carbide phases [17]. The exact reason for such
behavior of Zr is still not established, but could be attributed to the
stress effects experienced by misﬁt of larger zirconium atoms in
the Nb matrix. In the present case also, subsequent to extrusion
the carbides showed substantial increase in the Nb 2C carbides.
Such accumulation of Zr atoms paves the way for the formation
of other carbide phases. Farkas et al. [16] have carried out thermo-
dynamic analysis of the carbide phase forming in the Nb–1%Zr–

5. Summary
In the present study, following points have been established:
1. The Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy was successfully prepared and extruded
at 800 /C176C temperature. In order to ensure successful extrusion
of the product, hollow billet extrusion with the support of a
mandrel was carried out.
2. In order to achieve uniform grain size of nearly 30 lm in the
recrystallized Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy the optimized heat treatment
parameters for this alloy were identiﬁed as 1300 /C176C for 3 h.
3. The microstructural investigations of the samples of the Nb–
1Zr–0.1C alloy under as-cast, extrusion and annealing has
shown that:
a. The as-cast microstructure comprised a two phase micro-
structure where needle type of Nb 2C precipitates was dis-
tributed in the matrix phase.
b. In the deformed sample presence of two types of precipi-
tates- one with needle morphology ((Nb, Zr) 2C, orthorhom-
bic structure) and other with rectangular morphology ((Nb,
Zr)3C2, hexagonal structure) – was observed. The increase in
the volume fraction of carbide appears to be responsible for
increase in the strength of the alloy in temperature range of
800–1000 /C176C.
c. The recrystallized microstructure of the alloy showed that
most of the prior existing carbides were dissolved and a
new fcc carbide phase ((Nb, Zr)C) precipitates out in the
Nb matrix.
