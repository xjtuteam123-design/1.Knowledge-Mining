Creep Behaviors of Two Near-Eutectic Al-Si-Cu-Mn
Heat-Resistant Alloys Containing Dendritic Mn-Rich
Primary Phase or Modiﬁed Star-Like Cr-Mn-Rich
Primary Phase
SOBAN MUDDASSIR DAR, HENGCHENG LIAO, and BAOJUN ZHAO
In this study, the creep responses of two near-eutectic Al-Si-Cu-Mn alloys, Al-12wt pctSi-4 pct-
Cu-1.2wt pctMn alloy (Alloy-1, containing dendritic Mn-rich primary phase) and Al-12wt pct-
Si-4 pctCu-2wt pctMn-1wt pctCr alloy (Alloy-2, containing star-like Cr-Mn rich primary
phase), were investigated from 448 K to 523 K at 40 to 70 MPa applied stress. Results show
that with 100 hours of exposure at 448 K/40 to 70 MPa, true steady-state creep was not attained.
However, at 473 to 523 K/40 to 70 MPa, both the alloys showed a ﬁxed creep rate establishing
steady-state creep. The creep curves of the studied alloys illustrate that Alloy-1 exhibits lower
creep rates and less creep strains than Alloy-2 in each combination of temperature and applied
stress. Considering the creep rates and total creep strains after 100 hours of exposure, Alloy-1
possesses much better creep resistance than Alloy-2, even though the high-temperature strength
of Alloy-2 is higher than that of Alloy-1 at 448 K and 523 K. Higher strength at high
temperatures does not mean high creep resistance at high temperatures. At low to moderately
high temperatures (0.48 to 0.53 Tm where Tm is the equilibrium melting point of pure Al in K)
and applied stress of 40 to 70 MPa, the creep mechanisms in Alloy-1 and Alloy-2 are similar and
diﬀusion is the rate-controlling process.

I. INTRODUCTION
Al-Si-Cu-based casting alloys have been widely used
for manufacturing automotive components in the past
few decades because of their good cast-ability, low
coeﬃcient of thermal expansion, good corrosion resis-
tance, and high speciﬁc strength compared to cast
iron.[1–3] Al-Si-Cu alloys are used to produce structurally
complex components such as engine blocks and cylinder
heads. However, with the increasing importance of
energy-eﬃcient vehicles, the demand for superior
heat-resistant alloys increases daily. Many attempts have
been made to promote the formation of thermally
stable phases in these alloys. For example, it is observed
that Ni addition in Al-7Si-Cu alloys promotes the
formation of T-Al 9FeNi, c-Al7Cu4Ni, d-Al3CuNi, and/
or e-Al3Ni phases, and improves the high-temperature
strength.[4–7] Among these, d-Al3CuNi phase is found to
be the best for strength improvement. [8] Mn addition is
also found to have a positive eﬀect on creep resistance. It
is revealed that Mn addition in Al-Si-Cu-Mg-Ni alloy
imparts ductility to brittle T-Al9FeNi and prevents its
early cracking during creep, thereby improving creep
resistance.[9] Mo addition in Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy forms a
signiﬁcant quantity of a-Al (Fe,Mo)Si coarse particles in
the inter-dendritic regions of the Al-matrix and sup-
presses the formation of brittle b-Al5FeSi intermetallic
phase.[10] The combined addition of Mo and Mn
decreases the size of a-Al (Fe,Mo)Si phase particles
and induces uniform distribution. [11,12] Recently,
near-eutectic Al-Si-Cu alloys have received much atten-
tion. The addition of Ni to these alloys promotes the
formation of similar kinds of Ni-bearing phases as in
hypoeutectic alloys stated earlier. However, as the Cu
content increases in these alloys, Ni-bearing phases
transform from Al 3Ni ( e-phase) or Al 3CuNi ( d-phase)
to Al 7Cu4Ni ( c-phase). Their morphology also changes
from short-strip to reticular and then to annular or
semi-annular shapes. It is observed that the e-Al3Ni
phase determines the fatigue life of these alloys and its
deboning from the Al-matrix severely deteriorates the
fatigue properties. [13–15] The combined addition of
0.5 wt pct Cr and 0.8 wt pct Fe in Al-13Si-Cu-Ni-Mg
alloy promotes the formation of a-Al (Fe, Cr)Si phase. It

Recently, Liao et al . [17–20] developed a novel heat-resis-

positive eﬀect on high-temperature strength. However,

ment in high-temperature strength (ultimate tensile
strength of 106 MPa at 350 /C176 C).[22]
From an industrial point of view, alloy design with an
optimum combination of properties is a challenging
task. There is no consensus on a single property or
parameter that could be used to evaluate the high-tem-
perature performance of an alloy. High ductility and
high tensile strength are two important indexes to
prevent thermal fatigue; however, creep resistance is
also required for good performance. [23] Only a few
investigations [24,25] have been conducted to understand
the creep behavior of Al-Si alloys containing intermetal-
lic compounds. In this study, the creep behaviors of two
near-eutectic Al-Si-Cu-Mn heat-resistant alloys (con-
taining dendritic Mn-rich primary phase and modiﬁed
star type Cr-Mn-rich primary phase) are studied by
creep test at 448 to 523 K/40 to 70 MPa. Furthermore,
creep mechanisms and microstructural changes are
discussed to understand the performance of alloys at
high working temperatures.
II. MATERIALS AND METHODS
Two near-eutectic Al-Si-Cu-Mn alloys (Al-12wt pct-
Si-4 pctCu-1.2wt pctMn and Al-12wt pctSi-4 pct-
Cu-2wt pctMn-1wt pctCr) were prepared using pure
aluminum ingot, Al-20wt pctSi, Al-20wt pctCu,
Al-10wt pctMn, and Al-10wt pctCr master alloys as
raw materials. After processing, the melt was poured at
720 /C176 C into an iron mold (with a plate-like cavity of
170 9 100 9 20 mm 3, pre-heated at 250 /C176 C for 5 h).
The ﬁnal chemical composition of the studied alloys was
analyzed by MAXx LMF15 spark emission spectrom-
eter, as listed in Table I.
Samples for microstructural observation were cut
from the central part of plate-like castings and
heat-treated at 510 /C176 C for 5 hours + aging at 165 /C176 C
for 6 h. Microstructure was observed using OLYM-
PUS BX60M metallographic microscope and Sirion
200 Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope
(FESEM) equipped with a GENESIS 605 EDS detec-
tor. Tensile tests were performed for samples with a
gauge size of 18 9 3 9 3m m 3 on a CMT5105 machine
with a rate of 2 mm/min at 175 /C176 C (448 K) and 250 /C176 C
(523 K). The mechanical property data were taken as
the average of three samples. Creep behavior of the
heat-treated alloys was examined on an RD2 creep
testing machine up to 100 hours with applied stress of
40 to 70 MPa at 175 /C176 C (448 K), 200 /C176 C (473 K),
225 /C176 C (498 K), and 250 /C176 C (523 K). These tempera-
tures had a range of 0.48 Tm to 0.56 Tm of pure Al
where Tm is the absolute melting point of aluminum.
The samples for creep tests were prepared with a gauge
size of 15 93.592m m 3. Before the creep experiment,
samples were soaked at the desired temperatures for at
least 2 h. During the experiment, creep deformation of
samples was measured using a Linear Displacement
Transducer (LDT) with an accuracy of 0.001 mm. The
test temperature was controlled in the range of ± 2 /C176 C
during the creep tests.
III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Microstructure Before Creep Exposure
Figures 1(a) and (b) shows the optical micrographs of
as-cast Al-12wt pctSi-4 pctCu-1.2wt pctMn alloy (Al-
loy-1) and Al-12wt pctSi-4 pctCu-2wt pctMn-1wt pctCr
alloy (Alloy-2). In Alloy-1, the Mn-rich primary phase is
present as dendrites or rods (as can be seen in
Figure 1(a)). Previous investigation [17] reveals that in
Al-12wt pctSi-4 pctCu-xMn alloys two Mn-rich disper-
sants (Al 20Cu2Mn3 and Al 15Mn3Si2) are precipitated
during solution treatment. Al 20Cu2Mn3 phase disper-
sants possess rod and/or particle-like morphologies with
a base-centered orthorhombic structure ( a = 2.34 nm,
b = 1.32 nm, = 0.75 nm) while Al 15Mn3Si2 phase dis-
persants have particle-like morphologies with body-cen-
tered cubic structures ( a = 2.26 nm). The precipitation
of these dispersants is signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by solu-
tion treatment temperature and time. As the Mn content
increases in these alloys, the as-cast microstructure
consists of two competitive Mn-rich primary phases and
the mechanical properties do not improve further. The
two competitive phases are: (i) Al 13Mn4Si8 with lath-like
growth and (ii) Al 15Mn3Si2 with dendritic growth. [19,20]
These are seen in Figure 1(a), and have an average
length of 200 to 250 lm.
The detrimental eﬀect of excessive Mn ( ‡ 2 wt pct) is
reduced by the addition of Cr, which modiﬁes the size
and morphology of Mn-rich primary phases (by forming
Cr-Mn-rich primary phase). In Alloy-2, the Cr-Mn-rich
phase has star-type morphology with an average size of
approximately 50 to 100 lm (as seen in Figure 1(b)).
In Alloy-1 and Alloy-2, the precipitation behaviors
during solution formation and aging processes are
similar. The diﬀerence between them is the size and
morphology of the primary intermetallic compounds.
Alloy-1 contains a large dendritic or rod-like Mn-rich
primary phase and Alloy-2 contains a small star-like
Cr-Mn-rich primary phase. The mechanical properties
of two alloys at 448 K and 523 K are summarized in
Table I. Both the alloys (containing Mn-rich phase and
Cr-Mn-rich phase, respectively) exhibit superior
high-temperature strength compared with hypoeutectic
and other near-eutectic Al-Si-Cu alloys, of which
Alloy-2 (containing Cr-Mn-rich phase) has better
strength at 448 K and 523 K.

B. Creep Rate and Creep Strain
Typical creep strain vs time plots for materials show
the primary regime with the tendency towards a
secondary creep regime. In the primary regime, the
creep rate decreases with time until it reaches a
minimum value and does not decrease further. This
minimum value of creep rate marks the beginning of a
constant/steady-state creep regime. Figures 2(a)
through (d) and 3(a) through (d), respectively, show
the experimental creep curves (creep strain vs time
plots) of Alloy-1 and Alloy-2 under diﬀerent values of
applied stress and temperature. Creep rates determined
from these curves are summarized in Table II. These
values are used to calculate the activation energies and
to construct the constitutive equations. The compar-
ison of experimental data of Alloy-1 and Alloy-2 in
Table II shows that the variable amplitude of the
creep rate is signiﬁcantly increased with the applied
stress.
It is also observed from creep strain vs time plots that:
for Alloy-1, at 523 K/70 MPa the creep curve enters
into the third stage (tertiary creep regime) at 4400 min-
utes and then the sample fails quickly. For Alloy-2, at
523 K/60 MPa and 523 K/70 MPa the creep curves
enter into the third stage at 4800 and 2800 minutes,
respectively. In addition, inspection of the curves
suggests that both the alloys exhibit the primary regime
with a tendency towards the secondary creep stage.
However, when the log creep rate (on Y axis) vs creep
strain (on X axis) plots are drawn to describe creep
rate-creep strain evolution (shown in Figures 4(a)
through (d) and 5(a) through (d) for Alloy-1 and
Alloy-2, respectively), the understanding is diﬀerent.
Figures 4(a) and 5(a) show the log creep rate vs creep
strain plots at 448 K for Alloy-1 and Alloy-2, respec-
tively. It is seen that at 448 K as the creep strain
increases the creep rate continues decreasing exponen-
tially. This indicates that both the alloys have not yet
reached a stable state at this temperature. Thus, the
actual creep rate might be lower than that calculated
from the creep strain vs time graphs and both the alloys
show excellent creep resistance. Figures 4(b) through (d)
and 5(b) through (d) show the creep rate (logarithmic
scale) vs creep strain at 473 K to 523 K for Alloy-1 and
Alloy-2, respectively. In this temperature range, creep
behaviors of the studied alloys are diﬀerent from that at
448 K. Evidently, both the alloys show a ﬁxed creep rate
with creep strain (horizontal linear region in the graphs).
It demonstrates that steady-state creep rate is attained
for a reasonable time. However, at 523 K/60 to 70 MPa
the curves again start showing transient behavior. This
discussion brieﬂy shows that at low temperature (448 K)
steady-state creep rate cannot be strictly claimed under
40 to 70 MPa of applied stress; however, at moderately
high temperatures (473 to 523 K) and 40 to 70 MPa of
applied stress the creep responses of the two alloys
attained the steady-state condition for reasonable time.

Considering the observed creep rates and total creep
strains, it is concluded that both Alloy-1 and Alloy-2
demonstrate good creep resistance at 448 K by exhibit-

strongly imply that a high strength at high temperature
does not correspond to high creep resistance at high
temperature. This overturns the traditional understand-
ing and is the reason for further study. For components
operating at high temperatures, creep-damage is the
most common mode of failure rather than severe
overloading. Thus, to evaluate the high-temperature
performance of heat-resistant alloys, the creep resistance
at high temperature should be given much more
importance than the tensile strength alone at high
temperature.

size (as compared to the dendritic Mn-rich-phase in
Alloy-1), more load is transferred to them, which results
in their severe cracking and pore formation. A similar
mechanism is observed in a near-eutectic piston alloy in
a previous study. [38] More severe cracking and pore
formation in Alloy-2 is the main reason for the higher
creep rate in Alloy-2.
From the above discussion, the following conclusion
is drawn: at low to moderately high temperature (0.48 to
0.53 Tm of pure Al) under an applied stress of 40 to
70 MPa, diﬀusion creep is the rate-controlling creep
mechanism in both alloys (Alloy-1 and Alloy-2). How-
ever, beyond 0.53 Tm creep behavior is largely deter-
mined by the load-bearing capacity of reinforcing phases
(Mn-rich-phase in Alloy-1 and Cr-Mn-rich-phase in
Alloy-2).

IV. CONCLUSIONS
Creep behaviors of Al-12wt pctSi-4 pct-
Cu-1.2wt pctMn alloy (Alloy-1) and Al-12wt pct-
Si-4 pctCu-2wt pctMn-1wt pctCr alloy (Alloy-2) are
investigated from 0.48 Tm to 0.56 Tm (Tm= absolute
melting point of aluminum) under 40 to 70 MPa of
applied stress and following conclusions are drawn:
1. At 448 K, both Alloy-1 and Alloy-2 do not estab-
lish a stable creep-state at 40 to 70 MPa applied
stress even after 100 hours of creep exposure.

3. Considering the creep rate and total creep strain,
Alloy-1 is found to be more creep resistant than
