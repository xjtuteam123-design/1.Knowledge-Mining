ABSTRACT
Refractory High Entropy Alloys (RHEAs) are advanced materials suitable for high-temperature applications due 
to their remarkable properties, such as excellent strength, ductility, wear resistance, among others. In this study, 
the effect of temperature on the mechanical properties of the nanocrystalline bcc HfNbTaZr RHEA is explored 
under uniaxial loading using molecular dynamics simulations. Temperatures and grain sizes in the range of 
100 – 1200 K and 5 – 17 nm are considered here. Our results showed that the highest yield strengths and flow stress 
were achieved in the 10 – 15 nm range for grain sizes, where the transition from Hall-Petch (HP) to the inverse 
Hall-Petch (iHP) effect was clearly observed at low temperatures, with a weaker grain size dependence in the HP 
regime at high temperatures. The MD results align extremely well with a scaling model for the elastic modulus as 
a function of temperature, and with a strength model which have been extensively employed to understand 
experimental results up to high temperatures, but the reference strain-rate has to be adjusted to take into account 
the large values for atomistic simulations. These models are typically based on relatively simple scenarios, like 
the motion of single straight dislocations, either edge or screw, and lack of dislocation junctions and twinning. 
Here, deformation includes both edge and screw dislocations, dislocation junctions, grain boundary plasticity, 
twinning, etc., but the general trend of smooth softening with temperature is observed despite the complex 
scenario. Some models assume that plasticity in RHEAs would be controlled by edge dislocations but, under the 
simulated conditions, there are contributions from both edge and screw dislocations. In addition, we quantify 
twin volume-fraction and find a transition from twin-dominated deformation to dislocation-dominated defor -
mation as temperature increases, with twinning increasing for larger grains. Our results provide quantification of 
the mechanical properties and plasticity of a nanocrystalline bcc RHEA up to high temperatures, and the pro -
posed adaptation of a strength model allows connection between typical experiments and high strain rate 
simulations.
1. Introduction
The softening induced by temperature effects is known as a limiting 
factor in materials that need to be overcome in order to address several 
technological issues [ 1 – 3 ]. For instance, metals for high-temperature 
wear are necessary to withstand high-speed sliding in the automotive 
and machinery industries [ 4 , 5 ]. In the field of energy, temperature- 
resistant materials are necessary for both energy storage [ 6 ] and pro -
duction [ 7 ]. Additionally, the development of next generation nuclear 
reactors requires refractory materials capable of withstanding both 
extreme stress and temperature to ensure sustainable nuclear fusion 
energy [ 8 , 9 ]. In addition to advances in superalloys and refractory

In recent years, Refractory High Entropy Alloys (RHEAs) have 
emerged as promising materials due to their high hardness, strength, 
and creep-resistant over a wide range of temperatures [ 10 – 13 ]. More -
over, these properties can be further exploited by tuning the chemical 
composition of RHEA, allowing for the exploration of a broad range of 
outstanding mechanical properties [ 14 , 15 ], with the characteristic 
feature of having elevated melting points [ 16 – 18 ]. RHEAs composed of 
VNbMoTaW and NbMoTaW were proposed by Senkovet al. [ 16 , 17 ] 
showing yield strengths higher than 400 MPa for temperatures above 
1900 K. Feng and coworkers [ 19 ] reported that the CrMoNbV RHEA 
displays strengths of 1000 MPa for temperatures closer to 1300 K. 
Recently, AlTiVCoNi low-density RHEAs with L2 precipitates exhibited 
a strength of 1.1 GPa at temperatures of 1100 K. [ 20 ].
There is a growing interest in designing materials with excellent 
mechanical properties at high temperatures. [ 13 ] Several experiments 
and analytical models have been developed to unveil the softening 
mechanism of high-temperature bcc RHEAs. Varvenne and Curtin [ 21 ] 
proposed an analytical model to describe the temperature-mediated 
softening of fcc HEA in terms of the activation energy for dislocation 
motion. Recently, Maresca and Curtin [ 22 ] introduced a strengthening 
mechanism for bcc solid solution alloys at high temperatures, where 
strength is mediated by the athermal contribution of edge dislocations. 
Alternatively, Rao et al. [ 23 ] suggested a screw dislocation-mediated 
mechanism, where the strengthening at high temperatures was attrib -
uted to the formation of jogs resulting from kink-kink interaction in 
screw dislocations. Based on dislocation dynamics (DD) and Molecular 
Dynamics (MD) simulations, Zhou et al. [ 24 ] indicated that a mecha -
nism relying solely on screw dislocation cannot explain the strength of 
bcc alloys at high temperatures. Instead, they proposed a cross-kink 
contribution that slows the motion of screw dislocations.
MD simulations have been widely used to describe thermal and 
mechanical properties of alloys. Several works have discussed the

Although there is an atomistic understanding of fcc HEAs, studies on bcc

temperature. In addition, they reported that Zr-V clusters promoted

In this study, we address the limited knowledge on bcc HEAs by 
investigating the impact of temperature on the mechanical behavior of 
nanocrystalline bcc HfNbTaZr RHEAs through MD simulations. Our 
analysis includes the quantification of mechanical properties and plastic 
deformation in samples with an average grain diameter ranging from 5 
to 17 nm, and temperatures varying from 100 to 1200 K. Furthermore, 
we discuss empirical models from the existing literature to better 
interpret our findings.
2. Computational methods
Atomistic simulations of nanocrystalline HfNbTaZr RHEAs were 
conducted using the LAMMPS code [ 31 , 32 ]. The interactions among Hf, 
Nb, Ta, and Zr atoms were modeled following the interatomic potential 
proposed by Maiti [ 33 ]. This potential has been highly optimized to 
accurately describe dislocation activity, elastic constants (pressures up 
to 20 GPa), and phase stability (pressures up to 60 GPa) [ 34 , 35 ]. A fixed 
timestep of 1.0 fs and periodic boundary conditions were adopted during 
the entire simulation. There are several possible methods to thermostat 
the system at the desired temperature. In this case, we used a simple 
velocity re-scaling method, as given by LAMMPS “ fix temp/rescale ” 
command.

2.3. Analysis
Post-processing and visualization were performed with the Open 
Visualization Tool (OVITO) [ 39 ]. Atomic structure was analyzed using 
the Polyhedral Template Matching (PTM) algorithm [ 40 ], and disloca -
tions were identified with the Dislocation eXtraction Algorithm (DXA) 
[ 41 ], both implemented in OVITO. Crystalline structures are often 
determined by Common Neighbor Analysis (CNA), which is employed 
by DXA. However, CNA is based on neighbors determined within a 
spherical region, and often provides artificial results, especially due to 
the large anisotropic lattice distortions in HEA, particularly at large 
strains [ 35 ]. PTM is more reliable because it finds neighbors from the 
atomic Voronoi cells and compares Voronoi polyhedra to a template 
database. The “ root mean square displacement ” (RMSD) value in PTM 
accounts for possible deformation of a given cell compared to the ones in 
the database. Atoms not identified as any type in the database are given 
type “ Other ” , meaning “ unknown ” , and should not be automatically 
associated with an amorphous structure without further testing, for 
instance, using pair-correlation function characterization. A typical HEA 
“ perfect ” crystal, at finite temperature, will include some fraction of type

Voronoi polycrystal should have most GB atoms identified as type 
“ other ” . The exact value of RMSD has to be tuned to different alloys. 
Some alloys with larger lattice mismatch will require larger RMSD and

some other spurious phase. For instance, false fcc or hcp type atoms will 
appear in the grain boundaries of a bcc alloy, due to larger rmsd selec -
tion. On the other hand, decreasing the RMSD below 0.1 generally fails 
to capture intrinsic deformation in HEA and will misidentify a large 
fraction of atoms.
In order to obtain dislocation density of these samples, we first delete 
all atom type “ Other ” , as identified by PTM. This will remove mostly 
grain boundary atoms and will disregard several GB dislocation net -
works, including sessile junction of multiple dislocations, which do not 
play a significant role in deformation. There are some “ Other ” atoms in 
dislocation cores, but tests show that the error incurred by removing 
those atoms is very small. DXA is applied to this “ filtered ” sample to 
obtain only dislocations inside grains. A similar method was used in 
previous work [ 36 ].
Additional dislocation analysis allows quantifying edge and screw 
contributions, following the work in ref. [ 36 ]. To help quantify grain 
boundary activity, a cumulative shear strain analysis was performed 
over the grains to quantify their deformation. This calculation used the 
atomic strain algorithm implemented in OVITO over all atoms in the 
structure, using a cutoff radius of 0.34 nm which includes enough

3. Results
Stress-strain curves for samples at different temperatures and grain 
sizes are shown in Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Material (Fig. S1). They 
all exhibit similar characteristics: a linear quasi-elastic regime that 
transitions into a non-linear behavior, reaching a maximum value — the 
ultimate tensile strength ( UTS ), at approximately 0.07 strain. This is 
followed by a relatively rapid decrease in stress and a roughly constant 
stress at large strains. The UTS is higher and more pronounced at lower 
temperatures, as expected. There is only a small amount of hardening, 
especially at larger grain sizes with low temperatures. This is consistent 
with recent experimental results for NbTaTiV [ 43 ], which observed 
large hardening for quasistatic deformation and very small amounts of 
hardening for high-strain-rate deformation. Molecular dynamics simu -
lations of a RHEA do not display hardening in the simulated strain range, 
up to 0.20 strain [ 30 ].

3.2. Plastic deformation
Fig. 4 (a) and (b) display representative snapshots of the deformation 
process for the 8 nm and 17 nm HEA at 900 K, respectively, where atoms 
are colored according to their crystalline structure from PTM.
In the 8 nm sample, grain boundary (GB) undergoes progressive 
thickening due to GB sliding and rotation as reported elsewhere [ 60 ]. 
Further inspection reveals that the grain boundaries of samples with 
smaller grain diameters are thicker than those with larger grain di -
ameters, as expected due to the larger number of GB atoms [ 44 ].
The GB regions in Fig. 4 (a) include fcc, hcp and “ Other ” structures. In 
our simulations, these cannot be interpreted as true fcc/hcp phases, but 
only as a result of PTM dealing with a bcc lattice with highly anisotropic 
deformation. Significant disorder occurs at GB junctions, at locations 
with a large number of atoms type “ Other ” , suggesting local amorph -
ization. We emphasize that group “ Other ” are not necessarily “ amor -
phous ” atoms, as it is frequently claimed. One can only ascertain that 
those atoms do not belong to any of the pre-set crystal structures 
included in the PTM analysis. Other supplementary structural analysis, 
like the pair-correlation functions and orientational parameters, would 
be required to demonstrate amorphization.
In contrast to the 8 nm sample, the 17 nm sample does not show GB 
thickening, except at some GB junctions, at large strain. PTM displays an 
increasing number of point and extended defects. Some point defects are 
“ real ” , a result of the deformation and debris left by dislocation glide 
[ 52 ]. However, many isolated atoms identified as non-bcc are only ar -
tifacts of the structure detector due to the large inhomogeneous local 
strain field produced by the chemical complexity [ 35 ], and also due to 
the large thermal noise of the samples. The fractions of each crystalline 
phase versus deformation are shown in Figs. S3 – S7 of the supplementary 
material. We do not observe any significant phase transitions. Some 
experiments observed significant hcp phase at high temperature for 
HfNbTaZr [ 61 ] and for HfNbTaTiZr [ 62 ]. Similar to our results, exper -
iments in TiZrNbHf, maintained phase stability under mechanical 
deformation and heat treatment [ 63 ].
Fig. 5 displays the cumulative shear over the grains as a function of 
grain size and temperature. The cumulative shear, at least in the elastic 
regime, is quite similar for each temperature among grain sizes. Curves 
separate in the plastic regime, close to the initial twin nucleation, before 
0.1 strain. For ϵ = 0 . 4, the 17 nm sample shows a clear difference in 
cumulative shear compared to the smaller grain samples. This could be 
explained by means of the larger grain size (and smaller number of GB 
atoms) which absorbs the deformation by means of twins. In contrast,

5. Conclusions and outlook
Temperature effects on the mechanical behavior of a nanocrystalline 
bcc HfNbTaZr refractory high entropy alloy (RHEA) were investigated 
under uniaxial tensile loading using atomistic simulations. Several 
average grain sizes were explored, and plasticity involved grain 
boundary activity, dislocations and twins. A dislocation-based strength 
model gives very good agreement with simulation results, provided 
some parameters are fit. Our results for nanocrystals can support other 
studies which consider single dislocation glide to study the strength of 
RHEA.
The main results are summarized in the following: 
• A transition from Hall-Petch to inverse Hall-Petch effect was 
observed in the range of 12 – 15 nm grain size for the temperature 
range studied here. Consequently, the largest strength was attained 
in samples within this specific grain size range.
• Plasticity in samples with larger grain sizes, i.e. in the Hall-Petch 
regime, was primarily driven by nucleation of dislocations and 
twins. In samples with smaller grain sizes, i.e. in the inverse Hall- 
Petch regime, these plastic mechanisms were also observed, 
though to a lesser degree. However, grain-boundary activity was 
significant, including grain-boundary thickening with deformation, 
as expected.
• Similar results were attained for a broad temperature range, from 
100 to 1200 K. The overall impact of temperature was to reduce 
dislocation density and twin volume fraction, among other factors, 
ultimately inducing softening without promoting brittleness in the 
samples.
• A model describing the decrease of elastic modulus with temperature 
works well for our simulations, and there is indication that temper -
ature effects in the elastic properties will be reduced at larger grain 
size.
• The yield stress versus temperature is very well described by the 
Maresca and Curtin model [ 22 ], with some parameters adjusted to 
take into account our high strain-rate deformation and nano sized 
samples which lead to high yield stress at low temperatures. The 
Zerilli-Armstrong model [ 54 ] for high-strain-rate deformation also 
provides good agreement with our simulations.
All in all, our results show a detailed description of temperature ef -
fects dominating mechanical properties of HfNbTaZr HEA, and the un -
derling phenomena that govern their plastic behavior. The outstanding 
mechanical properties at elevated temperature suggest that HfNbTaZr 
could be of major interest for extreme condition applications, such as 
shock loading, nuclear fusion environments, and aerospace shielding, 
among many others.
Analytical models routinely used to explain experiments on the 
temperature dependence of strength in bcc HEA are given support by the 
dynamic atomistic simulations presented here, reaching beyond the 
simple scenario of a single nearly straight edge/screw dislocation 
moving in a perfect single crystal, and including dislocations of various
