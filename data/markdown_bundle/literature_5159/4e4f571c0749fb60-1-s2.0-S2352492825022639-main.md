ABSTRACT
Nickel-based superalloys face challenges in achieving a balance between high-temperature performance and 
cost-effectiveness. Additive manufacturing (AM) offers a pathway to efficient alloy design through precise in-situ 
compositional control. This study employs AM to fabricate nickel-based superalloy samples with tailored Ti/Al 
compositional gradients, aiming to elucidate the impact of these elements ’ distribution on microstructure and 
mechanical properties. Systematic microstructural characterization revealed the evolution across varying Ti and 
Al contents. Combined with mechanical property testing, an optimal range for Ti and Al additions was identified 
to achieve peak alloy performance. Furthermore, this research introduces a compositional gradient optimization 
strategy integrating thermodynamic calculations with experimental validation. This approach significantly en -
hances the strength-ductility synergy of the Ni-based superalloy. The optimized alloy achieved a tensile strength 
of 709 ± 24 MPa at 900 
◦
C with a high elongation of 28 ± 4.5 %. Comparative analysis with existing AM nickel- 
based superalloys demonstrates the feasibility and superiority of the proposed strategy, offering a novel approach 
for property enhancement and accelerated development of materials.
1. Introduction
Nickel-based alloys, celebrated for their remarkable mechanical at -
tributes, resilience to high-temperature oxidation, and corrosion resis -
tance, have become indispensable in sectors such as aerospace and 
energy generation [1,2] . However, in light of the accelerated pace of 
technological advancement and the growing intricacy of service envi -
ronments, the performance expectations placed on nickel-based alloys 
continue to rise [3,4] . It thus follows that the optimization of the 
composition of nickel-based alloys through the application of scientific 
alloy design methodologies represents a pivotal research objective 
within the field of materials science.
In recent years, additive manufacturing (AM) technology has 
emerged as a promising approach in the realm of material preparation, 
offering distinctive advantages that have the potential to transform the 
field [5] . By employing layer-upon-layer deposition, this technology 
enables the direct fabrication of complexly structured components that 
adhere to design specifications while simultaneously providing precise 
control over their composition and microstructure. This distinctive 
capability has facilitated the development of innovative alloy design and 
optimization strategies [6,7] . Furthermore, AM allows for the tailoring 
of alloy microstructures and properties through the manipulation of 
printing parameters and chemical compositions, thereby enhancing 
their performance [8,9] . The recent integration of AM with in-situ 
alloying techniques has led to a notable enhancement in the tensile 
[10] and fatigue properties [11] of titanium alloys. It is noteworthy that 
Song et al. [12] successfully integrated alloy design with the AM process 
design, resulting in the development of a series of Ti-O-Fe alloys with

development of an efficient high-throughput alloy design methodology 
is of paramount importance.
Composition gradient, an innovative high-throughput approach to 
materials design, enables precise tailoring of microstructure and prop -
erties by introducing continuous or discontinuous variations in 
composition within the material matrix [14,15] . The multiple diffusion 
technique, rooted in the principle of composition gradients, aims to 
generate high-throughput composition-microstructure-property data 
that, in conjunction with machine learning and other methods, has 
significantly aided the high-throughput design of Ni-based or 
Co-Ni-based alloys for casting applications [16 – 19] . However, due to the 
inherent non-equilibrium solidification characteristics of AM, the direct 
applicability of the diffusion multiple technique to AM scenarios is 
limited. Therefore, the development of AM-specific high-throughput 
experimental methods is imperative.
In the context of AM, gradient preparation technologies using dis -
similar materials facilitate the creation of materials with intricate multi- 
element compositional gradients, termed compositionally graded alloys 
(CGAs) [20] , exemplified by TC4/IN625 [21] , Invar/MnCu [22] , and 
304 L/IN625 [23] . Zhang et al. [24] used directional energy deposition 
and mechanical mixing of elemental powders to synthesize 144 ternary 
Ti-Al-V alloys with different compositions and rapidly assessed their 
microstructures, microhardness, and yield strength using advanced 
image processing and nanoindentation techniques. This approach pro -
vides excellent control over alloy composition, making it highly suitable 
for AM processes. By meticulously adjusting the mixing ratios of mul -
tiple materials, precise printing of composition gradients can be ach -
ieved, thereby integrating composition gradient control with AM 
technology to dramatically accelerate the optimization and design 
process of AM alloys. Lu et al. [25] exploited wire-arc AM to efficiently 
screen Al-gradient high-entropy AlCuCrHfNi alloys; and. Kürnsteiner 
et al. [26] designed laser-AM-specific maraging steel via Ti gradients. 
These studies validate CGAs as powerful tools for rapid alloy optimiza -
tion but leave nickel-based systems underexplored.
Nickel-based alloys, which typically comprise more than ten alloying 
elements, rely heavily on Al and Ti for the formation of the γ ′ phase, 
which significantly enhances their high-temperature performance [27] . 
With the development of high-temperature alloys, especially 
single-crystal high-temperature alloys, in order to obtain higher 
high-temperature performance, the content of Ta is gradually increased, 
and the content of Ti is reduced or even no Ti is added, so as to ensure 
the high-temperature strength of the alloy. However, the addition of Ta 
inevitably increases the density of the alloys, which limits the applica -
tion of the alloys in aerospace. Therefore, this study attempts to optimize 
the additions of Al and Ti in nickel-based superalloys without the 
addition of Ta by implementing a compositional gradient adjustment 
strategy focusing on these two elements, with a view to achieving higher 
high-temperature performance at 900 
◦
C.
In summary, the present study investigates the effect of Ti/Al content 
on the microstructure and properties of AMed nickel-based superalloys 
using the alloy ZGH451 as a model system. Using a compositional 
gradient design strategy, the Ti/Al content was optimized to improve the 
tensile properties of ZGH451 at 900
◦
C. Three Ti/Al gradient specimens 
were prepared and a comprehensive microstructural analysis was per -
formed to quantify the volume fraction, mean size and roundness (shape 
factors) of the γ ′ phase. This allowed the elucidation of the individual 
and combined effects of Al and Ti on the γ ′ phase. In addition, Vickers 
hardness and nanoindentation tests were carried out to assess the local 
mechanical properties at different deposition heights, thereby estab -
lishing a relationship between the Ti/Al composition gradient, micro -
structure and properties. This research provides the basis for data 
acquisition methods aimed at obtaining comprehensive microstructural 
and property data sets. This work elucidates how Ti/Al ratios control γ ′ 
phase morphology (cubic-to-spherical transition) and precipitate for -
mation ( η -Ni
3
Ti, MC carbides), providing guidelines for optimizing Al/ 
Ti = 0.03 – 0.04 to suppress detrimental phases and improve high- 
temperature mechanical properties. In addition, this study introduces 
a compositional gradient-based alloy design methodology that provides 
a valuable methodological cornerstone for the development of other 
high performance alloy materials.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Compositionally graded alloy design strategy
This study establishes a high-throughput alloy design framework for 
additive manufacturing (AM) by integrating compositionally graded 
alloy (CGA) samples with thermodynamic computational 
screening . The workflow ( Fig. 1 ) comprises two parallel modules: 
1. Experimental Module : 
o CGA Fabrication : Laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) was 
used to fabricate Al/Ti CGA samples and optimized alloy samples 
( Section 2.2 ).
o Microstructural Analysis : Compositional gradients, phase evolution, 
and γ ′ morphology were quantified via SEM-EDS and XRD ( Section 
2.3 ).
o Mechanical Characterization : Vickers hardness and nano -
indentation tests mapped localized properties across gradients; 
tensile tests evaluated room-temperature and 900
◦
C strength of 
optimized alloys ( Section 2.4 ).
2. Computational Module:
Phase equilibria, Scheil non-equilibrium solidification, γ / γ ′ lattice 
misfit, and strengthening mechanisms were simulated using Thermo- 
Calc 2023b with the TCNI10 database ( Section 2.5 ).
This dual-module approach correlates gradient composition data 
with thermodynamic predictions to resolve composition-microstructure- 
property interdependencies in Ni-based superalloys, enabling rapid 
identification of Al/Ti ratios for 900
◦
C strength-ductility synergy in AM- 
processed alloys.

3. Experimental results: microstructure and hardness evolution
3.1. Compositional distribution and gradient homogeneity
To investigate the influence of Al and Ti element distribution and Ti/ 
Al ratio variation in samples with different compositional gradients 
prepared via AM, EDS point scanning analysis was conducted on three 
samples designed with distinct compositional gradients. The results are 
presented in Fig. 3 . Firstly, for Sample 1 ( Al → Ti equiatomic gradient ), 
an equiatomic percentage compositional gradient from Al to Ti was 
established. The EDS analysis revealed that the deviations between the 
measured Al and Ti element contents and their preset values remained 
within 20 % as the deposition height increased. Additionally, a distri -
bution of Al elements not less than 0.4 at% was observed across all 
detected regions of Sample 1, as depicted in Fig. 3 (a). Notably, instead of 
the preset stepwise jumps, the Al and Ti contents above the interface 
exhibited relatively smooth increasing and decreasing trends, 
respectively.
Based on the above findings, the design strategy was adjusted, and 
Sample 2 ( Reduced Al-Ti gradient ) was prepared to further explore the 
element distribution with a reduced Al-Ti compositional gradient. While 
the composition below the interface in Sample 2 remained consistent 
with that of Sample 1, the composition above the interface was adjusted 
to a mixed region of Al and Ti. The EDS measurements indicated that the 
contents of Al and Ti in Sample 2 closely matched their preset values, 
and the variation trend of the Ti/Al ratio exhibited good agreement with 
the preset values, as seen in Fig. 3 (b). Interestingly, compared to Sample 
1, the variation in Al and Ti contents within the diffusion zone in Sample 
2 is more gradual, providing a larger compositional gradient region for 
subsequent microstructural characterization and property testing.
To further elucidate the potential impact of the AM process on 
element diffusion behavior, an inverse CGA sample, Sample 3 ( Inverse 
gradient ), was designed based on Sample 2. EDS analysis revealed 
significant differences in element diffusion patterns between Sample 3 
and Samples 1 and 2, as shown in Fig. 3 (c). The diffusion layer in 
Samples 1 and 2 was located above the interface, whereas in Sample 3, it 
appeared prominently below the interface. This phenomenon is related 
to elemental diffusion, and a detailed discussion will be presented in 
Section 4.1 .

3.2. Phase evolution and γ ′ morphology
3.2.1. Precipitate identification
Based on the aforementioned analysis, the elemental distribution in 
the three types of CGA samples has been obtained. This section further 
delves into the variations in microstructure under these distinct 
compositional gradients. Fig. 4 presents XRD patterns of three charac -
teristic regions in Sample 1 (Al → Ti gradient). Notably, all three regions 
are predominantly characterized by the γ matrix phase and the γ’ phase, 
evident from their prominent diffraction peaks. However, the precipi -
tated phases differ among these regions. To identify the diffraction peaks 
of low-content precipitated phases, y-axis breakpoints were introduced 
at ranges of 60 – 200k or 300k counts. The XRD patterns revealed the 
presence of small quantities of MC carbides in the Al-rich region. 
Conversely, the Ti-rich region exhibited MC carbides, M
6
C carbides, and 
a copious amount of η -phase. The intermediate interfacial region dis -
played a mixture of precipitated phases from both the Al-rich and Ti-rich 
regions but with relatively low diffraction peak intensities.
Furthermore, a more detailed microstructure analysis of Sample 1 
was carried out by SEM, as depicted in Fig. 5 . Due to the rationality of 
the process parameters, no microcracks were observed in Sample 1. The 
interface of Sample 1 is distinctly visible under optical microscopy, 
exhibiting a darker top and a brighter bottom, as shown in Fig. 5 (a). 
Further examination using SEM revealed that the darker area above the 
interface (Ti-rich region) was due to the precipitation of a substantial 
number of blocky precipitates. Along the compositional gradient, i.e., 
with the increase in Ti content, the quantity of blocky phases markedly 
augmented, and the morphology of the γ ′ phase transformed from cubic 
to spherical, as depicted in Fig. 5 (c, d). Simultaneously, with the rising 
Ti content, novel precipitates emerged, including petal-like eutectic 
structures ( Fig. 5 (e)) and acicular precipitates ( Fig. 5 (f)). The acicular 
phase, observed at the top of the sample with a higher Ti content, is 
attributed to the η -Ni
3
Ti phase [29,30] . Under backscattered electron 
(BSE) mode, the blocky phases exhibited distinct brightness levels, as 
shown in Fig. 5 (g). EDS point scans confirmed that Point 1 and Point 2 
corresponded to Ni- and Ti-rich η phases, which differed in orientation 
from the acicular η phase, as discussed in other literature [31] . Point 3, 
Point 4, and Point 5 were identified as Ti-, W-, and Mo-rich MC carbides. 
The point scan results are tabulated in Table 3 . Through EDS mapping, a 
small amount of M
6
C carbides rich in Cr, Mo, and W were also discov -
ered, as shown in Fig. 5 (h). Linear scanning analysis of the two primary 
precipitated phases in Fig. 5 (i-k) revealed that Precipitate 1 is rich in Ni, 
Ti, and Co elements but deficient in Cr and Mo, with other elements 
being consistent with the matrix; whereas, Precipitate 2 is abundant in 
Ti, W, Mo, and C elements but deficient in Ni, Co, and Cr. From the 
elemental point of view and crystallographic results, it is further 
confirmed that Precipitation 1 is an η -phase and Precipitation 2 is an 
MC-carbide phase, and the precipitation of such hard phases will inev -
itably increase the hardness of the superalloy to the detriment of the 
toughness, which is detrimental to the performance at elevated tem -
peratures. In contrast, only trace amounts of carbides were found in the 
Ti-rich regions of Samples 2 and 3, and no η phase was observed.

4. Analysis of Ti/Al effects with thermodynamic calculation
Section 3 explores the fabrication of CGA samples with varying Ti/Al 
ratios, uncovering that the alloy exhibits fine cubic-shaped γ ′ phases 
when containing Al exclusively and larger circular γ ′ phases when 
comprising Ti only. Notably, at an atomic fraction ratio of Al:Ti = 2:1, 
the γ ′ phases assume a coarse cubic morphology. Meanwhile, the other 
precipitated phases and mechanical properties all produce a certain 
pattern of change with the change of Al-Ti gradient. To delve deeper into 
this phenomenon, this section employs thermodynamic calculations to 
validate the experimentally observed elemental distributions, phase 
compositions, and performance gradient distributions. Furthermore, a 
strategy to manipulate the size, morphology and mechanical properties 
of the γ ′ phase through the Ti/Al ratio is proposed.

6. Conclusion
This study, taking a nickel-based superalloy as an example, in -
vestigates the influence of the Ti/Al ratio on microstructural types, 
morphology of the γ ′ phase, and mechanical properties across an Al-Ti 
compositional gradient through AM experiments and thermodynamic 
calculations. The key findings are summarized as follows:
(1) Three CGA samples were designed and fabricated: Sample 1 (Al- 
Ti gradient), Sample 2 (Al-(Al + Ti) gradient), and Sample 3 ((Al + Ti)-Al 
gradient). The reversed gradients in Samples 2 and 3 eliminated the 
influence of AM process factors. Characterization of these samples 
revealed the evolution of microstructure-property relationships with 
compositional gradients, uncovering a close correlation between 
composition and microstructure. An increase in Ti content led to the 
formation of MC, M
6
C, and η phases, which was further corroborated by 
phase diagram calculations, validating the correspondence between 
gradient composition and microstructure.
(2) Morphological parameters exhibited abrupt changes in Sample 1, 
with the γ ′ phase transitioning from small to large, numerous to sparse, 
and cubic to rounded as the composition shifted from Al-rich to Ti-rich 
regions. In Sample 2, a linear variation was observed, with γ ′ phase 
enlarging and reducing in number while maintaining a cubic shape. 
Sample 3 exhibited an opposite trend to Sample 2. Phase diagram cal -
culations and mismatch degree analyses confirmed the experimentally 
observed patterns in γ ′ phase morphology.
(3) Vickers hardness of the CGA samples increased with rising Ti 
content, as the introduction of MC, M
6
C, and η phases favored room 
temperature properties but offered limited benefits at elevated 
temperatures. Nanoindentation results indicated that when Al content 
significantly exceeded Ti, both Young ’ s modulus and hardness initially 
decreased and then increased with increasing Ti/Al ratio, peaking at a 
Ti/Al ratio of 0.03 – 0.04.
(4) A compositional gradient-optimized alloy design strategy was 
developed, offering guidance for subsequent material design and opti -
mization. The proposed alloy significantly enhanced the synergy be -
tween strength and ductility in the nickel-based superalloy, achieving a 
tensile strength of 1015 ± 37 MPa and an elongation of 24.5 ± 3.5 % at 
room temperature. Furthermore, it maintained a high tensile strength of 
709 ± 24 MPa at 900
◦
C while retaining substantial ductility (28 
± 4.5 %). Comparative analysis with existing alloys demonstrated the 
feasibility of the optimized alloy, validating the proposed alloy design 
approach. This methodology not only provides a novel perspective for 
optimizing the properties of nickel-based superalloys but also opens up 
new design avenues for developing other high-performance materials by 
exploring the effects of different elemental combinations and gradient 
distributions.
