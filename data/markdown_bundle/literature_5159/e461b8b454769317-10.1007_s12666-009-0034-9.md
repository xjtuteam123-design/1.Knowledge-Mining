iron based superalloys

Abstract
Effect of prior oxidation on the creep behavior of an iron based alloy (Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy) in comparison with three nickel base superalloys
with different aluminium and titanium content was investigated. A significant oxidation induced creep strengthening has been ob served in the
Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy while prior exposure was reported to result in creep weakening in nickel base superalloys. Oxidation-induced creep
strengthening in Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy can be attributed to the improved adherence of surface oxide caused by the presence of yttrium. The
oxidation-induced creep strengthening, however, is accompanied by considerable drop in creep ductility in Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy. The embrittlement
of grain boundaries caused by both surface oxide and oxygen diffused inwards along grain boundaries reduced the creep ductility of the Fe-
Ni-Cr-Al alloy. In contrast, oxidation induced weakening and embrittlement of nickel base superalloys caused the premature onse t and
shortening of tertiary regime, respectively, and thereby reduced both time- and strain-to-rupture considerably.
1. Introduction
Superalloy based components are exposed to corrosive
and oxidising environments during service. Therefore, studies
of varying nature have been conducted to understand the
effect of environment on the performance of the
superalloys [1-13]. The effect of prior exposure to high
temperatures in oxidising environment on the creep properties
has been one such aspect of investigation. Most of the
earlier studies on nickel based superalloys indicated
degradation of creep properties leading to creep weakening
as well as embrittlement following prior oxidation [4-13].
Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloys are relatively a new class of
precipitation hardenable austenitic alloys containing high
concentration of aluminium, typically in the range 4-5 wt.%,
and chromium 10-15 wt.%. These alloys have the special
feature in the sense they owe their creep strength to the
precipitation of ordered NiAl precipitates in austenite
matrix [14,15] in contrast to Ni
3Al in nickel base superalloys.
They possess excellent oxidation resistance due to formation
of an adherent film of Al

4. Discussion
Prior exposure has resulted in creep weakening in all
three nickel based superalloys leading to an increase in
minimum creep rate and reduction in time-to-rupture. The
factors reported to be responsible for creep weakening in
nickel-based superalloys are: γ′ (Ni
3Al) depleted zone [5-13]
in the near surface region and/or formation of CO/CO 2 gas
bubbles [5-8,12,18,19] along the grain boundaries. The γ′
depleted zone is a consequence of oxidation of γ′ forming
elements in the near-surface zone to form respective-oxides
at the surface or along grain boundaries close to the surface.
As the ensuing γ′ depleted zone is softer, the overall creep
resistance of the superalloys decreases. The presence of CO/
CO
2 gas bubbles along grain boundaries not only causes
embrittlement but also enhances creep rates. This is because
gas bubbles of CO/CO
2 are at high pressure [18,19] and
when stress is applied at elevated temperatures, these gases
exert additional pressure in the grain boundaries of the test
specimen.
The creep weakening observed in Nimonic-105 [13] has
been found to be due to γ′ depleted zone in the near-surface
zone as reported in Inconel alloy X-750 exposed to air at
1150°C [9]. Formation of CO/CO
2 gas bubbles has not been
observed in this alloy. While creep weakening is considerable
in case of Inconel alloy X-750 (minimum creep rate increased
by about half an order of magnitude and reduction in rupture
life by 40 %) it is marginal in case of Nimonic-105. This may
be attributed to the narrower γ′ depleted zone in Nimonic-105
[13] as compared to the wider depletion zone in Inconel
X-750 as the Ti + Al content ( γ′ forming elements) of the
former (7 wt. %) is much higher than that (3 wt.%) of the
latter. On the other hand, the creep weakening in Inconel
alloy X-750 exposed at 1050°C [10] and Nimonic-263 exposed
at 1020°C [12] is due to the combined effect of γ′ depleted
zone and CO/CO
2 formation along grain boundaries. Creep
weakening in Nimonic C-263 has resulted in increase in
minimum creep rate by about an order of magnitude and
reduction in time-to-rupture by about 80 %. Thus, operation
of any one or combination of these mechanisms is reported
to cause creep weakening in nickel base superalloys.
Contrary to the behavior observed in the nickel base
superalloys, prior exposure to air at 1200°C has resulted in
significant reduction in minimum creep rate (Fig. 6a),
extension of steady-state creep region and increase in time
to fracture (Fig. 6b) in the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy indicating
oxidation induced creep strengthening in this alloy in both
as-ESR and wrought conditions, with strengthening effect
being more pronounced in the former. Microstructural
changes that have occurred in Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy following
prior oxidation include the formation of (i) hard surface oxide
layer, consisting of chromium and aluminium oxides and
(ii) soft β (NiAl) depleted zone just below the surface oxide
layer (Fig. 4b). It has been observed that the hardness levels
in the β-depleted region are lower as compared to those in
the unaffected zone of prior oxidised specimens. The
strengthening phase β is either fully absent or its volume
fraction is very low in this region. In spite of the presence
of a soft zone, there is a net increase in creep strength
following prior oxidation in the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy. These
observations, therefore, suggest that the adherent surface
oxide layer consisting of chromium and aluminium oxides is
primarily responsible for the improvement in creep strength

Although the surface oxides reported in many of the
nickel base superalloys [7-13] are those of chromium and
aluminium, contrary to the behaviour observed in the Fe-Ni-
Cr-Al alloy, creep weakening has been observed in the former.
It is well known that surface oxide films in high temperature
engineering alloys, in general, are stronger and brittle. Hence,
during creep, oxide film is stretched and it may spall off
without contributing to creep strengthening. However, if
surface oxide is modified by an appropriate alloying addition
to improve its adherence, cracking / dislodgement of oxide
film is delayed and it may then contribute to creep
strengthening. In view of the above, oxidation-induced creep
weakening reported in nickel base superalloys can be
attributed to the poor adherence of oxide scale during creep.
It is to be pointed out that many of the nickel base
superalloys studied in the past did not contain active
elements like yttrium or rare earth elements like cerium,
lanthanum etc., while the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy contains yttrium.
 It is well known that minor additions of active (yttrium)
or rare earth elements (cerium, lanthanum etc.) [20-22] improve
the adherence of oxide scale even under severe thermal cyclic
conditions. Various mechanisms by which these elements
improve adherence of oxide scale have been discussed
elsewhere [20-22]. It has been postulated that the oxide
growth stresses and / or thermal stresses (arising during air
cooling following oxidation due to misfit in thermal expansion
coefficient between oxide and metal substrate) may, also, be
relieved by local plastic deformation of substrate [20] just
below the oxide scale. Formation of a narrow zone of
recrystallized grains and deformation markings, just below
the surface oxide in the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy, Fig. 7a (in as-
oxidized (1200°C/ 1h) plus aged condition (700°C/1h)),
supports this postulation. Further, the fact that the width of
recrystallized zone increases with exposure time, as can be
seen from Fig. 7b, reinforces this view. These observations
suggest that the presence of yttrium in the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy
(Table 1) appears to be primarily responsible for improved
adherence of oxide layer (containing chromium and aluminium
oxides) and concomitant improvement in creep strength of
the Fe-Ni-Cr-Al alloy.
Prior exposure has led to creep embrittlement in the Fe-
Ni-Cr-Al alloy (Fig. 6c) as in the case of nickel base
superalloys [10-13]. This can be attributed to the
embrittlement of grain boundaries due to formation of grain
boundary oxides near the surface and inward diffusion of
oxygen through the grain boundaries beyond grain boundary
oxide zone. Dyson and Osgerby [7] reviewed the aspect of
oxidation induced creep embrittlement and identified three
mechanisms for oxygen-induced embrittlement in nickel base
superalloys. They include: (a) formation of carbon dioxide
gas bubble along grain boundaries (b) formation of fine
oxides, such as Al
2O3, Cr2O3 and TiO2, which initiate cavities
during subsequent creep and (c) segregation of oxygen to
grain boundaries and thereby allowing cavities to become
stable at a smaller radius under a given applied stress. The
creep data and microstructural investigation on Fe-Ni-Cr-Al
alloy indicate that oxygen either in the elemental form or in
the form of very fine oxides (aluminium or chromium oxides)
[12] appears to be causing the embrittlement in this alloy as
also suggested in the case of Nimonic-105 [13]. On the other
hand, formation of CO / CO
2 gas bubbles along the grain
boundaries has been reported to be causing embrittlement in
Inconel alloy X-750 exposed at 1050°C [10] and Nimoni-263
exposed at 1020°C [12]. The presence of higher concentration
of strong oxide forming elements like aluminium and chromium
in Fe-Ni-Cr-Al and Nimonic-105 alloys (Table 1) makes the
oxygen potential along grain boundaries (below oxide) quite
low. Consequently, the chances for formation of CO
2 gas
bubbles in these alloys are less and hence, contribution from
this mechanism to creep embrittlement can be discounted. It
has been observed in Fe-Ni-Cr-Al and Nimonic-105 alloys
that the precipitates along grain boundaries immediately
below the oxide scale are intact and no voids are found along
the grain boundaries in this zone.
