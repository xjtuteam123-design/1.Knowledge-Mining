High Temperature Mechanical Properties and Microstructures 
of Thermally Stabilized Fe‑Based Alloys Synthesized by Mechanical 
Alloying Followed by Hot Extrusion
Hasan Kotan1  · Kris A. Darling2 · Tom Luckenbaugh2
Received: 24 August 2019 / Accepted: 2 December 2019 
© The Korean Institute of Metals and Materials 2019
Abstract
The key requirement to consolidate high-energy mechanically alloyed nanocrystalline powders is to achieve densification 
and particle bonding without impairment in the mechanical properties. Recent demonstrations of consolidation methods 
involving high shear, pressure and temperature have shown promising results for bonding high strength particulate materials 
produced by mechanical alloying. In this study, we report the ability of multi-pass high temperature equal channel angular 
extrusion to produce bulk ferritic alloys from nanocrystalline Fe–Ni–Zr powders. Subsequent microstructural characteriza-
tions indicate limited grain growth as the average grain sizes remain smaller than 100 nm after processing temperatures of 
600 °C and 700 °C, above which grains reach micron sizes. The compression test results reveal that the alloys exhibit high 
mechanical strength at room and moderately high temperatures compared to the pure Fe and Fe–Ni alloys without Zr addition.
Keywords Equal channel angular extrusion (ECAE) · High temperature mechanical properties · Mechanical alloying · 
Grain growth · Microstructural evolution · Steel
1 Introduction
The Hall–Petch relationship dictates a direct correlation 
between a reduction in the average grain size within a metal 
or alloy and an elevation of its strength and hardness [1–3]. 
Scientists and engineers sought to take advantage of this 
relationship when designing bulk nanocrystalline metals, 
where their extremely small grain size i.e. ≪ 100 nm can 
result in order-of-magnitude increases in the same proper -
ties [4–6]. However, the impractical downside of generating 
microstructures with such fine length scales are instabili-
ties related to microstructural coarsening with losses in the 
properties [7].
Grain growth in nanocrystalline metals is well known to 
occur upon exposure to moderate and in some cases low 
homologous temperatures [8 ]. This instability has greatly 
limited the application space for bulk nanocrystalline metals 
and is the major hurdle to overcome in the commerciali-
zation of useful structural components. Therefore, intense 
research efforts within the nanocrystalline metals commu-
nity have focused on reduction and/or elimination of grain 
growth and microstructural coarsening through various 
means. This collective effort has resulted in the publishing 
of many examples of successfully stabilized nanocrystalline 
alloys [9–18]. Specifically, results on thermally stabilized 
Fe-based binary alloys produced by high energy mechani-
cal alloying have indicated that the addition of Zr solute 
can retain nano or ultra-fine grain microstructure up to “the 
pressureless annealing temperatures” as high as 900 °C 
[19–21]. Extension of the binary system by the addition 
of Ni has proven successful and possible microstructures 
i.e. grain sizes spanning the nanoscale to the micron scale 
can be retained over a given temperature and composition 
range [22]. The iron based alloys with the addition of nickel 
have attracted considerable attention for the understanding 
of steels and other ferrous alloys and have been investi-
gated with respect to phase stability, microstructural and 
mechanical properties. Additionally, Fe–Ni alloys can be 
candidate alloys as structural materials for nuclear energy

Such strengthening was made possible by the secondary

Fe lattice [27]. Prior, work has also shown that it is possible

distributions are intimately tied to the alpha to gamma phase 
transformation, which has been linked to inducing abnormal 
grain growth in the nanocrystalline Fe–Ni–Zr ternary system 
[28]. The nucleation and growth of the gamma phase can be 
controlled and engineered through the composition of Ni 
present in the alloy as well as a number of processing vari-
ables such as time, temperature and deformation.
To successfully commercialize bulk nanocrystalline met-
als requires a synthesis process that is reproducible, inex-
pensive and scalable to industry. Severe plastic deformation 
(SPD) by mechanical alloying is one of the few manufac-
turing techniques capable of producing industrial-scale vol-
umes of nanocrystalline metals and alloys in an inexpensive 
and consistent manner [29]. However, the one drawback is, 
mechanical alloying is a powder synthesis approach and the 
generated powder particulates require a consolidation step 
(i.e. exposure to heat and pressure) in order to produce a use-
ful engineering component. This consolidation step becomes 
more challenging once the microstructure is stabilized. That 
is, such stabilized microstructures exhibit extremely high 
thermo-mechanical strength with considerably reduced sin-
terability and makes the bonding and full densification of 
metallic particles difficult via conventional industrial powder 
metallurgical (PM) consolidation processes. Accordingly, 
hot pressured-compaction assisted by the addition of intense 
shear deformation may provide a successful path to allow 
full densification and good particle–particle bonding dur -
ing consolidation. Equal channel angular extrusion (ECAE) 
is a scalable non-conventional severe plastic deformation 
method of applying shear deformation [30] and has been 
used to successfully consolidate nanocrystalline particulate 
materials in a bottom up approach to achieve bulk nanocrys-
talline metals [31].
In the present study, thermally stabilized nanocrystalline 
Fe–Ni–Zr powders produced by high energy mechanical 
alloying was consolidated via hot ECAE at various tem-
peratures. Its effects on the overall microstructural evolu-
tion and elevated temperature mechanical responses were 
investigated. The resultant microstructures were composed 
of nano/ultra-fine or micrometer-sized grains depending on 
the consolidation temperature. The mechanical tests results 
demonstrated impressive compressive yield stresses, ranging 
from 1560 MPa to 1080 MPa depending on the alloy compo-
sition and consolidation temperature. Since the mechanical 
property enhancement of Fe–Ni–Zr alloying system does not 
require nanocrystalline grain size, consolidation conditions 
by ECAE can be tailored to achieve the desire strength and 
ductility for engineering applications.
2 Experimental Procedure
2.1 Mechanical Alloying of Fe–Ni–Zr Elemental 
Powders
Fe91Ni8Zr1 and Fe88Ni8Zr4 (at%) alloy powders were synthe-
sized by high energy mechanical alloying in a SPEX 8000D 
shaker mill at room temperature for a total of 20 h. Appro-
priate masses of Fe (99.9%), Zr (99.5%), and Ni (99.8%) 
powders (commercially purchased from Alpha Aesar) with 
a weight of 10 g were mixed with 440 °C stainless steel balls 
at a ball-to-powder ratio of 10-to-1 by weight and sealed in a 
hardened steel vial under an argon atmosphere in a glove box 
where the oxygen level was 1 ppm. No processing agents 
were used for milling. X-ray diffraction patterns from as-
milled Fe–Ni–Zr powders were reported in the previous 
works revealing only the presence of bcc Fe and indicating 
the possible ternary solid solutions [22].
2.2 Hot equal Channel Angular Extrusion (ECAE) 
of Alloyed Powders
For the ECAE consolidation experiments, the as-milled 
powders were placed into nickel cans having dimensions 
of 25.4 mm × 25.4 mm × 90 mm and sealed inside the glove 
box. Prior to ECAE processing, the die assembly was heated 
to 350 °C. The nickel cans loaded with as-milled powders 
were equilibrated for 40 min in a box furnace purged with 
pure Ar gas at the respective temperatures of 600, 700, 800, 
900 and 1000 °C. The equilibrated cans were then extruded 
at an extrusion rate of 25.5 mm s
−1. This procedure was 
repeated four times following route Bc [32]. The ECAE tool-
ing had an L shaped channel meeting at an angle of 90°. The

2.3 Microstructural Characterization 
and Mechanical Testing
Microstructural evolutions of ECAE consolidated samples 
were characterized by ion channeling contrast imaging 
(FIB-CCI) using focused ion beam (FIB) microscopy as a 
function of alloy composition and consolidation tempera-
ture. Scanning electron microscopy—electron backscatter 
diffraction (SEM-EBSD) mapping after ECAE processing 
was conducted for texture evaluation. Compression sam-
ples were cylinders with 3 mm diameter with ± 10 μm tol-
erances in all dimensions. The samples were polished to a 
mirror finish to avoid the influence of any surface scratches 
on the measured mechanical properties. The quasi-static 
compression experiments were performed using an ISO 
certified Instron load frame, equipped with a 200 kN load 
cell, and a 1500 °C ATS clam shell furnace. The samples 
were loaded under a displacement of 0.1524 mm s
−1 after 
holding around 10 min for temperature equilibrium at the 
testing temperature. Compression tests were conducted at 
room temperature, 400 °C, 500 °C, and 600 °C for selected 
consolidation temperatures inside a furnace under ambient 
atmospheric conditions.

The detailed microstructural investigations of ECAE con-
solidated alloys were conducted previously for Fe
88Ni8Zr4 
and Fe91Ni8Zr1 steels by facilitating atom probe tomography 
(APT) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [24–26]. 
From the microstructural characterization it was reported that 
high temperature exposure during ECAE consolidation favors 
the kinetics of atomic diffusion in the microstructures and, 
therefore, promotes the formation of complex oxide phase 
particles, termed nano-sized superlattice (NSS) clusters in 
Fe–Ni–Zr based alloys thanks to the presence of intrinsic 
impurities, including oxygen that is presumably contamina-
tion from the milling media or air during processing. Oxide-
based clusters smaller than 5 nm as well as larger precipitates 
composed of Fe–Ni–Zr–O were reported, the latter was seen 
mostly in the alloys with higher amount of Zr added steel i.e., 
4 at% Zr. A later study investigating the effect of Zr (1 and 5 
at%) on the grain size stability of Fe at elevated temperatures 
also confirmed the formation of Zr–O based clusters and 
showed that small oxide based particles were coherent with 
the matrix [35]. Thus, it can be expected that precipitated 
second phases formed in Fe
88Ni8Zr4 and Fe91Ni8Zr1 steels 
through hot consolidation processes are the predominant 
sources of the microstructural stabilization based on Zener 
pinning theory [36] when compared with pure Fe and Fe
92Ni8 
steel. That being said, as the ECAE processing temperature 
increases, the diffusion distances traveled by the solutes is 
expected to be greater causing precipitates to grow bigger 
and eventually lose the coherency with the matrix through 
Ostwald ripening [37]. It was also reported that incoherent 
particles are less effective on pinning the grain boundaries 
when they are moving than coherent particles [35]. Hence, 
increasing ECAE processing temperature is expected to 
coarsen the particle size, which will increase the average 
interparticle spacing and eventually will lose the coherency 
between the particles and the matrix. This will weaken the 
pinning forces on the grain boundary according to the Zener 
pinning and lead to further grain growth as observed in this 
study for ECAE processing at and above 800 °C.

88Ni8Zr4 and Fe91Ni8Zr1

zontal and vertical channels) that intersect at an angle which 
is 90° for the present study. A 90° angle between vertical and 
horizontal portions of the die causes the material to undergo 
shear deformation which may result in a microstructure with 
texture [38, 39]. The presence of texture or preferred orien-
tation causes anisotropy of mechanical properties [40] and 
could result in vital failure of a material in service. Therefore, 
to obtain homogeneous deformation, the samples were rotated 
under path B

tributed. As seen in Fig.  2 by EBSD mapping no apparent 
preferred grain orientation is observed in the microstructure of 
the extruded steels. Although large regions seem to be divided 
into smaller grains for Fe

alloy consolidated at 800 °C is around 1450 MPa which

temperature increases to higher values (from Fig. 3a–d), the 
yield stresses of Fe

the ECAE consolidation temperature. The room temperature 
compressive yield stress of Fe

and 1080 MPa for the samples consolidated at 800 °C and

the failure increase significantly for Fe
88Ni8Zr4 alloy with 
the increasing ECAE processing temperature from 800 to 
1000 °C, both tested at room temperature. Hence, substan-

plasticity but higher strength in the 800 °C condition is seen 
with the finer grain size. The observed strain hardening is 
considered to be due to the interaction of dislocations with 
the barriers i.e., second phases and with each other which 
inhibits their motion through the crystal lattice. Zr solutes 
that remain in solution if any during low temperature pro -
cessing can also contribute to the observed strain hardening 
[40]. The thermal softening with increasing compressive test 
temperatures is observed for Fe
88Ni8Zr4 alloy as well.

4 Summary and Concluding Remarks
While the reduction in grain size leads to enhanced mechani-
cal properties of metals and alloys, it also leads to prob-
lems pertaining to grain growth which, in turn, limits the 
processing techniques of nanocrystalline materials as well 
as their applications to only moderate and sometimes low 
temperatures. In this study, we aim to consolidate thermally 
stabilized Fe–Ni–Zr alloys utilizing high energy mechanical 
alloying in combination with high temperature equal chan-
nel angular extrusion and investigate the low and high tem-
perature mechanical properties as well as microstructural 
evolution. Despite the extreme consolidation conditions, i.e., 
high temperature, high shear, and high pressure, the average
