Deformation twinning is an important deformation mechanism in nickel-based superalloys. For super- 
alloys, deformation twins are generally observed at low or intermediate temperatures and high strain 
rates; however, the appearance of microtwins (MTs) at high temperatures has rarely been reported. In 
this study, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to study MT formation in Ni-Co-based su- 
peralloys following compression at 1120 °C/1 s −1 . The deformation behavior was discussed in detail to 
reveal the mechanism of MT formation. The twinning mechanism at elevated temperatures was theoret- 
ically attributed to the low stacking fault energy (SFE) and poor dislocation-driven deformations caused 
by the high strain rate in speciﬁc directions.

1. Introduction 
Owing to their superior mechanical properties, Ni-based super- 
alloys are widely used in the preparation of disks and turbine 
blades for aviation engines [1–3] . Deformation twinning is an im- 
portant strengthening mechanism that can enhance the mechan- 
ical properties of Ni-based superalloys via the formation of mi- 
crotwins (MTs), that act synergistically to increase strength and 
ductility. While the generation of MTs via creep or tensile defor- 
mation at low and intermediate temperatures has been extensively 
studied [4–8] , only a few cases of MTs appearing at high tempera- 
tures above 10 0 0 °C have been reported. Previous studies have pri- 
marily focused on the chemical compositions and the effect of the 
deformation conditions (i.e., creep stress, strain rate, or tempera- 
ture) on the generation of MTs in polycrystalline and single-crystal 
superalloys [9–11] . 
In Ni-Co-based superalloys, MTs synergistically improve the 
tensile strength and ductility in the intermediate temperature 
regime [ 12 , 13 ]; however, the deformation mechanism switches to 
dynamic recrystallization (DRX) under a high-temperature process-

2. Experimental procedures 
2.1. Materials 
Directionally solidiﬁed (DS) Ni-Co-based superalloys were pre- 
pared, and the potential MT formation mechanism at temperatures 
above 10 0 0 °C was discussed based on the crystallographic tex- 
tures of the samples. All specimens were homogenized at 1180 °C 
for 2 h before the hot compression tests to alleviate elemental seg- 
regation [27] . The normalized microstructure and the characteris- 
tic γ′ phases for the alloys are shown in Fig. 1 . Two sizes of γ′ 
phases were observed, namely, the coarse γ′ precipitates (approxi- 
mately 200 nm) and the larger-in-number ﬁne γ′ precipitates (less 
than 30 nm); the former precipitates were surrounded by the lat- 
ter, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (b). The nominal chemical compositions 
of the alloys are summarized in Table 1 . The superalloys used in 
this study contained a large amount of Co, which effectively in- 
creased the number of electron holes and resulted in a lower SFE 
[6] . 
2.2. Hot compression 
The compression axes applied to the cylindrical samples (diam- 
eter: 10 mm; length: 15 mm) were parallel, angled at 45 °, and per- 
pendicular to the columnar grain direction; hereafter, these sam- 
ples are referred to as the 0 °, 45 °, and 90 ° deformed samples, as 
shown in Fig. 2 (a). The compression tests were carried out at sub- 
solvus temperatures (1120 °C) and constant strain rates (1 s −1 ) us- 
ing a Gleeble 3800 thermal simulation machine, which is schemat- 
ically illustrated in Fig. 2 (b). The compression specimens were 
heated to the test temperature at a 10 °C/s rate and then held for 
10 min prior to compressing to a 50% reduction in height; this was 
followed by immediate water quenching to preserve the deformed 
microstructure. To ensure the accuracy of the results, at least two 
specimens were tested under each set of test conditions. 
2.3. DTA and adiabatic heating analysis 
Fig. 3 shows the differential thermal analysis (DTA) curves of 
the Ni-Co-based alloys during the heating process, which provide 
details about the γ′ phase solvus temperature of this alloy. The 
heating curve ( Fig. 3 (a)) indicates the two-phase transformation 
temperatures, along with the solution temperature of the γ′ phase 
of the Ni-Co-based alloy into the columnar grain matrix (1122 °C). 
Based on the DTA curves, the alloy was deformed under a strain 
rate of 1 s −1 at 1120 °C, which was close to the solvus temper- 
ature of the γ′ phase precipitates. In addition, during the uniax- 
ial hot compression tests, deformation heating inevitably occurred 
during hot deformation, a phenomenon also known as adiabatic 
heating. The deformation heating values were closely related to the 
stain rate; for example, at high strain rates (great than or equal to 
1 s −1 ), the actual temperature of the specimens increased signiﬁ- 
cantly owing to the effect of adiabatic heating. Therefore, for the 
purpose of accurately understanding the microstructure evolution 
mechanism, it is necessary to discuss the effect of adiabatic heat- 
ing on the increase of temperature. In summary, combined with 
the adiabatic heating occurring at high strain rates during hot de- 
formation, it can be determined that the γ′ phase in the deformed 
samples were completely dissolved before deformation.

2.4. TEM and EBSD observations 
Microstructural observations were carried out using an elec- 
tron backscatter diffraction (EBSD; Zeiss Merlin Compact equipped 
with an Oxford Symmetry detector) diffractometer and a FEI Ta- 
los F200X transmission electron microscope (TEM) operated at 
200 kV. Before EBSD, the samples were ﬁrst mechanically pol- 
ished and then electrochemically polished in a solution of H 2 SO 4 
(20 mL) + CH 3 OH (80 mL) at 25 V for 20–25 s at room temper- 
ature. The deformation mechanism was analyzed from the EBSD 
data captured using the TSL-OIM software. Based on the misori- 
entation angles of the adjacent grains, low-angle grain boundaries 
(LAGBs, 2 °–10 °), medium-angle grain boundaries (MAGBs, 10 °–15 °), 
and high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, > 15 °) were identiﬁed. To 
prepare samples for TEM, slices were sectioned through the center 
of the compressed specimens along the compression axis, followed 
by manual grinding to 50 μm, and then electropolishing in a solu- 
tion of 10% perchloric acid and 90% ethanol at 20 V and -25 °C. 
3. Results & discussion 
The true stress-true strain curves with respect to different com- 
pression axes are shown in Fig. 4 ; the deformed specimens are 
shown in the insets. The most noticeable feature is that the defor- 
mation of the samples exhibits direction dependence. Notably, sig- 
niﬁcant cracking was observed on the surface of the 0 ° deformed 
samples. The 45 ° and 90 ° deformed samples exhibit a distinctive 
ovality with evident slip traces on their bulged surfaces. These re- 
sults indicate that alloys with different deformation directions ex- 
hibit different deformation resistance. 
The EBSD data were used to analyze the inﬂuence of the crys- 
tallographic texture on the deformation mechanisms, as shown in 
Fig. 5 . Because the grain orientation spread (GOS) values of the re- 
crystallized grains were lower than those of the deformed grains, 
the DRX grains and deformed microstructures can be separated 
by selecting proper grain deﬁnition parameters. The GOS method 
has been commonly and successfully used to identify recrystallized 
and deformed grains from the EBSD data [31–33] . The obtained 
EBSD data were analyzed using the TSL-OIM software, as shown in

A few irregular patches at the initial grain boundaries (GBs) 
were found in the 0 ° deformed sample ( Fig. 5 (b, e)). A high LAGB 
density appeared around the distinctly bulging grain boundaries 
in the 0 ° deformed sample. This phenomenon potentially occurred 
owing to the irremovability of the initial GBs and the occurrence of 
bulging only under the applied load. As the strain increased, LAGBs 
accumulated in the bulging region and formed sub-boundaries, 
which isolated them and subsequently helped them transform into 
new HAGBs via the continuous absorption of dislocations [34] . 
Hence, the primary nucleation mechanism in the 0 ° deformed 
sample was dominated by discontinuous dynamic recrystallization 
(DDRX), which was characterized by the bulging of grain bound- 
aries. This is in good agreement with the low level of both cumula- 
tive and local misorientation shown in Fig. 6 (a, b), which indicates 
a relatively low level of stored energy inside the deformed grains 
[35] . 
In the 45 ° deformed sample, an apparent increase in the 
DRX fraction was detected with the typical “necklace structure”
( Fig. 5 (f)). This suggests that the prevailing slipping along the ini- 
tial GBs enhanced the local strain, which in turn promoted DRX 
grain nucleation along the initial GBs. In addition, as can be ob- 
served in Fig. 6 (c, d), the cumulative misorientation (point-to- 
origin) across the grain interior hardly exceeded 5 °, indicating a 
low degree of rotation and only slight development of the sub- 
structure [ 36 , 37 ]. Therefore, DDRX was conﬁrmed as the domi- 
nant nucleation mechanism in the 45 ° deformed sample. On the 
contrary, for the 90 ° deformed sample, the formation of LAGBs 
and MAGBs in the partial enlargement region inside the deformed 
grains was evident, as shown in Fig. 5 (d, g). This phenomenon sug- 
gested that the misorientation accumulates and the substructure 
with LAGBs transforms into new DRX grains inside the deformed 
grains through MAGB stage conversion with an increase in strain. 
Meanwhile, the misorientation angle within the grains surpassed 
15 ° ( Fig. 6 (e, f)), further conﬁrming the manifestation of a strong 
subgrain development inside the original grains, which indicates 
the occurrence of continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) in 
the subgrain substructure [ 38 , 39 ]. Consequently, CDRX was con- 
ﬁrmed to play a prominent role in the deformation behavior of the 
90 ° sample. In summary, the larger volume fraction of DRX in the 
45 ° and 90 ° deformed samples, compared with that of the 0 ° de- 
formed sample, corresponds well with the signiﬁcant softening of 
the ﬂow stress curves in Fig. 4 .

Christian and Mahajan [45] stated that the formation of MTs in 
Ni-based superalloys is directly related to the presence of superlat- 
tice extrinsic stacking faults (SESF) in the γ′ phase. Knowles and 
Chen [46] proposed that two a /2[110] dislocations located on ad- 
jacent {111} planes react to produce a /3 [ 21 ¯1 ] and a /3 [ ¯1 21 ] partial 
dislocations (on different {111} planes) under intermediate temper- 
atures and high-stress conditions. A SESF is produced when these 
two partials shear across the γ′ phase along the adjacent {111} 
planes, and the above process is repeated to produce MTs. It is 
worth noting that the γ′ dissolution temperature of this alloy is 
approximately 1122 °C, as shown in our previous DTA results. How- 
ever, the increase in adiabatic temperature led to the rapid disso- 
lution of the γ′ phase within a short timeframe, under the present 
test conditions. Therefore, in this work, the γ′ phase appears to 
have a less direct effect on the twinning mechanism. As such, the 
twinning mechanism discussed above is not applicable here. In- 
stead, the twinning process at high temperatures may be related 
to the low SFE and high strain rate deformation along the speciﬁc 
direction that were applicable in our experiments.

Concurrently, deformation twinning and cross-slip are consid- 
ered as dependent relief mechanisms. According to the classical 
view of deformation twinning in fcc crystals, this phenomenon pri- 
marily occurs at low temperatures, which is consistent with the 
nano-twinning observed in the GH3536 alloy by Ding et al. [23] . 
However, it was also observed in this study that the suppression 
of cross-slip events is enhanced by a lower SFE and a high strain 
rate deformation along speciﬁc deformation directions. Since the 
contraction of the extended screw dislocations is a prerequisite for 
the manifestation of cross-slip, when the dislocation contraction is 
hindered, the cross-slip of the screw dislocations is further sup- 
pressed [ 49 , 50 ]. Under such conditions, stress-relief mechanisms 
are typically not triggered, and therefore deformation twinning is 
activated as an alternative mechanism to reduce the increased in- 
ternal stresses caused by the plastic deformation. As a result, the 
suppression of cross-slip events enables the deformation twinning 
mechanism to occur at elevated temperatures. 
The front of the deformation twin, which is identiﬁed as an 
incoherent twin boundary (ITB) in Fig. 9 (b), strongly contrasts its 
surrounding region. The dislocation structure of ITBs can be brieﬂy 
described as an array of Shockley partial dislocations on adjacent 
{111} planes [51] . Previous investigations [52–54] proposed that 
the formation of an inhomogeneous stress ﬁeld, as a result of de- 
formation at a certain stress level, plays a crucial role in the nucle- 
ation of ITBs. In this work, following the formation of ITBs, Shock- 
ley partials were emitted (due to shear stress) along the directions 
indicated by the arrows, allowing for the release of the high local 
stress concentrations ( Fig. 9 (c)).

by the indenters at both ends. Therefore, in this case, we can con- 
sider the sample to be equivalent to a monocrystal, and the GBs 
only assisted the plane rotation that occurred during the slip pro- 
cess. On one hand, during the uniaxial compression of the crys- 
tal, the specimen cannot undergo slip deformation and freely off- 
set its axis, owing to the restriction by the indenters. Instead, the 
crystal plane rotated under the action of the force couple so that 
the slip planes gradually rotated perpendicular to the ingot axis. 
Consequently, the slip direction and resolved shear stress on the 
slip plane varied accordingly. Schmid analysis reveals that when 
ϕ = 45 °, the resolved shear stress on the slip system becomes 
maximum. When the value of ϕ deviates from 45 °, the resolved 
shear stress gradually decreases, reducing the tendency for further 
slip occurrence. On the other hand, the deformation process led 
to the activation of additional slip systems owing to plane rota- 
tion, forming dense DTs as a result. However, dislocation annihi- 
lation by cross-slip is diﬃcult to attain at such high strain rates, 
resulting in the accumulation of intragranular strain. The deforma- 
tion incompatibility of the grains and the GBs caused GB cracking; 
therefore, the existence of dense MTs in the 0 ° deformed specimen 
can be rationalized at such high temperatures. The matrix disloca- 
tions multiplied rapidly to accommodate the severe plastic strain 
originated from the restricted slip along the GBs. Additionally, the 
stress-relief mechanism was rarely triggered, due to the suppres- 
sion of cross-slip events. In parallel, the propagation of twinning 
partials through the LAGBs and ITBs led to the formation of MTs 
at elevated temperatures, compensating for the elastic strain ﬁeld 
that was generated inside the deformed grains. 
4. Conclusions 
Isothermal hot compression tests were performed on a DS Ni- 
Co-based superalloy in three orientations at a strain rate of 1 s −1 
at 1120 °C. The following conclusions are drawn from this study: 
(1) The recrystallization level during the deformation of the as-cast 
superalloy with directional columnar grains was highly sen- 
sitive to variations in the imposed load and strain direction. 
Loading at 45 ° along the columnar grain direction was bene- 
ﬁcial for deformation at high temperatures. 
(2) CDRX was the dominant mechanism in the 90 ° deformed sam- 
ples during hot deformation, whereas DDRX was conﬁrmed to 
be a secondary softening mechanism. An opposite phenomenon 
was observed for the 45 ° deformed samples, where DDRX dom- 
inated the nucleation mechanism.
