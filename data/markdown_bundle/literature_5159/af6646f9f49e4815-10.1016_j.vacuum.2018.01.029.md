abstract
A bulk Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy has been successfully produced using Fe, Cr, Al, Mn and B powders
by Mechanical alloying (MA) and Spark plasma sintering (SPS). Alloying behavior, microstructure and
phase evolution of the sintered specimen have been investigated by using X-ray diffractometer (XRD),
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). It is shown that a
supersaturated solid solution with body-centered cubic (BCC) structure is formed during MA. After
sintering, Fe
2AlCr, CrFeB-type boride and Mn 2B-type boride precipitate in the g-Fe matrix, and no
network structures are observed. The hardness, three-point bending strength and fracture toughness of
the sintered alloy are 54.8 HRC, 2080 MPa and 8.14 MPa/m
1/2, respectively. The sintered alloy shows
compressive strength of 2553 MPa at room temperature, and especially has high compressive strength of
948 MPa at 600 /C14 C. The high hardness and compressive strength can be attributed to solid solution of Al,
Cr and Mn, precipitation of Fe 2AlCr, CrFeB and Mn 2B in the g-Fe matrix. The dense nanocrystalline
microstructure produced by the combine of MA and SPS can also help improving mechanical properties
of the sintered alloy.

1. Introduction
Fe-Cr-B cast alloys taking boride M 2B( M ¼ Fe, Cr) as strength-
ening phase have attracted more and more attention [ 1e4] due to
their signi ﬁcant increase in hardness, wear resistance and corro-
sion resistance compared to the Fe-Cr matrix. However, a massive
hardness drop appeared at elevated temperatures for most of Fe-Cr
alloys [ 5,6], which resulted in inadequate support for M 2B
corrosion-wear-resistant skeleton and limited corrosion-wear
application of Fe-Cr-B alloys at elevated temperatures. It has been
demonstrated that Al can improve the hardness and red hardness
of steel [ 7e9], which is bene ﬁcial in improving the wear resistance
of steel at elevated temperatures. Al can also improve the corrosion
resistance of steel at elevated temperature by forming a highly
stable and protective oxide scale [ 10], and therefore Fe-Cr-Al alloys
are often used as high-temperature corrosion resistant materials
[11e13]. Recently, J. Ju et al. [ 14] found that network broke obvi-
ously with the increase of Al content in Fe-Cr-B-Al and a few D0 3-
ordered Fe3Al intermetallic precipitated in the matrix when the Al
content was increased to above 6 wt.%. Therefore, it is expected that
corrosion and wear resistance at elevated temperatures can be
improved by the addition of Al into Fe-Cr-B alloy.
Moreover, the boride shape, size and distribution signi ﬁcantly
affect overall hardness and wear performance of Fe-Cr-B alloys
[15,16]. In Fe-Cr-B cast alloys, M 2B exhibits continuous networks
forms along the grain boundaries, which destroys the continuity of
the matrix and reduces the strength and toughness of the alloy.
Many attempts [15e21] have been made to disrupt the network of
M2B, but it is dif ﬁcult to fully eliminate the eutectic boride network
along grain boundaries by these measures. Previous works [ 22,23]
indicated that Fe-Cr-B alloys with Fe 2B phases uniformly distrib-
uted in the matrix could be fabricated by powder metallurgy
method (P/M). Due to the formation of Fe 2B phases, these Fe-Cr-B
alloys prepared by P/M showed high hardness (698 HV~498 HV)
but low bending strength ( /C20 592 MPa) and obvious brittleness [ 22].
Spark plasma sintering (SPS) is a novel PM process that uses pulsed
high DC current with uniaxial pressure to rapidly consolidate
powders of metals, ceramics and composite materials [ 24]. SPS can
produce a densely bulk material with a minimal grain growth and a
controlled microstructure within a short time, which is helpful to
improve the hardness, strength and toughness of the compact* Corresponding author.

In view of the above discussion, the objective of the present
work is to explore the possibility of fabricating an Fe-Cr-B-Al alloy
with a combination of high hardness and strength as well as good
fracture toughness, which could be potentially used as corrosion
and/or wear materials at elevated temperatures. Previous study
indicated that moderate Mn addition can improve the fracture
toughness of Fe2B borides [27], and high content of Mn is bene ﬁcial
to form austenite microstructure with excellent corrosion resis-
tance, heat resistance and workability. To the authors' knowledge,
there is no detailed study on the addition of high content of Al and
Mn into Fe-Cr-B alloy. So, Fe-Cr-B-Al-Mn alloy with high content of
Al and Mn is selected as the target alloy in this study. In this study,
the MA process is used to fabricate the Fe-Cr-B-Al-Mn alloy pow-
ders. The main advantages of MA are its simple process, low cost
and ability to prepare supersaturated solid solutions or nano-
crystalline structure materials [ 28]. Subsequently, the powders are
consolidated by using SPS technique. Alloying behavior, micro-
structure and phase constitute of the sintered alloy are studied in
detail. Hardness, compressive strength, three-point bending
strength and fracture toughness are also evaluated. Hardness, wear
and corrosion resistance at elevated temperatures of this Fe-Cr-B-
Al-Mn alloy will be the subject of future papers.
2. Experimental details
Elemental powders of Fe, Cr, Al, and Mn with high purity of
99.9 wt.% and particles size <45
mm, and B with high purity of
99.9 wt.% and particles size <5 mm were used as starting materials.
Fe, Cr, Al, Mn and B powders were physically mixed in a composi-
tion of Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn (percentage by weight is used un-
less otherwise speci ﬁed). After being blended for 5 h using a
Turbula Mixer, MA of 100 g of the blend elemental powders was
performed at room temperature up to 50 h in a high energy plan-
etary ball mill (QM-3SP4). Stainless steel vials and balls (6 mm,
10 mm and 20 mm in diameter) were used. The weight ratio of the
balls-to-powder was 10:1 and the rotating speed was 300 rpm. To
prevent oxidation during MA, the vials were purged several times
and ﬁlled with pure argon gas at 0.4 MPa. Cyclohexane was used as
the process controlling agent (PCA). After being dried, the powders
were passed through a 200 mm sieve to eliminate larger particles
which could affect the following experiments. Then, the sieved
powder was placed in a cylindrical graphite die with an inner
diameter of 20.2 mm, and then sintered by SPS (Dr. Sinter 825,
Sumitomo Coal Mining Co. Ltd., Japan). The MAed powders were
sintered to 1 100 /C14 C with a holding pressure of 50 MPa in vacuum
(residual cell pressure < 8 Pa) and holding time of 10 min. The
samples were heated to 600 /C14 C within 6 min, while from 600 /C14 Ct o
10 0 0/C14 C and from 1000 /C14 Ct o1 1 0 0/C14 C, heating rates of 100 /C14 C/min
and 50 /C14 C/min were used, respectively. During the SPS process, the
die temperature Td was monitored using an infrared radiation
thermometer focused into a hole Ø 5 mm /C2 10 mm.
Phase constitute of the sintered alloy was characterized by X-ray
diffraction (XRD) using Cu Ka radiation (Bruker D8 Advance). Pow-
der morphologies were investigated by a scanning electron mi-
croscopy (Phenom proX, Phenom-World B.V., Netherlands) using
back scattered electron image (BSE) compositional mode. Micro-
structure of the sintered specimen was investigated by a scanning
electron microscopy (Zeiss Supra 40, Carl Zeiss NTS GmbH, Ger-
many) using back scattered electron image (BSE) compositional
mode. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was used to map the
distribution of elements of the sintered composite. Thin foil spec-
imens were prepared by mechanical thinning followed by ion
milling at room temperature and was analyzed by a transmission
electron microscopy (TEM, TECNAI G2 S-TWIN F20, FEI, USA) with
selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis. The thermal
analysis was carried out in a NETZSCH STA 449C differential scan-
ning calorimeter (DSC) at a heating rate of 10 /C14 C/min under ﬂowing
high purity argon atmosphere.
All the test specimens were cut from the sintered bulk alloys by
electrical discharge machining. Compression test specimens are
cylindrical with the dimension of Ø4 mm /C2 6 mm. The dimensions
of specimens for ﬂexural strength and fracture toughness were
2m m /C2 4m m /C2 16 mm and 2 mm /C2 4m m /C2 20 mm, respectively.
Room temperature compression tests were performed on an INS-
TRON 5569 testing system at a constant strain rate of 1 /C2 10/C0 3/s
according to GB/T 7314-2005 [ 29], whereas elevated temperature
compression testing was performed on a GLEEBLE 3800 testing
machine. Each data point is an average of at least three samples.
Bending strength and fracture toughness of the sintered sample
were measured by three-point bending method according to
ASTM-E1290 [ 30] by a Shimadzu precision universal testing ma-
chine (AG-X100KN). The bars were loaded with spans of 10 mm and
16 mm, and the crosshead speeds for bending strength and fracture
toughness tests were 0.5 mm/min and 0.05 mm/min, respectively.
The single-edge-notched beam method (SENB) was employed for
determining the fracture toughness of the samples. The room
temperature hardness of test sample was measured on HR-160A
Rockwell-hardness tester under load of 150 kgf and time of 15s.
The reported hardness value is an average of 5 measurements to
ensure the reproducibility of the hardness. Microhardness of each
phase within the specimen was measured by nanoindentation
conducted at room temperature using an Hysitron TI-950 nano-
indenter under a small load of 10 mN.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Alloying behavior
Fig. 1 shows the BSE images of Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy
powders at different stages of ball milling. The starting material
(Fig. 1a), which is a mixture of Fe, Cr, Al, Mn and B powder, exhibits
almost irregular shape particles of various size (5 e30
mm) except
spherical Al particles. After 5 h of milling ( Fig. 1 (b)), most of the
elements particles have disappeared. Big particles or agglomerates
having rough surface are observed, which suggests that both frac-
turing and welding operates at this milling time. It has been known
that successful mechanical alloying depends on a critical balance
between cold welding and fracturing which enables powder par-
ticles to be always in contact with each other with atomically clean
surfaces, minimizing diffusion distance [ 31]. In this work, process
control agents are used to prevent the soft metallic materials such
as Al from excessive cold welding during mechanical alloying. After
15 h of milling, the agglomerated particle size start to decrease
(Fig. 1(c)), suggesting the fracturing process begin to become more
dominant over agglomeration process with increasing milling time,
probably owing to solid solution hardening by mechanical alloying
as well as dispersion hardening of the composite powder with
milling time. With further milling for 30 h ( Fig. 1 (d)), the size of
particles further decreased and appeared more uniform distribu-
tion in particle size. As the ball milling time increased to 50 h, there
is no obvious change.

1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy powders. It can be seen that peaks cor-
responding to Fe (BCC), Cr (BCC), Mn (FCC) and Al (BCC) are
observed in the diffraction pattern of the as-mixed powder.

3.3. Mechanical properties of Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy
Fig. 9 shows the SEM and Gradient Force images of indentation
on all phases of the bulk Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy after the
nanoindentation test under a small load of 10 mN. It can be seen
that there are several indentation mark sizes. Table 3 lists Hardness
(H) and reduced elastic modulus (Er) of alloy and different phases.
The average Rockwell hardness of the SPS-ed Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-
15Mn alloy is 54.8 HRC, which is a combination of the hardness of
matrix and different precipitates as well as the interfaces between
these phases. The average hardness (H) of the CrFeB, Mn
2B and
Fe2AlCr particles are 22.15 GPa, 19.1 GPa and 8.04 GPa, respectively.
The average reduced elastic modulus (Er) of the CrFeB, Mn 2B and
Fe2AlCr particles are 260.93 GPa, 246.00 GPa and 201.37 GPa,
respectively. The difference in the hardness of these phases origi-
nates from the difference in the chemical composition and crystal
structure. The average hardness (H) and reduced elastic modulus

due to solid solution of Al, Mn and Cr in the metallic matrix.
Fig. 10 shows the compressive stress-strain curves of the sin-
tered Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy at room temperature and at
elevated temperatures (600/C14 Cand 800 /C14 C). The alloy undergoes 21%
plastic deformation prior to fracture and compressive strength is
2553 MPa at room temperature. It can be seen that the test tem-
perature of 600 /C14 C and 800 /C14 C resulted in a signi ﬁcant decrease of
the compressive strength of the examined alloy. As the test tem-
perature increases up to 600 /C14 C, the compressive strength of the
compact decreases gradually from 2553 MPa to 948 MPa. However,
this compressive strength value is adequate for many applications
at 600 /C14 C. At 800 /C14 C, the compressive strength of the compact de-
creases signi ﬁcantly to 130 MPa. Even so, the alloy show excellent
compressive plastic deformability and keep unbroken after 50%
plastic deformation. At ﬁrst, Al, Cr and Mn can increase the matrix
strength by means of solid solution hardening [ 41]. Second, several
hard intermetallics such as CrFeB, Mn 2B and Fe 2AlCr precipitated
uniformly in the matrix can not only act as an additional source of
high strength conferred to the sintered alloy by supporting the
applied load, but also reinforce the steel matrix by simultaneously
impeding dislocation migration [42]. Finally, the initial compressive
stress occurring in compression is bene ﬁcial to the closure of pores
in the sintered product, leading to the delaying onset of cracks at
the interface between matrix and reinforcement. The compressive
strength of the compact decreases signi ﬁcantly at elevated tem-
peratures especially at 800 /C14 C is mainly related to their micro-
structure and crystallographic structure. The matrix of the as-
sintered Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy is g-Fe based solid solution
with face cubic centered structure, which soft fast at elevated
temperatures due to the power law creep as a consequence of
dislocation climb [ 43] and results in the massive drop of
compressive strength at elevated temperatures. The three-point
bending strength and fracture toughness of the SPS-ed Fe-1 1Cr-
2.3B-6Al-15Mn are 2080 MPa and 8.14 MPa/m 1/2, respectively.
Previous works [ 27,44] indicated that the entrance of Cr and Mn
atoms (with larger atom radius than Fe atom) into Fe 2B lattice can
improve the fracture toughness of Fe2B by enhancing the density of
surrounding electrons for B atoms adjoining Cr and Mn atoms and
strengthening the weakest B-B bond. In addition, fracture me-
chanics theory points out that brittle fracture includes the initiation
and propagation of crack [ 45,46], which is determined by the
amount, morphology and size of reinforced phases. In this work,
the reinforced phases of CrFeB, Mn
2B and Fe 2AlCr are distributed
over the matrix in the isolated short rod and block and granular
forms rather than the networks. Since the crack is dif ﬁcult to
propagate along these reinforced phases, the as-sintered alloy
possess high strength and fracture toughness correspondingly.
4. Conclusion
Bulk Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy has been successfully fabri-
cated by MA and SPS using element powders Fe, Cr, Mn, Al, and B as
starting materials. The results show that the fabricated alloy in-
cludes Fe 2AlCr, CrFeB, Mn 2B and g-Fe based solid solution matrix.
The combined application of SPS and MA disrupts the formation of
continuous grain-boundary CrFeB and Mn 2B network. CrFeB and
Mn2B boride exhibit isolated rod-like and block forms in the Fe-
matrix. Due to the solid solution of Al, Cr and Mn as well as the
precipitation of Fe2AlCr, CrFeB and Mn 2B in the Fe-matrix, the SPS-
ed Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn alloy possesses high Rockwell hardness
of 54.8 HRC and high compressive strength of 2553 MPa at room
temperature. The sintered alloy still shows high compressive
strength of 980 MPa at 600 /C14 C. The three-point bending strength
and fracture toughness of the SPS-ed Fe-1 1Cr-2.3B-6Al-15Mn are
2080 MPa and 8.14 MPa/m1/2, respectively. The fracture toughness
