High cycle fatigue behaviour of Ti–6Al–4V alloy at
elevated temperatures
K. Tokaji *
Department of Mechanical and Systems Engineering, Gifu University, 1-1 Yanagido, Gifu 501-1193, Japan
Received 1 November 2005; received in revised form 20 February 2006; accepted 25 February 2006
Available online 27 March 2006
Abstract
High cycle fatigue behaviour of Ti–6Al–4V alloy was studied at 623 K and 723 K. Fatigue strength decreased at elevated temperatures
compared with at ambient temperature. In the short life regime, fatigue strength was lower at 723 K than at 623 K, but in the long life
regime it was nearly the same at both temperatures. At elevated temperatures, cracks were generated earlier at applied stresses below the
fatigue limit at ambient temperature, indicating lowered crack initiation resistance. Small cracks grew faster at elevated temperatures
than at ambient temperature, which became more noticeable with increasing temperature. After allowing for the elastic modulus, small
cracks still grew faster at elevated temperatures.

1. Introduction
Alpha/beta Ti–6Al–4V alloy has been widely used in the
aeronautical industry, because of its high speciﬁc strength
and good balance of strength and ductility. It possesses
other excellent properties such as weldability and corrosion
resistance and also moderately high temperature resistance,
and so it has been used in compressor blades and disks in
gas turbines up to 573 K [1]. In order to further improve
operating eﬃciency and save fuel of light-weight machines
and structures, Ti–6Al–4V alloy is strongly expected to be
used in a wide variety of applications not only at ambient
temperature but also at elevated temperatures. Hence, it is
necessary to further understand the high-temperature
fatigue properties of this alloy system.
Studies on fatigue properties at elevated temperatures
have inevitably focused on near-alpha titanium alloys that
are classiﬁed as high temperature resistance alloys; thus
there have been a limited number of studies on the fatigue
behaviour at elevated temperatures in Ti–6Al–4V alloy [2–
4]. In particular, crack initiation and small crack growth at
elevated temperatures in high cycle regime have not been
studied. One of the reasons for this may be due to the
experimental diﬃculties of monitoring such behaviour in
detail.
In the present study, fully reversed axial fatigue tests
were performed on a Ti–6Al–4V alloy at ambient temper-
ature, 623 K and 723 K and the fatigue behaviour at
elevated temperatures is discussed on the basis of crack
initiation, small crack growth and fracture surface
analysis.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Material
The material used was a Ti–6Al–4V alloy, the same as
employed in previous works [5–7]. The chemical composi-
tion (wt.%) is N 0.08, O 0.16, H 0.04, Fe 0.19, C 0.006,
Al 6.29, V 4.13, Y < 0.001. Annealing treatment was
carried out at 978 K for 2 h followed by air cooling.

2.2. Specimen
Fatigue specimens with 6 mm width, 4 mm thickness

was parallel to the rolling direction. On one side of the
specimen, a shallow notch with 0.5 mm depth was intro-

tion and subsequent crack growth. Before fatigue testing,
the specimens were mechanically polished with emery
paper and then electropolished.

2.3. Procedures
Fully reversed axial fatigue tests were performed on an
electro-hydraulic fatigue testing machine operating at a
sinusoidal frequency of 10 Hz. Test temperatures evaluated
are ambient temperature, 623 K and 723 K in laboratory
air.
An induction heating system was used to control the
temperature of the specimen. Prior to fatigue testing, tem-
perature calibration was conducted between the centre of
the specimen and a location 20 mm away from the center.
Based on this temperature calibration, the temperature at
the latter position was controlled to give the desired tem-
peratures at the center of the specimen during experiment.
The accuracy of the temperature control was within 1% of
the desired temperatures.
Crack initiation and subsequent crack growth were
monitored with a replication technique. Experiments were
interrupted and the specimen was cooled down to ambient
temperature and then replicas were taken before tempera-
ture rise. This procedure was repeated until the crack
length reached a critical size. Prior to experiment, it was
conﬁrmed that there was no signiﬁcant diﬀerence in fatigue
life between the interrupted and consecutive tests.
After experiment, fracture surfaces were examined in
detail using a scanning electron microscope (SEM).
2.4. Mechanical properties
The tensile strengths obtained using fatigue specimens
were 602 MPa and 402 MPa at 623 K and 723 K, respec-
tively, indicating a considerable degradation of strength
with increased temperature.
SEM examination revealed that at ambient temperature,
the fracture surface was totally covered with dimples, while
at elevated temperatures, ductile dimples decreased with
increasing temperature and smooth facets became the pre-
dominant features. Hence, the reduction in tensile strength
at elevated temperatures may be attributed to this brittle
nature of the microstructure.

3.2. Fatigue crack initiation
Fig. 3 shows SEM micrographs revealing fatigue crack
initiation at a low applied stress at 623 K and 723 K. At
ambient temperature, cracks invariably initiated in the
alpha phase as indicated in a previous report [8]. On the
other hand, at elevated temperatures, multiple cracks initi-

crack initiation site in microstructure could not be identi-
ﬁed because of surface damage by oxidation and the diﬃ-
culty of detecting a crack immediately after initiation.
Since it has been indicated that oxide formation and oxy-
gen diﬀusion were enhanced at grain boundaries and phase
interfaces [9], it is believed that cracks initiated at alpha/
beta interfaces [10].

rial parameter inﬂuencing FCP behaviour. Since E
decreases with increasing temperature, its eﬀect on FCP
behaviour at elevated temperatures has to be considered.
In the present study, the E values were not determined,
thus they were obtained to be 115 GPa at ambient temper-
ature, 97 GPa at 623 K and 90 GPa at 723 K from a liter-
ature report that presented the temperature dependence of
E for Ti–6Al–4 V alloy annealed at 1023 K [11]. The crack
growth rates are characterized in terms of K

intrinsic crack growth resistance would be lowered by
temperature, but the eﬀects of the extrinsic factors such
as crack closure, crack branching and deﬂection, and
microcracks should be further studied.

3.5. Elevated temperature-assisted small crack growth
Diﬀerent growth mechanisms operate at elevated tem-
peratures rather than ambient temperature. As is well
known, oxide ﬁlm formed on the fracture surface can pro-
mote crack closure. In this study, however, it seems that the
extent of oxidation was not signiﬁcant at both tempera-
tures. If the eﬀect of oxide ﬁlm is considered, then crack
closure induced by oxide ﬁlm would decrease the actual
crack tip driving force. As indicated in SEM micrographs
of fracture surfaces, many secondary cracks were recog-
nized at elevated temperatures, suggesting that crack
branching frequently took place. Crack branching and
deﬂection also decrease the actual crack tip driving force.
Therefore, the eﬀects of crack closure due to oxide ﬁlm
formation and crack branching and deﬂection are com-
pletely contrary to the enhanced crack growth rate
observed at elevated temperatures.
On the other hand, as a factor contributing to the accel-
eration of crack growth rate, creep has to be taken into
account. However, a close analysis of the fracture surfaces
revealed no intergranular fracture even at 723 K, thus the
eﬀect of creep is less important in the present study.
At elevated temperatures, multiple cracks initiated and
grew. Such examples are shown in Fig. 8 Therefore, it is
believed that interference and linkage of such cracks would
be responsible for the acceleration of crack growth rate.

When some cracks are present in front of the main crack,
the crack growth rate of the main crack becomes faster
due to interference and transient increase of crack growth
rate takes place due to coalescence, leading to the enhanced
average crack growth rates at elevated temperatures.
Furthermore, diﬀerent fracture mechanisms can operate
at elevated temperatures from those that take place at
ambient temperature. Since fracture surfaces were brittle
with increasing temperature and applied stress as seen in
Fig. 7 , crack growth occurred in a brittle manner at
elevated temperatures. This may be attributed to oxygen
penetration into the crack tip region, particularly into the
alpha/beta interface.
4. Conclusions
1. The fatigue strengths at elevated temperatures decreased
signiﬁcantly compared with that at ambient tempera-
ture. In the short life region, the fatigue strength
decreased with increasing temperature, while in the long
life region, it was nearly the same for both temperatures.
2. The fatigue strength characterized in terms of the fatigue
ratio also decreased at elevated temperatures compared
with at ambient temperature.
3. Crack initiation occurred relatively much earlier at ele-
vated temperature, thus most of fatigue life was domi-
nated by small crack growth.
4. The small crack growth rates at elevated temperatures
were considerably faster than those at ambient tempera-
ture, increasing with an increase in temperature.
5. Even though the elastic modulus was taken into
account, small cracks still had signiﬁcantly faster growth
rates at elevated temperatures.
6. Fracture surfaces at elevated temperatures were rather
brittle compared with those at ambient temperature
and many secondary cracks were recognized on fracture
surfaces, which were more noticeable with increasing
temperature and applied stress.
