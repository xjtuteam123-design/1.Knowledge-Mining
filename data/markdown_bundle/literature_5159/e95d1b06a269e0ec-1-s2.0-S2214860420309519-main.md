ABSTRACT 
The microstructure and cracking behavior of pure tungsten and micron- and nanosized Y 2O3 dispersion 
strengthened tungsten fabricated by selective laser melting are comparatively studied in this work. The pure 
tungsten with high density (98.3 ± 0.3 %) is successfully fabricated by optimizing processing parameters, but 
there are still a few microcracks. The cracking mechanism of pure tungsten is attributed to the segregation of 
tungsten oxide impurities at the grain boundaries and the formation of high-angle grain boundaries. The 
agglomeration of Y
2O3 occurs only in the tungsten with the addition of micron-Y 2O3 while not in the tungsten 
with the addition of nanosized Y 2O3. The addition of Y 2O3 can reduce cracks due to the formation of a large 
number of low-angle twisted tungsten grains. The cracking mechanism of pure tungsten and the inhibition of 
cracking by the addition of Y
2O3 proposed in this work are of great significance for the SLM manufacture of high- 
performance and crack-free tungsten-based alloys. Our work may also provide relevant strategy for the sup -
pression of crack in other alloys with high melting point by SLM. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten (W), a transition metal with the highest melting point of 
~3420 ◦C, has widely used for plasma-facing components for divertors 
in future nuclear fusion devices and high-performance rocket nozzles in 
the past years [1–3], due to its excellent mechanical and physical 
properties, such as the high density (19.35 g/cm 3), the low thermal 
expansion coefficient, the high thermal conductivity, the low sputtering 
yield, high neutron load capacity and low tritium retention [4–7]. 
Tungsten with strong directional covalent bonds has a high 
ductility-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) (200 400 
◦C), 
resulting in poor fabricability of tungsten [8]. It is typically manufac -
tured by conventional methods, such as powder metallurgy (PM) [9] and 
powder injection molding (PIM) [10]. However, the above conventional 
methods have a limitation in producing components with complex in -
ternal and external structures [11]. It is necessary to develop a new 
method to fabricate tungsten parts with aim of easily broadening the 
application range of tungsten. 
Selective laser melting (SLM), as an additive manufacturing tech -
nique, has been attracted great attention in past years, which can pro -
duce parts with complex structures in near net shape to reduce 
production cost and improve processing efficiency [12–14]. Therefore, 
tungsten components with complex structures can be manufactured by 
SLM, such as a collimator [15,16]. It is still impossible to obtain fully 
dense and crack-free tungsten parts by SLM up to now, resulting in the 
limited applications of the SLM produced (SLMed) tungsten components 
[17–19]. Pure tungsten with a high melting point displays a high surface 
tension and high cohesive energy during the SLM process, resulting in 
the formation of balling phenomena of liquid tungsten [20]. The pres -
ence of impurities in the SLM process harms the grain boundary strength 
of tungsten, which is prone to cracking [21]. The above problems will 
seriously depress the density and mechanical properties of pure tung
-
sten. In response to the above problems, the introduction of 
secondary-phase particles is generally used to eliminate the defects of 
the SLMed alloy and improve its mechanical properties [22–24]. The 
secondary-phase particles that play a role of dispersion strengthening in 
tungsten matrix mainly include TiC [25–30], La
2O3 [31–33], Y 2O3 
[34–40] and ZrC [18,41,42] particles, which improves the thermal 
shock resistance and the high-temperature strength of the alloy [18]. 
Therefore, it is necessary to select the dispersion-strengthened sec
-
ondary-phase particles to introduce tungsten during the SLM process.

Y2O3 as the secondary-phase particles on the microstructure refinement, 
crack suppression, and texture formation of pure tungsten under a wide 
range of large processing parameters is studied in detail.

2.2. SLM process and specimen characterization 
The above three kinds of powders were all processed using a 
Renishaw AM400 SLM system (Renishaw Plc, UK) equipped with a 
400 W continuous Nd: YAG fiber laser and a laser beam diameter of 
70 μm. The SLM process of all the above powder was carried out in a 
flowing argon atmosphere (purity 99.999 vol%), and the initial residual 
oxygen content in the chamber was less than 0.1 vol% to reduce the 
oxidation. To reduce the effect of thermal stress on the specimen during 
the SLM process, the scanning rotations between adjacent layers of 67

orthogonal method is used to optimize suitable processing parameters

3.2. Mechanisms of crack initiation and propagation in SLMed pure 
tungsten 
To study the mechanism of crack initiation and propagation in 
SLMed pure tungsten specimens in detail, pure tungsten specimens with 
obvious cracks were selected. As shown in Fig. 4, it can be determined 
from the fresh fracture surface of the pure tungsten specimen that the 
cracking of tungsten is obviously brittle fracture formed by the existed 
microcracks along the grain boundary. The small droplet-like particles 
with a size of about 1 
μm form at the tungsten grain interface. It can be 
concluded through the EDX analysis of the small droplet-like particles at 
points 1 and 2 marked in Fig. 4(b) that they are mainly composed of the 
elements of tungsten and oxygen, thereby determining the impurities of 
tungsten oxide formed at the grain boundaries of pure tungsten. Oxygen 
will react with liquid tungsten and generate gaseous low melting point 
tungsten oxide impurities in the molten pool (the melting point of WO
3 
and WO2 are 1837 ◦C and 1730 ◦C, respectively), during the SLM process 
of pure tungsten (melting point: 3420 ◦C) [17]. The solubility of oxygen 
in solidified tungsten is extremely low, causing the oxygen to continue to 
accumulate in the unsolidified residual liquid in the middle of the 
molten pool (near the laser action point) [46]. Gaseous tungsten oxide 
impurities are retained at the grain boundaries of pure tungsten to form 
the segregated tungsten oxide impurities at the grain boundaries, due to 
the strong convection and high heat gradient in the molten pool. The 
presence of tungsten oxide impurities can increase the DBTT of tungsten 
to 400 
◦C [46], which reduces the surface energy [2] and decrease the 
strength of the grain boundary [47–49]. The thermal stress generated by 
the high cooling rates of the SLM can only be compensated by the 
cracking formed at these low-strength grain boundaries, rather than by 
uniformly distributed plastic deformation, due to the existence of 
segregated impurities at the grain boundary. 
The grain morphology and cracks of pure tungsten specimens were 
studied by EBSD analysis, as shown in Fig. 5. It can be clearly seen that 
the crystal grains grow from the boundary to the center of the scanning 
track (perpendicular to the direction of laser advance). The width of the 
crystal grain is about 50 
μm. It can be seen from Fig. 5 (a) and (b) that 
the network-like crack is constituted by the straight cracks propagating 
along the boundaries and cracks across the molten pool. The length of 
the crack can extend to hundreds of microns. It can be clearly seen from 
the image quality map of the pure tungsten specimen shown in Fig. 5(c) 
that these grain boundaries, which are easy to crack, are determined to 
be high-angle grain boundaries (>15
◦, as shown by the blue line). There 
are almost no cracks at the low-angle grain boundaries of pure tungsten 
(< 15
◦, as shown by the red and cyan line). The reason why the low- 
angle grain boundary can effectively prevent crack propagation is its 
meandering grain boundaries. It can be seen from the Fig. 8(b) that the 
high-angle grain boundary occupies 49.1 % of the whole grain boundary 
of pure tungsten from the overall field of view. The average grain size of 
pure tungsten can be determined from Fig. 8(a) as 18.5 
μm. It can be 
seen from Fig. 5(d) that the Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) values 
around the formed crack are lower than that of other crack-free regions, 
thus implying that the local plastic deformation of the specimen is

grains is not arbitrary. It is more necessary to coordinate the deforma -
tion between adjacent grains for the large-angle grain boundaries [46,

will hinder the movement of tungsten grain dislocations, which is not 
conducive to the uniform plastic deformation of brittle tungsten [51]. 
Therefore, the thermal stress in the SLM process cannot be eliminated by 
the uniformly distributed plastic deformation through dislocation mo

tion. On the contrary, the local plastic deformation caused by thermal 
stress may be concentrated on the high-angle grain boundaries. The 
high-angle grain boundaries would become the source of stress cracks, 
thereby causing intergranular cracks. 
The mechanism of crack initiation and propagation of pure tungsten 
during the SLM process can be clarified through the above analysis. 
Oxygen and liquid tungsten react in the molten pool to form gaseous low 
melting point tungsten oxide impurities during the SLM process. Tung-
sten oxide impurities accumulate at the grain boundaries of tungsten 
grains under the influence of Marangoni convection, due to the differ
-
ence in density and melting point of gaseous eutectic impurities and 
pure tungsten. Low-melting liquid tungsten oxide films formed between 
solidified tungsten grains can cause thermal cracking. Liquid tungsten 
oxide films solidify into the droplet-like solid tungsten oxide particles, 
due to the Plateau-Raileigh criterion during further solidification, which 
can seriously decrease the grain boundaries strength [47–49] and 
sharply raise the DBTT of the specimens [46]. The strength of the formed 
high-angle grain boundary with tungsten oxide impurities is weaker 
than the required force to cause the initiation and the movement of the 
dislocation and the plastic deformation, due to the liquid films and the 
solid tungsten oxide particles formed in sequence during the cooling 
process. Therefore, thermal stress cannot perform large-range uniform 
plastic deformation through dislocation movement at high-angle grain 
boundaries, but causes stress to concentrate on the crack source area 
(the high-angle grain boundaries with tungsten oxygen impurities) to 
form the microcracks. Subsequently, the cracks extend along the long 
and straight grain boundaries under the combined effect of thermal 
stress and micro-stress generated by the anisotropy in the thermal 
expansion coefficient and the elastic properties of the bcc tungsten unit 
cell between adjacent tungsten grains [50]. Eventually, the pure tung
-
sten manufactured by SLM forms network cracks in the grain boundary.

3.3. The effect of micron- and nanosized Y 2O3 particles on the 
microstructure of tungsten 
After clarifying the mechanism of crack initiation and propagation of 
pure tungsten, the effect of micron- and nanosized Y 2O3 on the micro-
structure and density of pure tungsten under a wide range of processing 
parameters was further explored. The microstructure of μm-ODS-W and 
nm-ODS-W is selected in the volumetric energy density range of 
600 1000 J/mm
3, as shown in Fig. 6. It can be clearly seen from Fig. 6 
(a) that the agglomeration of Y2O3 with a size of about 10 microns oc -
curs in μm-ODS-W specimens, and W-Y-O ternary compounds with 
nano-scale are also formed inside the tungsten grains. There is a serious 
difference in the concentration of elements in the molten pool leading to 
strong Marangoni convection, due to the addition of spherical micron- 
Y
2O3. Y2O3 with lower density (5.01 g/cm3) compared to the tungsten, 
tends to diffuse along the boundary of the molten pool to the upper half 
of the molten pool, resulting in the agglomeration of the second phase 
particles. The hardness of the 
μm-ODS-W specimen is 386 ± 7 HV, due to 
the segregation of micron-Y2O3, which is significantly lower than that of 
pure tungsten. Therefore, the addition of micron-Y2O3 is not suitable for 
tungsten manufactured by SLM. 
As can be seen in Fig. 6(c), no agglomerated Y 2O3 particles are 
observed in the nm-ODS-W specimen. The nm-ODS-W specimen pos -
sesses fewer, shorter and narrower cracks at grain boundaries compared 
with that of pure tungsten, which indicates that the addition of nano -
sized Y 2O3 particles can suppress the initiation and propagation of 
cracks in pure tungsten. W-Y-O ternary compounds with nano-scale can 
also be observed inside the tungsten grains, as shown in Fig. 6(d). The 
hardness of the nm-ODS-W specimen is 462 ± 5 HV, which is attribut-
able to the dispersion strengthening effect of the nano-secondary phase 
particles.

4. Conclusion 
The effects of SLM processing parameters on the microstructure, 
density, and hardness of pure W, μm-ODS-W, and nm-ODS-W are studied 
in detail. The crack initiation-propagation mechanism in SLMed pure 
tungsten and the effect of the addition of micron- and nanosized Y2O3 on 
the microstructure of tungsten are investigated. The main conclusions 
are as follows: 
1) Pure tungsten specimens with high-density (98.3 ± 0.3 %) and a few 
cracks can be manufactured by SLM in the volumetric energy density 
range of 600 1000 J/mm
3. However, pure tungsten still has cracks 
that cannot be eliminated by adjusting the processing parameters. 
2) The crack initiation-propagation mechanism of SLMed pure tungsten 
is mainly attributed to two reasons: (i) the formation of droplet-like 
tungsten oxide impurities segregated at grain boundaries, which 
depresses the grain boundary strength and greatly increases the 
DBTT of tungsten, and (ii) the formation of a large number of high- 
angle grain boundaries that are difficult to deform uniformly. The 
thermal stress is released by the formation of cracks at the high-angle 
grain boundaries with tungsten oxide impurities under the synergy of 
these two factors during the solidification process. Then the crack 
extends along the long and straight grain boundaries to form a 
network-like crack. 
3) The agglomeration of Y
2O3 occurs in the μm-ODS-W specimen due to 
the strong Marangoni effect caused by the concentration difference, 
while the agglomeration of Y 2O3 does not occur in the nm-ODS-W 
specimen. The addition of micron-and nanosized Y 2O3 has no 
obvious refinement effect on tungsten grains, whereas the addition of 
Y2O3 can reduce cracks due to the formation of a large number of 
low-angle twisted tungsten grains.
