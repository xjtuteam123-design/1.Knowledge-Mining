Abstract
Al–Nb alloys present considerable strength and hardness, being promising materials for structural applications and for
coatings. This paper reports results of a study of the structure and properties of Al–Nb surface alloys produced by laser alloying.
Surface alloys were produced by a two-step process: ﬁrstly, commercial purity Al substrates were alloyed with Nb by injecting an
Al–25 wt.% Nb powder mixture into the melt pool generated using a CO
2 laser, and, secondly, the microstructure was reﬁned by
laser melting. In the ﬁrst step, Nb concentrates near the surface of the melt pool during solidiﬁcation, resulting in the production
of surface layers about 700 mm thick of Al–37.6 ( 93) wt.% Nb alloy. These alloys are heterogeneous and present porosity and
undissolved Nb particles for all the processing parameters used. Their microstructure is formed of large dendrites of Al
3Nb and
interdendritic a-Al solid solution. Laser melting of the alloyed layers results in complete elimination of defects and homogenisa-
tion of the material. Previously undissolved Nb particles dissolve, raising the Nb concentration in the alloy and increasing the
volume fraction of Al
3Nb. The microstructure still consists of Al 3Nb and a-Al solid solution but the dendrites are ﬁner and the
volume fraction of interdendritic a is less than 5%. The Vickers microhardness of the surface alloys is high owing to the large
amount of this intermetallic compound. It varies from 480 to 650 HV, depending on the volume fraction and dendrite spacing of
Al
3Nb. Despite the brittleness of Al 3Nb no cracks were observed, probably because of the presence of the thin interdendritic ﬁlm
of ductile Al solid solution, which enables better accommodation of the generated stresses. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights
reserved.

1. Introduction
High-strength aluminium alloys are widely used in
high-performance automobiles, railway cars, aircraft
and spacecraft, light ships, etc., owing mainly to their
excellent mechanical strength, low speciﬁc weight, good
formability and relatively low cost. However, the wide-
spread use of these materials has been limited by their
low wear resistance, particularly at high temperatures.
This limitation accounts for the interest in surface
modiﬁcation of aluminium alloys, in particular using
lasers and electron beams.
Surface melting of aluminium alloys with high-pow-
ered laser beams produces very ﬁne microstructures
with improved hardness and corrosion resistance [1].
However, the improvement in properties is only moder-
ate and may not justify the cost increase arising from
the laser treatment. To achieve signiﬁcant improvement
in properties, the alloy must be hardened with precipi-
tates that do not coalesce signiﬁcantly during long-term
exposure to temperatures within the range 100–300°C.
Also, the precipitates must be small and densely dis-
tributed within the matrix. Al
xMy (M, transition metal)
intermetallic compound particles suit the requirements
well for long-term strengthening at moderately high
temperatures, since transition metals present low diffu-
sivity in aluminium, and therefore materials formed of
an Al
xMy phase dispersed in an a-Al solid solution
matrix are inherently temperature resistant.

Surface treatment of aluminium alloys by laser alloy-
ing with Cr, Mo, Ni, Ti, Si, Fe and Cu has been
reported by several authors [2–8] and leads to consider-
able improvement in corrosion and wear resistance and
in surface hardness. It has been found in previous work
that laser alloying of Al with Cr [7] and Mo [6] leads to
the formation of hard and corrosion-resistant surface
layers consisting of a ﬁne dispersion of intermetallic
compounds in a matrix of a-Al solid solution. Further-
more, preliminary results obtained in laser alloying of
Al with Nb [9] suggest that Nb is a very promising
alloying element to improve the wear resistance and
strength of aluminium alloys, since alloying with Nb
leads to the formation of a considerable proportion of
Al
3Nb, a hard intermetallic compound that melts con-
gruently at 1680°C and presents a vanishing homogene-
ity range [10]. Due to its body-centred tetragonal DO
22
crystallographic structure and to its deformation mech-
anism [11], Al
3Nb is extremely brittle up to its brittle-
to-ductile transition temperature (1000°C) [12] and its
presence may impair the toughness of the alloy, but
Al–Nb alloys produced by laser processing were crack-
free and reasonably tough [9]. Attempts to improve the
ductility of Al
3Nb by transforming the DO 22 structure
into a more ductile ternary ordered L1 2 variety by
replacing some of the aluminium with alloying elements
such as Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, etc. [13,14] were not
successful in the case of Al
3Nb [15,16] and ab initio
calculations of the structural energy of L1 2 and DO 22
structures showed that the energy difference between
L1
2 and DO22 structures is too large for the transforma-
tion to be possible for Al 3Nb [17–19]. Another ap-
proach to improve the ductility of Al 3Nb is to decrease
the order parameter by rapid solidiﬁcation [20]. Studies
of the constitution and phase structure of Al–Nb alloys
produced by rapid solidiﬁcation techniques lead to
contradictory results. Sircar et al. [21] studied the struc-
ture of Al–Nb and Al–Nb–V alloys produced by laser
cladding using Al
3Nb pre-alloyed powder. The authors
reported two new metastable phases, a primary phase
with a body-centred tetragonal structure and I4 :mmm
space group, and a phase with a base-centred or-
thorhombic structure and Cmmm space group that
appeared as a eutectic. The formation of these
metastable phases is attributed to non-equilibrium so-
lidiﬁcation due to the rapid cooling rates involved in
laser cladding [21], but previous work carried out by
the present authors using similar processing techniques
and parameter ranges failed to conﬁrm these conclu-
sions: the alloys consisted only of the equilibrium
phases Al
3Nb and a-Al solid solution [9,22]. This result
is in agreement with previous observations of Frasier et
al. [16], who found that Al–Nb–M (M Fe, Ni, Cu)
alloys with compositions similar to Al
3Nb produced by
rapid solidiﬁcation consist only of the equilibrium
phases. Furthermore, a detailed study of the crystallog-
raphy of Al
3Nb reported in a previous publication [23]
proved that Al 3Nb is almost completely ordered in the
solidiﬁcation speed range 0–25 mm s 1. Therefore, no
signiﬁcant improvement in ductility should be expected
by this mechanism in this range of solidiﬁcation speeds.
The absence of cracking in Al–Nb surface alloys
produced by laser alloying was explained by the exis-
tence of a thin interdendritic ﬁlm of ductile a-Al solid
solution, which accommodates stresses and prevents
crack propagation. Reip et al. [12] showed that the
toughness of Al
3Nb-based alloys was improved when a
relatively soft phase formed between Al 3Nb grains since
cracks stopped at the soft phase boundaries. These
authors also suggest that a way to further improve the
alloys’ toughness is to promote the formation of a ﬁner
distribution of Al
3Nb, suggesting that laser processing
may suit the requirements well for tough Al–Nb alloy
coatings production.
In the present paper, we demonstrate the possibility
of producing surface layers with high hardness and
sufﬁcient toughness for practical applications by laser
alloying with Nb.

Laser alloying was performed by the blown powder

1, corresponding to an

was achieved by overlapping consecutive tracks 50% of
the width of a single track. In order to eliminate defects
in the laser alloyed surface layers, to homogenise the
structure and to vary the solidiﬁcation speed in the
required range, a second step was used. The material

4. Discussion
An unexpected feature of the Al–Nb surface alloys
produced in the present work is the presence of a
layered structure formed of a bottom layer consisting of
nearly pure resolidiﬁed a-Al solid solution with a den-

layer consisting of dendrites of Al
3Nb and an interden-

homogenisation of the melt pool. Mazumder and co-

crostructure in most of the re-solidiﬁed layer can be

parameters and should not change signiﬁcantly. The
results obtained in the present work show that the
microstructure of Al–Nb alloys with composition simi-
lar to Al
3Nb, produced by laser processing using so-
lidiﬁcation speeds up to 40 mm s 1, consists only of the
phases predicted by the equilibrium phase diagram
published by Kattner [31], i.e. a-Al and Al
3Nb. How-
ever, the peritectic reaction predicted by that phase
diagram does not occur. The residual Al-rich liquid
solidiﬁes as an interdendritic ﬁlm of a-Al solid solution,
without reacting with Al
3Nb. The present results are in
agreement with those of Frasier et al. [16], who found
that the constitution of ternary Al–Nb–M (M Fe,
Ni, Cu) alloys with compositions near to Al
3Nb, pro-
duced by rapid solidiﬁcation and arc melting, are simi-
lar and consist of Al
3Nb with the DO 22 structure and
a-Al. By contrast, the conclusions of Sircar et al. [21],
who claim to have observed two previously unreported
metastable Al–Nb phases (a body-centred tetragonal
primary phase and a base-centred orthorrombic eutectic
phase) in Al–Nb and Al–Nb–V alloys produced by
laser cladding, in conditions that lead to solidiﬁcation
parameters similar to those used in the present work,
are not supported. It is interesting to note that some
phases in the Al–Nb system previously interpreted as
non-equilibrium phases were later shown to be ternary
equilibrium phases formed due to the presence of un-
controlled impurities in the alloys. For example, Barth
and Sanchez [32] claimed to have found what they
presumed to be an Al–Nb metastable phase that
seemed to result from the transformation of eutectic
Al
3Nb during ageing treatments at 1200 and 1350°C of
Al–Nb alloys containing between 57 and 70 at.% Nb,
produced by mechanical alloying, but this phase was
later shown to be the stable compound Nb
3Al2N, with
a b-manganese type structure [33]. A similar explana-
tion could be suggested for the unexpected results of
Sircar et al. [21].
The relative peak intensities of the Al
3Nb X-ray
diffractograms obtained from polished bulk samples are
very different from the theoretical values, due to the
presence of a strong solidiﬁcation texture in the laser-
processed material. The higher intensity of the (002)
and (004) peaks shows that in the laser-processed sam-
ples the (001) planes are predominantly parallel to the
surface. The texture effect increases with increasing
solidiﬁcation speed. The origin of this texture can be
understood by considering the solidiﬁcation mechanism
in laser surface treatment and the variation of the shape
of the melt pool as a function of scanning speed. The
microstructure observed in laser processed Al–Nb al-
loys is dendritic, showing that Al
3Nb grows with a
dendritic solid–liquid interface. In this type of growth,
there is a preferential growth direction, usually a low-
index crystallographic direction. During the initial
stages of solidiﬁcation competition between neighbour-
ing dendrites with different orientations occurs, and
those with the preferential growth direction nearest to
the heat ﬂow direction will be favoured, leading to
preferential orientation, and eventually to a strong tex-
ture. The melt pool created when a laser beam scans the
surface of a material is semi-hemispherical at low scan-
ning speed and becomes increasingly elongated as the
scanning speed increases. Since the heat ﬂow direction
is perpendicular to the solid–liquid interface, when the
scanning speed is low the heat ﬂow direction changes
progressively from the fusion line to the surface, leading
to a variety of preferential growth directions of colum-
nar grains. At high scanning speed, the shape of the
melt pool is elongated and the orientation remains
approximately constant along most of the depth on the
laser-processed layer. Since X-ray diffraction integrates
the effect of a surface layer a few microns thick, the
texture effect will be more noticeable at high scanning
speed than at low scanning speed. After ﬁlling, the
particles of Al
3Nb become randomly oriented, minimis-
ing texture effects and leading to a better agreement
between the relative intensities of the experimental and
calculated diffraction patterns.

These hardnesses are higher than those reported by
previous authors for Al
3Nb [16,32], a result that is
explained by the ﬁner dispersion of intermetallics
formed during remelting, since the hardness values
obtained in the alloyed layers are similar to those
reported by these authors.
Despite the brittleness of the Al
3Nb intermetallic
compound [11], no cracks were observed in the Al–
Nb surface alloys produced in the present work,
which is probably due to the existence of the thin
interdendritic ﬁlm of Al solid solution between the
Al
3Nb dendrites. The Al solid solution is soft and
ductile and contributes to better accommodation of
the generated stresses stopping the propagation of mi-
crocracks that may develop in the intermetallic. A
similar effect was found by Reip et al. [12] who stud-
ied the deformation of Al
3Nb in Al–Nb–Ni alloys
and concluded that the presence of a small volume
fraction of a relatively soft NiAl solid solution be-
tween the Al
3Nb grains increases ductility at low tem-
peratures as long as deformation is not too high.
5. Conclusions
Laser alloying of Al with Nb powder produces very
hard surface layers. However the distribution of Nb is
not homogeneous and the alloyed layers tend to
present undissolved Nb particles and pores.
The structural characteristics of the surface layers
are largely improved by laser remelting: most defects
are eliminated and the material is homogenised.
After remelting the surface layers contain 38.4–43.5
wt.% Nb. Their structure consists of ﬁne dendrites of
Al
3Nb intermetallic compound and a small volume
fraction of interdendritic a-Al solid solution.
The laser alloyed layers present very high hardness
that varies in the range 480–650 HV depending on
the primary dendritic spacing and on the a-Al volume
fraction. No cracking is observed, showing that their
toughness is acceptable.
