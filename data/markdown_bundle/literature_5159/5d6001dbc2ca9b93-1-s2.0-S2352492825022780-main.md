1. Introduction
Since being proposed by Yeh, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) and 
medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) are well-received due to their diversi -
form composition selection and favorable mechanical performance [1, 
2] . Until now, the majority of reported HEAs and MEAs with equal 
atomic ratios usually exhibit a single face-centered cubic (FCC) or 
body-centered cubic (BCC) structure with a high symmetry [3,4] . 
Compared with the latter, the former usually display excellent plasticity 
which is beneficial to mechanical processing, but insufficient strength at 
room temperature which greatly limits their applications as the struc -
tural components. Therefore, various strengthening methods, including 
solid solution strengthening [5] , dislocation strengthening [6] , grain 
refinement strengthening [7] , precipitation strengthening [8] , 
twin-induced plasticity strengthening and transformation-induced 
plasticity strengthening [9] are individually or jointly introduced in 
HEAs and MEAs to boost their comprehensive mechanical properties [5, 
10] . Among these methods, grain refinement and precipitation mecha -
nisms are found to be more efficient in the strengthening of alloys, 
especially when they synergistically work [4,11,12] .
Adding the substitutive or/and interstitial elements is a preferred 
and economical way to achieve the combination of grain refinement and 
precipitation strengthening in HEAs and MEAs, which not only could 
control the grain size and the species and quantity of precipitates, but 
also could additionally provide solid solution and dislocation strength -
ening effects [13 – 18] . Among various strengthening elements, the sub -
stitutive elements such as Al, Ti, and Si [19,20] and the interstitial 
elements such as B, C, and N [21 – 24] are frequently reported to perform 
excellently in the mechanical property optimization of alloys. The 
former ones mainly conduce to the formation of compounds, and the

3.1. Comparison of as-cast M, BM, ATM, and BATM alloys
The XRD patterns and tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the 
as-cast M, BM, ATM, and BATM alloys are shown in Fig. 1 (a) and Fig. 1
(b), respectively. As shown in Fig. 1 (a), all four alloys exhibit a single-

the yield and ultimate tensile strength of BM, ATM, and BATM alloys

tively strengthen by simultaneously introducing the moderate intersti -
tial and substitutive elements without the obvious structure change.

3.2. Effect of annealing temperature on the microstructure and 
mechanical properties of BATM alloy
The previous research results show that all the M, BM, and ATM 
alloys could achieve a favorable strength-plasticity balance through the 
proper thermo-mechanical treatment. Therefore, the BATM alloy was 
also experienced the cold rolling and recrystallization annealing pro -
cess. The XRD patterns of BATM alloy with different states are shown in 
Fig. 2 . As can be seen, both the as-cast and D90-BATM alloys present a 
single FCC structure, indicates that no phase transformation occurs 
during the rolling process. Whereas the position of diffraction peaks 
belonging to the FCC structure shifts slightly to the right due to the 
lattice deformation. In comparison, both the diffraction peaks owing to 
the FCC and BCC phases are detected in all annealed alloys, and the 
diffraction peaks intensity of BCC phase in D90-H800-BATM and D90- 
H900-BATM alloys is obviously higher than that in other alloys. In 
addition, the diffraction peaks of FCC phase move to the right first and 
then to the left with an increase in the annealing temperature. According 
to the Bragg diffraction equation, it is known that the lattice constant of 
FCC phase decreases first and then increases. The change of lattice 
constant in FCC phase may be related to its composition variation caused 
by the formation of BCC phase [35] .
The SEM images of annealed BATM alloys are shown in Fig. 3 , which 
clearly display the microstructure evolution characteristics of all 
annealed alloys. As shown in Fig. 3 (a-c), the granular precipitates with a 
size range from tens of nanometers to several microns are observed both 
in the grains and grain boundaries of all annealed alloys. Apparently, 
both the quantity and size of precipitates observably increase with an 
increasing in annealing temperature from 600 ℃ to 800 ℃ . Then the 
size of precipitates keeps increasing but the quantity gradually declines, 
as shown in Fig. 3 (d-e). The precipitates in D90-H800-BATM and D90- 
H1000-BATM alloys exhibit the maximum level in quantity and size, 
respectively. Combining the XRD patterns, the precipitates are deemed 
to be the BCC phase.
The tensile engineering stress-strain curves and the speculate me -
chanical property values of BATM alloy with different states are shown 
in Fig. 4 . The yield and ultimate tensile strength of the as-cast BATM 
alloy is 341 MPa and 613 MPa respectively, and the elongation is 
39.4 %, which exhibit a similar level as the regular FCC-structured al -
loys. In comparison, the yield strength of D90-BATM alloy is as high as 
1208 MPa, but the elongation is only 2.9 %, indicating a remarkable 
work hardening capacity. Both the yield strength of D90-H600-BATM 
and D90-H700-BATM alloys is higher than that of D90-BATM alloy. 
Combining the XRD and SEM results, it is speculated that the BCC- 
structured precipitates contribute to the strength improvement of 
these two alloys. With an increase in annealing temperature from 600 ℃ 
to 1000 ℃ , the yield and ultimate tensile strength decreases from 
1503 MPa and 1544 MPa to 575 MPa and 793 MPa respectively, and the 
elongation increases from 3 % to 27.5 %. In addition, the yield strength 
of D90-H1000-BATM alloy is still higher than that of the as-cast alloy, 
signifying an incomplete recrystallization process. The yield strength of 
D90-H800-BATM alloy is 1155 MPa and the elongation is 8 %, which 
shows a relatively favorable strength and plasticity combination. Ac -
cording to the mechanical property level, the BATM alloy could be 
simply divided into three categories: the high strength alloy, the 
favorable strength-plasticity matching alloy, and the good plasticity 
alloy, which could be represent well by the D90-H600-BATM, D90- 
H800-BATM, and D90-H1000-BATM alloy, respectively.

The BCC-structured precipitates with a size range from tens of 
nanometers to several microns form in grains and grain boundaries in all 
annealed BATM alloys. Both the quantity and size of precipitates reach 
the maximum level when the annealing temperature increases to 800 ℃ , 
and then decrease with a further increase in annealing temperature. The 
precipitates are enriched in Cr element, and significantly contribute to 
the high yield strength of alloys. Compared with the as-cast alloys, the 
annealed M, BM, ATM, and BATM alloys exhibit an obviously increase in 
yield strength. In addition, the BM, ATM, and BATM alloys also exhibit 
an obviously increase in yield strength than the M alloy with the same 
conditions, specially the BATM alloy annealed at 800 ℃ displays almost 
twice in yield strength than the annealed M alloy. The strength 
enhancement of annealed BATM alloy is mainly attributed to the syn -
ergistic effect of dislocations, fine grains, as well as the BCC-structured 
precipitates, by which the yield strength increments provided was 
189 MPa, 214 MPa, and 452 MPa, respectively. All of these research 
results would provide useful reference for the designing of high- 
performance HEAs and MEAs.
