Body-centred-cubic BCC-superalloys are a nascent materials class following a microstructure template of a dis-
ordered BCC matrix (e.g. refractory metal, Fe, Ti) reinforced by coherent precipitates that adopt a BCC-derived 
ordered superlattice structure, to enable enhanced high temperature performance. This is by analogy to the highly 
successful Ni superalloys whose properties are underpinned by their microstructure template of 𝛾/𝛾′ FCC-Ni solid 
solution and ordered Ni3Al.
BCC-superalloys represent a powerful Beyond Nickel Based Superalloy design approach for high temperature appli-
cations from aerospace engines and rockets to fusion energy. Various BCC-superalloy base systems oﬀer increased 
melting points, oxidation/corrosion/irradiation resistance, reduced density and/or cost advantages. However, 
challenges exist in phase stability, low temperature ductility, oxidation/corrosion resistance and the availability 
of predictive tools.
This BCC-superalloys Viewpoint Set addresses key challenges, theories and opportunities across the following 
overarching topics: Modelling, Phase transformations, Lattice misﬁt, Mechanical behaviour, Deformation mech-
anisms, Oxidation/corrosion, and Additive manufacturing.
Among metallic alloys, superalloys are the embodiment of materi-
als for extremes. Such face-centred-cubic (FCC) Ni-based superalloys are 
critical materials enabling present high energy-eﬃciency turbine engine 
designs for power generation, aerospace, and other extreme applica-
tions. They exhibit unparalleled creep, strength and corrosion resistance 
at application temperatures as high as 80% of their solidus temperature. 
However, almost a century after their ﬁrst discovery, incremental per-
formance improvements through alloy development are approaching a 
limit, being ever harder to realise [1]. However, increased maximum ap-
plication temperature is not necessarily the only design goal with many 
advanced technologies also demanding lower density, increased corro-
sion/oxidation/irradiation resistance as well as reduced cost or reliance 
on critical materials.
A promising idea to advance upon Ni-based superalloys has been to 
take inspiration from the successful FCC superalloys’ microstructure tem-
plate of coherent-precipitate-strengthening, 𝛾/𝛾′ (FCC-Ni/L11-Ni3Al), 
see Fig. 1, which fundamentally gives rise to the super properties of high 
temperature strength resistance that deﬁne them.
The FCC-superalloys microstructure template has successfully been 
transferred to other FCC base elements: for example to Co-based su-
peralloys [2] with a slightly higher melting point compared to Ni (Co 
T𝑚 = 1495 °C, Ni T𝑚 = 1455 °C) where work is still ongoing [3,4];

just had the ﬁrst dedicated workshop& conference symposium.

The BCC-superalloy microstructure template has been demonstrated 
across a range of systems, with varied aims of: maximum temperature 
capability, low density, environmental resistance and/or low cost:
– W-based BCC-superalloys strengthened by TiFe precipitates [10],
– Nb-superalloys with (Pd,Ru)Al type reinforcement [11],
– Ta-TaRu [12]and Ta-TaRe [13],
– Al-free refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs) with 
(Ti,Hf)-Ru [14],
– Refractory high entropy superalloys (RSAs) [15,16] and the family 
of (Ta,Nb,Mo)-(Ti,Zr,Hf)-Al alloys [17], and related systems [18,19],
– 𝛽-Titanium superalloys reinforced by TiFe [20],
– Cr-based BCC-superalloys reinforced by NiAl [21,22],
– Ferritic superalloys utilising B2 NiAl and/or L21 Fe2Al(V,Ti) (e.g. 
Fig. 1), which have been the most extensively studied of BCC-
superalloys [23–25],
– CoCrFeMnNi-Al type high entropy alloy alumindes comprising com-
positionally complex BCC matrix & (Ni,Co)Al type precipitates [26].
Wider systems exist that may not meet the BCC-superalloy deﬁnition, 
but can oﬀer signiﬁcant learnings for the ﬁeld:
– Monolithic intermetallics, namely B2 and L21 compounds [27,28]
– Eutectic alloys: such as Mo-NiAl [29], Cr-NiAl [30] and Ti-TiFe [31].
Applications that would beneﬁt from or even rely on BCC-superalloy 
materials are widespread, from gas turbines including new fuel cycles 
such as hydrogen, supercritical steam / CO2 turbines, heat exchang-
ers, next-generation ﬁssion reactors including for space applications, 
and fusion reactors ﬁrst wall & breeding blanket. Various communi-
ties have arrived at the potential of BCC-superalloys, from the Ni-based 
superalloy community, to refractory metal, high-entropy alloy and in-
termetallics communities. However, researchers have each come upon 
similar challenges, such as phase stability, the brittle-to-ductile transi-
tion temperature common to BCC metals, poor ductility in some BCC-
derived intermetallics, or the catastrophic oxidation behaviour of some 
of the prospective base elements, and the limited availability of opti-
mised CALHPAD databases. Despite these challenges, there is great po-
tential to harness enhanced capability and design ﬂexibility from BCC-
superalloys across: temperature capability, strength, creep resistance, 
oxidation/corrosion/wear resistance and irradiation damage resistance.
In this Viewpoint Set on BCC-superalloys, we seek to align re-
searchers in this emerging ﬁeld, outlining the achievements, current 
theoretical understandings and challenges across focused Viewpoints 
on: Modelling [32], Phase Transformations [33], Lattice Misﬁt [34], 
Mechanical Behaviour [35], Deformation Mechanisms [36], Oxidation 
& Corrosion [37] and Processibility by Additive Manufacturing [38]. 
By bringing together these diverse perspectives from BCC-superalloy re-
searchers across key topics, this Viewpoint Set highlights the critical 
overarching challenges in the ﬁeld, with the aim of catalysing onward 
research that overcomes these barriers to accelerate BCC-superalloys de-
velopment.
