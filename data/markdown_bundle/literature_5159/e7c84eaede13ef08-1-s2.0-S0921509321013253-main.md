ABSTRACT 
NbMoTaW-based refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are now at the research frontier of advanced metallic 
materials due to outstanding mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. However, the poor plasticity of 
NbMoTaW-based RHEAs at room temperature hinders further development. The present work proposes a new 
strategy to tailor in-situ forming heterogeneous structures and improve the room-temperature mechanical 
properties of NbMoTaW-based RHEAs. A novel series of as-cast Zr
x
NbMoTaW ( x = 0.1, 0.5, and 1, denoted by 
Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1, respectively) RHEAs was designed and prepared. The effect of Zr on the mechanical 
properties and microstructure evolution was investigated. The Zr1 alloy had a compressive yield strength of 
1558 MPa and a strain of 15.8% at room temperature, superior to most reported NbMoTaW-based RHEAs. 
Surprisingly, the Zr1 alloy also showed an ultra-high strength at 1000 
◦
C, which compressive yield strength was 
555 MPa without fracture at the strain of 25%. Moreover, all Zr
x
NbMoTaW RHEAs were composed of two 
disordered body-centered cubic (BCC) phases. The nanoindentation results showed that the hardness of the 
dendrite exceeded that of the interdendritic region. Such heterogeneous structure of the Zr1 alloy had a hetero- 
deformation-induced enhancing effect on strength and plasticity. The heterogeneous structure was mainly 
triggered by increased mixing enthalpy, severe lattice distortion, and non-equilibrium solidification. The double 
cross slipping was the dominant deformation mechanism of the Zr1 alloy, which served as a new dislocation 
source besides the Frank-Read one. Accordingly, the dislocation multiplication was promoted, enhancing the 
strength and plasticity. This study successfully introduced a high-strength and high-plasticity NbMoTaW-based 
RHEA, gaining more insight into the alloy design strategies of RHEAs. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) were introduced in 2010 by 
Senkov et al. [ 1 ] as lucrative high-temperature materials. RHEAs have 
become a hot spot in metallic materials due to their good microstructure 
stability and mechanical properties at elevated temperatures [ 2 – 4 ]. For 
example, Zou et al. [ 2 ] reported that the nanocrysallites of the NbMo -
TaW RHEA remained intact (nanoscale) after a heat treatment at 
1100 
◦
C for three days. High melting temperatures (T
m
) and slow 
diffusion effects of constituent elements are the main reasons for the 
microstructure stability of RHEAs. On the other hand, the 
first-generation VNbMoTaW RHEA possesses the attractive compressive 
yield strength of 477 MPa at 1,600 
◦
C [ 4 ]. The current research results 
show that the deformation mechanisms of body-centered cubic (BCC) 
RHEAs significantly differ from those of conventional BCC alloys [ 5 – 11 ]. 
Plastic deformation in RHEAs is mainly controlled by the edge disloca -
tions at elevated temperature, while that in conventional BCC alloys is 
dominated by screw dislocations [ 7 ]. The unique slipping mechanism of

RHEAs is considered as the origin of excellent mechanical properties at 
elevated temperatures [ 5 – 11 ]. However, the further development of 
NbMoTaW-based RHEAs is seriously restricted by their poor plasticity at 
room temperature (RT). For example, the RT-compressive malleability 
of the NbMoTaW is only ~2%, which makes its processing very prob -
lematic [ 4 ]. 
The heterogeneous structure (HS) composed of soft and hard regions 
was proved to be an effective approach for improving malleability 
[ 12 – 15 ]. Metallic materials with HS have a hetero-deformation-induced 
(HDI) effect on enhancing strength and malleability via back-stress 
hardening [ 12 ]. Liu et al. [ 15 ] produced superplastic bulk metallic 
glasses composed of hard regions surrounded by soft regions, which 
exceptional HS ensured a high compressive strain of over 160%. To 
obtain HS, complicated processes, i.e., rolling deformation and 
subsequent-partial-recrystallization annealing, need to be explored 
laboriously [ 12 ]. How to design in-situ forming HS during solidification 
remains a huge challenge. One of the preconditions is to tailor 
dual-phase microstructure. To achieve this goal in the NbMoTaW-based 
RHEAs system, we carefully choose zirconium (Zr) as a doping element 
from three aspects. Firstly, Zr has a positive mixing enthalpy with Ta 
[ 16 , 17 ]. While the effect of mixing entropy on phase selection in HEAs is 
important, the effect of mixing enthalpy is also indispensable. During 
solidification, the effect of enthalpy is likely to overpower the effect of 
entropy, especially at relatively low temperatures. Accordingly, phase 
separation will be triggered by the positive mixing enthalpy between the 
Zr – Ta binary system [ 16 , 17 ]. Secondly, Zr atoms have a larger atomic 
radius than Nb, Mo, Ta, and W atoms [ 18 ]. It was proposed that 
Zr-containing RHEAs are prone to generate phase separation due to 
severe local lattice distortions and the charge transfer effect [ 18 ]. 
Thirdly, Zr has a low valence electron concentration (VEC). Previous 
research via first-principles calculations showed that reducing average 
VEC can transform intrinsically brittle BCC refractory alloys into ones 
with intrinsically ductile behavior [ 19 – 25 ]. Therefore, the addition of Zr 
has the potential to improve the malleability of NbMoTaW-based 
RHEAs. 
Given the above, we designed and prepared a novel series of RHEAs 
with the composition of Zr
x
NbMoTaW ( x = 0.1, 0.5, and 1; denoted by 
Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1, respectively) to tailor in-situ forming HS. First, the 
microstructure evolution and mechanical properties were investigated. 
Furthermore, the mechanism of phase separation, solidification path, 
and the origin of high strength and plasticity were discussed. This pa -
per ’ s findings are considered instrumental in the RHEAs design 
optimization.

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure evolution 
Fig. 1 (a – g) illustrates the microstructure evolution of the as-cast 
ZrxNbMoTaW alloys. Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1 alloys presented a typical 
dendritic microstructure. The bright-contrast dendritic (DR) region and 
the dark-contrast interdendritic (ID) region were respectively identified 
as two disordered body-centered cubic (BCC) phases having different 
lattice constants according to the selected area electron diffraction 
(SAED) analysis [ Fig. 3 (b – c)] and XRD pattern ( Fig. 4 ). The ID volume 
fractions obtained by the Image-Pro software [ 27 ] were 11.3, 14.9, and 
23.8% for Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1 alloy, respectively. According to the 
backscattered electron imaging principle, the elements in the 
dark-contrast region were the ones with small atomic numbers and vice 
versa. As shown in Fig. 2 (SEM mapping) and Table 1 (EDS results), the 
ID was enriched with Zr and Nb and depleted in W, Ta, and Mo. Pecu -
liarly, a few particles were found in the ID region of the Zr0.1 alloy and 
identified as pure Zr by the EDS. In contrast, the DR region was enriched 
with W, Ta, Nb, and Mo and depleted in Zr. The Zr fractions in the DR 
region of Zr1, Zr0.5, and Zr0.1 alloy were 8.8, 0.5, and 0.3%, 
respectively.

4.3. The origin of high strength and plasticity 
Hetero-deformation-induced (HDI) hardening is an effective strategy 
to break the strength-plasticity trade-off [ 12 ]. Most HDI hardening 
metallic materials are prepared by complicated processes, including 
rolling and partially recrystallized annealing to fabricate HS consisting 
of soft and hard regions [ 41 ]. To explore the origin of the high strength 
and plasticity of the Zr1 alloy, a nanoindentation test composed of ~900 
test points was conducted. As shown in Fig. 7 (a), the nanoindentation 
hardness of the Zr1 alloy had two distribution ranges. The results of the 
corresponding statistical analysis are depicted in Fig. 7 (b). The bound -
ary between the soft and hard regions of hardness was preset at 6.5 GPa. 
The nanoindentation hardness of the DR and ID regions were 8.32 ±
1.51 and 3.53 ± 2.71 GPa, respectively. The DR hardness exceeded the 
ID one because the latter was mainly composed of Zr (~65%) with good 
ductility but low hardness. In this study, the Zr1 alloy had an in-situ 
forming HS including a hard BCC1 phase and a soft BCC2 phase. The 
unique in-situ forming combination of the soft and hard region had an 
HDI-hardening effect on enhancing strength and plasticity. The Zr1 alloy 
with a high volume fraction of the BCC2 phase achieved optimal HDI 
effect. 
Fig. 8 (a – d) shows the microstructure of Zr1 alloy at compressive 
plastic deformation of 8%. The density of dislocations in the ID region 
exceeded that in the DR one [ Fig. 8 (a)] because the hard DR contributed 
less to the plastic deformation than the soft ID at a plastic strain of 8%. A 
group of typical cellular dislocations can be observed in Fig. 8 (b), 
revealing that cross slip was the dominant plastic deformation mecha -
nism. Moreover, the DR region contained tangled dislocations providing 
a dislocation strengthening effect and a few dislocation rings, as shown 
in Fig. 8 (c) and (d), respectively. 
In conventional BCC refractory alloy systems (such as W- and Mo- 
based alloys), the addition of subgroup elements has a softening and 
plasticity-enhancing effect [ 42 ]. Alloying elements facilitate the effi -
ciency of double cross-slip. Therefore, dislocation mobility accordingly 
rises [ 42 ]. In the Zr1 alloy, local chemical fluctuations (LCFs) promoted 
by the addition of Zr had a similar solution-softening effect [ 43 ]. The 
BCC structure is not close-packed. Therefore, the dislocation can slip on 
different planes belonging to the < 111 > zone, e.g., the (011) and (112) 
planes [ 44 ]. LCFs can facilitate the cross slips due to the continuous 
change of energy barrier for dislocations slipping [ 43 ]. The dislocation 
rings in Fig. 8 (d) are formed by the double cross-slip mechanisms of 
screw dislocations, which schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 8 (e). 
When the < 111 > screw dislocation slip is obstructed, the LCFs will 
cause the obstructed slip plane to convert to another slip plane with low 
energy barrier to keep slipping [ 43 ]. The screw dislocations slip in a new 
slip plane, whose direction is the same as the original plane when the 
screw dislocations bypass the obstacles. Meanwhile, a pinned disloca -
tion dipole forms and further evolves into dislocation rings. These 
dislocation rings obtained by double cross-slip serve as new dislocation 
sources, besides the Frank-Read sources, and raise dislocation ’ s nucle -
ation and multiplication [ 44 , 45 ]. Accordingly, the Zr1 alloy possessed

5. Conclusions 
In this work, a novel ZrNbMoTaW RHEA with in-situ forming HS was 
designed and prepared. Furthermore, the effect of Zr on the mechanical 
properties and microstructure evolution was investigated. The main 
conclusions are described as follows: 
(1) The Zr1 alloy exhibited superior strength and plasticity both at 
RT and 1000 
◦
C. Specifically, the compressive yield strength and 
plastic strain of the Zr1 alloy at RT were 1589 MPa and 15.8%. 
The compressive yield strength of Zr1 alloy at 1000 
◦
C was 555 
MPa without fracture at a strain of 25%. 
(2) The strength and plasticity of the Zr1 alloy exceeded all reported 
classic NbMoTaW-based RHEAs at RT. The excellent mechanical 
properties of the Zr1 alloy resulted from (i) the HDI effect and (ii) 
enhanced dislocations ’ multiplication and mobility. 
(3) The Zr
x
NbMoTaW ( x = 0.1, 0.5, and 1) RHEAs were composed of 
two BCC solid-solution phases. The Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1 alloys 
had lattice constants of 0.3256, 0.3256, and 0.3250 nm for the 
BCC1 phase versus 0.3520, 0.3538, and 0.3597 nm for the BCC2 
phase, respectively. Zr
x
NbMoTaW RHEAs displayed a typical 
dendrite microstructure. The lattice misfit of the DR-ID interface 
was 7.60%, indicating that the interface was semi-coherent. The 
ID volume fractions of Zr0.1, Zr0.5, and Zr1 alloys were 11.3, 
14.9, and 23.8%, respectively. 
(4) Several empirical parameters predicted that Zr
x
NbMoTaW 
RHEAs formed solid solutions without intermetallic compounds. 
In addition, phase separation in the Zr
x
NbMoTaW RHEAs was 
triggered by increasing mixing enthalpy and lattice distortion 
from the standpoint of thermodynamics. 
(5) A few pure Zr precipitates were found in the Zr0.1 alloy. The 
degree of segregation in the ID decreased with the increasing 
content of Zr. The mechanisms of phase segregation were also 
caused by non-equilibrium solidification. The Zr addition 
changed the kinetic and thermodynamic solidification 
conditions.
