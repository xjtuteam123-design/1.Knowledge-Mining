ABSTRACT
We report the tensile properties and microstructural evolution of a high entropy alloy (FeCoCr) 40Ni40Al10Cu10
deformed at low temperature (233 K) and room temperature (293 K). The alloy presented a superior combination
of tensile strength (~1200 MPa) and elongation (~36%) at 233 K than at 293 K. Transmission electron micro-
scopy revealed that the enhanced tensile properties at low temperature were due to deformation twinning, the
formation of immobile Lomer-Cottrell dislocation locks, and the high density coherent nano-precipitates.
1. Introduction
The new concept of high entropy alloys (HEAs) was originally
proposed by Yeh et al. [ 1] to create extremely hard and ductile alloys.
Face-centered cubic (FCC) single phase HEAs, represented by the
equiatomic FeCoCrNiMn alloy, exhibit excellent ductility even at
cryogenic temperatures [ 2], but the strengths are hardly comparable to
those of conventional metallic materials [ 3]. To expand the practical
structural applications of such types of HEAs, novel preparation
methods are essential to achieve a desirable combination of strength
and ductility. Recent studies indicated that adding a small amount of
Al, Ti, Cu or Mo to the FCC single phase matrix induced phase se-
parations, and generated compounds, such as the L1 2 (γ′), B2, σ and μ
intermetallic compounds, which exhibited outstanding mechanical
properties [ 4–7]. Surprisingly, there have been only few reports on the
development of ultrahigh strength FCC-based HEAs, which maintained
good ductility at low temperatures [ 8]. Analyzing the above inter-
metallic compound phases, the L1
2 nano-precipitates, which were em-
ployed for strengthening Ni-based superalloys, embedded coherently
within the FCC matrix, and were observed in many Al-containing HEAs
[9–12]. Also the stability and behavior of the L1 2 nano-precipitates has
been previously investigated [ 13–16]. Zhang et al. [ 17] revealed the
Ni45-x(FeCoCr)40(AlTi)15Hfx HEAs exhibited great compressive and
tensile strength both at room temperature and elevated temperatures
due to the presence of coherent L1 2 nano-particles. Thus, L1 2 nano-
precipitates could be the appropriate phase to enhance ductility and
strength of HEAs. However, the precise underlying mechanisms of the
HEAs containing L1
2 nano-precipitates at relatively low temperatures as
well as their in ﬂuence on the mechanical properties of these HEAs have
not been well identi ﬁed. In this context, the present study aimed to
clarify the in ﬂuence of high density L1 2 nano-precipitates on the tensile
deformation in FCC-based HEA at room temperature (293 K) and low
temperature (233 K). The tensile properties related to di ﬀerent under-
lying deformation mechanisms at both temperatures were uncovered
using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
2. Experimental Procedures
To achieve the exclusive precipitation of L1
2 phase in the FCC
matrix, the composition of the alloy was slightly adjusted to be
(FeCoCr)40Ni40Al10Cu10 (in at.%) by increasing the Ni and Al contents
as well as lowering the Fe, Co and Cr contents, based on our previous
work [ 18]. The samples were produced by arc melting followed by
homogeneous annealing at 1200 °C for 24 h and cold rolling. The cold
rolled sheets were annealed at 800 °C for 1 h followed by annealing at
600 °C for 10 h to obtain high-density nano-precipitates in the FCC
matrix. Tensile tests were performed at an engineering strain rate of
1×1 0
−3 s−1 on a Zwick/Roell Z100 testing machine. The tests were
carried out at 293 K and 233 K. Liquid nitrogen was used to stabilize the
tensile specimens at 233 K. Three specimens were tested at each of
these temperatures to con ﬁrm the reproducibility of the results. Flat
dog-bone shaped tensile specimens with a gauge length of 10 mm,

3. Results
3.1. E ﬀect of Temperature on Tensile Properties of (FeCoCr)
40Ni40Al10Cu10
Alloy
Tensile stress-strain curves of the (FeCoCr) 40Ni40Al10Cu10 alloy at
both 293 K and 233 K are illustrated in Fig. 1. It can be seen from Fig. 1a
that the alloy exhibited a good combination of strength and elongation
at both test temperatures. It is worth noting that the tensile strength of
the sample at 233 K reached a maximum of 1168 MPa, which was ~8%
higher than that at room temperature (293 K) which was 1082 MPa.
Meanwhile, the corresponding tensile elongation values remained
constant. In addition, the tendency of the two curves ( Fig. 1a) emerged
that the divergence was about 25% engineering elongation. To con ﬁrm
this, we extracted the work hardening rate and plotted it as a function
of the true strain ( Fig. 1b). It is readily seen that the two curves ﬁt well
until a true strain of 0.2, beyond which an abrupt increase occurred for
the 233 K sample compared to the case of the 293 K sample. The dif-
ferent work hardening rates implied that di ﬀerent deformation me-
chanisms dominated at di ﬀerent temperatures. Thus, microstructure
characterization was carried out to determine the microscopic de-
formation mechanisms.
3.2. Microstructural Characterization of (FeCoCr)
40Ni40Al10Cu10 Samples
The XRD spectra in Fig. 2 indicate that the present HEA presented a
single FCC phase before and after tensile deformation. This implied that
no stress-induced phase transformation occurred during tensile de-
formation. In addition, the XRD pattern of the undeformed sample
(Fig. 2a) demonstrated that no detectable secondary phase of other
crystal type such as B2 and σ etc. were present.
Fig. 3 shows the EBSD image quality maps and corresponding grain
orientation maps of the HEA before and after deformation. As illu-
strated in Fig. 3a, nearly equiaxed grain structures were observed and
no obvious texture was found in the undeformed sample. It is clear from
the orientation map in Fig. 3b that only several annealing twins were
present within individual grains. The blue and red lines on the map
denote high-angle grain boundaries and twin boundaries, respectively.
As shown in Fig. 3c for the sample deformed under tensile stress at
293 K, many parallel straight lines occurred inside the grains. The or-
ientation boundary map in Fig. 3 d demonstrated that these straight
lines were discontinuous indicating that these lines were not twin
boundaries, instead, they corresponded to the slip bands caused by
dislocation gliding. The image quality map of the sample stretched at
233 K ( Fig. 3e) is similar to Fig. 3 c at 293 K, where many parallel lines
were present within a grain. However, the orientation boundary map
revealed that most of these lines were twin boundaries. Furthermore,
these twin boundaries exhibited di ﬀerent orientations and bent due to
the severe plastic deformation, as shown in Fig. 3f.

Further microstructural investigations on both the undeformed and
deformed samples stretched under di ﬀerent strains at two di ﬀerent
temperatures were carried out using TEM. The TEM image of the un-
deformed sample is depicted in Fig. 4. A representative low magni ﬁ-
cation BF-STEM image ( Fig. 4a) shows the general overview of the HEA
microstructure, which was composed of polycrystalline grains, where
the formation of several coarse annealing twins and nano-precipitates
was observed. To elucidate the type of precipitates, their size dis-
tribution and morphology, TEM analysis was performed on zone axis.
As shown in the BF-TEM image in Fig. 4b, the top side of grain oriented
along the 〈110〉 zone axis hardly presented any precipitates inside the
grain. By contrast, the center dark ﬁeld TEM image in Fig. 4c, which
was acquired using the di ﬀraction vector g of 001 illustrates a large
number of nano-precipitates smaller than 20 nm in size, uniformly
distributed throughout the grain. These nanoparticles were con ﬁrmed
to present a superlattice L1
2 structure and were coherent with the FCC
matrix, as determined by the SAED pattern in Fig. 4d. In addition, as
can be observed in the bottom right corner of Fig. 4b, when the sample
tilted o ﬀ the zone axis, we were unable to observe any coherent ﬁne
precipitates.
Fig. 5 describes the microstructure evolution of the sample with
tensile strains of 1, 5, and 20% and near fracture at 293 K. All the BF-
STEM images were recorded under a two-beam condition with a
diﬀraction vector g of 200 (see the insets). From Fig. 5a it is obvious
that several sparse dislocations were introduced and pinned at the re-
latively coarse precipitates, when the tensile strain was approximately
1%. When the tensile strain reached 5%, the density of dislocations
gradually increased and the dislocations had the tendency to pile up
around the coarse precipitates through a by-pass mechanism (Orowan-
type), as indicated by the black arrows in Fig. 5b. Further increasing the
tensile strain to 20% suggested that the proliferation and slipping of
dislocations were still the main deformation mechanisms. As can be
seen in Fig. 5c, signi ﬁcantly entangled deformation dislocations were
present, while in the near fracture sample ( Fig. 5 d), the dislocation
density was so high that the dislocations formed a large number of
micro-bands being full of network-like dislocations. No deformation
twins were detected except for the pre-annealing twins (see the white
arrows in Fig. 5d).
Fig. 6 shows the microstructure evolution of the HEA under tensile
strain at 233 K. As shown in the TEM images of Fig. 6 a and b, the
deformation mechanisms of the sample at the strain levels of 1% and
5% was similar to that of the 293 K sample ( Fig. 5a and b), where
dislocation slipping and proliferation were the main deformation me-
chanism at this stage. However, it is obvious from Fig. 6c that for the
233 K sample at 20% tensile strain, a certain number of thin deforma-
tion twins emerged in addition to the annealing twins (white arrow).

the (FeCoCr) 40Ni40Al10Cu10 alloy we used. Here, the high Ni content
not only stabilized the FCC structure matrix but also facilitated the
formation of L1 2 nano-precipitate phase. According to the Thermo-Calc
calculation for the phase equilibria diagram of Co 1Cr1Fe1Ni1Alx, adding
approximately 11.11 at.% Al to the alloy could result in the formation
of B2, which formed after the FCC_A1 phase [ 19]. However, the rela-
tively low Al content (< 11.11 at.%) guaranteed the formation of the
L1
2 phase and inhibited the precipitation of the B2 and σ phases. In
addition, the Al in the solid solution improved the strength of the HEA.
We found that annealing the heavily cold rolled alloy at 800 °C for 1 h
enabled the quick recrystallization and restricted grain coarsening.
Moreover, the subsequent annealing at 600 °C for 10 h guaranteed the
uniform precipitation of higher-density L1
2 nanoparticles with smaller
mean sizes than those obtained after annealing at 700 °C for 20 h, as
shown in Fig. 4c. This process resulted in the superior tensile strength
and elongation combination of the alloy at 293 K compared to the alloy
reported in our previous work [ 18]. Moreover, the mechanical prop-
erties of the present alloy at low temperature (233 K) was superior to
those of the alloy at 293 K, as shown in Fig. 2, as well as to those of the
CoCrFeNi HEA wires at 223 K [ 8].
In general, the yield strength and tensile strength increase as the
temperature decreases, and vice-versa for the elongation of HEAs. In
this study the yield strength di ﬀerence of 40 MPa between the samples
stretched at 293 K and 233 K was only caused by the temperature.
Interestingly, the tensile strength of the sample stretched at 233 K was
86 MPa higher than that of the sample stretched at 293 K, but the
elongations of both samples maintained at the same level, thus im-
plying di ﬀerent deformation mechanisms. It is well known that the
phase stability and deformation mechanism are correlated to the
stacking fault energy (SFE) in the widely studied high ‑manganese
twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) austenitic steels. A low SFE (i.e.
below 20 mJ/m
2) favors the austenite to martensite transformation,
whereas mechanical twinning is triggered by suppressing phase trans-
formation at high SFE values of over 20 mJ/m
2. At higher SFE, dis-
location gliding becomes the dominant mechanism during deformation
[20,21]. The SFE of the HEA studied in this work should be higher than
that of the HEA in our previous work because more Ni was added to this
(FeCoCr)
40Ni40Al10Cu10 alloy. Usually Ni is known to improve the SFE
of FCC based steels, and this was already con ﬁrmed by previously
published studies [ 22,23]. Thus, the deformation behavior of the pre-
sent alloy at 293 K was similar to that of our previously studied
Al0.2CrFeCoNi2Cu0.2 HEA [ 18]. In this study, we revealed that dis-
location gliding was the dominant mechanism during the entire tensile
deformation process ( Fig. 5) at 293 K. However, as shown in Figs. 3 e
and f and 6, the dominant deformation mechanism of the alloy stret-
ched at 233 K changed as the strain changed. Dislocation gliding was
predominant at low strain levels. However, when the true strain
reached 20%, deformation twinning was activated on one of the fa-
vorable {111} planes. As the true strain was further increased, de-
formation twinning on other {111} planes was also triggered and twin
density increased rapidly as well, as shown in Fig. 6d. The angle be-
tween the two deformation twins on di ﬀerent {111} planes was ap-
proximately 54
o, and some of the twins bent when the strain was too
high. Twinning is a stress controlled deformation process in FCC metals.
For the tensile deformed sample at 293 K ( Fig. 5), deformation twinning
would not take place because of the high SFE of the alloy. For the
sample deformed under tensile stress at 233 K, SFE decreased as the
temperature decreased, which triggered deformation twinning once the
ﬂow stress became su ﬃciently high. These deformation nanotwins ac-
companied large strain relaxation and delayed necking in localized
regions, which in turn improved both the strength and ductility of the
sample. The XRD analysis ( Fig. 2) revealed that the intensity of the
γ
(111) peaks was enhanced for both the deformed samples, especially for
the sample deformed at 233 K. This suggested that some of the grains
rotated toward the {111} orientation during tensile process, which fa-
vored secondary deformation twinning.
It should be pointed out that during the stress induced twinning
process, dislocation multiplication and gliding proceeded simulta-
neously. The continuous twin boundaries were considered e ﬀective
barriers for the dislocation movement. Dislocations accumulated at
these newly generated twin boundaries and the nano-precipitates in-
creases the dislocation density. As a result, the alloy exhibited higher
tensile strength and facilitated the deformation twinning at higher
stress values (see Fig. 1). Dislocation gliding following twinning also
resulted in the bending of deformation twins ( Figs. 3e and 6d). In ad-
dition, as shown in Fig. 1b, the work hardening rates of the HEA at both
233 K and 293 K were high and the tendencies were the same when the
true strain was < 0.2, where a small plateau was observed on the
curves near 0.1. The TEM images revealed dislocation pinning by nano-
precipitates ( Figs. 5b and 6b) and extensive LC locks ( Fig. 7 ) con-
tributing to high dislocation densities, which were expected to be re-
sponsible for the high work hardening rate of the alloy [ 24]. These

When deformed at 233 K, the present alloy exhibited good strength
and elongation combination, its tensile strength being above 1 GPa and
elongation approximately 35%. Recent studies [ 2,25,26] reported that
the CrMnFeCoNi alloy featuring single FCC structure displayed tensile
strength levels of over 1GPa, excellent elongation, higher than 70%,
and exceptional fracture toughness exceeding 200 MPa·m
1/2 at cryo-
genic temperature of 77 K. The crossover deformation mechanism was
determined to change from planar-slip dislocation activity at 293 K to
mechanical nanotwinning as the temperature decreased. This was si-
milar to the behavior of the present (FeCoCr)
40Ni40Al10Cu10 alloy.
Thus, we attribute the superior mechanical properties of the HEA we
studied at 233 K to the presence of high-density nano-precipitates and
formation of LC dislocation locks and deformation nanotwins.
5. Conclusions
In summary, an FCC-based (FeCoCr)
40Ni40Al10Cu10 HEA featuring
exclusively L1 2 nano-precipitates was obtained by adjusting both the
composition and heat treatment parameters. The alloy possessed an
excellent combination of tensile strength (~1200 MPa) and elongation
(36%) at 233 K. High-density nano-precipitates coherent with the ma-
trix, the formation of large numbers of immobile LC dislocation locks,
and the occurrence of deformation nanotwins were the main mechan-
isms allowing to achieve such superior properties. Our study explored a
practical way to design high-performance HEAs for low-temperature
applications.
