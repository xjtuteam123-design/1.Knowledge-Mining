Abstract
The purpose of this study is to investigate the potential of
zirconium (Zr) and nickel (Ni) in enhancing the strength of
319-type cast alloys at high temperature. The high-tem-
perature testing was performed on tensile bars either in the
as cast or T6 tempered (190 /C176C/2h) condition. High-tem-
perature tensile tests were performed at 25, 190, 250 and
300 /C176C. Tensile bars were held at each temperature for 10h
prior to pulling to fracture. The results revealed that
(Al,Si)
3(Zr,Ti), Al 3CuNi, Al 65Si16Ni13 or * Al4SiNi and
Al9NiFe phases are the main features in the microstruc-
tures of alloys containing Zr and Ni. In addition, other
phases containing Cu such as Al
5Fe2Cu2, AlSiFeNiZrCu
(probably Al 4SiNi (Cu, Fe, Zr) or Al 4SiNi with traces of
Cu, Fe, Zr), and Q-Al 5Cu2Mg8Si6 could also be formed. All
these phases result in depletion in the Cu content and
hence reduction in the alloy strength. Both Al
2Cu and
Al3Zr are the hardening phases during artiﬁcial aging. The
tensile properties were plotted as a function of aging
temperature and in the form of Q-charts. The fracture
behavior of selected samples was examined as well, using
FESEM microscopy.

Introduction
Alloying elements such as copper and magnesium are often
added to improve the alloy strength of B319-type alloys at
room temperature as well as at higher temperatures. When
they are affected by temperatures above 190 /C176C, the major
alloy strengthening phases which include the h’(Al
2Cu),
B’(Mg2Si) and/or S’(Al2CuMg) phases will tend to become
unstable and coarsen rapidly, leading to the production of
an alloy, which has an undesirable microstructure for high-
temperature applications.
1–5 To improve the strength of the
alloys under high-temperature conditions, a microstructure
containing thermally stable and coarsening-resistant
dispersoids is required. Nickel leads to the formation of
nickel aluminide Al
3Ni and Al 9FeNi in the presence of
iron, while zirconium forms Al 3Zr.6–10
These intermetallics improve the high-temperature strength
of Al–Si alloys, depending on the shape of the intermetallic
particles, their volume fraction and the contiguity with the
eutectic Si. The presence of such coherent precipitates as
Al
3Zr or Al 3Ni, which are essentially inert in the matrix,
affects the strength of the material directly because they act
as hard pinning points in the matrix, inhibiting dislocation
motion. The principal advantage of the precipitates formed
is their stability at elevated temperatures. Because of the
low solubility of the ﬁnely dispersed second phase parti-
cles, these alloys can resist over-aging and growth, thereby
preserving their mechanical properties to a greater extent.

In a number of studies on Al–Si casting alloys, it was
shown that Ni contents of less than 2% do not affect the
mechanical properties, whereas Ni variations of up to
2–3% affect only the speciﬁc gravity from among the usual
physical properties. With regard to aluminum casting
alloys, a high percentage of Ni does not seem to be prac-
tical, mainly because it would increase the cost of the
alloy.
11–18
Hernandez-Sandoval19 carried out a detailed study on
improving the elevated-temperature performance of the
A354 alloy through additions of Ni and/or Zr. The results
showed that there is no signiﬁcant difference in the UTS
and YS values of heat-treated alloys at different testing
temperatures. The same ﬁnding was reported for A356
alloy with additions of 600 ppm Ni and 1000 ppm V.
20
Hernandez-Sandoval19 also observed, however, that at
300 /C176C testing temperature, there is a slight enhancement
in the strength values (UTS and YS) associated with the
addition of Ni and Zr in the heat-treated condition com-
pared to the base alloy. Furthermore, the combined addi-
tion of 0.2 wt% Ni and 0.2 wt% Zr showed an
improvement in UTS in the as-cast condition compared to
the base alloy; the author attributed this observation to the
formation of Ni- and Zr-rich intermetallic compounds that
would obstruct further development of cracks.

Experimental Procedure
The B319 base alloy, coded A, was melted in a 120 kg
capacity SiC crucible, using an electrical resistance furnace
in which the melting temperature was initially maintained
at 850 ± 5 /C176C to add Zr in the form of Al–15%Zr master
alloy. Thereafter, the temperature was reduced to 750 /C176Ct o
add Ni (in the form of Al–25%Ni master alloy). All alloys
were grain reﬁned and modiﬁed using Al–5%Ti–1%B
master alloy, to obtain levels of 0.15% Ti in the melt. The
melts were degassed for *15-20 min with a rotary graphite
impeller rotating at *130 rpm, using pure dry argon. Five
minutes before the end of degassing, Al-10%Sr ( *200
ppm Sr) was added to minimize its oxidation. The melt
temperature was about 750 /C176C ± 5 /C176C, and the pouring
temperature was approximately 730 /C176C ± 5 /C176C. The
environmental parameters in the foundry during casting
were as follows: humidity level was about 23%, and tem-
perature of surroundings was about 22 /C176C.
Following this process, the melt was carefully skimmed to
remove oxide layers from the surface, and then carefully
poured into an ASTM B-108 permanent mold preheated at
460 /C176C, to prepare test bars for tensile testing. The solidi-
ﬁcation rate was about 8 8C/s. Each casting provided two
tensile bars, each with a gauge length of 70 mm and a
cross-sectional diameter of 12.7 mm, as shown in Figure 1.
Samplings for chemical analysis were also taken at the
beginning, middle, and end of the casting process to
ascertain the exact chemical composition of each batch of
test bars produced per alloy. For the alloys B-E, the master
alloys were cut into smaller pieces and then added to the
base alloy melt. The additions were made just before
degassing to ensure homogeneous mixing of the additives
together with the degassing. Table 1 lists the chemical
composition of the produced alloys. Boron content was
analyzed using induction-coupled plasma (ICP) technique.

All of the samples, whether as-cast, solution heat-treated,
or at high temperature, were tested to the point of fracture
using a Servo-hydraulic MTS universal mechanical testing
machine at a strain rate of 4 9 10

the percent elongation (%El) over the 25.4 mm gauge

For selected alloys/conditions, samples were sectioned
from fractured tensile test bars, 10 mm beneath the fracture
surface, for preparing metallographic samples. Each sam-
ple was mounted individually in bakelite and then ground
and polished to obtain a mirror-like surface. The samples
were examined using an Olympus PMG3 optical micro-
scope connected to a Clemex Vision PE image-analysis
system.
A Hitachi-SU8000 ﬁeld-emission scanning electron
microscope (FESEM), as was used in this study, can pro-
vide clear and less electrostatically distorted high-resolu-
tion images even at low voltages, with an image resolution
of 2.1 nm at 1 kV, and 1.5 nm at 15 kV. The FESEM
instrument is equipped with a standard secondary electron
detector (SE), a backscatter electron detector (BSE), an
energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) and a wave-

Results and Discussion
All samples were modiﬁed with strontium (Sr) unless it is
mentioned otherwise. Figure 1 shows the optical micro-
graphs of the as-cast alloys with individual and combined
additions of Ni and Zr, comprising an a-Al dendritic net-
work with the eutectic silicon particles segregated in the
interdendritic regions. Eutectic silicon particles
precipitated in the form of ﬁbrous crystals typical of a
modiﬁed structure constitute the main microstructural
feature observed. It can also be noted that iron precipitates
in the form of Al
8FeMg3Si6 phase, whereas the copper
phase is seen mainly as small pockets of the blocky Al 2Cu
phase. It may also be observed that there is no signiﬁcant
difference in the microstructures of the as-cast alloys as a
result of the additions of Ni and Zr, except for the pre-
cipitation of some intermetallic particles such as Al–Si–Zr–
Ti and Al
9NiFe, as seen in the microstructures of alloys B,
C, D and E. The Al 9NiFe phase takes irregular plate-like
morphologies and constitutes a noticeable proportion of the
total intermetallics found in the examined alloys.

Tensile tests at different temperatures were performed to
acquire knowledge of the effect of the additions of zirco-
nium and nickel on the high-temperature mechanical per-
formance of the alloys studied. Two conditions were
selected for the experiments at elevated temperatures, the
as-cast (AC), and the T6 condition (190 8C/2h) which
corresponds to the maximum strength aging condition
observed for the room temperature tensile results, both of
them for the alloys A, B, C, D and E. For the T6 condition,
the temperatures selected for the tests were 25, 190, 250
and 300 /C176C, while for the as-cast condition (AC), the
temperatures 25, 190 and 300 /C176C were selected.

properties of the ﬁve studied alloys. As can be seen, the

after T6 treatment of alloy E (containing 0.2%Ni ?
o.2%Zr) coupled with a decrease in the alloy ductility from

Fractography
In this section, the fracture surfaces of alloys C and E will
be discussed. It should be kept in mind that all analyses
made from the fracture surface are approximate due to the
topography of the broken sample surface. Figure 15a shows
the precipitation of Zr-rich phase throughout the matrix

alloy strength (UTS values are, respectively, 250 MPa and

stength resulted in the fracture of the Zr-rich phase partcles

Conclusions
From the analysis of the results obtained in the present
investigation, the following conclusions may be drawn:
1. The ultimate tensile strength and yield strength of
alloys containing Ni-and Zr-bearing phases are
higher than those of the base alloy for all testing
temperatures due to the presence of L1
2 elements.
2. In order to strengthen the cast alloys by addition
of Ni, the Zr content should be limited to lower
than 0.4 wt%, to develop the strengthening effect
of Ni to a better extent.
3. Additions of Ni and Zr to the alloy result in the
formation of high volume fractions of inter-
metallics such as Al
3NiCu and Al 9FeNi, and
(Al,Si)3(Zr,Ti), respectively, in addition to
Q-phase and Al-Fe-Cu phase and possibly a
complex Al–Si–Fe–Ni–Zr–Cu phase. As a result,
the amount of free Cu will be reduced, thus
reducing the alloy strength.
4. Best results in terms of resisting softening at 300
8C were obtained from the alloy containing
0.2%Ni ? 0.2%Zr.
