abstract
The microstructure and tensile properties of Mg e6Zne0.5Zr alloy processed by low-temperature indirect
extrusion utilizing arti ﬁcial cooling were investigated. Remarkable grain re ﬁnement was obtained by the
application of arti ﬁcial cooling, resulting in an increase in the yield strength of as much as 53 MPa at
room temperature, as compared to the case (245 MPa) of indirect extrusion without arti ﬁcial cooling.
Besides this, the ﬁne-grained alloy was found to have superplastic properties at elevated temperature,
revealing tensile elongations of 720 e810% at 200 /C14 C at strain rates of 2 /C2 10/C0 4e1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1. Mechanisms
responsible for the high strength and superplastic properties are discussed.

1. Introduction
Mg alloys are the lightest structural alloys with good speci ﬁc
strength and stiffness. Thus, they are considered promising metallic
materials that can contribute to enhancing the fuel ef ﬁciency of
moving vehicles such as automobiles [1,2]. Due to the unique
castability of Mg, the die casting process has already been estab-
lished for the fabrication of various Mg automobile components.
However, wrought processing for Mg alloy sheets or extrusions
generally suffers from slow production speed, and thereby low
productivity, restricting the widespread industrial application of
these materials due to the obstacle of high production cost [1].I n
the case of Mg alloy sheets, the recent development of the twin-roll
casting process has been shown to ef ﬁciently produce low cost and
high quality Mg alloy sheets with improved mechanical properties
[3e5]. However, attempts to overcome issues regarding the pro-
ductivity of Mg alloy extrusions have been seldom reported.
Generally, the extrusion speed of Mg alloys is slower than that of
Al alloys [1]. For instance, commercial high-strength Mg alloys such
as Mg e8Ale0.5Zn (AZ80) and Mg e6Zne0.5Zr (ZK60) exhibit
extrusion speeds of less than 5 m min /C0 1, which is over 10 times
slower than that of Al6061, which has a tensile strength equivalent
to these Mg alloys. This drawback in the Mg alloys is considered
closely related with their susceptibility to hot-cracking, which
easily occurs in alloys containing second-phases with insuf ﬁcient
thermal stability. Thus, modifying alloy compositions toward hav-
ing more thermally stable second-phases can be one method to
produce Mg alloy extrusions with better resistance to hot-cracking.
Promising alloying elements for this purpose include Ca and rare
earth elements, which have been utilized to enhance the creep
resistance of Mg alloys at high temperature [6e8]. Recently, Yu
et al. reported that the maximum extrusion speed of ZK60 alloy had
increased to 10 m min /C0 1 after alloying with Ce [9].
As an alternative way of coping with the abovementioned issue
in Mg alloy extrusions, the authors have proposed a low-
temperature indirect extrusion process that has been designed to
maintain low processing temperature by utilizing arti ﬁcial cooling
during indirect extrusion [10]. Previously, the bene ﬁcial effect of
artiﬁcial cooling on elevating the extrusion speed of Mg alloy
(AZ61) has been shown [1 1]. Besides this, the low-temperature
extrusion process has been found to be effective in the grain
reﬁnement of AZ31 Mg alloy, heightening the material's strength
level at room temperature [10]. Although grain size is also
considered a crucial factor affecting the high temperature plasticity
of Mg alloys, however, the hot deformation behavior of Mg alloys
applied to the novel extrusion process has rarely been studied thus
far. In this study, a high strength ZK60 Mg alloy was applied to the
low-temperature indirect extrusion process by utilizing arti ﬁcial

2. Experimental
The analyzed composition of the ZK60 alloy used here was
Mge5.7 wt.% Zn e0.54 wt.% Zr. Cylindrical extrusion billets were
prepared by induction melting in a graphite crucible under an inert
atmosphere with a CO 2 and SF 6 mixture and subsequent pouring
into a steel mould pre-heated to 200 /C14 C. Cast billets were homog-
enized at 440 /C14 C for 4 h, followed by water-quenching. The di-
mensions of each extrusion billet were 80 mm in diameter and
150 mm in length. Indirect extrusion experiments were imple-
mented at an initial billet temperature of 250 /C14 C, a ram speed of
1.3 mm s /C0 1, and an extrusion ratio of 25. Arti ﬁcial cooling was
selectively applied to realize low-temperature extrusion process-
ing, as schematically shown in Fig. 1. After extrusion was initiated,
cooling media consisting of water and air at ambient temperature
were provided through inlets on the stem surface. These materials
were transferred through holes inside the stem and then were
directly sprayed onto the extruded rod at the die exit. The water
feeding rate and air pressure were 1.8 l min /C0 1 and 0.8 MPa,
respectively. For comparison, indirect extrusion was also imple-
mented for the same alloy in natural cooling condition without
artiﬁcial cooling. During extrusion, temperatures at the die exit
were measured and found to be 180 and 290 /C14 C for the alloys with
and without arti ﬁcial cooling, respectively. Hereafter, the ZK60 al-
loys with the extrusion temperatures of 180 and 290 /C14 C are denoted
as low temperature-extruded (LT-E) and high temperature-
extruded (HT-E) alloys, respectively.
Microstructural examinations were conducted on midsections
parallel to the extrusion direction (ED) by optical microscopy,
electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) with the Quanta 200 ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscope and the TSL EBSD data
collection system, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
with the JEM-2100F Cs-corrected transmission electron micro-
scope. TEM samples were prepared using a jet-polishing method.
Grain size was measured by using a linear intercept method;
d ¼ 1.74 /C2 L, where d is the grain size and L is the linear intercept
size. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were conducted at a scan rate
of 2/C14 min/C0 1 and a step size of 0.02 /C14 with a Rigaku D/MAX-2500/PC
in back re ﬂection mode with Cu-K a radiation.
Tensile tests were basically performed at an initial strain rate of
1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 at temperatures in the range of 25 e250 /C14 C. Especially,
tensile tests at 200 /C14 C were additionally carried out at various strain
rates in the range of 1 /C2 10/C0 4e1 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1. The ﬂow stresses at
different temperatures were determined at a true strain of 0.1. To
analyze operating deformation mechanisms, load relaxation tests
were performed at 200 /C14 C at a strain rate of 1 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1. Round
tensile specimens with a 20 mm gage length and a 5 mm gage
diameter were used for both tensile and load relaxation tests;
specimens were stabilized for 10 min at the selected temperatures
prior to testing. The specimens for the load relaxation test were
loaded ﬁrst in tension to a strain of 1.5% before stopping the
crosshead motion. Three specimens were used in a given condition
during tensile tests at room and elevated temperatures and values
obtained from three measurements were averaged. During load
relaxation tests at 200 /C14 C, one specimen was used for each alloy.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure after extrusion
Fig. 2 shows optical micrographs of the LT-E and HT-E ZK60
alloys, revealing equiaxed a-Mg grains formed by dynamic recrys-
tallization (DRX) during extrusion. In addition, relatively coarse
grains elongated along the ED, representing incomplete DRX, are
found to exist with the DRX grains in both the LT-E and HT-E alloys.
The average area fractions of the band-like coarse grains were
measured and found to be 16.9 and 1 1.9% for the LT-E and HT-E
ZK60 alloys, respectively, indicating that DRX in the LT-E alloy re-
mains more incomplete after extrusion than it does in the HT-E
alloy because DRX is a thermally activated process.
In addition, a remarkable difference in grain size was detected
between the LT-E and HT-E alloys. It can be seen that decreasing the
extrusion temperature via arti ﬁcial cooling has a signi ﬁcant in ﬂu-
ence on grain re ﬁnement in the extruded state. The average grain
sizes were measured and found to be 1.9 ± 0.6 and 8.2 ± 6.9 mm for
the LT-E and HT-E alloys, respectively. It should be noted here that
this ﬁne-grained structure observable in the LT-E ZK60 alloy has
rarely been reported in extruded Mg alloys thus far, except for cases
subjected to severe plastic deformation processing such as equal
channel angular pressing (ECAP) [12e16]. Inverse pole ﬁgures ob-
tained by XRD are also provided in Fig. 2 . The extruded alloys
exhibited textures that are typically observed in extruded Mg alloys
[1 7,18]. The LT-E alloy revealed a slightly stronger texture than that

3.2. Tensile properties at room temperature
Fig. 4 provides stress-strain curves, showing that the LT-E alloy
has a higher level of tensile strength than the HT-E alloy, with
equivalent ductility at room temperature. The LT-E alloy exhibited
yield and ultimate strengths of 298 and 348 MPa, respectively, with
an elongation-to-failure value of 23%. On the other hand, the HT-E
alloy showed yield and ultimate strengths of 245 and 318 MPa,
respectively, with an elongation-to-failure value of 24%. It should
be noted here that the ZK60 alloy in the LT-E condition has a better
combination of strength and ductility than that of recently reported
high strength Mg alloys such as Mg e2.4Zne0.1Age0.1Cae0.16Zr
(at.%) or Mg e8Sne1Zne1Al (wt.%) [21,22].
One microstructural factor mainly responsible for the high
strength property of the LT-E alloy, as compared to the HT-E alloy, is
believed to be grain size. A difference in the amount of MgZn 2
precipitates cannot properly explain this difference because a
smaller amount of strengthening precipitates was found to exist in
the LT-E alloy than in the HT-E alloy. Also, it is not considered
reasonable to attribute this discrepancy to the small textural dif-
ference between the alloys.

3.4. Microstructural and textural changes during tensile
deformation at 200 /C14 C
To observe the microstructural and textural changes in the su-
perplastic LT-E ZK60 alloy during tensile deformation at 200 /C14 C and
at 1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1, EBSD orientation maps and inverse pole ﬁgures
were obtained from gage sections at different tensile elongations of
0, 50, and 500%, as shown in Fig. 7. The orientation maps revealed
that grain size becomes coarser as tensile elongation increases,
demonstrating the occurrence of dynamic grain growth. It should
be noted here that the shape of the Mg grains appears to remain
equiaxed even after a high tensile elongation of 500%, which may
be possible by active GBS [26]. Inverse pole ﬁgures provided with
the orientation maps indicate that the texture becomes slightly
weaker as deformation proceeds, also suggesting the operation of
GBS in the testing condition [22,26].

It is generally thought that grain re ﬁnement is one of the most
effective ways to enhance the plasticity of Mg alloys at elevated
temperature because large plastic deformation is possible via the
activation of GBS [26,31]. In the present study, as compared to the
alloy without artiﬁcial cooling, the ﬁne-grained alloy extruded with
artiﬁcial cooling showed highly enhanced tensile plasticity at
200
/C14 C, exhibiting elongations exceeding 800%. Basically, the
deformation mechanism responsible for the enhanced plasticity
can be assumed by measuring the strain rate sensitivity from the
ﬂow stress-strain rate curve. However, this method of using the
strain rate sensitivity is not considered useful in this study because
the strain rate sensitivity values of the LT-E and HT-E alloys in the
low strain rate regime are of little difference, at 0.33 and 0.29,
respectively.
On the other hand, results from load relaxation tests at 200 /C14 C
clearly indicate that GBS is more actively operative in the LT-E alloy
than in the HT-E alloy. If GBS is an important deformation mech-
anism enabling large plastic deformation in the LT-E alloy, tensile
elongation values obtained from tensile tests are expected to in-
crease as GBS fraction increases [32]. As mentioned above, a linear
relationship was observed between the tensile elongation and the
GBS fraction in the LT-E alloy when the GBS fraction values are in
the range of 0 e0.19, indicating that the operation of GBS plays an
essential role in enhancing the tensile plasticity of the LT-E alloy
and GBS is a decisive factor in maintaining plastic deformation in
the low GBS fraction range. In addition, superplastic properties
were realized with tensile elongations approaching 800% in the LT-
E alloy when GMD and GBS act as predominant and accommoda-
ting deformation mechanisms, respectively, suggesting that the
predominance of GBS over GMD is not necessarily required for the
alloy to have superplasticity.
4. Conclusion
Commercial ZK60 Mg alloy was subjected to a low-temperature
indirect extrusion process utilizing arti ﬁcial cooling; the micro-
structure and tensile properties of the extruded alloy were inves-
tigated. Signi ﬁcant grain re ﬁnement was found to result from the
novel extrusion processing; the ﬁne-grained alloy exhibited
remarkably enhanced plasticity at elevated temperature, as well as
high strength at room temperature, when compared with the same
ZK60 alloy subjected to a typical indirect extrusion process without
artiﬁcial cooling. The ﬁne-grained alloy extruded with arti ﬁcial
cooling exhibited yield and ultimate strengths of 298 and 346 MPa,
respectively, with an elongation-to-failure value of 23%. Besides
this, superplasticity was observed in the ﬁne-grained alloy, which
exhibited tensile elongations of 720 e810% at strain rates in the
range of 2 /C2 10/C0 4e1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 at 200 /C14 C. Load relaxation results at
200 /C14 C indicate that GBS plays an important role in the superplastic
properties even when GBS acts as an accommodating process
during tensile deformation.
