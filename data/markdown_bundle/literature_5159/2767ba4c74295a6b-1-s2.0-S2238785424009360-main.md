ABSTRACT 
Additive manufacturing (AM) is a cutting-edge technique that builds parts by adding layers from a digital design 
and depositing materials selectively where required. AM is vital in industries like aerospace, healthcare, and 
automotive because of benefits like design flexibility, cost-efficiency, and customization over conventional 
methods. Ni-based superalloys AM processes involve phenomena like solidification, phase change, different 
microstructure characteristics, and melting pool boundaries, affecting both microstructure and mechanical 
properties. Ni-based superalloys are regarded as potential candidates for high-temperature applications, thanks 
to their capability to benefit from various strengthening mechanisms such as precipitation hardening and solid 
solution strengthening. This comprehensive review explores the thermal stability and high temperature softening 
resistance of additively manufactured (AMed) Ni-based superalloys, from microstructural and mechanical points 
of view. This review explores the thermal stability and high temperature softening resistance of additively 
manufactured Ni-based superalloys. It explores the interplay of alloying elements at high temperature, additive 
manufacturing parameters, and initial microstructure, focusing on grain boundary/sub-boundary networks 
evolution. The influence of solute drag effect, second phases Zenner pinning effect, and post-processing treat -
ments on microstructural changes such as second phase characteristics, recrystallization (static and dynamic), 
grain growth phenomena, and textural variations is also analyzed. High temperature mechanical properties of 
AM-Ni-based superalloys are evaluated, including yield and tensile strength, elongation, work-hardening 
behavior, creep, and fatigue properties. Hydrogen embrittlement is a significant reason for failure, so this 
issue is briefly assessed in AM-Ni-based superalloys. Results show that understanding relationships between 
processing, microstructure, post-processing treatment, and properties is crucial for thermal stability control. 
1. Introduction 
Additive Manufacturing (AM) or 3D printing revolutionizes object 
creation through layer-by-layer construction, surpassing traditional 
methods like casting. AM provides economic benefits and opportunities 
in diverse fields like biomedical, aerospace, automotive, nuclear power, 
energy, transportation, and creating printed parts for various environ -
mental settings [ 1 – 3 ]. ISO/ASTM 52900 classifies AM as a blend of 
subtractive and formative processes, with metal AM emphasizing direct 
energy deposition (DED) and powder bed fusion (PBF). PBF process uses 
two laser/electron beams to melt materials, creating near-net shape 
products with high density and intricate geometries. LPBF systems have 
lower deposition rates for small parts, while powder bed systems have 
higher powder feeding costs compared to wire feed systems, but better 
accuracy and surface quality. Wire feed systems, with high deposition 
rates, are ideal for large parts but may have challenges like poor surface 
features, low accuracy, and high residual stress. AM is widely used in 
aerospace, automotive, and healthcare industries for its benefits. The U. 
S. AM market hit $3 billion in 2021 and is projected to grow at an 18.9% 
annual rate until 2030. AM offers advanced design possibilities like 
lattice structures and customized microstructures, especially valuable in 
aerospace and power generation. It minimizes waste, allows speedy 
prototyping, customization, cost-effectiveness, and eco-friendliness. 
The production of high-performance and high-temperature Ni-based 
superalloys is challenging due to the expensive subtractive techniques 
needed for cast superalloys [ 4 ]. Traditional precision investment casting

AM enables the production of strong, lightweight lattice structures with 
exceptional strength-to-weight ratios and better thermal properties 
through layer by layer processing methods. Its scalability benefits small 
businesses with low infrastructure needs. AM also cuts costs by speeding 
up design-to-fabrication cycles, time-to-market, and promoting inno -
vation through rapid prototyping.

Critical parameters in additive manufacturing processes, including 
feedstock characteristics and fabrication process, greatly affect micro -
structure and mechanical properties of final printed parts. Processibility 
and homogeneity are essential factors, with researchers focusing on pre- 
alloyed and atomized commercial alloys for Ni-based superalloys. 
However, these treatments are not recommended due to increased vis -
cosity of the powder compared to pure Ni, as well as challenges in pre- 
alloyed treatment and decreased uniform size distribution from atomi -
zation methodology [ 21 , 22 ]. Minimizing and developing new alloys, 
along with in-situ alloy methodology during the AM process, can 
improve processibility and mechanical performance of AM parts. In 
DED, this approach is beneficial due to a larger melt pool area, higher 
laser powers, and increased area of re-melting, resulting in a lower so -
lidification rate that effectively homogenizes the liquid mixture chem -
istry. Challenges in in-situ alloying include different energy absorbance, 
melting temperatures, and optical properties of alloying elements, 
impacting viscosity and surface tension of the melt pool and compli -
cating achieving homogeneity during solidification. Alloying elements 
can segregate to inter-dendritic regions or grain boundaries, increasing 
crack susceptibility during additive manufacturing. In-situ methods 
have benefits over pre-alloyed, but further studies are required for 
Ni-based superalloy improvement [ 23 , 24 ]. 
Powder bed fusion processes rely on flowdibility and packing density 
of powders, affected by inter-particle forces, gravity, and moisture. Poor 
flowdibility causes agglomeration, leading to uneven layers and low 
packing density, compromising part strength. Insufficient packing den -
sity results in rough surfaces, low densities, and weak mechanical 
properties. Voids impact packing density, altering thermal conductivity 
and melt pool behavior. Higher packing density improves heat transfer 
and reduces bonding defects in melt tracks. Powder size distribution and 
morphology can impact flowability and packing density. Smaller parti -
cles have higher surface areas, increasing particle interaction. Particles 
smaller than 10 μ m experience stronger inter-particle forces and energy 
absorption, leading to severe agglomeration, reducing flowability. Wide 
distribution of ultra-fine particles can hinder flowability by adhering 
small particles to larger ones. However, it can improve packing density 
by filling gaps between larger particles with smaller ones [ 25 – 27 ]. The 
form of particles is also an essential factor that affects the flow properties 
and packing density of powder. Smooth-surfaced spherical particles 
reduce friction, leading to improved flowability and density during 
packing. The water atomization process influences the higher porosity 
that occurs due to irregular particle shapes. Plasma rotating electrode 
processing (PREP) displays higher bulk density compared to gas atom -
ization (GA) as a result of enhanced circularity and flowability, leading 
to improved filling efficiency and decreased porosity [ 28 , 29 ]. 
Tracing oxygen content in Ni-based superalloy powders is crucial as 
it affects oxide inclusion formation, leading to issues like spattering and 
decreased ductility in AM processes. Entrapped gases can create bub -
bles, resulting in pores and decreased density in printed parts. Minor 
elements like Si, Mn, Ga, and Pb can cause cracking and reduced me -
chanical properties by lowering solidus temperatures and forming un -
favorable phases [ 30 – 32 ]. Contamination of elements like C and O can 
hinder thermal conductivity, building efficiency, and lead to defects 
during manufacturing processes. Surface modification treatment is rec -
ommended before using powders to mitigate these effects [ 33 , 34 ]. 
Recycled powder characteristics, such as spatter and oxidation, 
deformed powders, morphological and size effects can impact particle 
size distribution, flowability, density, and composition, consequently 
affecting microstructure and mechanical properties. Oxygen content in 
recycled particles increases with recycling, but research shows minimal 
changes in mechanical properties when using recycled powders for 
fabrication [ 35 – 38 ]. Overall, understanding and controlling chemical 
composition effects on microstructure and mechanical properties are 
essential in producing high-quality Ni-based superalloy components 
through AM.

42 ], micro-segregation [ 43 , 44 ], lack of fusion [ 19 , 42 , 44 ], and balling 
phenomenon [ 19 ]. The powder feed rate is also a key parameter in the

Defects, microstructures, and residual strains are closely linked to 
scan strategies in additive manufacturing, with thermal gradient dis -
tribution influencing grain structures. Chess, stripes, customized, and 
flow-optimized scan strategies impact microstructure, geometry, and 
mechanical behavior. Chess scanning improves hardness, while island 
scanning reduces thermal gradient directionality. Uniform scanning 
disrupts epitaxial growth, while non-uniform strategies lead to crack 
density increase in superalloys like Rene 104 and CM247LC. Optimal 
scan strategy and parameters are vital for additive manufacturing of Ni- 
based superalloys [ 51 ].

could result in greater denudation and balling, although the exact pro -
cess is not completely clear. Light noble gases have the potential to 
enhance cooling speeds in DED constructions [ 52 , 53 ]. Air pressure

Spattering is a frequent problem in fusion-based AM techniques, 
resulting from the intense fusion of material, which causes welding 
sparks to be expelled from the fusion area. Spattering can occur due to 
different factors like Marangoni flow, gas flow, and electron beam

can affect the distribution of powder size and the quality of recycled 
feedstock. When molten spatter reacts with oxygen, it can create oxide 
films that impact the characteristics of components crafted from Ni- 
based superalloys [ 54 – 56 ]. Controlling the energy input is essential in 
stabilizing the molten pool to minimize spattering. Suggestions involve 
controlling energy densities, gas flow rates, specimen placement, and 
beam characteristics. Analyzing the composition of recycled feedstock is 
also crucial to avoid splattering and guarantee top-notch AM compo -
nents [ 55 , 57 , 58 ].

Regarding the impact of solute atoms on enhancing the stability of 
materials in high-temperature environments, co-segregated elements 
hinder the motion of grain boundaries, thus affecting the migration of 
these boundaries. Enhanced entropy in bulk materials and grain 
boundaries leads to enhanced stability of grain boundaries. Nb, Mo, Ta, 
Zr, Cr, Y, and Ni atoms in solid solutions induce strain energy at grain 
boundaries by having varying atomic sizes compared to the solvent 
atoms, resulting in a reduction of dislocation energy. Elements with the 
lowest diffusion coefficient are crucial for maintaining thermal stability 
[ 75 , 76 ].

A popular method is to thoroughly use the characterization of Ni- 
based superalloys ’ mechanical properties, especially tensile properties, 
at both room temperature and high temperatures. The tensile charac -
teristics of IN718 alloy produced with AM vary greatly between as-built 
or non-heat treated (NHT) states and heat-treated states. The reason for 
this variation is credited to the creation of γ ′ ′ -precipitates during the 
following aging process. The tensile characteristics of NHT AM IN718 
are approximately comparable to NHT and heat-treated AM IN625 and 
Hastelloy X alloys. This resemblance is due to the low quantity of 
γ ′ ′ -precipitates in the NHT state of AM IN718 alloy, resulting in its tensile 
characteristics being similar to those of solid solutioned IN625 and 
Hastelloy X alloys. The heat treatment schedules can cause variations in 
the tensile properties of heat-treated AM Ni-based superalloys. Precise 
aging processes and solution treatments result in full precipitation of 
strengthen phases like γ ′ / γ’ ′ in Haynes 282/IN718 and grain boundary 
carbides in IN625/Hastelloy X, thus improving the strength of these 
alloys [ 82 , 83 ]. Solid solutioned Hastelloy X and IN625 alloys show the 
weakest strength at room and high temperatures. The IN718 superalloy, 
strengthened by γ ′ ′ , has the highest strength, exceeding even that of the 
γ ′ -strengthened Haynes 282 superalloy. Limited literature on AMed 
materials makes it difficult to draw conclusions about the elevated 
temperature tensile properties of Ni-based superalloys strengthened 
with γ ′ . The superior strengthening effect of the γ ′ ′ -phase compared to 
that of γ ′ -precipitates is attributed to the greater lattice misfit caused by 
the former within the matrix. Despite efforts to develop customized heat 
treatment procedures for Ni-based alloys, there is a knowledge gap for 
heat treatment development of AM alloys due to their unique micro -
structure characteristics. Unique microstructure characteristics of AM 
alloys, such as columnar grains aligned with the build direction and 
micro-segregation within inter-dendritic cellular regions, differ signifi -

Ni-based superalloys have different high-temperature tensile

The initial microstructure, which includes stored energy, character -
istics of grain boundaries, crystallographic orientation, and local dislo -
cation boundaries arrangement, is essential in determining the forces 
driving recrystallization and grain growth processes. Two primary 
methods have been recognized for inducing grain growth. The initial 
step includes pre-treatment methods such as cold-rolling to lower 
recrystallization temperature and time, whereas the second step in -
volves tuning additive manufacturing parameters to reduce dislocation 
density and trigger recrystallization. For instance, speeding up the 
scanning process can generate small microstructures and enhance the 
rates of recrystallization [ 84 , 85 ]. The orientation < 001 > , which is often 
preferred in AM, affects recrystallization rates due to the storage energy 
resulting from rapid solidification rates [ 86 ]. 
Grain boundary engineering affects thermal stability of Ni-based 
superalloys through various factors like SGBs, TBs, solute atom deco -
ration, second phases, immobile boundaries, and thick boundaries. SGBs 
can improve superalloy properties by manipulating grain boundaries, 
influenced by precipitated phases and lattice strain energy [ 87 ]. 
Immobile boundaries hinder grain boundary migration in AM-LPBF 
Hastelloy-X [ 84 ], while thick boundaries with Mo enhance thermal 
stability [ 88 ]. MPBs in AM can negatively impact elongation properties, 
but specific types like track-track MPBs result in satisfactory properties. 
Heat treatment can mitigate the detrimental effects of MPBs. Overall, 
grain boundary engineering plays a significant role in the mechanical 
and thermal properties of superalloys [ 89 , 90 ]. 
Grain boundaries (GB), particularly those that are perpendicular to 
the direction of the applied load, are the least strong areas when sub -
jected to tension load at high temperatures. The formation of grain 
boundaries during solidification makes GBs especially vulnerable to 
crack initiation, as brittle intermetallic/impurity phases can develop. 
Intergranular fracture is identified as the primary cause of failure in non- 
heat-treated (NHT) γ ′ ′ -strengthened AM superalloys such as IN718. 
Equivalent failure patterns have been detected in AM Hastelloy X ’ s 
fracture surfaces under room and elevated temperatures. Enhanced 
high-temperature tensile characteristics in Ni-based superalloys result 
from decreased grain boundary density, obtained by larger grain sizes, 
leading to fewer microcrack/void initiation points and paths for prop -
agation. Laser additive manufacturing (LAM) can lead to enhanced 
creep performance, particularly in the vertical direction, due to specific 
microstructural characteristics such as columnar epitaxial grain growth 
in line with the build direction. 
Recrystallization consists of two phases which are nucleation and 
growth. Static recrystallization starts with the development of subgrains 
during static recovery, whereas dynamic recrystallization (DRX) ne -
cessitates reaching a critical strain or dislocation density [ 91 , 92 ]. 
Recrystallization is influenced by factors such as deformation structure, 
grain boundaries, crystallographic orientation, second particles, solute 
atoms, and impurities [ 74 ]. Increased temperature, storage energy, and 
subgrain coalescence are factors that affect subgrain growth. Static 
recrystallization leads to structural transformations like decreased 
micro-segregation, the formation of annealing twinning, dissolution of 
primary precipitation phases, and reduced microstructural anisotropy 
[ 93 ]. DRX is a dominant mechanism during the softening stage, causing 
a significant drop in the work-hardening rate [ 94 ]. The existence of 
additional phases and solute atoms can hinder the growth of grains, 
whereas small particles and stationary grain boundaries serve as ob -
stacles for the movement of grain boundaries [ 95 ]. The grain growth is 
also influenced by the thermal stability of precipitated phases and the 
temperature-solvus of certain phases [ 96 , 97 ].

2. The significance of thermal stability 
The long-term mechanical and service life of materials depend on 
their thermal stability. Factors affecting the thermal stability of Ni-based 
superalloys made through AM include microstructure characteristics

3.1.1. Solute effect 
Higher grain boundary velocity compared to impurity diffusion rate 
minimizes impurity dragging effect on grain boundaries. Activation

microstructure, with low impurity concentrations in pure metals leading

dynamic and kinetic approaches. The solute drag effect enhances 
thermal stability by decorating grain boundaries and impeding recrys -

Certain atoms like Zr and Ta have a higher tendency for segregation 
compared to solute atoms such as Mo, Nb, Ni, and Cr. The enthalpy of 
segregation (Hseg) is crucial in designing a chemical composition that 
controls grain boundary migration [ 75 ]. Solute atoms can improve 
thermal stability depending on temperature, interactions between solute 
atoms [ 110 ], and diffusion rate [ 111 ]. Stabilizing grain boundaries 
through the Cottrell mechanism enhances material strength, but this 
effect weakens at higher temperatures [ 112 ]. 
The diffusion rates of solute atoms, varying atomic sizes, and bond 
energies all play crucial roles in reducing grain boundary energy. 
Metastable low-energy co-segregation of elements can create trapping 
sites for solute atoms, contributing to this reduction. Material design 
benefits from the differential adsorption rates of elements at grain 
boundaries. Grain boundary migration requires collective behavior from 
co-segregated elements, slowing down grain boundary mobility. Pure 
binary systems do not follow this mechanism. Elements with the lowest 
diffusion coefficient are key in reducing grain boundary migration. High 
entropy levels at bulk and grain boundary levels enhance grain bound -
ary stability by activating sluggish kinetic phenomena [ 113 ]. Re -
searchers focus on the thermal stability of nanostructures due to their 
favorable mechanical properties, with challenges arising in achieving 
metastable thermodynamic equilibrium in high-temperature environ -

Having a second phase present in the material has two main effects. 
Firstly, it obstructs the movement of dislocations, leading to their 
accumulation and an increase in dislocation density, which in turn 
boosts the driving force for recrystallization. Secondly, the second phase 
slows down the rearrangement of dislocations, preventing the formation 
of subgrains and delaying their transformation into HAGBs. The char -
acteristics of the second phase, such as size, morphology, distribution, 
coherence, incoherence, and volume fraction, impact the process of 
recrystallization. Larger second phases, like the Laves phase, are effec -

density. The shape factor of the second phase also plays a role, with 
irregularly shaped phases generating higher pinning forces and wavy 
boundaries ( Fig. 7 b) compared to granular or sub-micron-scale phases 
( Fig. 7 a). Additionally, the coherence or incoherence of the second 
phase with the matrix is important in hindering dislocation movement, 
with coherent phases being more effective than non-uniformly distrib -
uted phases [ 125 , 126 ].

3.4. Effects of initial microstructure 
The initial microstructure plays a crucial role in driving or dragging 
the recrystallization and grain growth processes. Factors such as storage 
energy, grain boundary characteristics, and local dislocation boundaries 
arrangement are considered as candidates for this. Two main ap -
proaches driving grain growth include pre-treatment processes like cold 
rolling, which reduces recrystallization temperature and duration, and 
adjusting AM parameters to minimize dislocation density and initiate 
recrystallization [ 85 ]. Voids on grain boundaries can exert a dragging 
force, slowing down grain boundary migration through Zener pinning. A 
study by Zhang et al. [ 156 ] used phase field simulation to analyze void 
drag effects (the white arrows in Fig. 10 a) on grain boundary movement 
in different microstructures. In the PF simulation model, the free energy 
function depends on two energies: grain boundary energy and storage 
energy. The free energy of grain boundary is related to both the 
recrystallized grain and deformed microstructure. The free energy of a 
deformed structure considers factors like free energy density, recrys -
tallized grain, deformed microstructure, and voids. Fig. 10 b1 shows 
grain boundary migration in an inhomogeneous microstructure without 
voids, with wavy movement. Fig. 10 b2 reveals variations in boundary 
speed due to storage energy distribution. Point 2, with lower storage 
energy, undergoes boundary segmentation and accelerated migration. 
Voids affect boundary migration in an inhomogeneous deformed 
microstructure. Fig. 10 c1 shows how void distribution can hinder 
migration. Point 1 has minimal impact on migration compared to points

3.7. Particle stimulated nucleation 
Particle Stimulated Nucleation (PSN) is used to control and refine 
microstructures by enhancing recrystallization rate through the addition 
of second-phase particles or precipitation phases [ 73 ]. These particles 
play a pinning role that inhibits grain boundary migration. Solute atoms 
and voids/bubbles also impact recrystallization, with rare earth ele -
ments delaying the process and pores/bubbles exerting a dragging force 
[ 74 ]. Second-phase particles can be coarse ( > 1 μ m) or fine (10 – 300 nm), 
with fine particles typically impeding recrystallization while coarse 
particles stimulate it. The interface energy between these particles and 
the matrix, whether coherent or incoherent, also affects recrystallization 
behavior. Understanding the classification, size, and interface energy of 
second-phase particles is crucial for controlling and manipulating mi -
crostructures in materials. 
The effects of second-phase particles on recrystallization are intricate 
and varied. These particles can either slow down or speed up the 
recrystallization process, serve as anchor points for grains, impact grain 
orientation and texture, and undergo changes like dissolution or re- 
precipitation during recrystallization. Decreasing second-phase parti -
cle values weaken their ability to impede dislocations, allowing for 
easier dislocation slip. This can reduce the likelihood of recrystallization 
through particle-stimulated nucleation. Non-deformable particles with 
significant size differences from the matrix can create geometrically 
necessary dislocations around them, hindering dislocation movement 
and promoting grain refinement [ 74 ]. Larger, incoherent particles are 
particularly effective in promoting localized strain distribution and 
encouraging recrystallization through the particle-stimulated nucleation 
mechanism. This is due to the deformation zones created around these 
particles, leading to local lattice rotation near the particles. Crystallo -

4. High temperature mechanical properties of AMed superalloys 
4.1. High temperature mechanical properties 
A comprehensive evaluation of microstructure is crucial for under -
standing variations in strength and elongation of superalloys, particu -
larly at elevated temperatures. At low temperatures, the strength of 
alloys is influenced by grain boundaries, which act as prominent ob -
stacles to dislocation movement, leading to form dislocation forests and 
pile-ups. This results in an increase in shear stress required for disloca -
tion motion and, consequently, an increase in strength. However, at high 
temperatures, grain boundaries become more susceptible to tempera -
ture, shifting the focus to the role of grains ’ interior [ 128 ]. 
Several strengthening mechanisms are applicable for describing 
mechanical responses of superalloys in high-temperature applications. 
These mechanisms include grain boundaries, precipitation strength -
ening, work hardening, twin strengthening, and solid solution 
strengthening. For instance, precipitation strengthening is influenced by 
the size and distribution of particles, and an increase in the size of 
γ ′ -particles leads to a decrease in yield strength, as observed in Haynes 
282 superalloy [ 170 ]. Grain boundaries act as barriers to dislocation 
motion, and precipitate phases act as pinning points, retarding grain 
boundary migration. Fig. 20 illustrates the high-temperature mechanical 
properties, including yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and

contributors to fatigue issues in AM metal alloys, leading to crack

powder bed can result in large subsurface voids due to melt pool ther -
mal/fluidic edge effects. Results reveal HIP has little impact on fatigue of 
AM IN625, but varies fatigue properties of AM IN718. HIP has negligible 
effect on AM IN625 fatigue, while slightly enhancing fatigue response of 
AM IN718 alloys. Both alloys show minimal influence of porosity on 
fatigue properties. Overall, HIP treatment has contrasting effects on 
fatigue behavior of AM IN718 and AM IN625 alloys, with IN718 expe -
riencing slight improvement and IN625 remaining largely unaffected. 
Findings suggest HIPed state of alloys mirrors performance of wrought

4.4. Creep properties 
Creep resistance is important for high-temperature metal alloys such 
as Ni-based superalloys. Research on creep performance of AMed Ni- 
based superalloys is limited. Studies have mainly focused on L-PBF 
processed IN718 and E-PBF processed IN718, but some research has 
been done on other alloys like L-PBF processed IN738LC, L-PBF pro -
cessed CM247LC, L-PBF processed C263, and L-PBF processed Hastelloy 
X [ 227 – 231 ]. 
The impact of anisotropy on the creep performance of AMed Ni- 
based superalloys has been extensively researched. Vertically built L- 
PBF processed IN738LC specimens, where stress aligns with the build 
direction, show superior creep resistance compared to horizontally built 
samples. Similar results are found for other alloys such as IN738LC, 
CM247LC, and IN718, indicating better resistance and strengthening 
mechanisms in vertically built specimens. Anisotropy ’ s influence on 
creep resistance remains notable even after heat treatments (as shown in 
Fig. 28 ). Creep life and ductility improve in vertically built AM Ni-based 
superalloys at reduced strain rates, attributed to the crack growth 
mechanism (as shown in Fig. 28 a and b ). Intergranular cracking 
(creating cleavage steps (see Fig. 29 g and h )) is observed in vertical and 
orthogonal built specimens, and horizontally between melt pools, 
resulting in quasi-cleavage (see Fig. 29 i), due to microstructure align -
ment with stress planes. However, anisotropy ’ s impact on crack growth 
appears less significant in the presence of surface micro-notches [ 227 , 
229 , 230 , 232 ]. 
The study examined how different grain structures (equiaxed/ 
columnar grains) and loading directions (longitudinal/transverse) 
impact creep deformation and failure in E-PBF processed IN718. Testing 
was done at 650 
◦

HIPed E-PBF processed specimens show similar creep lifetimes to 
conventionally processed counterparts. Vertically built specimens tested 
longitudinally perform the best, with creep damage primarily occurring 
in presence of micro-voids and cracks initiating at grain boundaries 
around δ -phases (see Fig. 30 d-f). Transverse grain boundaries aligned 
with the loading direction are found to be most damaging for equiaxed 
grain structured specimens due to maximum number of micro-voids and

and l )) and columnar grain structures, with ductile shear failure in 
longitudinally loaded columnar grain specimens (see Fig. 29 j). Overall, 
transverse grain boundaries negatively affect creep rupture properties, 
especially in equiaxed grain structured or horizontally built specimens 
[ 233 – 235 ]. 
Creep performance of AMed Ni-based superalloys is influenced by 
various process parameters like laser power, focus height, oxidation, 
surface integrity, and ceramic reinforcements. L-PBF processed IN718 
specimens with higher laser power (950 W) show inferior creep resis -
tance compared to lower power (250 W), leading to ductile fracture due 
to built-in defects. Changing scan strategy affects creep behavior, but 
using multiple laser beams does not harm creep performance. Rapid 
solidification in AM processes like L-PBF creates a refined grain structure 
in non-heat treated AM Ni-based superalloy components. This structure 
can negatively impact creep resistance by promoting grain boundary 
sliding and intergranular cracking, identified as dominant creep failure 
mechanisms. This degradation is particularly noticeable at low strain

and IN738LC, further exacerbated by severely segregated microstruc -

5.2. Dynamic/static recrystallization and grain growth phenomena 
Recrystallization includes recovery (static & dynamic) and recrys -
tallization (static & dynamic, continuous & discontinuous). Recovery 
involves dislocation annihilation & rearrangement with no texture or 
grain boundary changes induced by annealing or deformation. Static 
recrystallization, important to researchers, occurs through annealing, 
while dynamic recrystallization (DRX) in hot deformation results in 
major property alterations. Adjusting strain rate, temperature, and 
strain level can speed up DRX and promote growth of recrystallized 
grains [ 245 – 247 ]. Recrystallization is a two-stage process involving 
nucleation and growth. Static recrystallization begins with an incuba -
tion period for subgrain formation and growth during static recovery. 
Subgrain growth is hindered by obstacles like boundary curvature, 
which can be overcome by factors like temperature, stored energy, and 
subgrain coalescence. Conversely, dynamic recrystallization starts after 
reaching a critical strain or dislocation density. Recrystallization in

6. Hydrogen embrittlement 
Hydrogen is an important energy carrier in the hydrogen economy, 
but hydrogen embrittlement (HE) poses a significant problem in the 
transportation and storage of metals because it occurs in high- 
performance components exposed to moisture or hydrogen. Issues 
regarding high entropy in alloys produced through additive 
manufacturing must be resolved by leveraging the advantages of addi -
tive manufacturing. Superalloys demonstrate strong resistance to 
oxidation and creep at high temperatures, as well as corrosion at room 
temperature. Nonetheless, they can still be affected by HE and the 
impact of water vapor in high-pressure hydrogen atmospheres. Happens 
over a broader temperature spectrum in superalloys than in steels, with 
greater impact observed at elevated temperatures. HE is important for 
ensuring safe design in hydrogen environments because superalloys are 
commonly used in high temperature conditions. Hence, it is crucial to 
research HE in Ni-based alloys made through both additive 
manufacturing and conventional techniques [ 263 ]. 
Studies have indicated that when manufactured through conven -
tional processes, IN 718 alloy is extremely prone to HE. Localized 
hydrogen embrittlement is mainly found in high-stress regions of the 
alloy surface, and can be alleviated by lowering the hardness in those 
specific areas. Employing laser surface solutionizing has proven to be 
successful in reducing susceptibility to hydrogen embrittlement. The HE 
sensitivity is also influenced by the existence of γ and δ phases, where 
diffusible hydrogen is released primarily in the γ -matrix and damage is 
caused in the presence of hydrogen due to decohesion at the δ /matrix 
interface. The build-up of hydrogen at the δ /matrix interfaces results in 
weakened bonding strength and decohesion under strain. Furthermore, 
the research performed on evaluating how hydrogen embrittlement af -

8. Conclusion 
The microstructure of Ni-based printed parts is crucial in deter -
mining their mechanical properties. As-printed parts may have 
columnar or equiaxed grains, with defects impacting performance. At 
elevated temperatures, the microstructure of Ni-based printed parts 
undergoes changes, with the precipitation of strengthening phases, 
static/dynamic recrystallization and grain growth phenomenon, thereby 
leading to influence in their mechanical properties. Comprehensive 
understanding of the microstructural transformations in Ni-based su -
peralloys after printing and heat treatment is essential for controlling, 
optimizing, and managing their performance in demanding 
applications. 
The Zener effect improves thermal stability of alloys by the presence 
of solute atoms hindering grain boundary movement. Characteristics of 
solute atoms impact their effect on grain boundaries, with factors like 
diffusion rate and atomic size contributing to reducing grain boundary 
energy. Second-phase particles help maintain high-temperature stability 
with respect to by impeding grain boundary movement through a 
pinning mechanism known as the Zener drag mechanism. The strength 
of the pinning force depends on particle morphology, size, volume 
fraction, and coherence at the interface. 
The initial microstructure of Ni-based superalloys plays a crucial role 
in their thermal stability. It can either drive or impede grain boundary 
movement in high-temperature environments. Factors such as residual 
stress, cold rolling, and dislocation density (known as GNDs) impact 
recrystallization and grain boundary migration. Cold rolling treatment 
changes grain morphology from columnar to equiaxed, and alters dis -
tribution of Laves phase from inclined and dendritic to random. Laves 
phase morphology shifts from discrete islands to separate circular 
shapes. Fine microstructures with more nucleation sites promote faster 
recrystallization, while voids at grain boundaries slow down migration 
through the Zener pinning effect. The microstructure of AM parts typi -
cally exhibits a weak texture, particularly < 001 > . Consequently, the 
driving force for recrystallization in AM parts is low. The storage energy 
in preferred orientations differs, and can be ordered as follows: E110 >
E111 > E112 > E001. 
Changing additive manufacturing parameters can affect the thermal 
stability of Ni-based superalloys by modifying microstructure charac -
teristics. For instance, adjusting scanning speed can create a finer 
microstructure and higher residual stress, influencing recrystallization

The Particle Stimulated Nucleation mechanism (PSN) is utilized for 
microstructure control and refinement by introducing nucleation sites to 
promote recrystallization. Second-phase precipitation and the addition 
of particles can trigger PSN. This process is affected by the size of the 
particles, with coarse particles stimulating recrystallization and fine 
particles potentially inhibiting it through a pinning effect. 
The solution + aging treatment (SA) is effective in enhancing 
strength properties of Ni-based superalloys by activating precipitation 
phases that contribute to strength improvement. The thermal stability of 
precipitated phases in Ni-based superalloys is crucial for high- 
temperature applications. Overheating or prolonged heat treatment 
can alter phase characteristics, affecting morphology, size, interface, 
and volume fraction. Time and temperature impact precipitation sta -

Increased thermal temperature can lead to δ -phase formation and 
γ’’ -phase dissolution. Prolonged exposure increases δ -phase area frac -
tion and transforms γ’’ -phase to δ -phase. The (Al + Ti)/Nb ratio predicts 
γ ′ - and γ’’ -phase precipitation sequence, favoring γ’’ -phase formation 
with a ratio of 0.65. 
Recrystallization and grain growth are important physical properties 
of materials that impact their mechanical, physical, and chemical 
properties. Factors like microstructure characteristics and deformation 
conditions play crucial roles in these processes. For instance, high GND 
density, HAGB, and irregular second phase sizes can increase recrys -
tallization rate. At high temperatures, material work-hardening is 
important under mechanical stress. Recovery, DRX, and grain growth 
affect this property and can change material hardening behavior. have 
low SFE, leading to DRX and steady-state regions dominating the strain- 
hardening curve. Deformation temperatures and strain rates impact flow 
stress. Higher temperatures and lower strain rates reduce flow stress, 
promoting DRX and steady state. Lower temperatures and higher strain 
rates increase flow stress, showing more resistance to deformation. DRX 
is a key softening mechanism by forming new grains with fewer dislo -
cations in Ni-based superalloys. 
Texture formation in additive manufacturing is influenced by high 
solidification rates, impacting plastic deformation in hot processes. It 
affects behavior in plastic deformation, recrystallization, and grain 
growth compared to traditional methods, in terms of stored energy. 
Control of heat treatment and AM parameters can alter texture direction 
and intensity, promoting transformation of columnar to equiaxed grains. 
Studies have shown that nickel-based superalloys have high 
hydrogen embrittlement susceptibilities, leading to two common failure 
mechanisms: hydrogen-enhanced local plasticity (HELP) and hydrogen- 
enhanced decohesion (HEDE). HELP involves hydrogen atoms promot -
ing dislocation slip, reducing critical stress for dislocation migration, 
and causing early material failure. HEDE involves hydrogen reducing 
bonding forces between atoms, leading to brittle fracture at high 
hydrogen concentrations.
