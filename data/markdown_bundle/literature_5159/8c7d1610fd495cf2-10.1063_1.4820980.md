Effect of CeO2 Addition on the Properties of FeAl based 
Alloy Produced by Mechanical Alloying Technique
Deni S. Khaerudini*, Muljadi, P. Sardjono, Anggito P. Tetuko, 
P. Sebayang and M. Ginting 
Research Center for Physics, Indonesian Institute of Sciences (LIPI) 
Gd. 440, Kawasan Puspiptek Serpong Tangerang 15314 Banten INDONESIA 
*Email: deni.shidqi.khaerudini@lipi.go.id 
Abstract. Iron aluminides based on FeAl is notable for their low materials cost, ease of fabrication and good corrosion, 
suffixation and oxidation resistance. However, the application based on these unique properties still require the 
development of Fe-Al based alloy since it shows some drawbacks such as a lack of high temperature strength and low 
ductility. To improve the mechanical properties of FeAl based alloy, ceria (CeO 2) will be added to this compound. FeAl 
based alloy produced by the mechanical alloying (MA) technique. The developed specimens then assessed with respect 
to oxidation behaviour in high temperature, scale microstructure and hardness. The surface morphologies of the alloy 
evaluated and observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDX). The phase structures of oxide scale formed on them were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The results 
found that the FeAl intermetallic compound containing CeO 2 0.5 wt.% is less pores and CeO 2 1.0 wt.% is more 
homogen in powder and solid form, higher hardness and increase in their resistance to oxidation behaviour in high 
temperature compared with another percentage of CeO2. 
Keywords: FeAl, mechanical alloying, CeO2, high temperature, oxidation.

INTRODUCTION
From the mid-1900s, accelerating an effort has 
been directed towards increasing the temperature 
capability of existing materials systems and 
developing new material types. Understanding the 
material behaviour and control of component 
manufacture to ensure the desired behaviour have been 
key elements of these activities for all materials 
systems [1-5]. The requirement to operate at 
progressively higher temperatures will remain an on-
going need for the foreseeable future. Many industries 
will benefit from increased operating temperatures. As 
for example, in electricity generation, the efficiency of 
ultra-supercritical pulverized coal power plant can be 
increased from the current 47% to 50% if the steam 
parameters can be increased from 290-bar/580 /g113C to 
325-bar/625/g113C [6]. This will give major saving in fuel 
and consequent environmental benefits. Materials with 
higher temperature capability are essential if these, and 
many other objectives, are to be met. High temperature 
materials research in the metals and alloys area are still 
an extremely important field, and new alloy and 
composite systems are continually being developed for 
new applications [7]. High-temperature intermetallics 
have been vigorously studied since the early 1950s for 
various applications, such as in the aerospace and 
power-generation industries. Many efforts have been 
made to improve materials high temperature oxidation 
resistance. There are two kinds of methods in 
improving the oxidation resistance, one is alloying and 
the other is surface treatment [8,9]. 
Aluminides, like NiAl, TiAl and FeAl alloys, are 
one of the most materials considered for high-
temperature applications. A subsequent intermetallics 
such as iron aluminides have been developed because 
of their potential benefits in replacing steel in various 
high-temperature applications. Iron aluminides or 
FeAl is of great interest because of their many unique 
properties. The most exciting of these properties 
include low density (5.8-6.3 g/cm
3), lower cost 
(compared with corrosion and heat resistance steels) 
and a corrosion resistance at high temperatures in a 
variety of aggressive environments. They show 
excellent oxidation resistance, which is achieved by 
the formation of a protective Al 2O3-scale [10-12]. 
Since it still shows some drawbacks such as low room-
temperature ductility and lack of high-temperature 
strength [13,14]. Efforts to improve their ductility at 
room temperature have been made by appropriate 
processing, micro alloying, and oxide dispersion 
strengthening (ODS), which led to the enhancement of 
grain boundary cohesion, grain structure refinement, 
control of grain shape and adjusting the 
recrystallisation condition [15-18]. In this study, FeAl 
based alloys had been developed by using the powder

in alloy corrosion resistance at the high temperatures, 
the most interesting chemical elements to incorporate 
into iron-base alloys is oxygen-active elements or rare 
earths, such as cerium. The small amounts of this

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES 
Mechanical alloying of a mixture of elemental 
powders which consist of 72 wt.% Fe (60 /g80m, 99.9% 
purity), 28 wt.% Al (44 /g80m, 99.9% purity) with two 
different additions of CeO 2 at 0.5 and 1 wt.% were 
conducted using a high-energy planetary ball mill for 
46 hours and the vials rotated at a speed of 200 rpm. 
Milling was undertaken at room temperature using 
hardened steel balls and the balls to powder ratio was 
set at 10 : 1 by weight. Milling was conducted in a 
dried argon atmosphere and 2 wt.% of stearic acid was 
added to the system to prevent excessive welding in 
the chamber. The milled powders were then hot 
pressed at 25 MPa into a cylindrical shape with 33 mm 
diameter and 3-4 mm height and sintered at 1150 oC 
for 1 hour. The hot pressed samples were cut into 6 
pieces before being grinded to 240-1000 grit SiC 
papers and polished using 1 /g80m diamond paste until 
the surface scratches were removed. After 
ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for about 30 minutes, 
the samples were then oxidized at 1100 oC for 5 
cycles, and each cycle consists of 8 hours heating in 
the tube furnace in laboratory atmosphere. The sample 
weights were recorded after each cycle as need to plot 
the weight change versus oxidation time graph to 
observe the oxidation kinetics. The accuracy of the 
weight measurement is /g114 0.1 mg. The hardness test 
was determined based on the Rockwell method on the 
as consolidated material. Characterization was 
conducted on the as milled powders and on as 
consolidated material. Changes of sample surface were 
determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
with energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX). The 
oxide layers were analysed by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD). 
 
 
 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
In this study, the elemental powders of Fe and Al 
were used as starting materials to produce Fe-Al based 
alloy with CeO 2 as alloying element via mechanical 
alloying technique. Figure 1 shows the SEM 
micrographs of the as milled FeAl powder. As can be 
seen clearly in the images, the powder size of the 
sample with addition 1 wt.% CeO 2 (Fig. 1b) is much 
smaller and more homogen compared to the sample 
with addition 0.5 wt.% CeO 2 (Fig. 1a). According to 
mechanical alloying process, the ductile Al and brittle 
Fe powders were subjected to a heavy plastic 
deformation and this led to changes in particle size, 
morphology and microstructure. For this ductile-brittle 
combinations of starting powders, mechanical alloying 
initially proceeds by the flattening and work hardening 
of ductile aluminium powders, which is followed by 
extensive cold welding between particles, the 
formation of lamellar structures, and coarsening of the 
powder particle size distribution. Meanwhile, the 
powder size and shape with addition of CeO 2, beside 
as alloying element, usually added to produce a fine-
grained microstructure [5]. Therefore, the powder size 
which contain higher CeO 2 addition is much fine-
grained microstructure.

The EDX results for as milled FeAl powders are 
shown in Table. 2. The EDX analysis was conducted 
to check whether the composition corresponds to the 
earlier composition before the mechanical alloying 
process. The analysis indicates that there were some 
changes in composition can be either due to loss of Al 
due to sticking of the powder on the container wall and 
to the grinding balls and/or to gain of Fe from steel 
balls and container wall. The existence of carbon and 
oxygen were also found in the composition. The 
existence of carbon was due to the stearic acid used as 
a process-control agent for preventing the powder 
from excessive cold welding and agglomeration during 
mechanical alloying. The existence of oxygen is 
expected from ceria (CeO
2) as one of alloy elements. 
However, the EDX results show the reasonable 
percentage of elements and not contain other elements 
that are not expected to be present in the sample. 
 
 
 
FIGURE 2. XRD for as milled 72 wt.% Fe + 28 wt.% Al 
with addtion (a) 0.5 and (b) 1 wt.% CeO2. 
 
The crystallinity and purity of the prepared sample 
have been confirmed by powder XRD analysis. The 
XRD data for as milled powder are presented in Figure 
2. The XRD analysis was conducted to check whether 
the as milled powder has established the expected 
intermetallic phase, FeAlCeO 2. The XRD pattern of 
sample contains 0.5 wt.% CeO 2 (Fig.3a) shows the 
strong diffraction peaks at 2 /g84 = 30.8, 44.2, 64.3 and 
81.3 can be assigned to scattering from (100), (110), 
(200) and (211) planes, respectively, of the FeAl 
crystal lattice; and at 2/g84 = 28.6, 33.1, 47.5, 56.4, 76.68 
and 88.4 are corresponding to (111), (200), (220), 
(311), (331) and (422) planes, respectively, of the 
CeO2 crystal lattice. In the as milled powder contain 
1 wt.% CeO 2 (Fig. 3b), the strong diffraction peaks 
show at the scattering angles of 44.2 and 81.3 can be 
assigned to scattering from (110) and (211) planes, 
respectively, of the FeAl crystal lattice; and at the 
scattering angles of 28.6, 33.2, 47.5, 56.4 and 88.5 are 
corresponding to (111), (200), (220), (311) and (422) 
planes, respectively, of the CeO 2 crystal lattice. The 
analysis indicates that there were two main phases, 
FeAl and CeO2. The existence of FeAl phase shows in 
very strong peaks, specifically at 2 /g84 = 44.2 in both 
samples. It proves that the intermetallic phase FeAl 
was formed, even though not in the form as the 
expected intermetallic phase FeAlCeO2.

Figure 3 shows the SEM images of as hot pressed 
samples. The microstructure for both samples show 
there were a lot of voids. FeAl sample containing 0.5 
wt.% CeO 2 shows less voids, however 1 wt.% CeO 2 
shows smaller voids or pores. It is very important to 
avoid or minimize the voids formation since the void 
formation at the oxide-substrate interface can decrease 
the adherence and lead to failure. The void present in 
the samples is due to the large amount of gases 
produced during hot pressing process. The analysis 
indicates that difference CeO
2 addition could affect the 
surface morphology of FeAl samples.

CONCLUSIONS 
The present study demonstrates FeAl alloy with 
CeO2 addition can be synthesized by powder 
metallurgy followed by hot pressing technique. The 
CeO2 alloyed FeAl show improved microstructure, 
oxidation resistance and hardness. Increasing trend in 
the inherent properties of samples with an increase in 
wt.% of ceria shows that CeO 2 particles as active 
element can impart superior properties to FeAl alloy.
