Microstructural Development in High Volume Fraction Gamma 
Prime Ni-Base Oxide-Dispersion-Strengthened Superalloys 
S.K. KANG and R.C. BENN 
The mechanical alloying process has successfully combined oxide-dispersion-strengthening with con- 
ventional gamma prime precipitation hardening for advanced gas turbine materials. INCONEL* alloy 
MA 6000, a mechanically alloyed Ni-base superalloy, has the highest temperature capability among 
commercially available superalloys. Further improvement of the intermediate temperature strength has 
been pursued by both increasing the gamma prime content up to 80 vol pct and controlling the 
additions of refractory metals. The microstructural development of these new experimental alloys is 
reported in this paper, especially for an alloy, nominally identified as Alloy 51, having the com- 
position Ni-9.3 pct Cr-8.5 pct A1-6.6 pct W-3.4 pct Mo-0.15 pct Zr-0.01 pct B-I.1 pct Y203 
(wt pct). Both the primary and the secondary recrystallized microstructures of the alloy were charac- 
terized in terms of gamma grain structure, gamma prime precipitate morphology, orientation re- 
lationships, dispersoids, carbide/nitride particles, and chemical composition of intermetallic phases. 
The microstructural stability of the alloy under stress rupture conditions was also investigated in terms 
of coarsening/coalescence of gamma prime precipitates. Correlation of the microstructural informa- 
tion with the high temperature properties of the alloy is also briefly discussed. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
PRODUCTION of superalloys by the powder metallurgy 
(P/M) route has become increasingly attractive in recent 
years relative to conventional ingot metallurgy (I/M) su- 
peralloys.l 6 This is mainly due to the fact that the P/M 
route can be used in the production of oxide-dispersion- 
strengthened (ODS) superalloys, 7'8'9 rapidly solidified al- 
loys, ~~ clean alloys with less segregation, or alloys 
having fine grains suitable for superplastic forming. ~3'~4'~5 
The design of conventional superalloys in Ni- or Fe-base 
systems relies heavily upon gamma prime [Ni3(A1, Ti)] pre- 
cipitation for high temperature strength. However, the effec- 
tiveness of the gamma prime strengthening increment is lost 
as the operating temperature approaches the gamma prime 
solvus temperature. To circumvent this shortcoming, a fine 
dispersion of thoria particles was incorporated in the early 
ODS alloys developed by the DuPont Co. 16 Although some 
alloying elements such as Cr or Mo were subsequently 
added for oxidation resistance or solid-solution hardening, 
the addition of the reactive elements A1 and Ti, required for 
gamma prime hardening as well as oxidation resistance, was 
never sucessfully done in the early ODS alloys. Hence, their 
intermediate temperature strength was quite inferior to that 
of the conventional alloys, even though they showed supe- 
rior high temperature strength above 1000 ~ (1832 ~ 
The advanced ODS alloys, in which not only A1 or Ti but 
other alloying elements were successfully added with an 
yttria dispersion, became available when a new powder 
production process called "Mechanical Alloying" (MA) was 
announced by INCO in 1970. 7,~7 This process has suc- 
cessfully combined ODS with conventional precipitation

In response to the ever-increasing demand for high per- 
formance in jet engine components, further improvement of 
intermediate temperature strength has been pursued through 
the MA processing route by both increasing the content of 
gamma prime up to approximately 70 to 90 pct in volume 
and controlling the additions of refractory metals. Detailed 
alloy development work was reported previously, identi- 
fying various ternary, quaternary, and more complex alloys 
with improved performance. 2~ In the present paper, mi- 
crostructural aspects of these new alloys are reported, espe- 
cially for an alloy, presently referred to as Alloy 51, having 
the composition of Ni-9.3 pct Cr-8.5 pct AI-6.6 pct W- 
3.4 pct Mo-0.15 pct Zr-0.01 pct B-I.1 pct Y203 (wt pct). 
The high volume pct y' ODS alloys, typified by Alloy 51, 
represent a new class of turbine blade alloys. These alloys 
are being developed as a supplement to the successful 
INCONEL alloy MA 6000 which is now being produced 
commercially. Each alloy system has its own distinctive 
properties in addition to many common attributes inherent 
in ODS alloys, such as excellent high temperature strength 
and fatigue resistance. In particular, Alloy 51-type alloys 
possess higher intermediate temperature strength than 
INCONEL alloy MA 6000 and superior oxidation resistance 
to all established superalloys. These characteristics are 
derived through some property trade-offs compared with 
INCONEL alloy MA 6000 such as high temperature 
strength and Burner Rig sulfidation resistance. However, 
recent work 24 indicates that these aspects may be improved 
through secondary hot working and coating, respectively. 
Following a brief discussion on the alloy processing and 
properties, microstructural development during alloy pro- 
cessing, as well as microstructural stability under stress- 
rupture conditions, will be presented. Some correlation of 
the microstructural information with the high temperature 
properties of the alloy is also briefly discussed. 
II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES 
A. Alloy Processing 
Elemental Ni, Cr, W, and Mo powders were blended with 
preground Ni-A1, Ni-Zr, and Ni-B master alloys and Y203. 
Powder batches were then mechanically alloyed in attritor 
mills under controlled conditions. The resultant mechani- 
cally alloyed powder was characterized using chemical and 
screen analyses and metallographic examination. After 
screening to remove the coarse + 12 mesh particles, pow- 
der batches were cone blended and packed into extrusion 
cans which were then sealed, and extruded to round bar 
(20.4 mm/0.8 inch diameter). 
B. Directional Recrystallization 
Stationary gradient anneals were used to determine the 
recrystallization behavior as a function of annealing tem- 
perature. Extruded bar samples 102 mm (4 inches) long 
were individually annealed in a gradient furnace having a 
temperature profile ranging from 800 ~ (1472 ~ to 
1350 ~ (2462 ~ over the 102 mm (4 inch) length. The 
annealing time was one-half hour. After this heat treatment, 
the bars were surface ground parallel to the extrusion direc- 
tion to reveal the recrystallized grain structure as a function 
of position along the thermal gradient. This is a simple 
method for determining the temperature range over which 
recrystallization to a coarse grain structure will take place. 
It also pinpoints the critical heat treatment temperature at 
which the best coarse elongated grain structure is achieved.

chanical alloying process, have been mostly annihilated in 
the recrystallization. Some dislocations are still observed 
locally in some grains. Careful observation also revealed the

Preliminary studies 24 on hot rolling Alloy 51 indicate that 
improvements in grain shape control are possible, which 
increase the high temperature strength capability. At high 
operating temperatures, the oxide dispersion and elongated 
grain shape are the predominant strengthening components. 
The strengthening contribution from the 3" phase decreases 
as the 7' solvus temperature is approached. In addition, in 
Alloy 51, it is possible that the lower strength, as compared 
to INCONEL alloy MA 6000, can be explained in part by 
the above microstructural observations in Alloy 51, for 
which the gamma prime phase became completely con-

(as shown in Figures 9 and 10), and the 3'/3" interfacial

As in certain high 3" RSR alloys, 32 the dominant micro- 
structural features associated with creep resistance in these 
high ODS alloys at intermediate temperatures are the high 
volume fraction of 3", the large amount of 3'-3" surface 
area, and the 3'-3" interfacial dislocations. The high tem- 
perature and/or long term strength is, of course, attributed to 
the oxide dispersion effects. Coalescence of the 3" phase 
under stress rupture conditions to form a rafted structure 
normal to the applied stress has been shown 33 to improve 
creep resistance in the [100] direction. The mechanism is 
believed to be that dislocation climb around 3" precipitates 
is eliminated, forcing cutting of the 3'-3" interface by mo- 
bile dislocations. Rafting has been observed in INCONEL 
alloy MA 6000,18 and its influence on rupture strength will 
be discussed elsewhere. 34 In the high y' ODS alloys, such 
as Alloy 51, the coalescence of the 3" phase appears to 
occur more uniformly in relation to the applied stress and 
does not develop this pronounced alignment. Both alloys 
display the same nominal [ 110] texture in the recrystallized 
condition so that orientation effects may be discounted and 
the difference in behavior related primarily to gamma prime 
content and/or composition. In this regard it is interesting to 
speculate on whether lower gamma prime contents (e.g., 
lower A1 levels) in these alloys would develop rafted struc- 
tures that may be desirable. Further alloy development will 
determine this 35'36 and whether any significant loss in the 
excellent oxidation resistance 24 and potential coatability 37 of 
Alloy 51 will be incurred. 
Improvements in creep strength in both INCONEL alloy 
MA 6000 and Alloy 51 could be expected with the devel- 
opment of textures other than [110], for example, [100] or 
[111]. There is some evidence ~9'36 that, by controlled ther- 
momechanical processing and/or directional recrystalliza- 
tion, texture can be altered. From work on RSR analogues 38 
such as RSR 116, it is known that development of a [100]

IV. SUMMARY 
The microstructural development of high volume fraction 
gamma prime ODS Alloy 51 was investigated by character- 
izing its microstructure in the various intermediate process- 
ing stages. The primary and secondary recrystallization 
were recognized as salient features of the microstructural 
development. 
The primary recrystallized structure which occurred dur- 
ing extrusion is basically submicron grains of gamma phase 
with random orientations. Fine dispersoids and large 
carbide/nitride particles were also uniformly distributed in 
the primary recrystallized structure. 
The secondary recrystallization, induced either by gra- 
dient or zone annealing, resulted in large elongated gamma 
grains of dominant [110] texture. Submicron size, faceted 
gamma prime phase occupied approximately 75 pct in 
volume.
