1. Introduction 
The development of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) or complex 
concentrated alloys (CCAs) challenges the traditional alloying strategy 
consisting of only one or two predominant elements [ 1 , 2 ]. The greatly 
broadened alloy composition space by the idea of HEAs results in un -
expected solid-solution related structures and hence outstanding inte -
grated properties, which attracts growing attentions in the material 
science community over the past decades [ 3 – 8 ]. Refractory high- 
entropy alloys (RHEAs) are an important group of HEAs that mainly 
consist of refractory elements. These elements render RHEAs body 
centered cubic (BCC) or hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure with 
high strength and hardness, decent ductility and excellent corrosion 
resistance [ 9 – 11 ], thereinto, the high and soften resistance at elevated 
temperatures are the most potential advantage [ 12 – 14 ]. For example, 
WMoTaNb(V) can retain over 500 MPa at 1600 
◦
C [ 15 ], yet their high 
density, room temperature (RT) brittleness, and poor oxidation resis -
tance greatly limits the application potential of these W/Mo contained 
RHEAs [ 16 , 17 ]. In the consideration of a good balance of strength, 
ductility, density, and oxidation resistance, antioxidant elements with 
low density, e.g., Al, Cr, Si are usually added in RHEAs. These newly 
designed RHEAs include AlMo

2. Material and methods 
Novel series of Ti
2

atmosphere on a water-cooled copper crucible. Raw metals of Ti, Zr, V, 
Nb, Al with purity of 99.95 wt% and Hf with purity of 99.5 wt% were 
used. The ingots were flipped and remelted at least seven times to assure 
chemical homogeneity. All samples were cut by electric discharge 
machining, prepared by grinding, polishing in diamond suspension, and 
cleaned by ultrasound before further experiments. 
The crystal structure was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
EMPYREAN) using Cu-Ka radiation target operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, 
and the angles of 2 θ scanning was from 20
◦
to 100
◦
with a scanning rate 
of 4
◦

composition was carried out by electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, 
JXA-8530F PLUS) and scanning electron microscaopy (SEM, Zeiss Supra 
55). More details of microstructure at nano-scale were revealed by 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20), and the spec -
imens for TEM were prepared by a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB, 
Helios G4UX, Thermo Fisher Scientific USA) instrument. 
Compression tests (test standards: ASTM E9 – 09) were conducted on 
cylindrical specimens with the dimension of Φ 6 × 9 mm using a Wipper- 
mann (WPM) materials testing machine at room temperature and a 
dynamic thermal simulation testing machine (Gleeble-1500D) at high 
temperatures (873 K and 1073 K), respectively. The strain rate was set at 
1 × 10
 3
/s. During high temperature test, the specimens were first 
heated to the testing temperature at a heating rate of 5 K/s and held for 
10 min before loading. Vickers hardness (test standards used ASTM: 
E384-11e1) was measured using a Vickers hardness tester (MH-50). The 
loading force is 1000 g and the loading time is 15 s. The measurements 
were repeated seven times on each sample. The density of the series 
alloys was measured by the Archimedes drainage method (Mettler- 
ME204E balance with precision of 0.0001 g). 
Isothermal oxidation tests were conducted at 1073 K and exposed to 
static laboratory air. The dimensions of the prepared samples were 10 ×
10 × ~3 mm after surface grinding down to 2000-grits. Mass gain of the 
oxidation samples was measured using a high sensitivity balance 
(0.0001 g) and recorded from 1 h to 50 h. Three samples for each time 
interruption were used to ensure data accuracy. The phase constitutions 
and microstructures of the oxidized samples were investigated by XRD 
and EPMA.
