 A B S T R A C T
The mechanical properties and microstructural evolution of as-cast and heat-treated dendritic dual phase high-
entropy alloy (HEA) Al0.5CoCrFeNi are investigated to provides valuable insights into their plastic deformation, 
particularly under varying strain rates and temperatures. The as-cast Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloy, consisting of 
face-centered cubic (FCC) dendritic and body-centered cubic (BCC) interdendritic phases, is compared to a 
heat-treated variant, which contains additional needle-shaped BCC precipitates within the dendritic domains. 
Uniaxial compression tests are conducted across a strain rate range of 10−3 to 2000 s−1 and a temperature 
range of 173 to 673 K for both types of the alloys. The results show that the yield strength of the alloys 
increases with higher strain rates or lower temperatures. The heat-treated alloy, benefiting from the precipitate 
strengthening of additional needle-shaped precipitates, exhibits higher yield strength than the as-cast alloy. 
Dislocation slip and kink bands dominate the plastic deformation firstly. As the true strain increases, nano 
twins are observed. Cryogenic temperatures promote more nano twins with different variants. Additionally, 
different plastic deformation partitioning behaviors between the FCC and BCC phases are observed due to the 
microstructure differences between the two types of alloys. Furthermore, the Johnson-Cook-Cowper Symonds 
(JC-CS) constitutive models are developed and successfully describe the plastic flow of both types of the 
alloys over a wide range of strain rates and temperatures, providing a valuable tool for future research and 
applications.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) represent a novel class of materials 
designed via maximizing configurational entropy [1–3]. These alloys 
typically consist of at least five principal elements, each contribut-
ing 5% to 35% of the atomic composition [4–6]. The significantly 
broadened compositional space of HEAs opens up vast possibilities 
for diverse microstructures and exceptional properties, including high 
strength, outstanding hardness, excellent wear and corrosion resistance, 
enhanced toughness at low temperatures, and superior thermal stabil-
ity [7–9]. These qualities make HEAs ideal for applications in demand-
ing environments in defense, aerospace, and automotive industries [10–
12].
One key research area in HEAs is the development of dual-phase 
HEAs, featuring a soft phase for ductility and a hard phase for strength

Despite numerous studies on the microstructure evolution and me-

and temperatures is still not well understood. At high strain rates, 
deformation involves complex interactions among work hardening, 
strain rate sensitivity, thermal softening and phonon drag effects. This 
interplay can result in an increase in flow stress and changes in plastic 
deformation mechanisms [30–32]. There is also an urgent need for 
developing constitutive models that can accurately describe plastic flow 
over wide ranges of strain rates and temperatures.
In this work, the mechanical properties of as-cast and heat-treated 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi HEAs are systematically studied across a wide range of 
strain rates and temperatures. The effects of strain rate, microstructure 
and temperature on mechanical behavior are thoroughly analyzed. For 
both types of the Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys, the deformation mechanisms 
and plastic deformation partitioning behaviors between the FCC and 
BCC phases are examined. Additionally, a modified Johnson–Cook (JC) 
model is employed to characterize the dynamic flow behavior of the 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi HEAs.
2. Materials and experiments
2.1. Materials
The as-cast Al0.5CoCrFeNi HEA is produced via arc melting with 
a Ti-getter in an ultra-pure argon atmosphere using high-purity raw 
materials (≥99.9%). Some as-cast alloy ingots are subjected to ad-
ditional heat treatment at 1273 K, followed by rapid quenching in 
water. Our experiments are conducted on the as-cast and heat-treated 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys.
The as-cast and heat-treated Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys before and after 
dynamic compression are characterized via X-ray diffraction (XRD), 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and transmission elec-
tron microscopy (TEM). XRD measurements is performed on a Pan-
alytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer at 40 kV and 40 mA [33]. 
The scattering angle (2𝜃) ranges from 30◦ to 100◦. SEM, EDS, and 
EBSD characterizations are performed with an FEI Quanta 250 scanning 
electron microscope equipped with the Oxford X-MaxN EDS detector 
and Oxford Nordlys Nano EBSD detector. Before the EBSD characteri-
zations, the specimen is ground with sandpaper, and then polished with 
a silicon solution. Electro-polishing is performed in a solution mixture 
of 10 mL perchloric acid and 90 mL ethanol at 10 V and −15 ◦C. For 
the TEM characterization, samples are mechanically ground to 80 μm , 
and then thinned to an electron-transparent thickness using a twin-
jet polishing system with the same solution as for the EBSD. TEM 
characterizations are performed in a JEM 2100F.
The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for the as-cast and heat-treated 
alloys are presented in Fig. 1. Both types of alloys consist of FCC and 
BCC dual phases. The BCC peaks in the heat-treated alloy are more 
pronounced, indicating a higher BCC phase content.
As shown in Fig. 2a and b, the as-cast alloy exhibits a typical 
dendritic structure, including the FCC dendritic phase and BCC in-
terdendritic phase. After heat treatment, In addition to the dendritic 
structure, abundant needle-like BCC precipitates appear in the dendritic 
phase (Fig. 2d and e). The grain sizes of the FCC dendritic phase in both 
as-cast and heat-treated alloys are about 300–500 μm , while the grain 
size of the BCC interdendritic phase in heat-treated alloys (0.75 μm ) 
is smaller than that in as-cast alloys (1.71 μm ) due to the newly 
formed needle-like BCC precipitates. The microstructure differences of 
the BCC phase affect the plastic deformation partitioning behaviors 
between the FCC and BCC phases, which will be addressed in next 
section. Moreover, the kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps (Fig. 
2c and f) indicate minimal residual strain in both alloys. The BCC 
domains (interdendritic phase or precipitates) are aligned in the same 
crystallographic direction in each grain (for example, grains 1–4 in 
Fig. 2a and d). The crystallographic relationship between the FCC and 
BCC phases is illustrated via the {110} and {111} pole figures from 
grains 1–4. The FCC and BCC phases obey the Kurdyumov–Sachs (KS) 
orientation relationship [34,35]:

⟨110⟩FCC ∥ ⟨111⟩BCC. (2)
The EDS elemental maps of Al, Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni are displayed in 
Fig. 3. The elemental segregation in the dendritic HEA Al0.5CoCrFeNi
is similar to that in the eutectic HEA AlCoCrFeNi2.1 [36], which also

4.1. Strain rate effects
The slope of logarithmic flow stress versus the logarithmic strain 
rate is known as the strain rate sensitivity (SRS), and it characterizes 
how mechanical response varies with different strain rates [52]. The 
yield strength versus strain rate plot (Fig. 12) can be divided into two 
distinct regimes (I and II) corresponding to low and high SRS. The strain 
rate sensitivity factor, 𝑚 = 𝜕 ln 𝜎∕𝜕 ln ̇ 𝜀, is much higher under dynamic 
loading (high strain rate) than that in quasi-static loading (low strain 
rate). Thermally activated glide [53] and dislocation drag [54] are two 
competing strain-rate dependent mechanisms of slip [55], and govern 
regimes I and II, respectively.

come obstacles, such as Peierls–Nabarro barriers, via thermal fluctua-

be expressed as

4.2. Deformation mechanisms
During dynamic compression, dislocation slip plays the dominant 
role in plastic deformation. Temperature has a significant effect on 
dislocation activity, which subsequently affects yield strength. Low 
temperature limits the thermal activation of atoms, thereby restrict-
ing dislocation motion. As temperature rises, dislocation movement 
becomes more pronounced, and yield strength normally decreases [61,
62].
The critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) of Al0.5CoCrFeNi HEA is 
1223 MPa at room temperature [29]. Generally, deformation twins 
are more readily activated at higher strain rates and lower tempera-
tures [57,63]. For Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys upon dynamic compression at 
293 K, the dominant deformation mechanisms are dislocation slip and 
kink bands at 10% strain, nano twins are observed at 23% strain. More 
nano twins with different variants are found at 173 K.
According to the TEM results, no obvious metastable phase trans-
formation are observed. For Al0.1CoCrFeNi alloys with lower (𝛾sf ) than 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys [64], phase transformation from FCC to BCC 
phases occurs at strains over 35% and 77 K under extreme uniaxial 
tension [65]. In terms of the free energy-based theory, the difference in 
Gibbs free energy (𝐺) between different phases can be directly related 
to the stacking fault energy (𝛾sf ) [66]. Thus, given the higher (𝛾sf ) in 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi, less loading strain and higher loading temperature in 
this study, it is reasonable that no metastable phase transformation 
occurs in this study.
The kink bands in Fig. 6 exhibit different orientations relative to 
the matrix [67], different from the consistent orientation relationship 
observed between deformation twins and the matrix. Formation of kink 
bands is driven by dislocations and lattice distortion during plastic 
deformation, with dislocation walls forming to relieve back stress from 
the curved lattice. Dislocations, lattice distortion and the misorientation 
of dislocation walls increase with increasing plastic strain, eventually 
leading to the formation of kink bands [68,69].
4.3. Microstructure effects
The as-cast and heat-treated alloys subjected to SHPB loading to 
different strains are characterized (Figs. 6a–f and 7), and GND density 
is used to compare the plastic deformation capacity of the FCC and BCC 
phases (Fig. 9).
For the heat-treated alloys, the soft FCC dendritic phase accom-
modates more plastic deformation. Phase boundaries can serve as 
sources/barriers for dislocation nucleation/transmission [70,71]. More-
over, due to strain incompatibility between these two phases, the 
harder BCC phase hinders dislocation motion, leading to dislocation ac-
cumulation in the soft FCC dendritic phase. As the strain increases, GND 
density in the FCC phase rises sharply. After the plastic deformation in 
the FCC phase is saturated, it gradually accumulates in the BCC phase 
and results in high GND density [72,73].
However, in the as-cast alloy, the GND density in the BCC phase 
remains consistently higher than that in the FCC phase during compres-
sion. This difference can be attributed to the absence of the BCC precip-
itates in the as-cast alloy. Without the widely distributed needle-shaped 
fine precipitates in the dendritic phase, the as-cast alloy contains fewer 
FCC/BCC phase boundaries, which serve as dislocation nucleation sites. 
Additionally, the short duration of impact loading restricts dislocation 
propagation in the FCC phase. Thus, high GND density regions are 
found only near the phase boundaries (in the as-cast alloys), rather than 
evenly distributed in the dendritic phase as in the heat-treated alloy.

5. Conclusions
Mechanical responses and corresponding microstructural character-
istics of the as-cast and heat-treated Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys are investi-
gated over wide strain rates and temperatures. The main conclusions 
are list as follows:
• The as-cast alloy is composed of a Ni-Al lean FCC dendritic 
phase and a Ni-Al rich BCC interdendritic phase. After the heat 
treatment, additional needle-shaped fine BCC precipitates appear 
in the FCC dendritic phase. The KS orientation relationship exists 
between the FCC (dendritic phase) and BCC (interdendritic phase 
and precipitates) phases.
• Compared to the as-cast alloy, the heat-treated alloy exhibits 
higher yield strength due to precipitation strengthening and 
demonstrates a greater temperature sensitivity.
• For Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloys upon dynamic compression at 293 K, the 
dominant deformation mechanisms are dislocation slip and kink 
bands at 10% strain, nano twins are observed at 23% strain. More 
nano twins with different variants are found at 173 K. Disloca-
tions preferentially nucleate at the phase boundaries. These two 
types of alloys exhibit different plastic deformation partitioning 
behaviors between the FCC and BCC phases due to the differences 
in their microstructures.
• The effects of temperature and strain rate on the plastic flow of 
the as-cast and heat-treated alloys are modeled using a JC–CS 
constitutive model.
