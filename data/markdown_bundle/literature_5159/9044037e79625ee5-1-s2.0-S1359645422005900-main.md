We design and investigate a ferritic multicomponent alloy with high electrical resistivity, low tempera- 
ture coeﬃcient of resistivity ( TCR ) in a wide-temperature range, high strength, and moderate deformabil- 
ity, aiming to overcome the bottlenecks limiting the development of cost-eﬃcient high-quality resistors. 
The developed bulk alloy with nominal composition Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 12 Ti 3 Si 2 (at. %) shows a body centered cu- 
bic (BCC) matrix phase embedded with ordered coherent multicomponent nanoprecipitates in as-cast 
condition, which enables a high resistivity of 183 μ/Omega1·cm, a low TCR of -35 ppm/K (from 298 to 673 K), 
a high yield strength of 1055 MPa, a high ultimate compressive strength of 1980 MPa and a moderate 
uniform compressive strain of 33% (at 673 K). The fully coherent and ordered multicomponent L2 1 nano- 
precipitates in the BCC- α matrix enhance the stress required for dislocation propagation, leading to the 
improved strength and work hardening ability, according to the comparative study on the alloy variants 
with and without nanoprecipitates. Also, the multicomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates enable the effective 
tuning of Kondo-like scattering and contribute to the low TCR of the material. The work thus demon- 
strates a design strategy for low-cost precision resistance alloys to achieve high and stable resistivity in 
wide temperature ranges combined with high strength and moderate compressive deformability by in- 
troducing well-tuned ordered coherent multicomponent precipitates in multicomponent alloy systems.

1. Introduction 
Resistance materials with high resistivity ( > 100 μ/Omega1·cm) 
and low temperature coeﬃcient of resistivity ( TCR ) values 
( < 100 ppm/K) [1] are essentially required in many key ﬁelds such 
as in high precision electronic measuring systems like global po- 
sitioning systems (GPS) [ 2 , 3 ], data storage solutions [3] , thermo- 
electrical devices [ 4 , 5 ], and in temperature and ﬂow control sen- 
sors for automobile applications [ 3 , 6 ]. Iron-chromium-aluminum 
(FeCrAl) ferritic alloys are widely used as resistance materials ow- 
ing to their high electrical resistivity, excellent high-temperature 
oxidation resistance and cost eﬃciency [ 7 , 8 ]. For instance, a com- 
mercial FeCrAl alloy with nominal composition Fe 74.5 Cr 20 Al 5.5 (at. 
%) exhibits excellent performance for resistance heating [9] . The 
room-temperature resistivity ( ρRT ) of Fe 50 Cr 30 Al 20 (at. %) alloy

reaches as high as 186 μ/Omega1·cm with a TCR of around -90 ppm/K 
in the temperature range from room-temperature to 773 K [7] . 
Despite the excellent combinations of high resistivity and low 
TCR achieved in some established FeCrAl ferritic alloys, the lim- 
ited dislocation mobility in the supersaturated BCC lattice leads 
to the intrinsic brittleness. Yet, the developing industry calls for 
strong and deformable high resistivity alloys with low TCR to im- 
prove the machinability and sensitivity, e.g., for miniaturizing the 
electronic devices [10] . In this regard, there are some aspects that 
limit the further development and practical applications of the cost 
eﬃcient FeCrAl alloys as resistance materials. First, a common way 
to enhance the strength and electrical resistivity of Fe-Cr-A1 alloys 
is increasing Cr and Al contents, but the high Cr and Al contents 
usually promote the brittleness of the materials, leading to a poor 
deformability [8] . Second, although adjusting the ratios of Fe, Cr 
and Al can be employed to tune the TCR values, it is still challeng- 
ing to simultaneously obtain fairly high resistivity and low TCR [7] . 
Third, the limited compositional ranges of the conventional FeCrAl 
alloys restrict the space for tuning the multiple properties includ- 
ing resistivity, TCR , strength and deformability. Fourth, although

the electrical and mechanical properties of the FeCrAl alloys can 
be expected to be closely related to their microstructures, very 
few research efforts have been devoted to microstructural con-

properties. 
In recent years, the concepts of multicomponent high-entropy 
alloys (HEAs) [ 11 , 12 ], complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) [13] or 
multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) [14] have signiﬁcantly ex- 
panded the compositional space for exploring alloys with excel- 
lent mechanical and functional properties [15] . For instance, ex- 
cellent combinations of mechanical and soft magnetic properties 
have been achieved in FeCoNiTaAl HEAs through introducing co- 
herent and ordered L1 2 nanoprecipitates into the face-centered 
cubic (FCC) matrix [ 16 , 17 ]. HEAs are also promising candidate 
materials for achieving high resistivity since the multiple princi- 
pal elements are likely to induce signiﬁcant lattice distortion for 
promoting the scattering of conduction electrons [ 18 , 19 ]. For in- 
stance, the resistivity of an Al 2.08 CoCrFeNi HEA with an ordered 
pseudo-binary B2-BCC structure is 117.24 μ/Omega1·cm at 4.2 K and 
119.90 μ/Omega1·cm at 300 K, respectively [18] . Recently, highly dis- 
persed precipitates have been demonstrated to have a strong ef- 
fect on the TCR values of Al x CoCrFeNi (1.0 ≤ x ≤ 3.0) HEAs 
[10] . However, the electrical resistivity values of the multicom- 
ponent HEAs reported so far are still not fairly high (usually 
lower than 120 μ/Omega1·cm) and the TCR values are mainly tested 
in a narrow temperature range, e.g., from 4.2 to 300 K. In 
this regard, substantial research effort s are still required for fur- 
ther developing multicomponent resistance alloys with improved 
performance. 
It has been recognized that the scattering of conduction elec- 
trons by the lattice defects such as solute atoms, grain bound- 
aries and phase interfaces can greatly enhance electrical resis- 
tivity [20] . For instance, solute elements such as Ti, Si and Mo 
in α-Fe matrix have been shown to increase electrical resistiv- 
ity effectively due to the lattice distortions caused by the atomic 
size differences [ 21 , 22 ]. It has also been reported that Ti, Si, Mo 
and Nb can enhance the yield strength of ferritic FeCrAl alloys 
via solid solution strengthening and/or precipitation hardening 
[ 23 , 24 ]. Therefore, further alloy development by introducing mi- 
nor elements into the FeCrAl alloy matrix is promising for achiev- 
ing improved combination of mechanical and electrical resistivity 
properties. 
Based on the above considerations, in this work, we then design 
a novel ferritic multicomponent alloy with enhanced resistivity and 
mechanical properties by integrating minor alloying elements (i.e., 
Ti and Si) into a ferritic matrix composed of multiple principal el- 
ements (i.e., Fe, Cr and Al). The newly developed bulk alloy with 
nominal composition Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 12 Ti 3 Si 2 (at. %) shows a BCC- α ma- 
trix phase embedded with ordered coherent multicomponent L2 1 
nanoprecipitates in as-cast condition. The strength, deformability, 
electrical resistivity and TCR in a wide-temperature range (300 
to 1273 K) have been investigated for alloy samples in various 
processing conditions. The microstructural evolution and elemen- 
tal distribution in the various alloy samples have been analyzed 
by multi-scale techniques including scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atom probe to- 
mography (APT). The mechanisms responsible for the enhanced re- 
sistivity and mechanical properties of the newly developed mul- 
ticomponent FeCrAlTiSi alloy compared to the conventional Fe- 
CrAl alloys are then discussed based on the experimental results. 
The systematic studies provide useful insights for developing cost- 
eﬃcient precision resistance alloys with high and stable resistivity 
in wide temperature ranges combined with high strength and large 
deformability.

2. Experimental methods 
2.1. Alloy processing 
An ingot of 2 kg with nominal composition Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 12 Ti 3 Si 2 
(at. %) was prepared by magnetic levitation melting using small 
pieces of pure metals (purity higher than 99.8 wt. %). Alloy plates 
with a thickness of 10 mm were cut by electrical discharge ma- 
chining from the as-cast ingot. Some plates were then homoge- 
nized at 1300 °C for 3 h in Ar atmosphere. Both the as-cast and 
homogenized alloy samples were prepared for the subsequent mi- 
crostructural, electrical, and mechanical investigations. The exact 
chemical composition of the bulk alloy in as-cast condition was an- 
alyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and the 
results are provided in Table 1 . 
2.2. Property measurements 
Resistivity measurements were performed under continuous 
heating from 300 to 1273 K on cuboid samples with dimensions 
of 21 × 3 × 1 mm using the conventional four-terminal poten- 
tiometric method with a constant current of 10 mA being passed 
through the specimens [25] . The measurement accuracy of resis- 
tance is 1 μ/Omega1. The heating rate during resistivity measurements 
was controlled at about 4 K/min. 
Compressive tests were performed at a constant engineering 
strain rate of 1 × 10 −3 s −1 using cylinder samples with a diameter 
of 6 mm and a height of 8 mm. Nano-indentation tests were con- 
ducted at room temperature using a KLA G200X indentation tester 
equipped with a Berkovich tip upon a load-control method, to a 
maximum load of 50 0 0 μN at a constant rate of 50 μN/s. The spac- 
ing between two neighboring indentations was 12 μm. Thermal 
drift was conﬁned below 0.1 nm/s and corrected during unloading 
stage by the measurement system automatically. The samples for 
nano-indentation analysis were mechanically polished by sandpa- 
pers, followed by electrochemical polishing with a mixed solution 
of 50 mL perchloric acid and 450 mL methyl alcohol. 
2.3. Microstructural characterization 
X-ray diffraction analysis for determining phase constituents 
was performed by using a D/max 2550 X-ray diffractometer with 
a Cu-K α radiation ( λ = 0.154 nm). A scan speed of 2 °/min was 
used with polished sample surfaces. Microstructural observations 
were performed via back-scattered electron imaging (BSEI) and 
electron channeling contrast imaging (ECCI) using a TESCAN CLARA 
SEM system. The BSE images were collected at 20 kV, and the 
ECC images were collected at 30 kV. Electron backscatter diffrac- 
tion (EBSD) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analy- 
ses were also carried out using the EDAX instrument equipped in 
the SEM system. High-resolution TEM and scanning TEM (STEM) 
analyses were performed on the twin-jet electropolished thin foils 
using an FEI Talos F200X microscope operating at 200 kV. Elemen- 
tal distributions in the alloy samples were examined by TEM-EDS 
at nanoscale and APT (LEAP 50 0 0XR, Cameca) at near-atomic scale.

3. Results 
3.1. Initial microstructure 
Fig. 1 a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the as- 
cast and homogenized alloy samples, suggesting that both the two 
types of samples contain a body-centered cubic (BCC- α) phase. For 
the as-cast sample, apart from the BCC- α matrix phase, diffraction 
peaks corresponding to an Fe 2 Ti-type Laves phase with hexagonal 
close-packed (HCP) structure are identiﬁed. Also, the as-cast sam- 
ple contain L2 1 nanoprecipitates as revealed by later TEM and APT 
analysis, but the nanoprecipitates cannot be unveiled by XRD as 
their diffraction peaks are overlaid with that of the BCC- α matrix 
phase. The BCC- α matrix phase in the as-cast and homogenized al- 
loy samples were further identiﬁed by EBSD, as shown in Fig. 1 b 
and 1 f, respectively. Both the as-cast and homogenized alloys show 
nearly equiaxed grain morphology and no dendrite structure is ob- 
served. The alloying elements, e.g., Al and Ti, have the potential 
to reduce the constitutional undercooling during solidiﬁcation, and 
therefore promote the formation of nearly equiaxed grain morphol- 
ogy [26–28] . The HCP Laves phase in the as-cast sample was con- 
ﬁrmed to be distributed along grain boundaries ( Fig. 1 d and e) 
with an area fraction of 1.2% ± 0.2%. More EBSD phase maps and 
BSE images taken from different local regions of the bulk samples 
are shown in Figs. S1 and S2, respectively. Based on multiple EBSD 
maps and BSE images, the average grain sizes (GS) of the as-cast 
and homogenized specimens are 201 ± 30 μm and 220 ± 62 μm, 
respectively. 
Fig. 2 a–f show the BSE images of the as-cast and homoge- 
nized alloy samples, respectively. For the as-cast sample, discontin- 
uous precipitates distribute along grain boundaries (marked by yel- 
low arrows) and precipitate free zones exist near grain boundaries 
( Fig. 2 b). Dispersed nanoprecipitates are shown in grain interiors 
( Fig. 2 c). After homogenization, minor GB Laves phase remained 
( Fig. 2 e). Prolonging homogenization time at 1300 °C and elevat- 
ing temperature to 1350 °C did not fully remove the residual Laves 
phase particles (Fig. S3). The grain-interior dispersed nanoprecipi- 
tates, presented in the as-cast sample, cannot be observed in the 
homogenized material ( Fig. 2 f). According to the SEM-EDS map- 
ping results shown in Fig. 2 g (corresponding to the sample region 
in Fig. 2 e), all of the alloying elements, i.e., Fe, Cr, Al, Ti and Si, are 
uniformly distributed in the homogenized sample at microscale. 
This suggests that the multiple elements are completely dissolved 
into the BCC matrix and a super saturated solid solution is formed 
after homogenization treatment. For comparison, BSE images of a 
nanoprecipitate-free reference Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 17 (at. %) alloy in as-cast 
condition are also shown in Fig. S4a–d. 
To reveal the structure of the dispersed nanoprecipitates in 
the as-cast alloy, high-resolution (HR) TEM and STEM analysis 
were performed. Fig. 3 a shows the high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld 
(HAADF) STEM image of a typical sample region in grain inte- 
rior. A large number of nearly spherical particles can be observed. 
Based on the multiple STEM images, the mean area fraction and 
the average diameter ( d ) of the nanoprecipitates are 33% ± 4% and 
45 nm ± 11 nm, respectively. The average thickness of the ob- 
served sample regions in the TEM foils is estimated to be about 
100 nm [ 29 , 30 ], and thus the volume fraction of the nearly spher- 
ical L2 1 nanoprecipitates is estimated to be about 4.0% ± 0.2%. 
Fig. 3 b shows the corresponding selected area electron diffraction 
(SAED) pattern, in which the diffractogram is composed of two sets 
of reﬂections stemming from the BCC- α matrix and the L2 1 nano- 
precipitates. The bright ﬁeld (BF) and corresponding dark ﬁeld (DF) 
TEM images are shown in Fig. 3 c and d, respectively. The HRTEM 
images in Fig. 3 e and f show the fully coherent phase interface be- 
tween the L2 1 nanoprecipitate and the BCC matrix (marked by the 
yellow dashed line). The fast Fourier transformation (FFT) images 
( Fig. 3 g, h) acquired from the BCC- α matrix (red dashed square) 
and the L2 1 region (yellow dashed square) further conﬁrm the 
structures of the two phases and their orientation relationship of 
[110] α // [110] L2 1 .

corresponding STEM-EDS maps ( Fig. 4 b–f) for a typical grain- 
interior area in the as-cast alloy sample. The elemental maps re-

the Laves-phase precipitates distributed along grain boundaries in 
the as-cast alloy. Fig. 6 a shows that the GB region exhibits a core-

3.2. Mechanical properties of the bulk alloy samples 
Fig. 7 a shows the representative engineering compressive 
stress-strain curves of the as-cast (As-cast, RT) and homogenized 
(Homo, RT) alloy samples tested at room temperature, and the as- 
cast alloy sample tested at 673 K (As-cast, 673 K). The compres- 
sive mechanical properties are summarized in Table 2 . The average 
values of yield strength, ultimate compressive strength and uni- 
form compressive strain of the as-cast alloy at room temperature 
are 1096 MPa, 1694 MPa and 17%, respectively. The yield strength 
(1096 ± 8 MPa) is signiﬁcantly higher than that of the reference 
Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 17 alloy (385 ± 9 MPa) (Fig. S4e). At the temperature 
of 673 K, signiﬁcantly increased ultimate compressive strength of 
1980 MPa and uniform compressive strain of 33% combined with a 
high yield strength of 1055 MPa are obtained in the as-cast alloy. 
This indicates that the mechanical properties of the as-cast alloy 
containing L2 1 nanoprecipitates can be even signiﬁcantly enhanced 
at elevated temperatures (e.g., 673 K) compared to that at room 
temperature. The homogenized alloy without L2 1 nanoprecipitates 
tested at room temperature displays a slightly higher yield strength 
of ∼1191 MPa, while the ultimate compressive strength and com- 
pressive strain decrease signiﬁcantly, compared with that of the as- 
cast alloy containing L2 1 nanoprecipitates.

4.2. Compositional and microstructural effects on resistivity at 
room-temperature 
We discuss the effects of chemical compositions and mi- 
crostructures on the high electrical resistivity at room-temperature 
in the present FeCrAlTiSi multicomponent alloy. According to the 
Matthiessen’s rule [53] , electrical resistivity of crystalline metal- 
lic materials arises from the scattering of conduction electrons by 
the imperfections and lattice vibrations (phonons) in the crystal. 
At room temperature, electrical resistivity mainly depends on the 
amount of scatterings that electron waves will experience due to 
the lattice irregularities [22] . These irregularities may be caused by 
the lattice defects such as solute atoms, impurities, grain bound- 
aries and phase interfaces [54] . In the present study, the primary 
effective parameters determining the electrical resistivity of the 
as-cast alloy containing coherent multicomponent L2 1 nanopre- 
cipitates are explained in the following. (1) The volume fraction 
and size of coherent L2 1 nanoprecipitates: The increase in surface 
area of nanoprecipitates (per unit volume) by increasing their vol- 
ume fraction and size would enhance electrical resistivity by pro- 
moting the scattering of conduction electrons [55] . (2) The inter- 
spacing between the nanoprecipitates: For cases where the inter- 
precipitate spacing is smaller than the mean free path of electrons, 
the precipitates would contribute to enhancing electrical resistiv- 
ity [56] . Otherwise, the effect of precipitates would be negligible. 
(3) The concentrations of alloying elements in the BCC- α matrix 
[ 57 , 58 ]: Higher contents of Al, Ti and Si causing more signiﬁcant 
lattice distortion and then leads to a larger electrical resistivity. 
Fig. 12 a shows the gaussian-distributed diameters of the L2 1 
nanoprecipitates in the as-cast alloy sample and the mean value 
is 45 ± 11 nm. The inter-precipitate spacing ( λ) concentrates at 
about 55 nm ( Fig. 12 b). The mean value of λ is 63 ± 20 nm, 
which is signiﬁcantly smaller than the mean free path of elec- 
trons in Fe ( ∼116 nm) [59] . Thus, both the dispersed coherent mul- 
ticomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitate (area fraction, 33% ± 4%) with 
composition of Fe 50.9 Cr 5.85 Al 16.50 Ti 18.69 Si 8.06 (at. %) and the co- 
herency misﬁt strain induced by the coherent interfaces promote 
the additional scattering effect of electrons. For the as-cast alloy, 
three aspects, i.e., conduction electron scattering caused by so- 
lute atoms in the BCC- α matrix, the multicomponent L2 1 nano- 
precipitates and the coherent interfaces, are supposed to con- 
tribute to the total electrical resistivity. Although precisely evalu- 
ating the above contributions is diﬃcult, we make the following 
approximations to give a trend. The resistivity of the Fe 58 Cr 30 Al 12 
(at. %) alloy can be estimated to be ∼141 μ/Omega1·cm according to 
the relationship between the room temperature electrical resistiv- 
ity and Al content in the Fe 70-x Cr 30 Al x alloy system (Fig. S7) [7] . 
In the present study, the homogenized alloy sample with nomi- 
nal composition Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 12 Ti 3 Si 2 (at. %) holds a room tempera- 
ture electrical resistivity of ∼225 μ/Omega1·cm. The solute atoms, i.e., 3 
at. %Ti and 2 at. % Si, nearly lead to the enhanced resistivity of 
84 μ/Omega1·cm compared to that of the Fe 58 Cr 30 Al 12 (at. %) alloy. For 
the as-cast alloy sample, the BCC- α matrix holds a composition of 
Fe 54.91 Cr 31.43 Al 12.02 Ti 0.68 Si 0.96 (at. %). When assuming that the con- 
tribution of 0.68 at. % Ti plus 0.96 at. % Si on enhancing resistivity 
is a third of that of 3 at. % Ti plus 2 at. % Si, the solute atoms 
in the BCC- α matrix of the as-cast alloy lead to the enhanced re- 
sistivity of about 28 μ/Omega1·cm. Accordingly, the multicomponent L2 1 
nanoprecipitates contribute to about 14 μ/Omega1·cm. This indicates that 
the severe lattice distortion caused by the dissolution of Ti and 
Si in the BCC- α matrix can be more effective than the L2 1 nano- 
precipitates for enhancing the resistivity of the current alloy sys- 
tem. The ﬁnal electrical resistivity in the as-cast alloy compared 
with the homogenized variant without nanoprecipitates is deter- 
mined by the competing mechanisms between the additional con- 
duction electron scattering from the coherent L2 1 nanoprecipitates 
and the relatively decreased scattering from the matrix due to the 
decreased solid solution of Ti and Si. Therefore, the electrical re- 
sistivity of the as-cast alloy is lower than that of the homogenized 
variant but higher than that of the reference Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 17 (at. %) 
alloy (Fig. S4f). 
4.3. Compositional and microstructural dependence of TCR over a 
wide temperature range 
In this part, we then discuss the correlation of TCR with compo- 
sition and microstructure. For simple metals, electron-phonon in- 
teraction gives rise to the high temperature resistivity and com- 
monly display positive TCR values [60] . However, the current fer- 
ritic multicomponent FeCrAlTiSi alloy exhibits negative TCR over 
the temperature range from 298 to 1273 K ( Fig. 9 a). A negative 
TCR would enable the control on the inrush current in sensitive 
electronic components for protecting them from gradual damage 
[10] . For the present as-cast alloy containing L2 1 nanoprecipitates, 
the TCR is -35 ± 10 ppm/K from 298 to 673 K and -147 ± 10 
ppm/K from 673 to 1273 K ( Fig. 9 a). This suggest that the effect 
of electron-phonon interaction determining the TCR changes with 
increasing temperature above a critical temperature, i.e., around 
673 K for the as-cast alloy.

the temperature around 673 K, the room-temperature resistivities 
and microstructures of the alloy samples heated to various temper- 
atures followed by water-quenching were also characterized. Ac-

contributes to the slightly enhanced room temperature resistivity 
of the cast sample quenched at 1273 K (198 ± 6 μ/Omega1·cm). It has 
been recognized that the Kondo temperature ( T K ) of Ti impurities 
in Cu/Au matrix reaches as high as 10 0 0 K [68] . Higher Ti con- 
tent may result in more signiﬁcant Kondo-like scattering and leads 
to a more negative TCR . Thus, it can be deduced that the phase 
transformation from L2 1 nanoprecipitates to Laves-phase precip- 
itates enhance the Kondo-like scattering thus contribute to the 
more negative TCR at the temperature range from 673 to 1273 K 
(-147 ± 10 ppm/K). For the homogenized alloy sample, the TCR is - 
163 ± 10 ppm/K from 298 to 940 K and -585 ± 7 ppm/K from 940 
to 1273 K ( Fig. 9 a). As shown in Fig. S12, the homogenized alloy 
sample quenched at 673 and 940 K mainly exhibit a BCC structure, 
while numerous Laves-phase precipitates exist in the homogenized 
alloy sample quenched at 1273 K. The formation of Laves-phase 
precipitates contributes to the more negative TCR for the temper- 
ature range from 940 to 1273 K compared to that for the tem- 
perature range from 293 to 940 K. This further illustrate the key 
role of the multicomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates on the small TCR 
achieved in the present study. 
We next discuss the phase transformation process upon heating 
which is important for the purposeful microstructural control to 
further improve the resistivity-temperature stability in future. For 
the as-cast alloy, the concentrations of Al, Ti and Si atoms in the 
multicomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates (Fe 50.9 Cr 5.85 Al 16.50 Ti 18.69 Si 8.06 
(at. %)) are signiﬁcantly higher than that in the BCC- α matrix 
(Fe 54.91 Cr 31.43 Al 12.02 Ti 0.68 Si 0.96 (at. %)), which causes crystal lattice 
mismatch between the matrix and nanoprecipitates. This indicates 
a strong tendency of increasing the surface free energy upon el- 
evating temperature. Based on the STEM-BF images of the as-cast 
alloy sample ( Fig. 13 a), and the cast samples quenched at 673 K 
( Fig. 13 b), 973 K ( Fig. 13 c), and 1237 K ( Fig. 13 d), as well as the cor- 
responding chemical compositions ( Tables 4 and S1), we schemat- 
ically illustrate the microstructure evolution process upon heating, 
in Fig. 13 e–h. As shown in Fig. 13 f, high density of L2 1 nanopre- 
cipitates tend to coarsen into relatively larger sized precipitates to 
decrease the total interfacial area [69] . Nucleation occurs and the 
concentration of solute atoms (e.g., Ti and Si) in the matrix adja- 
cent to the newly formed precipitate increase. The ratios of the so- 
lute diffusion coeﬃcients of Cr, Al, and Ti to the self-diffusion coef- 
ﬁcient of Fe have been estimated as D Al / D Fe = 1.4, D Cr / D Fe = 1.4, 
and D Ti / D Fe = 3.9 [70] . Si has even higher diffusivity than Ti in 
α-Fe [44] . The concentration gradient drives the diffusion of Cr, Ti 
and Si into the newly formed precipitates, but Al diffuses out of 
them. The higher concentrations of Ti and Si lead to the larger lat- 
tice distortion, promoting the loss of coherency of the interface be-

5. Conclusions 
In this work, we developed a promising ferritic multicomponent 
resistance alloy by introducing ordered coherent multicomponent 
L2 1 nanoprecipitates into the alloy matrix. The following main con- 
clusions can be drawn: 
(1) The developed bulk alloy (Fe 55 Cr 28 Al 12 Ti 3 Si 2 , at. %) shows a 
BCC- α matrix embedded with ordered coherent multicom- 
ponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates in as-cast condition. The as- 
cast alloy exhibits a high electrical resistivity of 183 ± 2 
μ/Omega1·cm, a small TCR of -35 ± 10 ppm/K (from room temper- 
ature to 673 K), a high yield strength of 1096 MPa, a high 
ultimate compressive strength of 1694 MPa and a moderate 
uniform compressive strain of 17% at room temperature. 
(2) The multicomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates not only enhance 
the stress required for dislocation propagation but also con- 
tributes to the strain delocalization, and thus enhance the 
strain-hardening ability of the as-cast alloy. At 673 K, the 
decreased back stress and promoted dislocation mobility 
alleviate strain localization, and lead to the further im- 
proved strain-hardening ability. Accordingly, enhanced ulti- 
mate compressive strength of 1980 MPa and uniform com- 
pressive strain of 33% can be achieved at the maintained 
high yield strength of 1055 MPa with the as-cast alloy at 
673 K compared to that at room temperature. 
(3) Conduction electron scatterings from the solute atoms in 
the BCC- α matrix, the multicomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates 
and the coherent interfaces contribute to the high electri- 
cal resistivity of the as-cast alloy. The highly dispersed mul- 
ticomponent L2 1 nanoprecipitates enable the effective tun- 
ing of Kondo-like scattering and contribute to the low TCR 
(-35 ± 10 ppm/K) from 300 to 673 K.
