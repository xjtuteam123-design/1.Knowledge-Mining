abstract
Ni-base superalloys are materials which are designed to resist extreme thermal and mechanical condi-
tions. In this regard, an essential factor is their microstructure consisting of g0 precipitates embedded in a
g matrix. The application of superalloys at high temperatures can however induce the topological phase
inversion, where the g0-phase topologically becomes the matrix phase, resulting in subpar material
properties. In this work, the topological inversion is analyzed via experiment and phase- ﬁeld simulation.
The evolution of the microstructure has been quanti ﬁed in the second generation single crystal Ni-base
superalloy ERBO/1, which belongs to the family of CMSX-4, submitted to long-term aging at 1 100
/C14
C for
up to 250 h. Phase- ﬁeld simulations carried out using a multi phase- ﬁeld approach deliver insight into
the microstructure evolution driven by the loss of coherency of the g0 precipitates, which is induced by
the accumulation of dislocations at the g/g0 interfaces. The obtained simulation results are in good
agreement with the experimental results, and indicate that the mechanisms causing the topological
inversion are linked to the accommodation of the lattice mis ﬁt, which enables coalescence and ripening
of g0 precipitates.

1. Introduction
Ni-base superalloys are the material of choice for many high
temperature applications due to their excellent properties at
elevated temperatures such as high creep and oxidation resistance
as well as high tensile strength. These unique properties are strongly
linked to the microstructure of the Ni-base superalloys, consisting of
the ordered L1
2 g0-phase Precipitates embedded in the disordered
FCC g-phase. Due to the ordered nature of the g0-phase, the dislo-
cations are restricted to the g-matrix up to a cutting stress [1].
Thereby the dislocation movement is con ﬁned to the g-channels.
Under service conditions, i.e. at high temperatures, the micro-
structure can be subjected to changes. During creep the g0-phase
gradually interconnects and is no longer conﬁned by theg-channels.
Therefore, it becomes topologically the matrix phase [2]. This pro-
cess is known as the topological phase inversion. During creep, the
topological inversion has been considered in the frame of
microstructural stability [3], nevertheless the microstructural sta-
bility is a more general term implying not only the resistance to
rafting, but also to precipitation of TCP-phases and the formation of
pores.
Few investigations on the causes of topological inversion are
found in the literature, most of them are made under creep con-
ditions. Caron et al. [4] analyzed the area fraction and the inter-
connectivity of the g0-phase during creep at 1050

different g0 fraction. They found that there is an important rela-
tionship between the g0-phase fraction and the topological inver-
sion. According to their work, the driving force for this
phenomenon is the reduction in g/g0 interface area, responsible for
the diffusion controlled coarsening of the g0 particles. A g0 volume
fraction lower than 50% would result in coarser microstructure
with fewer particles, whereas more than 50% would result in the
topological inversion.

The kinetics of the topological inversion under creep conditions has
also been analyzed by Epishin et al. [2]. According to their work, the
inversion is explained by the formation of junctions connecting
neighboring g0-rafts and bridging through the g-phase. These
junctions result from the generation of dislocations in the g-
channels combined with the dissolution of g0-edges in the g-phase
towards the dislocations [2]. Moreover, they explain that cutting of
the g0-phase and formation of low-energy {1 1 1} interfaces accel-
erate surface diffusion, spheroidization and loss of coherency.
Another aspect of their work is that the microstructural and creep
analysis indicates that the topologically inverted microstructure
consisting of g0-matrix isolating g-inclusions is mechanically
weaker than the original one [2].
Topological inversion does not only take place under applied
load but also without applied load during long-term aging. Caron
et al. [4] have also analyzed the topological inversion without
applied load. It is remarkable that establishing g0 connections is
much slower during long-term aging than under creep conditions,
which is explained by the oriented coalescence of the g0 pre-
cipitates during creep on the one hand, and by the relaxation of the
stresses at the g/g0 interfaces caused by the plastic strain in the g-
matrix on the other hand. Other works dealing with long-term
aging between 850 and 1000

so-called spontaneous rafting. Long-term aging of CMSX-10[8] leads
to coarse and irregular g0 precipitates. The microstructures
observed in the aforementioned works indicate that in these cases
the g0-phase has topologically become the matrix phase.
The present work investigates the topological phase inversion
occurring during long-term aging through both experimentation
and phase-ﬁeld simulation. For this purpose, the multi-phase- ﬁeld
approach is applied [13]. In the model applied in this work, the
elastic component has been described such that the reduction of
misﬁt affects the eigenstrain and the wetting condition of the g/g0
interfaces. The results of the simulation and experiments are
compared with respect to the g/g0 microstructure, as well as the
evolution of g0-fraction, number density of particles and their size.
2. Experimental methods
In this work the second generation single crystal Ni-base su-
peralloy ERBO/1 in the as-cast state is studied. ERBO/1 is an alloy of
type CMSX-4 and was provided by Doncasters Precision Casting,
Bochum (Germany). A detailed characterization of the material can
be found in Ref. [9]. In order to observe the microstructural evo-
lution, the as-cast material was isothermally heat treated at 1 100
/C14
C
with holding times of 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 h.
Heat treatments were performed in a laboratory furnace at atmo-
spheric pressure and in an inert (argon) gas atmosphere. After each
heat treatment the samples were water quenched. The as-cast
material has a higher g0 phase fraction in the interdendritic re-
gions than in the dendritic regions, (49,0 and 63,4% area fraction,
respectively) and with that an accelerated topological inversion of
the microstructure in the interdendritic areas.
The characterization of the heat-treated specimens was carried
out using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). Specimens were
oriented using a real time Laue camera, then cut in sections parallel
to the [001] plane (perpendicular to the solidi ﬁcation direction),
mounted in a conductive resin and mechanically polished to mirror
ﬁnish. An etching solution of 40 ml H 2O, 20 ml HCl and 10 ml H 2O2
was used to etch out the g0-phase. The microstructure was inves-
tigated using the Zeiss Leo Gemini 1530 VP equipped with a ﬁeld
emission gun (FEG), In-Lens detector and energy dispersive X-ray
(EDX) system. For each studied sample, micrographs were taken at
the dendrite cores and interdendritic areas by using the secondary
electron detector with 20 kV acceleration voltage as well as by
using the In-Lens detector with 10 kV. For each studied area, four
images at 1000/C2 were taken. The quantiﬁcation of the g0 fraction, g0
particles per unit area and average particle size was obtained by
using the software ImageJ [10]. Chemical compositions of dendritic
and interdendritic areas were measured by EDX with 20 kV accel-
eration voltage. Dislocation networks were observed using the In-
Lens detector applying a similar procedure as described in Ref. [1 1].
3. Simulation approach
The phase- ﬁeld method is a simulation method used to solve
interfacial problems. In this work, the multi-phase- ﬁeld (MPF)
model by Steinbach et al. [12e14] is used. Each phase, and in
general each grain or precipitate, is assigned with a phase- ﬁeld
parameter fa. Phases/grains are separated by a diffuse interface,
in which the phase- ﬁeld parameter smoothly transitions from 0 to
1. Phase transformation processes are driven by the minimization
of the total free energy [12], which in this case consists of interface,
chemical and elastic contributions.
A binary Ni-Al system is used as a model system, the starting
composition for the simulation was determined using thermody-
namic calculations. The composition in the interdendritic areas in
the as-cast condition of the ERBO/1 alloy was measured by using
EDX (Section 2). The Thermo-Calc [15] software and TTNI7 [16]
database are used to calculate the equivalent binary composition
in order to obtain the same g/g0 fractions as in the experiment. This
binary system can be seen as an analogy to a tie-line in the CMSX-4
system, where the Al concentration has been taken such that the
resulting g0 phase fraction is close to the experimentally observed
one in the interdendritic regions. The diffusion coef ﬁcient of Al in a
g matrix employed here is 6 $10/C0 15, which is close to the value
obtained for a CMSX-4 alloy [18]. The use of the binary Ni-Al system
leads to accelerated diffusion kinetics. Unfortunately a realistic
simulation time is computationally not achievable for the 10
component system of the ERBO/1 alloy. Moreover, the kinetics of
the simulation is limited primarily by the channel stabilization
model (as described below), therefore the use of the equivalent
binary system is feasible. The other input parameters (e.g. elastic
constants, lattice mis ﬁt) are chosen to represent the CMSX-4 type
alloy ERBO/1. The elastic constants are obtained from Ref. [19]. The
elasticity model by Steinbach et al. [20] is used to solve the me-
chanical equilibrium problem. The lattice mis ﬁt is assumed to
be /C0 0.3% [21], therefore the lattice parameter of the g0 phase is
smaller than the one of the g phase. The input parameters are listed
in Table 1. The starting microstructure is obtained by simulating the

To model the long-term aging process of Ni-base superalloys the
high stability of the g/g0 microstructure has to be understood: Why
do g0 precipitates not coalesce spontaneously to reduce interfacial
energy if theg0-phase fraction exceeds 50%? With ag0-phase volume
fraction of usually above 60% a topological inversion of the micro-
structure is energetically bene ﬁcial, since the overall g/g0 interface
area would be reduced. A stabilization by anti-phase boundaries
between different g0 particles has been proposed in Refs. [22e24],
however it does not explain the uniform g-channel width in the
range of several tens of nanometers as found in experiments [25].
An alternative explanation is proposed in Ref. [30] where the
repulsion of g0 precipitates is due to an increase in elastic energy in
the g channel. If the elastic energy density increases for a decreasing
g channel width, it would result in an increasing repulsive force on
the g0 precipitates, the so-called dis-joining potential [26].T o
further investigate this mechanism, a case study is carried out in
this paper. This serves as a means to validate the wetting condition
used to support the stabilization of the g/g0 interfaces.

resulting from the elastic energy exerted by the mis ﬁtting pre-

In the full scale phase ﬁeld simulation a problem arises from
the diffuse interfaces. The dis-joining potential between g0 pre-
cipitates rises for a decreasing g channel width. However, due to
numerical limitations of the phase- ﬁeld method related to the
diffuse interface, if the channel width becomes too thin and two
interfaces overlap, they would collapse into a single interface,
eliminating the g channel. Therefore a supplementary stabiliza-
tion mechanism is used by applying a wetting condition to the g/
g0 interfaces. This is achieved by increasing the interface energy
between phase- ﬁelds of the g0-phase by factor 2. Thereby a
complementary energy penalty is applied to g0/g0 interfaces,
which accommodates for the lost elastic stabilization by having
diffuse interfaces.
During long-term aging the introduction of equilibrium inter-
facial dislocation networks is observed. These networks subse-
quently created during overaging consist of equidistant, mostly
geometrically necessary dislocations, which relieve the lattice
misﬁt between g and g0 phases [27]. Therefore the effect of the
misﬁt of the g0-phase decreases with increasing dislocation density.
In the phase- ﬁeld model, a suitable mesoscopic approximation for
the introduction of interfacial dislocation networks has to be found,
since individual dislocations are not resolvable on the scale of the
phase-ﬁeld simulation. Thus in the current approach the reduction
of the mis ﬁt effect and with that the loss of coherency in the g/g0
interfaces is modeled by artiﬁcially lowering the eigenstrain as well
as the strength of the wetting condition, which supplements the
elastic repulsion of the g0 precipitates, with time.
The 250 h of the long-term aging experiment is impossible to
simulate with the phase- ﬁeld model in a realistic time-scale. Since
the reduction of the mis ﬁt effect is the primary rate controlling
factor, the simulation is accelerated by accelerating the reduction of
the mis ﬁt. The reduction rate of the mis ﬁt is chosen such, that
sufﬁcient time is left for the microstructure to adjust to the change
in mis ﬁt. The process is accompanied by ripening, which is not
subject of this investigation.

5. Discussion
During long-term aging two main mechanisms induce the
change in microstructure. Coarsening of g0-particles follows Ost-
wald ripening and is driven by surface energy minimization.
Additionally, through formation of interfacial dislocation networks
in the g/g0-interfaces the g-channels are no longer stabilized by the
elastic energy induced by coherency stresses and strains and
therefore the g0-particles are able to coalesce [30].
In the early stages of aging (until 32 h/12% simulation time), g0-
precipitates coarsen driven by ripening ( Fig. 4 a), e) and b), f)).
During this stage the g0 phase fraction is relatively constant and
mostly the size and the number of particles change ( Fig. 5a) and c),
respectively). The driving force of the ripening process is the
interface curvature, where the precipitate with the higher curva-
ture shrinks. For spherical precipitates, the smaller precipitate al-
ways has the higher interface curvature for obvious reasons. For
cuboidal precipitates the curvature can be very close for

The mechanism, in which the dislocations are responsible for
changes in the mis ﬁt, loss of coherency and ﬁnally the topological
inversion, could also give insight in former results taken from the
literature. In Ref. [4], the kinetics of the inversion of the alloy AM1 is
accelerated under creep conditions compared to the process
without applied load. Moreover, in the same work the alloy MC544
under creep conditions at 1050
/C14
C/150 MPa shows topological
inversion, whereas at the same temperature without applied stress
the inter-connectivity of g0 is slower and even after 3000 h the
inversion is not completely achieved. The introduction of disloca-
tions during creep can explain why the kinetics of the topological
inversion observed in Ref. [4] of alloy AM1 is accelerated, as well as
the slower kinetics of the topological inversion of the alloy MC544
(related to the Rhenium addition in Ref. [4]), where the higher
creep resistance associated to a lower mobility of dislocations.
Recent works have shown the role of the dislocation networks at
the g/g0 interfaces in the creep anisotropy of LEK-94 [31e33].I n
these works, the activated slip systems and the types of dislocations
segments differ depending on the load direction, being responsible
for the dissimilar values of minimum creep rate. Another obser-
vation of these works is that the topological inversion proceeds
faster during [001] than during [1 10] tensile creep.
From the technological point of view, the heat treatment history
of the superalloy has a large in ﬂuence on the topological inversion.
Single crystal Ni-base superalloys have a signi ﬁcant segregation of
alloying elements (in the case of ERBO/1 refer to [9]). This segre-
gation strongly inﬂuences the morphology, phase volume fractions,
misﬁt, and density of interfacial dislocation networks [34]. There-
fore it can be expected that after long-term aging applied to a
conventional heat treated alloy (after homogenization and pre-
cipitation hardening), the kinetics of the topological inversion
would be signi ﬁcantly slower, due to the fact that the composition
of the material would be more uniform, the dislocation density
would be lower and the
g/g0 microstructure would be more ho-
mogeneous than in the as cast state.
The temperature during aging (or creep) plays a signi ﬁcant role
in the topological inversion. It determines the equilibrium g/g0
volume fraction and in this way the possibility of the inversion to
occur. Additionally the temperature in ﬂuences the diffusion
controlled processes, such as Ostwald ripening. Another effect
controlled by the temperature is the tendency of the system to form
TCP-phases. In the alloy ERBO/1 there is a tendency to form Laves,
s
and m phases [35], which have a high solubility for elements like Ta
and Ti (g0 formers), Re or W (g formers), and therefore inﬂuence the
g/g0 partition.
The direct comparison of the simulation results and the
microstructure obtained experimentally shows clear morpholog-
ical similarities ( Fig. 4 ). Nevertheless the simulation model has
some limitations. The ﬁrst one is the simpli ﬁcation of a complex
alloy to a binary equivalent Ni-Al system, even though good results
were achieved. Secondly, one has to take into account that the
phase-ﬁeld model is not able to simulate microstructural hetero-
geneities on two length scales being separated by several orders of
magnitude due to computational limitations. In this case it is
possible to simulate the microstructural evolution of the low-scale
heterogeneities (the ordered L12 and the disordered FCCA1 struc-
ture) but not the large-scale heterogenities (segregation involving
the interdiffusion of the different alloying elements). In the case of
the simulation the volume fraction of the phases is nearly constant,
since only the equivalent concentration to the interdendritic area is
considered and no interdiffusion between dendritic and interden-
dritic area could be simulated because of technical restrictions to
the system size. Therefore the concentration in the supersaturated
g-phase at the beginning of the simulation is uniform, leading to
homogeneously distributed g0 precipitates.
The large differences of the diffussion scales (of few nanometers
in case of the g-channels) compared with the interdendritic dis-
tances (of ca. 500 mm) may de ﬁne the ﬁrst consequence, that is,
why the inversion occurs only in the interdendritic areas. In case of
ERBO/1, in the as-cast condition the interdendritic areas are
enriched in Al, Ta, in Ti, which are g0 formers. In this case the ki-
netics of the inversion is faster than that of the interdiffusion of
heavy elements such as W, Ta and Re, and in such a way the g0 is
locally (in the interdendritic areas) more stable. As a second
consequence the measured g0 phase fraction stays constant or even
increases after the inversion in the interdendritic areas ( Fig. 5 )
instead of decreasing towards the thermodynamic equilibrium of

6. Summary and conclusions
Long-term aging was applied to the single crystal superalloy
ERBO/1 in the as-cast condition. The long-term aging process was
also simulated using the phase- ﬁeld approach. Based on the image
analysis of SEM micrographs and cross-section of the 3D phase-
ﬁeld simulations the evolution of the
g0-phase fraction, number
of g0 particles and their size were quanti ﬁed.
After 250 h of long-term aging at 1 100
/C14
C the g/g0 microstructure
is topologically inverted, i.e. initially precipitating g0-phase topo-
logically becomes the matrix phase, surrounding regions of the g-
phase. The topological inversion occurs in the interdendritic re-
gions of the sample, but not in the dendritic ones. This strongly
suggests the importance of the g0-phase volume fraction for the
topological inversion, since the g0-phase volume fraction is
approximately 65% in the interdendritic regions compared to a
volume fraction which is signi ﬁcantly lower than 50% in the den-
dritic ones.
Dislocation networks wrapping the g0 precipitates are visible
in SEM micrographs of the long-term aged samples. These dislo-
cation networks, which are slowly established during the long-
term aging process are the basis of the proposed phase- ﬁeld
model used to simulate the long-term aging process. The dislo-
cation networks relax the lattice mis ﬁt, therefore in the phase-
ﬁeld model the lattice mis ﬁt is relaxed with time. By using this
simple model very similar microstructures to the experimental
ones are obtained ( Fig. 4). The evolution of the g0 particles per unit
area and the average g0 particle size is very similar, only limited by
the system size of the simulation at high aging times. The g0 area
fraction however increases for the experiment while it is nearly
constant in the simulation. We assume that the changes to the g0
area fraction in the experiment are caused by diffusional effects
between dendritic and interdendritic regions, which is not
captured in the simulation due to the constant overall composi-
tion in the phase- ﬁeld simulation which corresponds to the
thermodynamic condition in the interdendritic region.
The phase- ﬁeld simulation yields another interesting result:
while it is obvious in the 2D pictures that the topological inversion
has occurred for 100% simulation time (full removal of the lattice
misﬁt, Fig. 4 d) and the
g0 phase surrounds regions of g, in the 3D
picture (Fig. 3 ) it can be seen that the g phase is still continuous.
The process of the topological inversion consists of different
phenomena which all can be deduced from the formation of
equilibrium dislocation networks around the g0 precipitates.
