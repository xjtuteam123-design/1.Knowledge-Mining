Effects of Nb and Mo on 1200 /C176 C Steam Oxidation
and Mechanical Properties of FeCrAl Alloys for Fuel
Cladding Materials
Chundong Wang, Huawei Zhang, Jie Pan, Jiwei Lin, Ping Cao, Cheng Su, Chunyu Yin, Cong Li, and Xueshan Xiao
Submitted: 10 January 2023 / Revised: 31 March 2023 / Accepted: 8 April 2023 / Published online: 28 April 2023
Iron–chromium–aluminium (FeCrAl) alloy compositions should be optimised to improve performance. The
effects of Nb and Mo on the high-temperature steam oxidation of FeCrAl alloys were investigated at
1200 /C176C. The oxide ﬁlm of a series of alloys with different Nb contents mainly comprised Al 2O3 and the
surface morphology was wrinkled. For the series alloys with different Mo contents, when Mo was greater
than 2 wt.%, the oxide ﬁlm component also contained MoO
3, in addition to Al 2O3, with the presence of
micropores. The oxidation weight gain decreased with Nb, owing to a great many Laves phases, which
impeded the outward diffusion of Al. The oxidation weight gain increased with Mo, and this was attributed
to the volatilisation of MoO
3, which promoted the diffusion of oxygen. The yield strength of FeCrAl alloys
increased with increased Nb and Mo and decreased with oxidation time. Nb and Mo promoted the pre-
cipitation of the Laves phases and increased the hindrance to grain boundaries, which favoured ﬁne grain
strengthening. This was critical to obtaining FeCrAl fuel cladding alloys with great steam oxidation
resistance and mechanical properties.
Keywords FeCrAl alloys, mechanical testing, molybdenum,
niobium, oxidation
1. Introduction
The concept of accident-tolerant fuel (A TF) was proposed
after the Fukushima nuclear accident. At temperatures above
900 /C176 C, zirconium reacts violently with steam, producing a lot
of hydrogen, which results in an explosion when the pressure
gets too high and the temperature reaches about 1200 /C176 C,
which is what happened at Fukushima (Ref 1, 2). In the light
water reactor (LWR), the fuel cladding was subjected to high
temperatures and high pressures, which increased the danger
from ﬁssion gas pressure, corrosion, fuel swelling, and
hydrogen embrittlement. With the threats of coolant pressure,
erosion, vibration, corrosion, and hydrogen embrittlement, the
structural integrity of the reactor was of great signiﬁcance to the
safe operation of the reactor and the prevention of nuclear fuel
leakage (Ref 3). FeCrAl alloy was considered to be the most
promising A TF material because of its excellent oxidation
resistance and good mechanical properties (Ref 4-8). It has
been used in numerous applications, such as cladding materials
for nuclear power plants, furnace heating elements, and
automotive catalyst supports (Ref 9-12).
The stable Al
2O3 phase can easily form when alumina-
forming alloy is oxidised at high temperatures ( T > 1000 /C176 C)
(Ref 13); this is consistent with previous studies that found that
FeCrAl alloys oxidised in high-temperature air to form alumina
ﬁlm, which ulteriorly inhibits oxidation (Ref 14-16). However,
the working environment of FeCrAl in a nuclear reactor was
not air but high-temperature steam (HTS). Thus, it is crucial to
research the oxidation performance in high-temperature steam.
Dong Pan (Ref 17) researched the formation of scale in Fe-
13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1Nb fuel cladding material at 1200 /C176 C and
found that the oxidative product on the alloy surface was only
a-Al
2O3. Similar results have been reported in previous (Ref 5,
18). Cr can increase the relative amount of Al 2O3 at lower
aluminium concentrations, which contribute to the formation of
Al2O3 scale (Ref 19). The studies revealed that, incorporating a
trace amount of rare earth elements into the alloy that forms
chromic oxide or alumina has a signiﬁcant positive function on
the material’s steam oxidation performance at high tempera-
tures. The diffusion and distribution of rare earth elements on
the alumina surface makes a difference to the growth of
alumina (Ref 20, 21), which then affect the oxidation perfor-
mance of the alloy. Rare earths could slow down the growth of
alumina and enhance the adhesion of chromic oxide and
alumina (Ref 22, 23). Similarly, research has shown that rare
earth dopants also have a very important effect on steam
oxidation behaviour (Ref 24).
Nb and Mo have a lower thermal neutron absorption cross
section and better strength (Ref 25). Besides this, they have

composition in order to obtain FeCrAl fuel cladding alloys with
better high-temperature steam oxidation behaviour and

The purpose of this article is to analyse the effect of Nb and
Mo content on oxidation behaviour at 1200 /C176 C in steam. We
designed and successfully prepared FeCrAl alloys with differ-
ent Nb and Mo contents. The steam oxidation behaviour of the
FeCrAl alloys at 1200 /C176 C was studied from the aspects of
oxidation weight gain and oxide ﬁlm features. By observing
and analysing the microstructure and mechanical properties
after steam oxidation, the effects of Nb and Mo on the high-
temperature steam oxidation resistance of FeCrAl alloy for fuel
cladding are better understood. This study also provides
additional guidelines for the development of FeCrAl fuel
cladding alloys to achieve better steam oxidation resistance.
2. Experimental Methods
2.1 Materials
In order to study the effect of Nb and Mo content on high-
temperature steam oxidation behaviour and the mechanical
properties of FeCrAl-based alloys for fuel cladding materials,
Fe-13Cr-6Al-2Mo-xNb (where x = 0.15, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0
wt.%, hereafter abbreviated as /C212xNb alloys /C213) and Fe-13Cr-6Al-
0.5Nb-0.2Y -xMo (where x = 2, 3, and 4 wt.%, hereafter
abbreviated as /C212xMo alloys /C213) systems were designed, respec-
tively, to carry out experimental analysis. Eight hundred grams
of raw material were prepared by vacuum induction furnace
melting (VIM) to produce a slab ingot of dimensions L
mm 9 50 mm 9 15 mm, where L was the length of the slab
ingot. The polished ingot was kept at 1050 /C176 C for 30-45 min,
then hot rolled to form a 3-mm thin plate by multi-pass hot
rolling with small deformations. The hot-rolled 3-mm alloy was
then annealed at 1050 /C176 C for 15 min. After the oxide scale was
removed, the alloy was cold rolled to about 1 mm by multi-pass
and small deformation. The cold rolled specimen needed to be
annealed at 1050 /C176 C for 10 min to improve the mechanical
performance of the FeCrAl alloy. The actual ingredients of the
xNb and xMo alloys tested are shown in Table 1. It can be seen
that the composition obtained was similar to that in the design.
2.2 High-Temperature Steam Oxidation Test
The experimental samples were cut into 10 9 10 9 1m m
size pieces with an electric spark wire cutter. The tops of these
specimens were ground with 400#, 800#, and 1200# SiC
sandpaper. Finally, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned
with acetone, to wipe off the surface impurities. The samples
were ﬁxed in a pre-dried alumina crucible and put into a tube
resistance furnace with water vapour and argon, for isothermal
oxidation. The specimen was heated up to 1200 /C176 C, at a rate of
5 /C176 C min
/C0 1, and the temperature was maintained for 0.5 h,
2.0 h, and 4.0 h, respectively. The oxidised sample was
covered with the same alumina material, immediately after
being taken out, to prevent the oxide ﬁlm from peeling off and
splashing out of the crucible during the cooling process, which
would cause the scale to fail. The total mass of the samples and
crucible during the oxidation process was recorded using a
BP211D electronic analytical balance with a precision of
0.1 lg. The weight gain of at least three different samples was
measured repeatedly for each component and then averaged to
ensure the accuracy of the values.

Combined with the above research (Ref 33), the formation,
coarsening of folds, and formation of the interface cavity of the
xNb alloy oxide ﬁlm can be summarised as follows. As long as
the oxide ﬁlm grows laterally, there will be compressive stress
due to the constraint of the matrix (Ref 35). First of all, the
oxide ﬁlm was wrinkled at various surface defects, which
provides stress relaxation, and part of the oxide ﬁlm was
stripped from the substrate. As the oxide ﬁlm continued to grow
laterally, compressive stress continued to occur. The oxide ﬁlm
was compressed in the valley and pulled in the peak, causing
the metal to plastically ﬂow from valley to peak via interface
diffusion, or /C212creep/C213, and the folding degree increased. The gas
diffusion of aluminium at the separation of the oxide ﬁlm and
the matrix, resulted in the interface moving backwards and the
cavity becoming larger. However, with the increased thickness
of the oxide ﬁlm, its lateral growth rate slowed down until it
stopped, after which the oxidation continued.

Compared with xNb alloys, xMo alloys did not generate
folds during the 1200 /C176 C steam oxidation. This phenomenon
may be due to the addition of Mo and Y elements. Tolpygo (Ref
35) found that Y can reduce the growth strain of scales,
effectively controlling the transverse growth of the oxide ﬁlm,
thus avoiding the occurrence of the wrinkled morphology and
improving the adhesion of scales. The results (Ref 36) showed
that when Mo was added to the alloy, the folds were partly
reduced, and the adhesion between the matrix and the oxide
ﬁlm was improved. Yang (Ref 37) indicated that the addition of
Y also improved the adhesion of scales. Although the addition
of Nb reduced the weight gain during oxidation, the wrinkles
weakened the resistance to steam oxidation. The adhesion
between the scales and the matrix was increased by adding Mo
and Y elements, and the folding degree was reduced or even
eliminated.

Figures 6, 7, and 8 show that the Laves phase was mainly
distributed along grain boundaries in the xNb and xMo alloys
during the oxidation process at 1200 /C176 C, and the amount of
Laves phase increased with the increased Nb and Mo contents.
The pinning effect (Ref 38) of the Laves phase could inhibit
grain boundary movement and grain growth. The decrease in
grain size increased the amount of crystal boundaries, which
increased the diffusion channels of ions and promoted oxida-
tion (Ref 39). However, the Laves phase could also impede the
outward spread of ions and diminish the oxidation rate of the
alloys (Ref 40, 41). The ion diffusion hindrance effect, in the
non-solvus Laves phase, was made stronger than the promotion
of ion diffusion by increasing the grain boundary in the
oxidation process of the xNb alloys; Nb improved the high-
temperature steam oxidation performance of the xNb alloys.
The oxidised scale thickness of xNb alloys oxidised at 1200 /C176 C
for 4.0 h, supports this viewpoint. This also corresponds to
Fig. 1(a) and (c). Zhang (Ref 22) investigated the oxidation
properties when alloys were exposed to high-temperature steam
and found that the increased amount of Nb improved the
oxidation resistance. At the same time, Xu et al. (Ref 42, 43)
made clear that Nb could also increase the activity of Cr and
promote the preferential oxidation of Cr. The mechanism by
which Nb improves the steam oxidation behaviour at 1200 /C176 C,
through the Laves phase, is explained in Fig. 9.
The analysis of 1.0Nb alloy oxide ﬁlm peeling (after 4.0 h
oxidation at 1200 /C176 C) using the scanning energy spectrum, is
shown in Fig. 10. It was found that Nb and Mo were segregated
in the matrix, which means that the Laves phase existed in the
peeling off the oxide ﬁlm, and that no new oxide ﬁlm was
formed because of enrichment of oxygen wasn /C213t found.
Therefore, it may be that the Laves phase reduced the adhesive
forces, leading to the spalling of oxide ﬁlm due to thermal
stress during cooling. Although the addition of Nb could reduce
the oxidation weight gain, the oxidation behaviour would be
weakened due to cracks and spalling of the oxide ﬁlm, so the
Nb contents should not be too high. Sun (Ref 38) agreed that
further development of FeCrAl alloys, with Nb below 1 wt.%,
was appropriate.

steam oxidation at 1200 /C176 C and the mechanical properties of
