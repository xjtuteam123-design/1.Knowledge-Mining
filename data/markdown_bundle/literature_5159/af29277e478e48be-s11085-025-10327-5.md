Abstract
In most cases, platinum-based alloys are mainly used in high-temperature oxida-
tion environments, and mastering their oxidation behavior and the impact of oxida-
tion on performance is crucial. Two new platinum-based high-temperature alloys, 
Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr and Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr-0.2Y, were designed and prepared in this study. 
The research focuses on the high-temperature oxidation behavior of the alloys in 
air and the influence of oxidation on the room temperature mechanical properties 
of the alloys. The results show that the relationship between oxidation weight loss 
and temperature of these two platinum-based alloys conforms to the Arrhenius equa-
tion within the temperature range of 1400–1600  ℃, and the oxidation resistance of 
Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr-0.2Y alloy is better than that of Pt-10Rh.0.5Zr alloy. Examination 
of the surface and fracture morphology of these oxidized platinum-based alloys 
revealed that zirconium and yttrium oxide particles, such as ZrO2 and Y2O3, with 
different morphologies and structures were formed. The study also found that add-
ing a small amount of zirconium and yttrium can significantly improve the room 
temperature ultimate tensile strength of Pt-10Rh alloy. However, after 20 h of high-
temperature oxidation treatment at 1400 and 1500 °C, the tensile strength and plas-
ticity at room temperature of both alloys showed a significant downward trend. 
Especially, the room temperature plasticity of Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr-0.2Y alloy decreased 
by more than 80% and exhibited a brittle fracture mode. Our research will contribute 
to the design and development of new high-temperature platinum-based alloys.

Introduction
Platinum-based alloys are widely used in experimental analysis and crystal 
growth vessel materials, preparation of high-quality glass and glass fiber cru-
cible and leakage plate materials, as well as aerospace engine thruster nozzles 
due to their high melting points, excellent high-temperature mechanical, oxida-
tion resistance, corrosion resistance properties, and chemical stability [1 –3]. The 
strengthening mechanisms of platinum-based alloys mainly include solid solu-
tion strengthening, dispersion strengthening, and precipitation strengthening 
[4–6]. Solid solution strengthened platinum-based alloys are an alloy system that 
has been researched and developed earlier, with mature alloy compositions and 
preparation processes. Precious metals such as Ru, Ir, Rh, and Pd are the main 
solid solution strengthening elements of platinum [7 ]. Currently, the most studied 
platinum-based solid solution alloys include binary alloys such as Pt–Rh, Pt–Ir, 
Pt–Ru, Pt–Ni, and Pt–W and ternary alloys such as Pt–Pd–Rh and Pt–Rh–Ru. 
Up to now, Pt-10Rh alloy is still the most stable binary high-temperature alloy 
in terms of its performance. As a functional material, such as thermocouples 
for high-temperature furnaces, they can be used for up to 5  years in static air 
at 1400  ℃ [8]. Since the 1980s, platinum-based alloys have been applied in the 
aerospace field, such as in the propulsion nozzles of aerospace engines [9 ]. As 
a nozzle material for load-bearing structures, its working temperature can reach 
1500  ℃ or even higher [10, 11]. However, the high-temperature mechanical 
properties of Pt-10Rh alloy are still insufficient and need further improvement. 
Increasing the Rh content is one of the effective means to improve the mechanical 
properties of Pt–Rh alloys. However, when the content of Rh reaches or exceeds 
30 wt.%, the increase in the mechanical properties of the alloy is limited, and 
the processing performance significantly deteriorates [12]. More importantly, the 
high price of Rh greatly limits its use in Pt–Rh alloys with high Rh content. One 
of the main research directions currently is microalloying by multi-elements to 
further improve the performance of Pt–Rh alloys with low Rh content.
In order to further improve the mechanical properties of Pt or Pt–Rh alloys, a 
series of dispersion strengthened platinum-based composite materials with oxide 
particles as the strengthening phase have been studied and developed since the 
1970s [13– 15]. These composite materials have been successfully applied in the 
glass fiber industry as leakage plates and nozzle materials for drawing glass fib-
ers [16, 17]. The high-temperature mechanical properties of dispersion strength-
ened platinum alloy composites are generally higher than those of solid solution 
platinum-based alloys of the same composition. However, the plasticity of the 
composite material decreases greatly after oxide dispersion strengthening, and 
the welding strength decreases to an unreinforced level due to the condensation of 
oxide particles during welding [7 ].
Platinum can form high melting point Pt3X or Pt5X intermetallic compounds 
with transition and rare earth metals on the platinum-rich side, laying the foun-
dation for the development of precipitation strengthening platinum-based super -
alloy materials [18]. Since the late twentieth century, precipitation strengthened

platinum-based alloys have become a new research hotspot [19– 26]. Pt3X and 
 Pt5X phases mainly appear in Pt with transition metal and rare earth alloy sys-
tems, respectively. Hill provided a detailed description and evaluation of the 
structure and properties of Pt3X and other precipitates [27]. Research on binary 
systems such as Pt-Zr and Pt-Hf, as well as ternary alloys such as Pt–Rh–Zr and 
Pt–Rh–Hf, has shown that zirconium and hafnium elements form γ’-phase Pt3Zr 
and Pt3Hf that are coherent with the platinum matrix [28]. The solid solution 
strengthening and γ’-phase precipitation strengthening endow these alloys with 
higher high-temperature mechanical properties. However, due to the high solid 
solubility of easily oxidizable Zr and Hf in Pt matrix, it is necessary to add more 
than 16 at.% alloy elements to form a γ’-phase, which deteriorates the oxida-
tion resistance of platinum-based alloys, and the resulting oxides lead to embrit-
tlement of the alloys [29]. A quaternary alloy combining Pt3Al precipitation 
strengthening and solid solution strengthening was studied and developed by add-
ing Al, Cr, and Ru in platinum, which significantly enhancing the high-temper -
ature strengthening effect and the oxidation resistance of platinum-based alloys 
[30, 31]. Unfortunately, the addition of Al exceeding 10 at.% (the Al content 
required to form Pt3Al phase) reduces the melting point of the alloy to around 
1500 ℃, and resulting the alloy’s usage temperature not exceeding 1300 ℃ .
The previous studies have shown that adding a small amount of Y and Zr ele-
ments to pure Pt can refine alloy’s grains, increase recrystallization temperature, and 
significantly improve the room temperature and high-temperature mechanical prop-
erties of the alloys [32, 33]. The research of Rdzawski [34] shows that adding 0.2% 
Y element to Pt-10Rh alloy can improve the mechanical properties of the alloy, 
especially the strengthening effect of Y at high temperature of 600 ℃ is more signif-
icant. Existing researches have focused more on the mechanical properties of plati-
num alloys. However, platinum-based alloys are mainly used in high-temperature 
oxidation environments in fact. Therefore, exploring the oxidation behavior at high 
temperature of platinum-based alloys and the impact of oxidation on their mechani-
cal properties is very important for their high-temperature applications. In the cur -
rent work, two new platinum-based alloys were designed and prepared by adding a 
small amount of Zr and Y alloy elements to Pt-10Rh alloy. The high-temperature 
oxidation behavior of the alloy and the effect of oxidation on the alloy’s mechanical 
properties were studied systematically.
Experimental Procedures
The Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr and Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr-0.2Y alloys were fabricated by arc melting 
in an argon atmosphere using Pt, Rh with purity of 99.95% and Zr, Y with purity of 
99.90%. The ingot was re-melted four times with inversions and then homogenized 
at 1200 ℃ for 5 h in a vacuum environment. Subsequently, the homogenized ingots 
were hot rolled to a sheet thickness of 2.0 mm at 1100 ℃, followed by cold rolling to 
1.5 mm at room temperature. Finally, stress relief annealing for the rolled sheets was 
performed at 1000  ℃ for 15 min after rolling, and then, surface treatment was car -
ried out by acid washing or polishing to obtain the test samples.

Specimens were cut into oxidation test samples with the size of approximately 
5 × 5 × 1.5 mm. Then, the samples were set in an oxidation furnace for constant tem-
perature oxidation experiments in static air. The experimental temperature range is 
in 1400–1600  ℃, and the oxidation time is 20 and 25  h, respectively. Each alloy 
sample underwent oxidation testing at a set temperature and time, with a total of 
12 experiments conducted. A high-precision balance (Mettler Toledo AL 104-IC, 
precision 0.0001 g) was used to measure the mass difference before and after oxida-
tion test, and the weight loss per unit area was calculated of the alloys. An electric 
discharge wire cutting machine was applied to cut the alloy sheets into the required 
samples for the tensile test, as shown in Fig.  1. Parts of the tensile samples were 
subjected to oxidation treatment in atmospheric environment for 20 h at 1400 and 
1500 °C, respectively. The tensile tests at room temperature were conducted utiliz-
ing a Shimadzu X100KN electronic universal testing machine, with a loading rate of 
3 mm/min. The morphology, composition, and structure of the oxide layers formed 
on the reacted samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi 
SPM-S3400N), electron probe microanalyzer (JEOL, JXA-8283), and transmission 
electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X, resolution 0.16 nm). 
TEM samples are extracted from the fracture surface and prepared using FIB 
method.

and RhO2 have lower Gibbs free energy and thus preferentially form [36]. The 
high-temperature oxidation weight loss of Pt-10Rh alloy is mainly caused by the

volatilization of platinum and rhodium oxides [37]. According to thermomechan-
ical analysis, Y2O3, ZrO2, and Y2O3·ZrO2 are easily formed in the platinum–rho-
dium–(zirconium)–yttrium alloys in high-temperature oxidation environments 
[38]. Although ZrO2 and Y2O3 are difficult to volatilize at the experimental tem-
perature in this work, there is a larger difference in thermal expansion coefficients 
between these two oxides and platinum at room temperature, resulting in the 
oxides spalling from the alloy matrix and losing weight.
The research results of this article indicated that adding 0.2% Y to Pt-10Rh-
0.5Zr alloy increased the oxidation activation energy and significantly reduced 
the oxidation weight loss rate of the alloy, as shown in Table  1. The mechanism 
by which Y element enhances the oxidation resistance of alloy is still unclear, 
and may be related to Y2O3 improving the chemical stability of ZrO2. In addition, 
it was found that, two different forms and crystal structures of zirconia particles 
were formed in the alloys after high-temperature oxidation, and other researchers 
have also obtained similar results. For example, in Pt-20Rh alloy reinforced with 
zirconia and yttria, spherical zirconia with monoclinic and tetragonal structures, 
and yttria particles with cubic structure are formed [38], while in Pt material rein-
forced with dispersion, different zirconia morphologies form appear [33]. Indeed,

the formation of oxides with different morphologies and structures in platinum-
based alloys is influenced by various factors, including stress, grain boundary 
migration, and heat treatment processes [39], which is an interesting and worth-
while topic for further research.
The solid solution strengthening of zirconium is the main strengthening mecha-
nism of Pt–Rh–Zr(Y) alloys [1]. In addition, the dispersed oxides and intermetallic 
compounds formed during the alloy preparation process also have good strengthen-
ing effects [33]. The preparation of dispersion strengthened platinum-based alloys 
by internal oxidation process first requires processing the alloy into thin sheets or 
filaments, and then heat treatment in a high-temperature oxidation environment to 
obtain dispersed ZrO2 or Y2O3 reinforced composite materials. The size of oxide 
particles generally does not exceed 500 nm, and the strengthening effect is stronger 
when the particle size is in the range of 20–100 nm [40]. The strengthening of dis-
persed alloys is achieved through the interaction between dislocations and oxide 
particles, such as the pinning or stacking of dislocations on the surface of oxide 
particles [41]. Due to the low solubility and diffusion rate of oxygen in platinum, 
if bulk alloy materials are used, such as the sample in this work, oxygen can only 
diffuse into the alloy through grain boundaries, causing abnormal growth of oxide 
particles at the grain boundaries, thereby reducing the strength and elongation of 
the alloys. It is worth noting that adding a small amount of yttrium to Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr 
alloy reduces its plasticity remarkably after high temperature and long-term oxida-
tion. Yttrium tends to segregate at grain boundaries due to its extremely low solubil-
ity in platinum and a 30% higher atomic radius than platinum [32]. After long-term 
oxidation treatment at high temperatures, Y2O3 formed at grain boundaries coarsens 
and connects into a network structure, leading to rapid deterioration of the plastic 
properties of the alloy material [33, 42].
Overall, adding rare earth yttrium element is beneficial for the mechanical and 
oxidation resistance of Pt-10Rh alloy, but oxidation has a notable negative impact on 
the mechanical properties of the alloy, especially seriously damaging its room tem-
perature plasticity. Therefore, it is necessary to consider comprehensively the perfor-
mance requirements of alloy materials in the actual service environment.
Conclusions
The high-temperature oxidation behavior of the Pt-10Rh-Zr(Y) alloys in static air in 
the test temperature range of 1400–1600 °C was investigated. Meanwhile, the influ-
ence of oxidation on the mechanical properties of platinum-based alloys was also 
studied. The primary conclusions are summarized as follows:
(1) The oxidation behavior of Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr and Pt-10Rh-05Zr-0.2Y alloys within 
the experimental temperature range conforms to the Arrhenius equation, with 
oxidation activation energies of 23 and 43 kJ/mol, respectively. The oxidation 
resistance of Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr-0.2Y alloy is superior to that of Pt-10Rh-0.5Zr alloy.
(2) After high-temperature oxidation, zirconia or yttria particles with different 
shapes and structures were formed in these two platinum-based alloys. The ZrO2
