Evolution of precipitates in Ni–Co–Cr–W–Mo superalloys
with different tungsten contents
Han-Sheng Bao* , Zhi-Hua Gong, Zheng-Zong Chen, Gang Yang
Received: 6 November 2019 / Revised: 14 January 2020 / Accepted: 17 March 2020
/C211The Nonferrous Metals Society of China and Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2020
Abstract The Ni–Co–Cr–W–Mo system is critical for the
design of nickel-based superalloys. This system stabilizes
different topologically close-packed (TCP) phases in many
commercially superalloys with high W and Mo contents.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission elec-
tron microscopy (TEM) and thermodynamic calculations
were applied to investigate the thermodynamics of the
precipitates in two different W-contained Ni–Co–Cr–W–
Mo superalloys (Alloy 1 and Alloy 2). Computational
thermodynamics veriﬁes the experimental observation of
the l phase formation as a function of temperature and
alloy chemistry, but the kinetics for the precipitation of the
M
6C phase do not agree with the experimental ﬁndings.
The major precipitates of Alloy 1 at temperatures of
700 /C176C and 750 /C176C during long-time exposure are M 23C6,
c0 phase and MC; for Alloy 2, they are M 23C6, c0 phase,
MC, M6C and l phase. W addition is found to promote the
precipitation of M 6C and l phase during exposure. M 6C
has higher W and lower Ni content than l phase, whereas
M6C is an unstable phase that would transform into M 12C
after 5000-h exposure at 750 /C176C. A great quantity of nee-
dle-like l phases precipitated after exposure at 750 /C176C for
5000 h, which have no effect on the impact properties of
Alloy 2.
Keywords Nickel-based superalloys; M 6C; l phase;
Long-time exposure; M 12C
1 Introduction
With the development of advanced ultra-supercritical coal-
ﬁred power plants, the steam pressure and temperature
reach 35 MPa and 700 /C176C, respectively, while thermal
efﬁciency will be over 50% [ 1–5]. Conventional Fe-based
heat-resistant steels used for main steam piping, and re-
heater (or super-heater) tubes and blades do not comply
with the manufacturing requirements of key high temper-
ature components, for steam temperature exceeding 650 /C176C
[6, 7]. Thus, some nickel-based alloys including Waspaloy,
USC 141, ACC 617 and Inconel 718 are candidates for
turbines and pipes [ 8–10].
Tungsten (W) has a larger atomic radius and lattice
distortion than molybdenum (Mo) to produce a stronger
solution strengthening effect. Additionally, the reduced
stacking fault energy of alloys containing tungsten can
improve creep fracture strength [ 9, 11]. As a result, W has
partially replaced Mo in some heat-resistant steels, such as
the TOS110 and HR1200 [ 12], to improve creep rupture
strength. In addition, W is also widely used in nickel-based
single crystal alloys to increase the service temperature
[13–15].
Waspaloy is a nickel-based superalloy strengthened by
nanometer-size c
0 phase and M 23C6 carbide. As W can
improve the stability of c0 phase through changing the
distribution of the alloy elements in c0–c matrix, some W
element is added to the Waspaloy nickel-based superalloy.
Our group recently developed a novel wrought nickel-
based superalloy based on Waspaloy that is a promising
candidate for use in steam turbine. The addition of excess
refractory elements such as W and Mo to the alloy will
promote the formation of a phase family known as topo-
logically close-packed (TCP) phases during long-time

2 Experimental
The main alloying addition of these two alloys including
nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), W, Mo and chromium (Cr) were
molten using a vacuum induction furnace. The 25-kg ingots
were then forged into a round bar with 16 mm in diameter.
The chemical compositions of the new designed alloys are
given in Table 1.
The as-processed samples were subjected to a standard
heat treatment process, which consists of (1) soaking at
1080 /C176C for 4 h and oil cooling, (2) soaking at 845 /C176C for
24 h and then air cooling to room temperature and (3)
aging at 760 /C176C for 16 h and then air cooling to room
temperature. The as-treated samples were subsequently
aged in a mufﬂe furnace. Both alloys were aged at 700 /C176C
for 1000, 3000, 5000 and 10,000 h and 750 /C176C for 3000
and 5000 h.
Charpy impact properties were evaluated for the sam-
ples subjected to standard heat treatment and long-term
exposure. The absorbed energies were obtained using a
Charpy V-notch machine at room temperature.
Precipitates extracted from aged samples were identiﬁed
by a small-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD, Pert MPD) tester.
Precipitates powder for XRD tests was prepared with a
solution consisting of 10 g /C L

10 g/C L-1 aqueous citric acid, and the extraction current
density was 0.03 A /C cm2 at a temperature range from
- 15 /C176Ct o2 0 /C176C. Specimens for ﬁeld emission scanning
electron microscopy (FESEM) were cut from aged samples
and prepared using mechanical polishing and etching with
a solution containing 200 ml hydrochloric acid, 200 ml

carried out using a FEI Quanta 650 microscope equipped

3.1 Thermo-calc dynamics calculation
Thermo-calc dynamic calculation software predicts the
phase formation and stability of alloys [ 19, 20]. In this
study, thermal-calc thermodynamic software with TTNi 8
database was used to calculate the content of precipitation
of alloys with different W and Mo contents at varying
temperatures. Figures 1 and 2 show the calculated results
of the tentative alloys. Figure 1a shows all the predicted
equilibrium phases and their content in Alloy 1, which
contains 4 wt% W at a temperature range of 500–1500 /C176C.
Only the liquid phase forms at 1380 /C176C (above the liq-
uidus). The c matrix, l phase, c
0 phase and M23C6 carbide
have been separated out in turn with temperature decreas-
ing and their contents change with temperature. The cal-
culated precipitation temperature of l phase, c
0 phase and
M23C6 phase is 1130, 1010 and 920 /C176C, respectively.
Figure 1b shows all the predicted equilibrium phases and
their contents in Alloy 2, which contains 8 wt% W. One
can see that there are c matrix, l phase, c0 phase, r phase,
R phase and M 23C6 carbide. And the precipitation tem-
perature of r phase and R phase is 725 /C176C and 580 /C176C,
respectively. Meanwhile, a very little MC phase precipi-
tates from two alloys at temperature above 900 /C176C.
Figure 2a, b shows the effect of W content on the
equilibrium precipitates of the alloy with 3.5 wt% Mo
addition at 700 /C176C and 750 /C176C, respectively. At 700 /C176C, the
precipitation amount of l increases along with W content
when the latter is less than 6 wt%, and then remains
unchanged. At 750 /C176C, the precipitation amount of l
increases with W content, while the latter is less than 8.2
wt%, and then remains constant. Meanwhile, with the same
W content, the precipitation amount of l phase at 700 /C176Ci s
higher than that at 750 /C176C. SIGMA phase begins to pre-
cipitate when W content reaches 7.8 wt% and 8.3 wt% at
700 /C176C and 750 /C176C, respectively.

ferent W contents after long-term exposure at 700 /C176C and
750 /C176C. Only c

detected in Alloy 1 after exposure at 700 /C176C and 750 /C176C,
whereas c0 phase, MC, M 23C6 and M 6C were detected in
Alloy 2 before and after long-term exposure; l phase was
found after exposure at 750 /C176C for 3000 and 5000 h.

tungsten in the alloy exceeds 10 wt%, it is reasonable to
speculate the existence of l phase [25, 26].

Because refractory W and Mo elements have larger
atomic size and weight, their diffusion rates are very low;
therefore, solution heat treatment did not completely
eliminate the segregation of W and Mo. As a result, the
content of refractory elements is much higher in some
regions than in others. The elevated content leads to the
formation of c matrix and causes these regions more easily
reach super saturation state and induce M
6C and TCP
phase precipitates [29]. Thermodynamic calculations show
that the only phase that can precipitate from c matrix at 700
and 750 /C176Ci s l phase. Some reports have shown that M 6C
is an unstable phase and a mesophase of l phase. As M 6C
has a similar atomic arrangement structure to l phase, it
can transform into l phase under speciﬁc thermodynamic
conditions [30]. M6C transforms into M 12C during 5000-h
exposure at 750 /C176C, but M6C transforming into l phase has
not been observed during exposure, indicating that the
formation of l phase requires a higher thermodynamic
driving force and precipitation temperature.

Because fracture behavior of the aged alloy is found to
be intergranular, the distribution and quantity of carbides
around the grain boundary have a decisive inﬂuence on the
impact properties of the alloy. Because the MC phase
distributed inside the grains remains stable during the long-
term aging process, the effect of these particles on impact
properties is negligible. The impact properties of the alloy
do not decrease signiﬁcantly with an increased amount of
M
6C phases (Table 4 and Fig. 11). Moreover, the dominant
precipitation around the grains is conﬁrmed to be M 23C6.
The larger amount and the shift in distribution from
intermittent to continuous during the initial stage of aging
reduce the impact absorbing energy signiﬁcantly during the
ﬁrst 1000-h aging.
Alloy 2 has lower impact absorbing energy than Alloy 1
because the higher W content in Alloy 2 increases the
precipitation of M
23C6, which in turn reduces the disloca-
tion movement and results in more stress concentration
during deformation. Consequently, the crack spreads more
easily along the grain boundary.
The main precipitation period of l phase at 750 /C176Ci s
between 3000 and 5000 h, but the impact absorbing energy
was unchanged during this exposure time. Therefore, l
phase has no distinct effect on the impact absorbing energy
of Alloy 2. This may be because l phase mainly precipi-
tates in grains, whereas the fracture of the alloy is mainly
intergranular.

4 Conclusion
Thermodynamic calculation performed in the temperature
range of 500–1500 /C176C shows that the principal secondary
phases in the alloy under equilibrium state consist of
M23C6, c0 phase, MC, l phase and M 6C. The latter two
phases were only found in the alloy with higher W content.
The M6C particles were found after solution heat treatment.
During the subsequent 3000 h, the amount of this phase
increased continuously with aging and then remained
almost constant. The amount of precipitation at 750 /C176C was
much higher than that at 700 /C176C. Part of M
6C transformed
into M12C after 5000-h exposure at 750 /C176C. Needle-like l
phase precipitated after 1000-h exposure at 750 /C176C, and the
amount increased signiﬁcantly during the following expo-
sure time. The main elements of the l phase were Ni, W,
Co, Cr and Mo. The impact energy of the alloy with high
W content was low. However, l phase has no marked
effect on the impact property during aging at 750 /C176C.
