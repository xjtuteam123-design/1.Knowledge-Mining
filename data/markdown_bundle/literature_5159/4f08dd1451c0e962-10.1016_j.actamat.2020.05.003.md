Abstract
The possibility of designing multi-principal element alloys in the Ni-Co-Ru system is investigated by ﬁrst-principles
and experimental means. A high-throughput ﬁrst-principles approach is employed to probe the e ﬀect of composition
on planar fault energies. Of most interest, a FCC alloy, Ni40Co40Ru20, exhibits a high room temperature strength
compared to existing nickel-containing solid solution FCC high entropy alloys. The presence of Ru imparts the alloy
with unique properties. First, the low stacking fault energy provides a low critical resolved shear stress for twinning,
allowing a TWIP e ﬀect. Second, the high lattice friction and the associated high yield strength enables the critical
resolved shear stress for twinning to be reached at low strains at room temperature, resulting in the formation of a
dense network of planar defects for increased work hardening. A TRIP e ﬀect is also observed at higher strains at
room temperature.
Keywords: High Entropy Alloys, Alloy design, Density Functional Theory, planar fault energies, TWIP, TRIP
1. Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEAs) have gained tremendous interest since the discovery of the exceptional properties of
the face centered cubic (FCC) solid-solution NiCoCrFeMn , the Cantor alloy. In this alloy, damage tolerance, yield
strength and ductility all increase from room to cryogenic temperatures [1, 2]. HEAs refer to single phase alloys
that contain ﬁve or more elements in near equiatomic quantity. Their unique properties have been attributed to four
main eﬀects: high (conﬁgurational) entropy, sluggish di ﬀusion, severe lattice distortion and cocktail e ﬀects [3]. As
reported by Otto et al. [4], increasing the number of elements maximizes the conﬁgurational entropy but does not

minimize the total Gibbs free energy of the system, hence high conﬁgurational entropy is not a su ﬃcient condition
for obtaining a single phase alloy. In parallel, by studying the phase stability and mechanical properties of equiatomic
two-, three- and four-element subsets of the NiCoCrFeMn system, Wu et al. [5] have shown that some medium
entropy alloys (MEAs), NiCoCrFe and NiCoCr , possess higher strength than NiCoCrFeMn at room temperature.
The improved properties of HEAs and MEAs at cryogenic temperatures are due to the activation of twinning induced
plasticity (TWIP) [2, 6], allowed by decreasing stacking fault energies from room to cryogenic temperatures. A
network of nano-twins forms upon straining, which reduces the mean path of dislocations, resulting in increased
strain-hardening and delayed necking [7, 8]. The lower stacking fault energy of NiCoCr (18− 22mJ.m−2 [9–11])
compared to NiCoCrFeMn (27− 30 mJ.m−2 [10, 12, 13]) enables not only the formation of a denser network of
nano-twins [11], but also transformation induced plasticity (TRIP) at large strains [14]. The occurrence of the γ→ϵ
martensitic transformation promotes further increases in work-hardening. Following these observations, numerous
eﬀorts that combine ﬁrst-principles calculations and experimental trials have been conducted to design TWIP and
TRIP-HEAs [15–22]. As a result of these defect engineering approaches [23], Zaddach et al. [24] have designed
the multi-principal element alloy Ni14Co20Cr26Fe20Mn20 (MPEA), which has a stacking fault energy as low as 3 .5
mJ m−2. Li and Raabe [25] have designed metastable HEAs which exhibit tremendous TRIP behavior for increased
work-hardening. High entropy steels and high entropy cobalt base alloys are also now emerging as new classes of
materials [26, 27].
To date, most investigations have been focusing on tailoring the elemental composition of the NiCoCrFeMn
system rather than exploring possibilities with other elements. Considering the excellent properties of the NiCoCr
alloy and considering that lattice friction is a key strengthening mechanism in HEAs, the present study focuses on
the possibility of designing MPEAs in the NiCoRu system. Ru was selected for several reasons. First, Ru possesses
a larger atomic radius than Cr, which would lead to increased lattice friction. In HEAs, lattice friction is one key
strengthening mechanism [23, 28, 29]. In addition, the native HCP structure of Ru makes it a promising element for
tuning the stacking fault energy of FCC the Ni-Co system. While any HCP element soluble in the Ni-Co system could
be used to tune the stacking-fault energy,Ru also has unique mechanical properties. With a value of 173 GPa, the shear
modulus of Ru is among the highest of all HCP elements, and is also higher than that of Cr (115 GPa). Ru is thus an

2. Methods
Shearing of one part of the crystal with respect to the other with a non-lattice translation results in a change in
stacking sequence and thereby the bonding environment in the vicinity of the planar fault created by the shear. The
three types of possible planar faults in FCC lattices are shown in ﬁgure 1. An intrinsic stacking fault (ISF) is formed
when one half of the crystal is sheared by a Shockley partial ( bs=1/6⟨11¯2⟩), resulting in a change in the initial FCC
stacking sequence (ﬁgure 1-a,b). A Shockley partial passed on the adjacent layer L1 creates an extrinsic fault (ESF,
ﬁgure 1-c), equivalent to the insertion of an extra layer in the FCC crystal (layer L0 in ﬁgure 1-c). Repeated shearing
by Shockley partials (bs) on every plane leads to the creation of a twin, with an interface passing through the layer L0
( ﬁgure 1-d). The energy penalty associated with a planar defect disrupting the long range crystal periodicity is the
planar fault energy,γPFE . The ISF, ESF and twin energies (γIS F,γES F andγtwin, respectively) govern the deformation
behavior of FCC MPEAs [23, 30, 31].

2.2. Experimental methods
Alloys were prepared using 99 .95, 99.5 and 99.98wt% pure Ni, Co and Ru, respectively. Buttons of targeted
compositions, 40 grams in weight, were arc-melted under a titanium-gettered argon atmosphere on a water-cooled
copper hearth. The buttons were ﬂipped and remelted at least four times to ensure chemical homogeneity. They were
then submitted to a homogenization treatment consisting of 24 hours exposure at 1250 ◦C under argon atmosphere,
followed by water-quenching. For comparison, a button of NiCoCr was also cast, and homogenized at 1200 ◦C for
24 hours. Cylinders of 4 mm in diameter by 4 mm in height were cut by electrical discharge machining (EDM).
The EDM-aﬀected layer was removed with abrasive paper. The samples were deformed in compression at room tem-
perature at a rate of 10−3s−1 with platens lubricated by molybdenum disulﬁde. The microstructure of the deformed
specimens was characterized in the center of their cross-section.
Splat-quenching was used as a tool to quickly produce uniform ﬁne-grain microstructures. Chunks of about 0.03mm3
(0.2 grams) of the homogenized material were remelted using a tri-arc splat-quenching system. This device consists
of three tungsten rods pointing at a water-cooled copper base topped with a TZM (molybdenum alloy) hammer and
operated under high purity argon. An arc is formed with the tungsten rods and pointed towards the material. Once
the material is melted, the hammer is released and the arc is interrupted simultaneously. With the fast cooling rate
(about 106K.s−1), the material solidiﬁes extremely rapidly and thin foil of about one inch in diameter is obtained. Its
thickness is about 250-300 µm at its center and 100-150 µm at its rim. This solidiﬁcation path enables production of
ﬁne grain sizes (ranging from 1 to 15 µm) with no texture (typical maximum texture coefﬁcient is 2.1 times random)

3.1. Stacking fault energies from ﬁrst-principles calculations
The eﬀect of Ru on lattice parameter and various PFEs in (Ni0.5Co0.5)1−xRux (where x = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1),
constrained to an FCC lattice, are summarized in ﬁgure 2. As expected, the addition of Ru leads to an increase in the
lattice parameter (ﬁgure 2-a). Both constrained and unconstrained datasets showed similar values of a. For the sake
of clarity values from the unconstrained datatset only are presented. The e ﬀect of Ru on PFEs is shown in ﬁgure 2-b
to -d. Hollow and solid markers represent estimates from datasets with full relaxation and dimensions constrained
to FCC, respectively, and can be viewed as the PFE bounds. The estimated ISF energies of Ni0.5Co0.5 are 15 mJ/m2
(relaxed) and 7 mJ/m2 (constrained), in good agreement with values reported by Chandran and Sondhi [42] using an
Axial Ising model (AIM) [43]. Both ISF and twin energies remain relatively constant and positive while ESF energies
are less than zero up to 50% Ru. All energies then dramatically decrease and become all negative. The dramatic
reduction inγ above 50% Ru can be explained by the native HCP structure of Ru. Since the EHCP− EFCC < 0, ESF
energies are less than the other two PFEs and a general trend ofγIS F >γ twin >γ ES F is observed for all compositions.

4. Discussion
4.1. Mechanical properties
4.1.1. Lattice friction and yield strength
As already noted by Wu et al. [5], increasing the number of elements in a HEA does not guarantee a higher
strength. The same behavior is observed for hardness values (ﬁgure 7) as the Cantor alloy NiCoCrFeMn exhibits a
18

## Page 21

lower hardness than some of the three and four elements alloys ( NiCoCrFe and NiCoCr ). The FCC Ni40Co40Ru20
alloy has a higher hardness than all other nickel-containing solid solution FCC alloys shown here, including NiCoCr
[5, 45, 47]. The linear ﬁt of the hardness data presented in ﬁgure 7 lines up with the hardness measurement from the
bulk sample and leads to a H0 of 153 HV for Ni40Co40Ru20. This is equal to or higher than the value found for the
NiCoCr alloy: 151 HV from Wu et al. [5] and 129 HV from Huang et al. [45]. Additionally, the lattice parameter
of Ni40Co40Ru20 measured by X-ray di ﬀraction is 0.359 nm (ﬁgure 4-c), while that of NiCoCr is 0.3567 nm [11].
In nickel-containing HEAs, Zhao et al. [31] have shown that the lattice friction increases with the lattice parameter.
Following their conjecture, a slightly higher lattice friction is to be expected for the Ru-containing alloy. The com-
pressive stress-strain curve shown in ﬁgure 5-a shows a yield strength of about 430MPa for Ni40Co40Ru20 while only
190 MPa for the NiCoCr , for a similar grain size. The Hall-Petch behavior of NiCoCr has been studied in detail
by Yoshida et al. [49] as well as Schneider et al. [50], who have shown that the strengthening contribution of high
angle grain boundaries and annealing twin boundaries are equivalent, suggesting that the crystallite size (measured
by including annealing twin boundaries in the interception method) is the right parameter for the determination of the
Hall-Petch behavior in this alloy. They also report that the yield strength measured by tensile and compression testing
are similar. Therefore, the yield strength of NiCoCr having the exact same microstructure (grain and crystallite size)
as the Ni40Co40Ru20 specimen of ﬁgure 5-a can be estimated using their regression coeﬃcients. For a crystallite size of
250µm, their Hall-Petch parameters lead to a yield strength of 208 MPa (σ0 = 150 MPa, kHP = 815 MPa.µm). The
parameters found by Yoshida et al. [49] (σ0 = 218 MPa, kHP = 265 MPa.µm) leads to a yield strength of 235 MPa.
Both are in the same range than the measured value from ﬁgure 5-a, supporting the conclusion that Ni40Co40Ru20
possesses a higher intrinsic yield strength than NiCoCr .
4.1.2. TWIP and TRIP e ﬀects
Most of the strength and hardening of HEAs has been shown to arise from nano-scale deformation structures
formed dynamically upon straining. The low stacking fault energies in these alloys suggest a large separation width
between partials and limited cross-slip. A high density of faults is thus expected in the deformed microstructures.
The stress-strain curves shown in ﬁgure 5-a indicates a slightly higher strain-hardening coe ﬃcient in Ni40Co40Ru20
compared to NiCoCr (0.38 against 0.34). The ECCI images shown in ﬁgure 5-b reveal a high density of planar
19

## Page 22

defects, identiﬁed as nano-twinsm HCP laths and extrinsic stacking faults by TEM analyses. Similar structures are also
observed in both NiCoCr [6] and the Cantor alloy [2], though to a lower extent. The volume fraction of planar defects
is a critical parameter that confer HEAs their excellent mechanical properties: the formation of nano-twins (TWIP
eﬀect), facilitated at cryogenic temperatures, is the reason for the remarkable fracture toughness, work-hardening and
elongation to rupture observed in the Cantor alloy [2, 6–8, 14, 51, 52]. For similar strains and grain sizes, this nano-
twin network is denser in the NiCoCr alloy, compared to theCantor alloy. Laplanche et al. [11] have shown that even
though both alloys exhibit similar critical stresses (CRSS) for twinning (260± 30 MPa for NiCoCr against 235± 10
MPa for the Cantor alloy [12]), the higher yield strength in NiCoCr enables the twinning stress to be reached earlier,
hence the denser network and steady work hardening. Similar strengthening mechanisms are active in Ni40Co40Ru20.
At a strain of 0.16, several twinning systems are already activated in most of the grains (ﬁgure 5-b). However, as the
twinning stress decreases with increasing grain size [53], a lower twin density is to be expected in microstructures
with ﬁner grains. Future tests on samples of ﬁner grain sizes will be conducted to address the inﬂuence of grain size.
Meyers and Murr [54] have correlated the twinning stress of FCC alloys to their shear modulus, stacking fault energy
and magnitude of their Burgers vector in equation 4:
σT = K( γ
Gb )1/2 (4)
where γ is the intrinsic stacking fault energy, G is the shear modulus and b is the magnitude of the Burgers vector.
According to this criterion, the high shear modulus and low stacking fault energy (19 mJ/m2) of the Ni40Co40Ru20
alloy, suggests a very low twinning stress compared to other nickel containing HEAs. The calculation of the ratio of
σT,NiCoCr andσT,Ni40Co40Ru20, using a shear modulus of 87 GPa [5] and a Burgers vector magnitude of 0.252 nm [11]
for NiCoCr and a shear modulus of 111 GPa and a Burgers vector of 0.254 nm (from X-ray diﬀraction data of section
3.3.1) leads to a ratio of 0.79. In other words, the twinning stress ofNi40Co40Ru20 is 0.79 times that ofNiCoCr . Using
a twinning stress of 260 MPa in NiCoCr [11], the estimated twinning stress of Ni40Co40Ru20 would be 205 MPa. The
detailed characterization of the deformation mechanisms, density of twins and critical stresses for twinning will be
investigated in detail in future work.
Aside from nano-twins, the steady work-hardening of NiCoCr has also recently been attributed to another type of
nano-structure that forms at larger strains: HCP nano-laths [14, 55]. Niu et al. [56] have shown from ﬁrst-principles
20

## Page 23

calculations that while the martensitic FCC-HCP transformation (TRIP effect) was prevented by the magnetic frustra-
tion of Mn in the Cantor alloy, it is enabled inNiCoCr . In Mn-containing steels, the TWIP eﬀect is typically triggered
for SFEs of about 20 mJ.m−2 while the TRIP eﬀect requires lower SFEs, of about 8-10 mJ.m−2 [57]. The SFE of the
Cantor alloy falls within the range enabling TWIP (18 .3− 27.3 mJ.m−2 [58], 26.5± 4.5 mJ.m−2 [10], 18.3− 27.3
mJ.m−2 [24]), while that of NiCoCr is lower (22 mJ.m−2 [11]). The SFE values calculated for the Ni40Co40Ru20 are
in agreement with the occurrence of a TRIP e ﬀect, which supports the observations of ﬁgure 8-d,f. In addition, the
relative PFE values obtained from ﬁrst-principles calculations lead to: γES F < γtwin < γIS F, for all compositions.
These reveal that the overall energy of the system can be minimized with a high density of ESFs, followed by twins,
rather than a high density of ISFs. This is consistent with the TEM characterization shown in ﬁgure 9, which indeed
reveals a high density of ESFs and twins allowed by the TRIP eﬀect as the strain increases.
4.2. Phase stability and fault energies
Microstructure characterization post deformation revealed a high density of twins, HCP laths and ESFs. This
observation is consistent with the trends in the planar faults energies presented in ﬁgure 2. γES F < 0 and it is also
lower than the other PFEs. The average structural energy di ﬀerence between HCP and FCC for any composition in
(Ni0.5Co0.5)1−xRux is negative indicating that HCP should be the stable structure for these compositions. Interest-
ingly, the 0K DFT calculations predict that the binary NiCo alloy is also stable in HCP compared to FCC. However,
Nishizawa and Ishida [59] report that the FCC domain of the Ni-Co phase diagram extends up to 65% Co at room
temperature. Recently, Chowdhury et al. [60] reported the existence of a stable FCC Ni-Co alloy with as much as
67% Co at room temperature. Thus, there is enough experimental evidence suggesting the equiatomic NiCo to be
stable in FCC at room temperature. Moreover, the present experimental results show that Ni40Co40Ru20 is FCC after
arc-melting and homogenization at 1250 ◦C (ﬁgure 3-c,d). Additional characterization shows that it remains FCC
after long term exposure at 1000 ◦C (see ﬁgure 12 in supplementary material), further reinforcing its stability in the
FCC structure. This comparison with experimental observations highlights the inﬂuence of temperature on stacking
fault energies in these alloys, which is not taken into account in the present DFT calculations. In a similar manner,
ﬁrst-principles calculations by Zhang et al. [61] on NiCoCrFeMn and NiCoCr HEAs showed that HCP is the ground
state structure, while the alloys are experimentally FCC at room and high temperatures. According to these authors,
21

## Page 24

this indicates a metastable FCC alloy at low temperatures, where most stable structure would be an FCC stacking
containing many planar faults. When the vibrational contributions to free energies are included to the 0 K calcula-
tions on NiCoCr and the Cantor alloy, Huang et al. [51] have shown that the FCC structure becomes more stable
than HCP with increasing temperature. A similar evolution of the stacking fault energy can thus be anticipated in the
(Ni0.5Co0.5)1−xRux alloys. In addition to vibrational entropy, Ma et al. [62] have shown by ﬁrst-principles calculations
that the electronic and magnetic contributions to free energies can contribute up to 50% of the conﬁgurational entropy
value, potentially aﬀecting phase stability. However, as the absolute value of the vibrational entropy increases signif-
icantly with temperature, up to four times the conﬁgurational entropy value, this term is of greater importance. The
Ni40Co40Ru20 alloy exhibits a similar hardening behavior compared to NiCoCr upon straining at room temperature.
Similar planar defects are observed in both alloys. Interestingly, ﬁrst-principles calculations suggest that HCP should
be the ground state structure for NiCoCr , as negative stacking fault energies are obtained not only at 0K [24, 61], but
also at ﬁnite temperatures [51]. The large variations in experimentally measured stacking fault energies derived from
measurements of Shockley partials separation widths reveal the strong inﬂuence of local environment in metastable
HEAs [63]. This is in accordance with ﬁrst-principles calculations by Ding et al. [30], who showed that the stacking
fault energy of NiCoCr varies over a wide range depending on the local chemical order. The suspected existence of
short-range ordering (SRO) in NiCoCr has been recently adressed experimentally by Zhang et al. [64] and Zhang et
al. [65] (another team).
To summarize, the metastablility of most FCC HEAs at room temperature is most likely explained by vibrational
contribution to the free energies and the presence of short range ordering. Whether SRO exists in the Ru-containing
MPEAs is beyond the scope of the present study and will be discussed elsewhere.
Conclusion
High-throughput ﬁrst-principles calculations have enabled exploration of the ( Ni0.5Co0.5)(1−x)Rux ternary system
and design of a new stable FCC multi-principal element alloy,Ni40Co40Ru20. Interestingly, this alloy exhibits a higher
strength than other single phase nickel-containing FCC solid-solution HEAs reported in the literature to date. This is
due to the combination of a high lattice friction, modulus, and easily activated TWIP and TRIP e ﬀects at room tem-
22

## Page 25

perature. The readily activated TWIP phenomenon confers Ni40Co40Ru20 similar work hardening rate than NiCoCr .
Both alloys also possess similar grain boundary strengthening. The observed planar defects are in accordance with
the supporting ﬁrst-principles calculations, revealing high densities of ESFs and twins, followed by increased TRIP
eﬀect as strain increases.
