Abstract 
The mechanical behaviour of an alloy based on Fe-40A1 prepared from mechanically alloyed powders was examined over a 
wide temperature range in the fine-grained, as-extruded state as well as after recrystallizing to a large-grained state. While the 
fine-grained material was strong and reasonably ductile at room temperature, in contrast with the weaker and more brittle 
large-grained material, at high temperature the strength fell to low values, similar for both materials. This behaviour is interpreted 
in terms of a contribution to strengthening due to the particles present, by Orowan hardening at low temperatures and by 
dislocation -particle interactions at high temperature, a contribution due to the grain size, which can harden at low temperatures 
and soften at high temperatures, and a contribution due to the matrix. The room temperature ductility ~ems to be dependent 
mostly on the grain size, since fine grain sizes can inhibit brittle crack formation.

I. Introduction 
Iron aluminides are receiving extensive attention as 
materials showing potential for application as high 
temperature oxidation and corrosion resisting steels. 
The materials are fairly light, show good resistance to 
oxidation at high temperature, and particularly good 
corrosion resistance in the sulphur-bearing environ- 
ments. Materials containing about 25%-30% A! are 
generally examined for intermediate temperature appli- 
cations, about 500 °C, where the presence of DO3 order 
allows increased strength and where the AI content 
endows sufficient oxidation resistance. For cases where 
additional oxidation resistance is required, for higher 
temperature applications, or where lower density or 
higher specific stiffness is required, alloys containing 
about 35%-45% AI are considered [1-3]. A major 
limitation to their application is the low ductility at 
room temperature, explained recently by a reaction 
with water vapour which causes embrittlement by in- 
jecting hydrogen into the material near a propagating 
crack [4-9]. At the same time, the strength of the 
material, particularly at high temperature, is not very 
good [10,11]. Most attempts at alloy modification 
which try to improve the strength tend to reduce the 
ductility [2,12], and so the overall properties are not 
enhanced. 
Recently, there has been a large amount of activity 
on the mechanical alloying (MA) of iron aluminides 
[13,14] and this has led to the development of an MA 
version of the Fe--40% AI alloy (throughout this report 
atomic concentrations are given) where very high 
strength at room temperature is coupled with a certain 
amount of tensile ductility. These properties arise from 
the retention of a fine grain size and from the presence 
of a dispersion of fine, typically oxide, particles. 
The present study examines the room temperature 
and high temperature behaviour of such a material in 
an attempt to determine the relative contributions of 
grain size hardening and particle hardening to the 
overall strength. Behaviour at high temperatures may 
be expected to be complicated beacuse of the possibility 
of microstructural instabilities as well as because of the 
changing strengthening roles of dispersed particles and 
grain boundaries. As an example of such variations in 
behaviour, it has been shown (for example [10,11]) that 
grain boundaries generally strengthen materials at low 
test temperatures but soften at high temperatures as

4. Discussion 
Some of the most interesting aspects of the present 
work are the very high strength of the as-extruded 
material at room temperature, with notable tensile duc- 
tility (0.75%), and the sharp fall in strength at interme- 
diate to high temperatures. At the same time the 
recrystallized material is much weaker and more brittle 
(tensile ductility about 0.45%) at room temperature but 
retains the same strength as the as-extruded material at 
the high temperatures. It is of interest, then, to analyse 
this strengthening behaviour and, in particular, to iden- 
tify the contributions of the fine grain size, the many 
particles, and the base matrix, to the total strength.

strengthening in both the as-extruded and the recrystal- 
lized materials, and both at room temperature and at 
700 °C. The strengthening contribution of the particles

thc particles are sufficiently large that they are not cut

Before considering in detail the strengthening role of 
the particles, it is useful to examine whether the use of 
the threshold stress approach is valid for the present 
data. As a first point, it should be remembered that 
such interpretations of deformation behaviour were 
developed for describing steady state creep behaviour 
and not flow stress variations. From the load exten- 
sion variations during high temperature testing, illus- 
trated in Figs. 4 and 5, it is clear that considerable, 
continuous work hardening is found at low test temper- 
atures, and it is only at 700 °C (and higher tempera- 
tures) that a near steady state flow stress is found after 
a smal amount of deformation. At 700 °C, the differ- 
ence between 0.2% flow stress and the steady state flow 
stress is about 20% of the 0.2% flow stress in all cases, 
and describing the steady state data by the power law 
equation or by the threshld equation leads to little 
change (except that the value of the threshold stress is 
also increased by about 20'7,,, for example for the 
as-extruded material, from 125 MPa (Table i) to about 
150 MPa). Accordingly, based on this analysis, and 
apart from this rather small difference in the numerical 
values, there seems no reason why the equations devel- 
oped for steady state deformation conditions should 
not be used for the present high temperature flow stress 
data. 
As a second point, when considering the validity ot" 
the threshold stress approach, it should be remembered 
that the use of this approach is based essentially on the 
high stress exponent found when using the power law 
equation to relate the deformation rate and flow stress, 
and the concept is supported by the microstructural 
observation of dislocations encountering and being at- 
tached to many particles inside the grains. It is interest- 
ing to recall, however, that high stress exponents have 
been found when testing FeAI alloys with AI contents 
over the range of about 40%-50'7,, [10,11], even when 
no strengthening particles are present to produce a 
threshold stress. Hence a direct association between 
high stress exponent in the power law equation and 
threshold effects due to particles cannot be made. Ex- 
amination of the previous data [10,11] shows, however. 
that high stress exponents are found for the single- 
phase materials when they have a large grain size and 
only for the smaller grain size materials (typically grain 
sized near 10/~m) at high stresses and strain rates. The 
appearance of such high stress exponents was appar- 
ently related to the formation of a subgrain structure 
inside the large grains [11], which, presumably, could 
not form within the smaller grains. Based on this 
reasoning, it seems reasonable to deduce that the 
present as-extruded material, with its very fine grain 
size, should show a low stress exponent (n ~. 3 to 4) 
unless particle-induced threshold stress effects are im- 
portant. However, it may be sufficient to associate a 
high stress exponent deformation behaviour of the re- 
crystallized material with inter-dislocation interactions 
without the need to invoke dislocation-particle thres- 
hold effects.

threshold stress is to search for the expected dramatic 
fall in deformation rate as the applied stress falls to

slow strain rate deformation and differing conclusions 
have been made about the role of the particles present. 
Based on the variations of strain rate with applied 
stress, Schneibel et al. [28] deduced that the behaviour 
of their oxide dispersion strengthened FeAI could be 
interpreted by the threshold stress model. In another 
study on an FeAI alloy containing a few large particles 
(4% by volume of 0.25/~m size intermetallic particles), 
Whittenberger et al. [29] found no evidence of threshold 
behaviour at low strain rates and deduced that several 
strengthening mechanisms were operating, leading to 
high stress exponents in the power law equation, similar 
to those found for large-grained single-phase FeAI. A 
possible criticism of this study, based on the comments 
made above, and bearing in mind that the Orowan 
stress for this only slightly particle-strengthened mate- 
rial is low, is that the stress and strain rate conditions 
examined have not been sumciently small for significant 
threshold effects to appear. 
In view of the previous discussion, the threshold 
stress approach is considered for interpreting the high 
temperature, slow strain rate behaviour of the present 
materials. The justification for using this approach, and 
assuming that the particles are responsible for creating 
a threshold stress, is that the stress exponent found 
when using the power law is high, and that many 
paarticles are present inside the grains which clearly 
interact strongly with the dislocations. Examination of 
the stress variation of the strain rate over a much wider 
range, and particularly at very low stresses and strain 
rates, would be important to test the assumption made. 
The appearance of high stress exponents in the power 
law found in previous studies on larger grain sized, 
single-phase FeAI in not seen to be an obstacle to the 
present discussion.

Finally, the ductilities of the present materials at 
room temperature, about 0.75% and 0.45% for as-ex- 
truded and recrystallized materials respectively, can be 
analysed in terms of the roles of grain size and particle 
dispersions. It is well known [4-9] that the ductility 
values obtained on tensile testing these materials de- 
pend sensitively on testing conditions such as the envi- 
ronment, strain rate, and so on, and hence the 
discussion considers only the relative ductilities and not 
the absolute values obtained. The ductilities measured 
on both materials here are in fact low relative to 
literature values, for example 1% 2% ductility mea- 
sured [1,17] on material with grain size of the order 200 
/lm and of strength 260-380 MPa or 2%,-3%, ductility 
[1,2] on material with grain size about 10-30/lm and 
strength 500-800 MPa, and this may suggest that the 
present values could be improved by more careful 
tensile testing or sample preparation, or that the mate- 
rial studied is imperfectly prepared with grain size or 
particle inhomogeneities or with poorly bonded powder 
particles. The recrystallized material here clearly shows 
poor ductility because the large grain size allows the 
easy generation and propagation of brittle cleavage 
cracks, and the particles play only a minor role in 
resisting such cracking. The as-extruded material shows 
better ductility than the recrystallized material, appar- 
ently since the grain size is small enough to prevent 
brittle cleavage crack formation. The ductility is, never- 
theless, much smaller than would be expected for such 
a fine grain size, by comparison with the literature data 
tl,21. One possible cause is the higher strength, due to 
the particle strengthening, which allows sufficiently high 
stresses to be generated that brittle cracking can still 
start. For both as-extruded and recrystallized materials 
it is likely that a better refinement of the grain size in 
relation to the strength would be beneficial to the 
ductiliy. Further studies should also be carried out to 
determine whether material defects due to imperfect 
fabrication procedures are responsible for the poor 
ductilities. 
5. Conclusions 
An alloy based on Fe-40A! containing an yttria- 
based oxide particle dispersion, prepared by mechani- 
cally alloying and extruding, has been characterized 
and the mechanical properties examined both in the 
as-extruded state and after recrystallization to a large 
grain size. The extruded state has a fine grain and 
particle size (about l /~m and 20 nm) and is very strong 
with some ductility (yield stress about 900 MPa and 
ductility near 1%), while the recrystallized state has 
larger grains and particles (about 250/~m and 150 nm) 
and is much weaker and less ductile (yield stress about 
450 MPa and ductility near 0.5%). Above about 500 °C 
the strength of both materials falls sharply to low 
values, similar for both, above 800 °C. 
Analysis of the strengthening due to the particles, 
based on Orowan strengthening at room temperature 
and considering a threshold stress approach at high 
temperature, makes it possible to distinguish the 
strengthening contributions of particles, grain size and 
the matrix. The assumption that the threshold stress 
approach can be used is based on the high stress 
exponent of deformation rate found in the power-law 
equation, and the presence of many fine particles which 
clearly interact strongly with the dislocations. For the 
as-extruded material at room temperature, the particles, 
the fine grain size, and the matrix each contribute 
approximately the same amount to the strength; at 
room temperature the recrystailized material owes most 
of its hardening to the matrix, with minor contributions 
from the particles and the grain size. At high tempera- 
ture, the as-extruded material has a significant contri- 
bution of matrix hardening, significant particle 
hardening, and grain boundary weakening, whilst the 
recrystallized material is mostly hardened by matrix 
terms. 
The ductility appears to be significantly affected by 
the grain size, with large grained materials allowing 
brittle cleavage cracks to propagate, while fine grained 
materials may inhibit such cracking, allowing improved 
ductility and a ductile fracture behaviour. Part of the 
reason for the rather poor ductility observed, relative to 
previously reported values, seems to be the high mate- 
rial strength which ensures that high stresses operate 
during crack initiation and propagation.

The analysis above clearly shows that different mi- 
crostructures are required according to the temperature 
of interest. For high temperatures, it is better to have a 
coarser grain structure and fine particles; improved 
recrystallization behaviour over that achieved here, 
whereby coarser grains are obtained while retaining 
fairly fine particles, is desirable. For application at 
relatively low temperatures, the fine grained structure, 
probably stabilized during material fabrication by the 
many fine dispersoid particles, is the best state for good 
strength and ductility.
