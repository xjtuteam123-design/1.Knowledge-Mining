Abstract 
During compression testing of NiAl + Zr alloys at 1200 K and a strain rate of 2.1 X 10m5 SC’, strength was found to 
depend on time at temperature prior to testing. The observed strength loss appears to provide additional evidence that creep 
in Zr-modified NiAl is controlled by Zr atmospheres surrounding gliding dislocations. 
Keywords: Intermetallic; NiAI; NiAl + Zr; High temperature strength; Creep; Annealing 
1. Introduction 
During a study [I] to measure the effect of small 
Zr additions on the elevated temperature mechanical 
properties of the B2 intermetallic NiAl, normal be- 
havior was found except for one particular set of test 
conditions. An example of this paradox is illustrated 
in Fig. 1 by the flow stress-strain rate-temperature 
data for the single phase alloy NW-O.OSZr (at%). 
Over a temperature range from 1000 to 1300 K and a 
stress range from 20 to 400 MPa (Fig. la), the 
deformation rate of NiAl-0.05Zr is monotonic, inde- 
pendent of test procedure and falls into two regimes: 
a power law at low strain rate/high temperature 
conditions and a exponential law under fast strain 
rate/low temperature conditions 111. However, test- 
ing NiAl-O.OSZr at 1200 K-2 X 10P5 s-l seemed 
to defy this pattern (Fig. lb), where the flow stress

3. Results and discussion 
The true stress-strain curves for binary NiAl and 
NiAl + Zr alloys tested at 1200 K under constant 
velocity compression are presented in Fig. 3 as a 
function of prior annealing time. As is clearly 
demonstrated in Fig. 3a, heat treatment at 1200 K 
has no practical effect on the subsequent mechanical 
properties of the binary NiAl. This is not the case for 
the Zr-containing alloys (Fig. 3b-3e), where prior 
annealing produces changes in strength and deforma- 
tion behavior. For example, the strength of all four 
Zr-modified alloys decreases with increasing anneal- 
ing time (Fig. 3b-3e), and comparison of the com- 
pressive properties after 0.1 h and approximately 16 
h of annealing prior to testing indicates that the 
strength losses are substantial: reductions of = 70 
MPa in the single phase alloys (Fig. 3b, 3~) and 
1 lO+ MPa in the two phase materials (Fig. 3d, 3e).

Furthermore, the prominent yield point in the two 
phase alloys (Fig. 3d, 3e) disappears after prolonged 
heat treatment, and the stress-strain diagrams then 
simply consist of a work hardening stage followed 
by continuous flow at a more or less constant stress. 
The stress-strain diagrams for the single phase also 
display similar behavior as a function of time at 
1200 K (Fig. 3b, 3~1, except the initial yield points 
are much less pronounced. 
Light optical metallographic examination of se- 
lected samples after compression testing or simple 
thermal exposure did not reveal any perceptible dif- 
ferences among these conditions nor any differences 
from the as-received state for any of the five alloys. 
Specifically no changes in grain size from those 
noted in Table 1 were seen, no optically visible 
precipitates were formed in NiAl, NiAl-O.OSZr or 
NiAl-O.lZr, and precipitates were still clearly visi- 
ble in NiAl-0.3Zr and NiAl-0.7Zr. 
Strengthening of NiAl by Zr appears to be primar- 
ily due to solid solution effects rather than second 
phases [ 1,9-l 11. This can be seen in Fig. 3, where 
the addition of 0.05Zr (Fig. 3b) promotes a six fold 
increase in deformation resistance over that of pure 
NiAl (Fig. 3a), while the appearance of Heusler 
precipitates in the 0.3 and 0.7 at% Zr alloys (Fig. 3d, 
3e1, at best, improves the strength by 50% over that 
of the 0.05Zr material (Fig. 3b). It has been recently 
proposed [ 1 I that the elevated temperature strength of 
NiAl + 0.05/0.1Zr alloys can be described by the 
Cottrell-Jaswon model [ 121, where Zr atmospheres 
surround gliding dislocations. If this is the case, then 
strength of NiAl-Zr alloys would be dependent on 
the amount of Zr in solution. Therefore a reduction 
in the solid solution Zr through precipitation of a 
Zr-rich second phase (or growth of existing precipi- 
tates) during thermal exposure and/or testing would 
weaken the NiAl + Zr alloys. Furthermore, if contin- 
ued growth of such precipitates was diffusion con- 
trolled, then (1) time dependent weakening should 
take place, (2) strength as a function of time should 
approach some asymptotic level because of a small, 
but finite solid solubility limit at 1200 K and (3) the 
final strength levels of all the alloys should be nearly 
the same. 
Clearly time dependent weakening is seen in Fig. 
3b-3e, and an asymptotic limit appears to exist for 
the 0.7Zr alloy (Fig. 3e), where the strength after 
= 64 h at 1200 K is only slightly less than that after 
= 16 h at that temperature. The existing data are 
insufficient to determine whether or not all the alloys 
have the same strength after long term 1200 K 
exposures; however, the results in Fig. 3f do indicate 
that after = 16 h at 1200 K the strengths of all four 
Zr-modified alloys lie within a band about 30 MPa 
wide centered at approximately 200 MPa. In compar- 
ison the strengths of these materials after 0.1 h 
exposure at 1200 K range from 230 to 350 MPa (Fig. 
3b-e). 
The present results support the concept that pre- 
cipitation is occurring during 1200 K exposure of 
NiAl + Zr alloys. Unfortunately, the current light 
optical microstructural information is unable to con- 
firm this hypothesis; however, evidence does exist 
that NiAl-O.lZr will form precipitates after testing 
at 1000 K [ 131 and that the solubility of Zr at 1073 K 
is less than 0.1% in NiAl + MO alloys [l 11. Addi- 
tionally, a very recent field ion microscopy study of 
a NiAl-0.7Mo-0.4Zr alloy by Jayaram and Miller 
[14] reported that prolonged 673 K annealing re- 
sulted in nanometer sized Zr-rich regions and a 
matrix solid solubility limit of 0.007 at% Zr. Al- 
though they did not find any nanometer sized regions 
among the larger, presumably Heuslar precipitates in 
this alloy after it was heat treated for 1 h at 1273 K, 
Jayaram and Miller [14] also contended that the Zr 
solid solubility limit was less than 0.01 at%. If 
exposure at 1200 K results in approximately the 
same solubility limit, then precipitation or growth of 
Zr-rich phases had to occur in all the four ternary 
alloys in this study.
