ABSTRACT
Alloys containing a dispersion of fine, stable oxides can develop useful creep strength to 90 
percent of their melting temperature, providing a capability of 100 to 150°C or more over 
conventional high-temperature alloys. Such oxide dispersion-strengthened alloys allow other 
alloying additions to be optimized to provide superior oxidation resistance. Activities to 
develop such alloys for severe high-temperature applications are described, together with 
opportunities for further understanding of the development and growth of protective oxide 
scales of chromia and alumina. In particular, the use of oxides such as ThO2 and Y2O3 as 
dispersd phases resulted in the generation of important observations of the ‘reactive element 
effect’ in high-temperature oxidation. By analogy to the separation of the requirements for 
alloy strengthening and environmental resistance by insertion of a dispersion of oxide parti -
cles, examples are provided of other issues that can be addressed by various dispersed phases, 
without compromising existing alloy properties.

Introduction
The need for materials capable of operating at increas -
ingly higher temperatures has long been a driver of 
innovation to overcome limitations of those materials 
currently available. An early recourse was to attempt 
to use metals with inherently high melting tempera -
tures (such as tungsten, MPt. 3410°C), and the need 
for improved creep strength at the very high operating 
temperatures led to the discovery of the effectiveness 
of incorporating particles of stable phases such as 
oxides [ 1 ]. Since the standard process for producing 
W wires (for lighting filaments for instance) started 
with powder processing, incorporation of such dis -
persed phases was a natural development.
Typical wrought austenitic steels and Ni-based 
alloys typically are strengthened by elements such as 
Ni, Co, Mo, and W in solid solution and by precipi -
tated carbides (including Cr, Nb, V, Zr, B), and have 
useful creep strength to about 650°C [ 2 ]. Oxidation 
resistance from the formation of chromia scales is 
acceptable to around 850°C, when loss of the protec -
tive scale through exfoliation as well as evaporation 
when exposed to flowing gases becomes problematical, 
especially where water vapour is present [ 3 ]. 
Improvements in the strength-temperature character -
istics of wrought Ni-based alloys by, for instance, pre -
cipitation of a dispersion of Ni
3
(Al,Ti) (gamma prime) 
particles raise their applicability to possibly 800°C [ 2 ]. 
However, since all such precipitates are viable over a 
finite temperature range, from where the diffusion 
rates of the constituents (Al, Ti) are too slow for the 
desired precipitates to form to that at which the pre -
cipitates become unstable and the constituent ele -
ments redissolve into the alloy matrix, all such 
precipitate-strengthened alloys have a specific tem -
perature range over which the desired properties are 
available.
Creep strength to 1100–1150°C can be obtained by 
extreme control of the microstructure of Ni-based 
alloys through directional-solidification (DS) or 
growth of single-crystals (SX) [ 4 ]. A major issue here 
is that such refinements typically are limited to alloys 
in the cast form. Adherent alumina scales needed for 
environmental resistance at these higher temperatures 
lead to added complications of accommodating in the 
alloy sufficient aluminium (and chromium) to reliably 
form such scales, while allowing freedom to optimise 
the composition of DS and SX alloys [ 4 ]. This problem 
is overcome by using an external coating to provide 
the required reservoir of aluminium and chromium. 
For the most severe applications (combustion condi -
tions in advanced gas turbines), the use of DS/SX 
alloys typically is combined with internal cooling of 
the component, as well as the application of external 
ceramic coatings (thermal barriers, or ‘TBCs’) to limit 
their temperature. In such cases the metallic coating 
providing the aluminium-chromium reservoir scale 
also is used to secure the TBC to the alloy; the physical 
structure of such ‘bond coatings’ then becomes an 
important consideration [ 5 ].

While the approach of separating the composi -
tional requirements for strengthening and environ -
mental resistance through the use of a dedicated 
protective external coating is viable for specific, 
high-value components used in gas turbine engines 
that are subject to rigorous inspection, it is not very 
practicable for most other applications. An alterna -
tive is to make use of a dispersion of thermodyna -
mically-stable ‘foreign’ particles (i.e. not derived 
from the base alloy) to provide the equivalent of 
precipitation strengthening, thus gaining some free -
dom to adjust the composition of the alloy to 
optimise environmental resistance. The effective -
ness of a dispersion of a small volume fraction of 
fine particles of a stable oxide such as ThO
2 
to 
provide creep strength at very high temperatures 
was demonstrated more than 100 years ago in 
various alloys systems associated with lighting fila -
ments [ 6 ]. By the early 1960s fabrication of a high 
creep strength version of a standard heater alloy, 
Ni-20Cr, as thin sheets and foils had been demon -
strated on a commercial scale [ 7 ], and was soon 
extended to a version capable for forming an alu -
mina scale [ 8–10 ].
These developments paved the way not only for 
incorporating high-temperature strength and environ -
mental resistance into a given alloy, but also suggested 
possible expansion of the concept to use ‘inserted’ 
dispersed phases to provide specific functions, as a 
means of addressing other issues without necessarily 
compromising existing alloy properties. Examples are 
mentioned in the following text of using dispersed 
phases as ‘reservoirs’ of protective scale-forming ele -
ments that otherwise could not be incorporated into 
the alloy, and as ‘sinks’ for trapping elements entering 
the alloy surface from the environment that would be 
detrimental to its properties.
Technical challenges to the practical realisation of 
such changes in alloys that readily can be produced and 
fabricated obviously will arise. Nevertheless, demon -
stration of the ability to achieve exceptional or unique 
properties on a small scale is a necessary step in defin -
ing where innovation is needed for progress towards 
practical viability. Developments in recent years in 
additive manufacturing technologies and collaboration 
among different disciplines would appear to provide a 
promising means of advancement in this area.
Early drivers
Lighting filaments
Probably the earliest example of oxide-dispersion 
strengthening is that of tungsten wire with a disper -
sion of thoria particles [ 1 ]. From around 1905 
research at the General Electric Company in 
Schenectady, New York, to develop materials for 
light bulb filaments capable of improved perfor -
mance compared to Edison’s carbon filaments led to 
interest in producing fine wires of metals capable of 
high-temperature operation [ 6 ]. Successful produc -
tion of tungsten wire eventually was achieved using 
sol-gel processing to produce powder that was con -
solidated into wire using multiple working steps. 
Filaments made from these wires proved acceptably 
durable for stationary lighting applications and by 
1916 they predominated in the US. Further improve -
ments to reduce degradation by creep (‘sagging’) and 
grain growth of the tungsten wires were realised by 
incorporating a dispersion of 0.75 to 1 % of thoria 
particles (alloy compositions in weight percent, 
unless stated otherwise). Parallel efforts explored 
incorporation of other stable oxides such as zirconia, 
yttria and lanthana by sol-gel processing, and by a 
variety of other powder techniques, including inter -
nal oxidation of powdered W intermetallics, or 
vapour deposition onto W particles, all followed by 
sintering. Consolidation typically involved high-tem -
perature swaging [ 1 , 11–13 ].
The discovery that dispersion-strengthening 
improved the high-temperature strength of W and 
lowered its ductile to brittle transition temperature 
(DBTT) led to exploration of its use in highly-specia -
lised structures, such as components for hypersonic 
flight. On a more mundane level, an oxide dispersion -
strengthened (ODS) version of W (W-2ThO
2
) gained 
significant application as welding electrodes because 
of their minimal wear due to melting and erosion, 
despite safety concerns about release of airborne 
radioactivity.
Re-entry heat shields
One of the critical materials issues in the initial design 
of NASA’s space shuttle was the selection of material 
for the thermal protection system (TPS) required on 
its main underside surface. The design life of the TPS 
(without repair) was set at 100 missions, experiencing 
exposure to temperatures up to 1200°C for 30 minute 
at each re-entry [ 14 ]. Both active and passive cooling 
systems were considered. Active cooling was expected 
to require the use of a superalloy skin structure incor -
porating a liquid coolant system, as suggested in 
Figure 1 [ 15 ]. The projected stress and temperature 
capabilities required in an active TPS led to specifica -
tions for materials properties then found only in 
superalloys. The main candidates for passive cooling 
were based on ceramic insulating materials, for which 
extensive R&D efforts also were under way at the time. 
A further major consideration was the maximum 
allowable weight of the TPS, which favoured porous 
ceramics.

A further consideration was that the emissivity of 
the surfaces of thermally-grown alumina scales (as 
measured after arc jet testing) was significantly lower 
than that of thermally-grown chromia scales– 
0.70 ± 0.05 for TD-NiCrAl compared to 0.85 ± 0.05

surfaces subjected to peak heating from the dynamic 
oxidation conditions experienced during re-entry 
have the highest possible emittance, and for the cata -
lytic activity of those surfaces to be as low as possible 
to minimise recombination of dissociated oxygen and 
nitrogen present in the boundary layer of the TPS and 
hence minimise the surface temperature along the 
lower centerline of the vehicle [ 45 ]. While vapour- 
deposited coatings such as aluminosilicates were 
found to have a notable beneficial effect on the cataly -
tic activity of alumina scales (as grown on alloy 
Inconel MA956), reducing the equilibrium surface 
temperature by 316°C compared to chromia scale- 
forming Inconel 617, such coatings did not improve 
emissivities.
An attempt to increase the emissivity of thermally- 
grown alumina scales on DS-NiCrAl by doping with 
Mn, Fe and Co proved only partially successful [ 46 ]. 
These elements were expected to be present in the 
initially-formed (‘transient’) oxide layer that typically 
resides on the outside of the main alumina scale. Trials 
of a pack-diffusion process using master alloys of Ni-

possibly a small reduction in rate compared to the

Despite various demonstrations of the ability of 
ODS-NiCrAl alloys to meet the mechanical property 
and oxidation resistance criteria set for the TPS [ 14 ], 
the lack of a routine technique for reliably joining or 
repairing large sheets of ODS alloys, the increased 
weight of active TPS approaches (including pumps 
and plumbing) compared to various ceramic insula -
tion systems under development, and the marginal 
emissivity values of the protective scales led to the 
abandonment of the proposed active TPS approach. 
The resulting main passive TPS adopted for the space 
shuttle was a reusable ceramic surface insulation sys -
tem based on tiles made from silica (MPt 1610°C) or a 
C-C composite (2000°C max. with an oxidation-pro -
tective coating), depending on the expected surface 
temperature [ 15 ]. While problems including suscept -
ibility to physical damage and permeability to water 
were experienced with these systems, routine post- 
flight refurbishment and management activities were 
developed to enable their continued use.
FeCrAl heating elements and filaments
An alumina-forming Fe-based alloy, Kanthal A, 
was developed in the 1920s and introduced to 
the market in the 1930s as an upgrade to existing 
Nichrome (Ni-20Cr) and ODS-W wires for heat -
ing elements, with the advantage of being capable 
of reliable use at temperatures up to 1350°C. 
Further developments to increase high-tempera -
ture strength and reduce sagging (creep) led to a 
rapid-solidification powder-metallurgical route to 
introduce a fine dispersion of second-phase parti -

FeCrAl-RE nuclear fuel elements
During the 1960s extensive investigations were made 
of FeCrAl alloys containing reactive element addi -
tions such as Y, ranging from Fe-5Cr-6Al-1Y to Fe- 
25Cr-4Al-1Y (‘FeCrAl-RE alloys’), in the Fuels and 
Materials Development Programmes of the US 
Atomic Energy Commission conducted by the 
General Electric Company [ 48 ]. However, around 
1968 interest in these alloys was downgraded in 
favour of austenitic steels and Cr-base alloys due to 
deficiencies in high-temperature mechanical proper -
ties, low-temperature embrittlement, and concerns 
over diffusivity of U through a FeCrAl cladding 
[ 48 ]. In recent years incidences of accidental over -
heating of nuclear fuel cladding have led to a resur -
gence in interest in FeCrAl-RE (and ODS-FeCrAl) 
alloys for nuclear applications because of their super -
ior inherent resistance to high-temperature oxida -
tion, hydro-thermal corrosion, stress-corrosion 
cracking, and radiation-induced swelling compared 
to those of nuclear-grade materials such as Zr-based 
alloys and austenitic stainless steels [ 49 ]. Recent 
focus has been on ODS-FeCr and ODS-FeCrAl alloys 
with somewhat lower Cr levels [ 50 , 51 ].

Dispersed-reservoir (DR) alloys
The basis of this approach is the reduction of the role 
of the substrate alloy in the protective scale-formation 
process, allowing it to be optimised for strengthening, 
that is, the opposite to that used in ODS alloys. The 
ability to choose which protective scale is formed (e.g. 
chromia, alumina, or silica) is based on incorporation 
of a ‘reservoir’ of the scale-forming element in the 
form of a dispersed phase. If the DR concept were 
applied to a coating, the coating could have the same 
basic composition as the substrate alloy with the addi -
tion of the reservoir phase, so that interdiffusion 
effects and property differences (such as CTE) between 
alloy and coating would be minimised. There are 
requirements of the reservoir phase that will change 
with different alloy bases; an obvious consideration is 
that any constituent of the DR phase that does not play 
a part in the formation of a protective oxide layer 
should not lead to detrimental interactions with either 
the substrate or the oxide scale.

some ceramics from high-temperature oxidation, and

‘dispersed reservoirs’ can be incorporated in sufficient

continuous layer.

Realising the potential of dispersed phases in 
PM-produced alloys
This class of materials is capable of exceptional char -
acteristics that hold the potential for implementing 
step changes in performance of critical components, 
particularly in applications where the ability to handle 
high temperatures is an absolute requirement. In light 
of increasing concerns about fuel availability and 
security, economics, and limits on CO
2 
emissions, 
the desirability of realising these property improve -
ments is obvious, especially in industries based on 
Carnot or Rankin cycles where increased efficiency 
equates directly with higher process temperatures.
Given the current state of foundational knowledge 
of the properties and behaviour of such alloys, a pru -
dent focus of further research would be the compre -
hensive addressing of one of the major perceived 
limitations of alloys based on powder metallurgy, 
that is, the high cost of the overall manufacturing 
process. Several avenues appear possible. While 
emphasis is placed here on ODS alloys, application 
of the techniques mentioned for incorporating non- 
oxide particles for implementing DR and ODD 
approaches appears quite feasible.

and C from prolonged contact with the cover gas and 
the internals of the mill, as well as costs inherent in 
batch processing [ 152 , 153 ]. Even though these 
changes require significant revisions in mill design, 
the presumed increase in first cost is offset by 
improvements in control of the final product.
In addition, approaches that avoid a mechanical 
alloying step have been examined as means of not 
only avoiding long powder processing times, or the 
handling powder at all, but providing better control 
over the microstructure of ODS alloys [ 154 ]. For 
instance, starting with a consolidated form of an 
alloy that contains additions of reactive element(s), 
an internal dispersion of oxides of REO can be pro -
duced by internal oxidation in a controlled environ -
ment, as demonstrated with a Co-15Cr-RE alloy [ 155 ], 
with differences in the solubilities of the additions 
providing additional interest. Some level of control 
of the size and spacing of such REO precipitates can 
be exerted by selection of variables such as the oxygen 
partial pressure and temperature of the oxidising 
environment.
Alternatively, starting with an RE-containing alloy 
in powder form, the oxygen required to form the 
desired REO may be provided by pre-oxidation of 
the surface of the powder to form an oxide of the 
base alloy that readily will be reduced in contact with 
the RE content of the alloy during consolidation by 
HIP, for instance. RE-containing ODS-precursor pow -
der made by gas atomisation followed by in-situ oxi -
dation to form an oxide shell has been shown to 
develop REO particles on prior particle boundaries 
after HIP consolidation [ 156 ]. Since exceptionally 
fine microstructures can be achieved with the very 
high cooling rates possible with, for instance, gas ato -
misation and melt spinning, the spacing of the result -
ing REO particles can be quite uniform. Thermo- 
mechanical treatment to modify resulting equiaxed 
alloy grain structures also can result in a more uniform 
dispersion of the REO particles. Such approaches also 
have the potential for producing ODS powder at high, 
possibly continuous rates.
Consolidation/fabrication
Consolidation of ODS alloy powder produced by 
mechanical alloying typically involves canning, degas -
sing, extruding, de-canning and possibly further heat 
treatment to develop desired microstructures. The 
costs associated with these steps have prompted exam -
ination of other approaches, starting with ODS-alloy 
powder, and substantial progress has been made in 
know-how for producing tubes, sheet, and near net- 
shaped products [ 157 ]. In particular, procedures such 
as flow forming have been demonstrated for increas -
ing the hoop strength in creep of ODS-FeCrAl tubes 
by controlling or modifying the grain size and aspect 
ratio. Combination of elongated grains and a spiral/ 
twisted structure also has been demonstrated. 
Wrought billets, rods, or tubes can be formed directly 
by shear compaction, which has the potential for 
essentially continuous operation [ 158 ]. Selective laser 
melting has been shown capable of making complex 
shapes directly from ODS-FeCrAl powder (e.g. alloy 
PM2000) while retaining the oxide dispersion [ 157 ]. 
Shapes such as honeycombs, meshes, or coatings (also 
used as a route for self-canning) are possible with this 
technique. For components such as combustor cans 
for gas turbine engines, melt spinning has been used to 
impart tangential flow in the alloy to modify the cir -
cumferential grain structure, hence properties in Alloy 
PM2000 after secondary recrystallisation.
For alloys which rely on dispersed phases for devel -
opment of their particular properties, joining repre -
sents a challenge since changes in the size and 
distribution of those phases and associated alloy 
microstructure can severely compromise local proper -
ties. Since conventional fusion welding is therefore 
unacceptable, fabrication (and repair) of such alloys 
will require shop facilities. This is probably not a 
severe limitation, given that the components requiring 
the use of such alloys are likely to be very specialised 
and in most cases probably relatively small, with the 
exception of where ODS alloys are used as heat 
exchanger tubes, for instance. There are several non- 
fusion approaches that are viable for the successful 
joining of ODS FeCrAl alloys, for instance, especially 
where discontinuity in the alloy microstructure across 
the joint is acceptable [ 159–161 ]. Explosive bonding 
[ 64 ], as well as robust inertia-welding and flash-upset 
methods, have been demonstrated for butt joints 
between alloy MA956 tubes, and between MA956 
and Ni-base alloys (though not yet with precipita -
tion-strengthened alloys such as IN740) exhibiting 
≈90% of the matrix creep strength after post-joining 
secondary recrystallisation. Lap joints in recrystallised 
MA956 tubes made by magnetic pulse joining have 
demonstrated minimal disturbance of the alloy micro -
structure, and required no post-weld heat treat -
ment [ 159 ].
Joints made in 6 mm-thick plates of alloy Kanthal 
APMT by friction stir welding (FSW) have been 
shown to have microstructures that remain stable for 
>670 h at 750°C, and demonstrate similar creep rup -
ture properties to the base material to 750°C [ 160 ]. 
FSW of ODS-FeCrAl alloy PM2000 also has been 
shown to produce joints with excellent microstruc -
tures and properties after post-weld heat treatment. 
The finding that the grain structure of these welds can 
be modified by a small amount of further deformation

Additive manufacturing technologies
Additive manufacturing technologies and the general 
renaissance in powder metallurgy have led to re-exam -
ination of possibilities of bringing together disparate 
processing approaches such as indicated above as a 
way of realising new materials properties. This strategy 
is well suited to providing routes by which the poten -
tial of dispersed phases in PM-produced alloys can be 
realised and exploited. Recognition of new ways for -
ward is evident from efforts by several groups world -
wide [ 153 ], at present largely in pursuit of ways of 
improving control over specific properties of ODS- 
ferritic steels. This particular current interest in low- 
Cr ODS-FeCr and ODS-FeCrAl alloys is for applica -
tion in fuel cladding in fission reactors [ 51 ], and as 
structural elements in designs for next-generation fis -
sion reactors (GenIV) and fusion reactors [ 162–165 ] 
Experience of the successful application of the capabil -
ities of such cooperative ventures appears to bode well 
for other potential applications of dispersed phases in 
powder metallurgically-produced alloys.
