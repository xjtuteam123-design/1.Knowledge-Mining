ABSTRACT 
We have investigated the strengthening mechanisms of as-cast CrMoNbTiW BCC refractory high entropy alloy 
(RHEA). The cast RHEA was deformed at a constant strain rate of 10
 3 
s
 1 
and a temperature range of 
1100 – 1300 
◦
C. The various factors contributing to the overall strength of the cast alloy are explored with 
comprehensive experimental evidence. In as-cast RHEA, solid solution strengthening is the dominant mechanism 
among other factors. A modified Varvenne ’ s solid solution strengthening model is used to predict the yield 
strength (YS) of the RHEA at high temperatures. The experimentally determined YS exhibits a strong temperature 
dependence, and the predicted YS values are significantly affected by the temperature-dependent material 
constants. The absolute value of the predicted YS depends on the shear modulus, whereas the variation of YS 
with temperature is affected by the Poisson ’ s ratio. Moreover, our study demonstrates that it is feasible to predict 
the high temperature YS using material constants from literature besides DFT studies. 
1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are multicomponent alloys composed of 
five or more elements with their composition ranging from 5 to 35 at.%. 
Predominantly, they form solid solutions having simple crystal struc -
tures of fcc/bcc [ 1 , 2 ]. The simpler structures of the phases observed in 
these HEAs are hypothesized due to entropy stabilization, while binary 
enthalpies contribute to some extent. However, besides simple solid 
solution phases, most of the HEAs reported so far exhibit precipitation or 
formation of secondary phases upon heat-treatment and during or after 
deformation. The formation of secondary phases is attributed to the 
local composition fluctuations/short-range order/clusters/presence of 
elements capable of forming intermetallic phases upon heating/th -
ermomechanical processing. Up to now, the number of HEA composi -
tions studied is enormously large [ 3 ]. However, the physics behind the 
strengthening of these HEAs is yet to be completely understood. The 
major strengthening in most of the cast HEAs is attributed to solid so -
lution strengthening, followed by secondary phase strengthening, 
Hall-Petch strengthening, ordering (B2/L1
2
), twinning, stacking faults, 
and so on. These contributions to the HEAs ’ overall yield strength (YS) 
are generally calculated only through empirical equations without 
thorough microstructural analysis. Among HEAs, refractory HEAs 
(RHEAs) exhibits strength retention at high-temperature, which is pri -
marily attributed to their solid solution strengthening (SSS) [ 4 ]. Recent 
studies focus on understanding the SSS of these HEAs [ 5 – 9 ]. The 
solute-dislocation interaction leads to SSS in conventional alloys, as 
proposed by Labusch [ 10 ] and Fleischer [ 11 ]. 
In HEAs, SSS originates from lattice distortion, which is given by 
atomic misfit and modulus misfit due to the presence of different ele -
ments. Based on Labusch theory, Toda-Caraballo [ 12 ] proposed a lattice 
distortion model for SSS. In contrast, Varvenne proposed a 
parameter-free model, which assumes an average lattice parameter, a 
singular shear modulus value, and stacking fault energy for the matrix in 
a random solid solution [ 13 ]. The recent viewpoint article on 
strengthening mechanisms in HEAs discusses the Labusch parameter 
and other factors affecting the SSS model [ 5 ]. Varvenne ’ s model applies 
to alloys, in which Hall-Petch contribution towards strengthening can be 
subtracted while considering only the chemical/alloying effect [ 14 ].To

studies are in agreement with Varvenne ’ s model, there is a discrepancy 
between the experimental and predicted YS values. Few critical points

predictions are lower than the experimentally observed YS values. 
Recently, Thiel et al. [ 9 ] and Hu and Yang [ 17 ] have pointed out some of 
these limitations, indicating the breakdown of Varvenne ’ s model. Be -
sides, the following issues have not been addressed with regard to SSS in 
HEAs. a) absence of detailed investigation predicting the variation of YS 
with temperature, b) why does the predicted YS values are lower than 
the experimental values? and c) why does the variation of predicted YS 
with temperature has a specific curvature? A critical analysis of these 
observations suggests that the model is sensitive to the value of material 
constants, especially the shear modulus ( μ ) of the alloy. In the previous 
studies, the material constants (lattice parameter, shear modulus, Pois -
son ’ s ratio, Burgers vector) were obtained from density functional the -
ory (DFT) calculations, Vegard ’ s law, and embedded atomic model 
(EAM) potentials [ 6 , 12 , 13 , 16 , 18 , 19 ]. However, the above-mentioned 
studies fail to predict the athermal region of the YS variation with 
temperature. The use of material constants from literature (depicting the 
athermal region) for YS prediction is a plausible approach, which we 
have explored in this study. Thus, it is worthwhile to investigate the 
effect of the values of these material constants on the model predictions, 
especially while predicting the YS at high temperatures. 
Here, the strengthening mechanisms in cast CrMoNbTiW RHEA are 
studied while considering different contributions to the overall strength 
of the alloy. The same composition synthesized using the powder met -
allurgy (PM) route has been investigated in the very recent reports. The 
CrMoNbTiW RHEA synthesized using PM route (mechanical alloying 
and consolidation), resulted in the formation of BCC solid solution, 
along with different secondary phases [ 20 – 24 ]. The formation of these 
secondary phases depend significantly on the synthesis condition. The 
high strength of the RHEA was attributed to the solid solution 
strengthening and second phase strengthening (carbides and oxides) 
[ 22 – 24 ]. It is important to note that studies on CrMoNbTiW RHEA, 
devoid of any contamination/secondary phases is not yet reported. 
Thus, it becomes important to study the deformation and various 
strengthening mechanism in pristine CrMoNbTiW RHEA. Moreover, to 
understand the solid solution strengthening, the modified Varvenne ’ s

2. Materials and methods 
The synthesis and characterization details of the as-cast CrMoNbTiW 
RHEA are described elsewhere [ 21 ]. Cylindrical samples (6 mm dia × 9 
mm height) for hot compression studies were cut from the as-cast and 
heat-treated (HT) alloy using wire electrical discharge machining. These 
samples were deformed at a constant strain rate of 10
 3 
s
 1 
over a 
temperature range of 1100 – 1300 
◦
C using Gleeble 3800 (thermal, me -
chanical, physical simulator in hydra wedge module). The samples were 
heated to the test temperature by resistive heating at a heating rate of 10 
K min
 1 
and soaked for 120 s before deformation. The test temperature 
was monitored using an S-type thermocouple spot welded on the spec -
imen. Nickel paste and tantalum foil (~ 0.2 mm thick) assembly were 
placed between the sample and WC anvils to reduce friction. Immedi -
ately after deformation, the samples were water quenched in-situ to 
retain the post deformed microstructure. 
Detailed pre-and-post-deformation microstructural characterization 
of the alloy was done using an FEI-INSPECT-F (Thermo Fisher Scientific 
Inc., Oregon, USA) scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with 
electron dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD). The BSE-SEM micrographs (~10) were used to 
obtain the volume fraction of phases using FIJI image analysis software. 
A step size of 0.5 μ m was used for the EBSD measurements using the TSL- 
OIM data acquisition and analysis system. The grain size was calculated 
using the mean linear intercept method. The room temperature nano -
indentation (NI) experiments were carried out on a Hysitron TI 950 
TriboIndenter (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) with 
a cono spherical diamond indenter to obtain the individual phase/region 
hardness. Accelerated property mapping (XPM) was acquired from a 
matrix of 15 × 15 indents (1 μ m spacing between consecutive indents). 
Tests were performed under constant load mode with a peak load of 
5000 μ N (0.1 s loading, 0.1 s holding, and 0.1 s unloading). The overall 
hardness of the alloy presented is based on the average of the readings 
from 15 × 15 indents. The site-specific electron transparent TEM lamella 
was prepared using focused Ga ion beams with the help of FIB-SEM 
(Dual Beam Focused Ion Beam Scanning Electron Microscopy Helios 
G4 UX). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using 
TECNAI G2 T20 TEM operated at 200 kV (FEI, The Netherlands). 
3. Results 
3.1. High temperature deformation behavior 
The as-cast RHEA consists of a single-phase BCC solid solution and 
exhibits a dendritic microstructure, as shown in Fig. 1 . The bright region 
is rich in W, the grey region is rich in Mo, Nb, Cr, and the black region is 
rich in Ti. The segregation is due to the difference in their melting point. 
The IPF map shows random orientation, and the alloy has an average 
grain size of 34.5 ± 3.5 μ m. The color gradient observed in the IPF map 
is a feature of the as-cast RHEA due to the segregation of elements, 
consistent with the literature and not due to sample polishing [ 29 ]. 
Fig. 2 illustrates true stress – true strain curves of the as-cast RHEA at 
high temperature (1100 – 1300 
◦

◦

as-cast RHEA failed in a brittle fracture fashion at T < 1100

flow stress decreases with an increase in temperature and exhibits a 10% 
elongation at 1300

(600 MPa) is greater than that of HfMoNbTaTiZr (550 MPa) and MoN -

3.3. Nanoindentation studies 
The contribution of different regions/phases to the RHEA ’ s overall 
strength can be obtained from the room temperature nanoindentation 
studies. The room temperature XPM-Hardness map and reduced 
modulus map for the as-cast alloy and deformed alloy (at 1200 
◦

shown in Fig. 6 (a) and (b), respectively. The scanning probe microscopy 
images of the cast and deformed sample before and after NI are shown in 
Fig. S3 of Supplementary data (displaying the surface smoothness of the 
sample). The hardness values of different regions/phases are obtained 
by correlating their individual profiles with the matrix and are given in 
Table 1 . It can be seen that the bright and grey regions have similar 
hardness (11 GPa), whereas the interdendritic Ti-rich region (dark) has 
the highest hardness (16.6 GPa). The deformed specimen showed a 
decrease in the hardness values of the bright and grey region (9.8 GPa) 
with an increase in hardness of the Ti-rich region (23.7 GPa). The 
Young ’ s modulus of the as-cast and deformed RHEA estimated to be ~ 
215 ± 15 and 186 ± 20 GPa, respectively, utilizing the Poisson ’ s ratio of 
0.27 obtained from synchrotron data (Supplementary data, Fig. S4 ). 
Although the Laves phase has higher hardness than bright and grey re -
gions, there is no significant change in the alloy ’ s overall hardness after 
deformation ( Table 1 ). 
Thus, such negligible change in hardness even after precipitation of 
the Laves phase confirms SSS from the chemical/alloying effect as the 
major contribution to RHEA ’ s overall strength [ 14 , 35 ]. This is further 
confirmed by calculating the contribution of Laves phase towards the 
cast RHEA ’ s overall strength. The as-cast sample deformed at 1200 
◦
C 
was subjected to TEM analysis to examine the presence of dislocation 
activity and the development of subgrain structure. The TEM-BF 
micrograph showing the presence of Ti-rich interdendritic region, 
Laves phase, and BCC phase are illustrated in Fig. 7 . We can observe 
some dislocation activity inside BCC grain and twins within the Ti-rich 
interdendritic region. When imaged at near two-beam condition, sub -
grain formation and signature of significant dislocation glide are seen in 
the BCC grain. The subgrain formation indicates the occurrence of dy -
namic recovery during deformation. Fig. 8 is a representative image 
revealing dislocation loops and dipoles within the BCC grain. The dis -
locations observed in Fig. 8 have been utilized to estimate the contri -
bution of dislocation strengthening to the cast RHEA ’ s overall observed 
strength.

3. The various factors contributing to the overall strength of the cast 
RHEA are discussed while comparing with experimental observa -
tions. The solid solution strengthening mechanism is the predomi -
nant strengthening mechanism of the cast RHEA at high 
temperatures among various strengthening mechanisms. 
4. Maresca ’ s solid solution strengthening model with experimental 
values for material constants has been used to predict the yield 
strength of the RHEA at high temperatures. The elastic constants ( μ 
and ν ), mainly the shear modulus ( μ ) and its error, significantly affect 
the absolute values of predicted yield strength. 
5. The values of each strengthening mechanism depend strongly on the 
absolute values of the material ’ s constant ( μ , ν , and b ) at the test

constants, which affects yield strength, is crucial for theoretical 
estimates.
