Corrosion behavior of Ni-base superalloys at 1373 K 
in simulated HTGR impure helium gas environment 
Isao Mutoh, Yuji Nakasone, Keijiro Hiraga and Tatsuhiko Tanabe 
National Research Institute for MetaLs, Tsukuba, Sengen I-2, Tsukuba-shi, Ibaraki-ken 305, Japan 
Received 19 January 1993; accepted 24 July 1993 
The present paper describes the corrosion behavior of recently developed Ni-base superalloys: an oxide dispersion 
strengthened (ODS) alloy, two kinds of Ni-Cr-W alloys and Hastelloy XR at 1373 K in a simulated HTGR impure helium 
gas. Corrosion tests were carried out using a transparent quartz retort at 1373 K for up to 3.6 MS in circulating helium gas 
which contained a small amount of Hz, CH,, CO, CO, and HzO. The mass change versus exposure time relation revealed 
that the ODS alloy exhibited excellent oxidation resistance. One Ni-Cr-W alloy (113MA) and Hastelloy XR showed 
exfoliation of the oxide films after the tests. All the alloys were decarburized from the early exposure times. These results 
are discussed on the basis of phase stability diagrams for constituent elements in the alloys. 
1. Introduction 
In order to utilize the nuclear process heat of a high 
temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) effectively, it 
is desirable that the outlet temperature of the primary 
coolant (helium) from the reactor should be higher. 
The Japan Atomic Energy Research Institute (JAERI) 
is now constructing a high temperature engineering 
test reactor (HTTR) with outlet coolant temperatures 
of 1123-1223 K and is also developing a long-term 
target alloy (Ni-Cr-W alloy) as a component material 
at service temperatures around 1273 K [l]. 
When the service temperature of the HTGR reaches 
around 1273 K, the behavior of the materials used for 
high-temperature structural components at around 
1373 K in the coolant must be taken into account. 
In this paper we investigate the corrosion behavior 
of recently developed heat-resistant alloys (Ni-base su- 
peralloys) at 1373 K in a simulated HTGR helium gas 
environment to examine their compatibility with the 
environment. The results are discussed on the basis of 
phase stability diagrams for the constituent elements of 
the alloys. 
2. Experimental procedures 
2.1. Materials 
The materials used in this study were two kinds of 
recently developed wroughtable Ni-base superalloys: 
an oxide dispersion strengthened superalloy CODS), 
MA754, and a Ni-Cr-W alloy, 1408. As references for 
the alloys, other Ni-base superalloys, Hastelloy XR [2], 
which is a modified version of the conventional Hastel- 
loy alloy X and is used for high temperature structural 
components of the HTTR, and 113MA [3], which is 
one of the best Ni-Cr-W superalloys developed in the 
R & D of the nuclear steelmaking project conducted 
by the Ministry of Trade and Industry, were also used. 
Alloy 1408 was developed at the National Research 
Institute for Metals (NRIM). The alloy is strengthened 
by the precipitation of fine M,,C, carbides and a-W 
(W-rich bee solid phase), and has good creep resistance 
at 1273 K [4].

2.2. Corrosion test 
Corrosion tests were carried out using a transparent 
quartz retort at 1373 K for up to 3.6 MS (1000 h) in a 
simulated HTGR impure He gas (He-2’1, the impurity 
contents of which are shown in table 2. The gas was 
supplied using a recirculating loop system with purifi- 
cation units and impurity gas addition equipment. The 
average heating rate was 357 K/h and the test temper- 
ature was attained within 3 h from the commencement 
of heating. After the test, the specimens were furnace 
cooled, whereby the temperature dropped to 950 K 
within 1 h, to 530 K within the next 4.5 h and to 300 K 
after 22 h. The flow rate of the gas was 26.5 
ml/cm2/min and its pressure in the retort was 0.137 
MPa. Each specimen was placed in one quartz tube, 
the tubes were bundled then put in the center of the 
retort. 
2.3. Analysis of the corroded specimens 
The specimens were metallurgically and gravimetri- 
tally analyzed. Bulk carbon analysis was conducted 
using a LECO IR12 carbon analyzer. Scanning elec- 
tron microscopy (SEMI, X-ray microanalysis (XMA) 
and X-ray diffraction were also used to observe and 
identify the corrosion products.

were taken into consideration. The oxidation potential

One of the features of the Ni-Cr-W alloys treated 
here is the existence of a-W precipitates. The W-rich 
precipitates shown in figs. 4 and 5 correspond to these. 
They are neither WC nor W oxide. This can also be 
seen from the phase stability diagram shown in fig. 11; 
the oxidation potential and carbon activity of the envi- 
ronment demand the existence of metallic W. So, When 
a-W precipitates along the surface grain boundary, or 
in the grain near the surface, regions are left unoxi- 
dized in the process of corrosion. This can be seen in 
figs. 3, 4 and 5, where parts of the grain boundaries or 
parts of the grain interior are left unoxidized. In the 
latter case, o-W could exist in the oxide or at the 
matrix/ oxide interface. 
So far the contribution of evaporation of the materi- 
als from the specimen surface to the mass change has 
been neglected. Shindo and Kondo [8] took this into 
consideration and estimated the evaporative loss of Cr 
and Mn from Hastelloy XR corroded in JAERI type B 
helium gas in the temperature range from 1123 to 1323 
K. From their data, the evaporative loss of both ele- 
ments increases rapidly above 1273 K and the mass 
gain of Hastelloy XR corroded at 1323 K for 3.6 MS 
has a negative value in spite of the existence of oxide 
films on the surface. Therefore, the contribution of 
evaporation to the mass change was evaluated in the 
case of MA754 as follows. Firstly, the volume of oxide 
was estimated from EPMA data (fig. 7). The thickness 
of the oxide in MA754 is about 15 urn and assumed to 
be uniform throughout the surface. The specimen size 
was 2.7 X 1.25 X 0.5 cm’. The volume of the oxide was 
0.016 cm3. If the oxide is assumed to be pure Cr,O, 
with density 5.21 g/cm3, oxygen pickup through oxide 
formation is 0.0263 g. This value is the net mass gain in 
the case of no evaporation. The obtained mass gain 
due to oxidation in this experiment was 0.0217 g and is 
almost comparable to the net mass gain. So, we con- 
clude that in MA754 evaporation of Cr is negligible. 
The slight discrepancy between experimental and cal- 
culated values may be due to the assumption of oxide 
thickness, its density, slight exfoliation of the oxide, the 
existence of internal oxidation or the oxidation of Ti. 
Other materials were similarly examined. In 1408, 
the oxygen pickup and the mass gain due to oxidation 
were 0.0277 and 0.0237 g, respectively. Also, the evapo- 
ration of Cr is negligible in 1408. 113MA has hardly 
any oxide films because of exfoliation. The oxide film 
shown in fig. 5 was found by accident. If about a 13 urn 
thick oxide film was exfoliated, then the mass loss due 
to exfoliation reaches 0.0438 g. The experimental mass 
loss was 0.0414 g. Again, the evaporation of Cr is 
negligible in 113MA. 
Although the imperfect exfoliation of the oxide 
films in Hastelloy XR made the above estimation diffi- 
cult, we could evaluate, from the same treatment men- 
tioned above, how far the exfoliation progressed. The 
mass gain due to oxidation would be 0.0128 g (mass of 
oxygen pickup in 0.04 g of Cr oxide with a thickness of 
17 urn (fig. 6)). The obtained mass loss after subtrac- 
tion for the decarburization effect was 0.00415 g. Then 
the real mass of exfoliated oxide would be 0.0128 + 
0.00415 = 0.01695 g. So 0.01695/0.04 x 100 = 42.3(%), 
i.e. about a half of the oxides, exfoliated. On the other 
hand, the evaporative loss of Cr (estimated value at 
1373 K from the data of Shindo and Kondo [S]) was 
0.039 g, about ten times larger than 0.00415 g. There- 
fore, in this alloy also, the evaporative loss of Cr could 
be negligible. Here, the evaporative loss of Mn was 
neglected because of its small mass in the alloy (lower 
than 1 mass%). 
At present we cannot understand why the alloy did 
not exhibit evaporative loss of materials in He-2’. One 
possible reason could be the difference of gas pressure 
between JAERI B type helium (0.098 MPa) and He-2’ 
(0.137 MPa); however, the difference is not so large. 
Further study of this alloy is needed. 
The,mass gain of MA754 was considerably smaller 
than that of 1408. The possible explanation for the 
superiority of MA754 to 1408 in corrosion resistance to 
oxidation might be that the former has a smaller 
amount of Cr (20 mass%) than the latter (26 mass%). 
In fact, in the low oxygen potential environment 
(JAERI type B helium) at 1273 K, the mass gain of 
Ni-Cr binary alloy depends on the Cr content; the 
mass gains of the alloys become larger with their Cr 
content [9]. The mass gains of the commercial base 
alloys under the same conditions also depend substan- 
tially on their Cr content, however they might be 
affected by the presence of minor active elements such 
as Al, Mn, rare earth elements, etc. [lo]. In the case of

5. Conclusion 
The corrosion behavior of recently developed heat- 
resistant alloys (Ni-base superalloys), i.e., oxide disper- 
sion strengthened alloy, Ni-Cr-W alloys and Hastelloy 
XR, was investigated at 1373 K for up to 3.6 MS in a 
simulated HTGR impure He gas environment. The 
results obtained were as follows: 
1) Severe exfoliation of the scales was observed in 
113MA, partial exfoliation occurred in Hastelloy XR, 
and a slight descaling was observed in the rest of the 
alloys. 
2) MA754 had the best oxidation resistance among 
the alloys. 
3) Severe decarburization occurred in all of the 
alloys. 
4) Oxidation and carburization or decarburization 
behaviors of the alloys were qualitatively understood 
from the stability diagram of the constituent elements 
of the alloys. 
5) In MA754 and Ni-Cr-W alloys treated here, 
evaporative loss of Cr could be ignored.
