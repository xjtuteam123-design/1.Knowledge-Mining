ABSTRACT
Alloy 718 is a high-strength, corrosion-resistant nickel chromium-based superalloy frequently used for appli-
cations, such as aerospace, marine, nuclear reactor and chemical industries, due to its outstanding inherent
properties such as high strength and corrosion resistance at high temperatures together with good creep be-
haviour.
Although, the use of conventional manufacturing processes is prevalent for their use on Alloy 718, alternative
manufacturing technologies are gaining importance. This work compares the eﬀ ects of alternative manu-
facturing processes, such as Abrasive water jet (AWJ), Wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM) and ul-
trasound vibration assisted milling (UVAM) with conventional milling during the manufacture of Alloy 718
parts. Surface integrity, hardness, residual stress and fatigue strength obtained from these machining processes
have been examined for cutting alloy 718. Results show that both residual stresses and surface roughness are
correlated with fatigue strength. UVAM results shown an improvement on the surface integrity of the ﬁnal
workpiece. AWJ and WEDM show poorer results, further work on post-process technologies or process condition
selection must be carry out to establish them as an alternative in Alloy 718 cutting operations.
1. Introduction
The demand of airplanes is expected to increase greatly during the
coming 20 years according to the Airbus Global Market Forecast [ 1].
This generates a notably growing necessity of producing turbo-ma-
chinery parts of high-temperature resistant materials. Superalloys, such
as titanium alloys and nickel based, are generally used in aerospace due
to their exclusive combination properties like being resistance to che-
mical deterioration, high strength at elevated temperatures and wear
resistance [ 2].
These alloys are commonly named as di ﬃcult-to-cut alloys because
of their capacity to preserve their properties at high temperatures
which acutely hinders their machinability. Heat generation due to the
high temperatures identi ﬁed with the deformation process and with
friction at tool-chip and tool-workpiece interfaces, makes machining
more di ﬃcult. Superalloys used in aerospace have other main char-
acteristics including their austenitic matrix which helps them harden
promptly; their capacity of reaction under atmospheric conditions with
tool materials; their reaction with tool materials under atmospheric
conditions; their propensity to weld to the cutting tools and to acquire
built-up edge form and their microstructure composed by abrasive
carbides as shown in [ 3]. Rapid tool wear and low material removal
rates entail high associated manufacturing costs. Due to the physical
and mechanical properties of these alloys its machining is distinguish
by low process stability and low productivity [ 2,4]. Very high me-
chanical and thermal tool loads occur while machining these alloys
resulting in long machining times and excessive tool wear and thus low
cutting speeds and so high manufacturing costs [ 5– 7].
Non-conventional machining methods such as Abrasive Water Jet
(AWJ) and wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM), do however
oﬀer great possibilities since these methods are not sensitive to the
workpiece material [ 8,9]. The extent of subsurface plastic deformation
that results from AWJ machining have been studied on di ﬀerent metal
materials on late twentieth century studies [ 10]. In [ 11], the in ﬂuence
of machining parameters on the surface integrity in electrical discharge
machining was addressed. More recent works have shown that these
diﬀerent non-conventional methods applied to the machining of nickel
based alloys, have also shown to generate di ﬀerent surface integrity

The ﬁnal surface properties after machining are critical since the
surface integrity strongly in ﬂuences the components fatigue strength

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Workpiece material and equipment
Strength at elevated temperatures and good corrosion resistance of
Alloy 718 make it the most commonly used age hardening nickel – base
superalloy [ 16]. Use of Alloy 718 often implies treating the material
(solution and aged conditions). Heat treatments are used for modifying
material properties such as: yield and tensile strength, hardness, grain
microstructure, fatigue and corrosion resistance as it is described on
[17].
Alloy 718 test specimens were taken from the same batch to ensure
chemical and metallurgical homogeneity. Chemical composition of
material is shown in Table 1.
Several pieces were cut from the original and heat treated, as it can
be seen Fig. 1, using full-precipitation hardening conditions.
Alloy 718 in SGA (small grain ageing state) a diameter between
15– 32 micrometers of mean grain size after heat treatment was
achieved ( Fig. 2), corresponding to number 7– 8 in ASTM scale. The
measured hardness of this material is 49 HRC.
Workpiece Surface roughness was measured using a vertical scan-
ning interferometry, Sensofar S-neox. Roughness measurements were
performed over a 659 x 878 μm surface in the center of the sample at
three di ﬀerent positions with a lateral resolution of 0.25 μm. Measured
data was ﬁltered using a spatial median with window size of 5 × 5
points to reduce short wavelength noise and a robust Gaussian ﬁlter
with a nesting index of 250 μm to remove longer wavelengths. Surface
hardness of tested materials has been measured with a diamond in-
denter using a portable tester, results have been expressed in the
Rockwell C scale.
X-ray di ﬀraction was applied to measure residual stresses with a
Stresstech G2R XStress 3000 di ﬀractometer equipped with a Mn X-ray
tube (wavelength: 0.21031 nm). A collimator (spot size) of 2 mm is
used. A sin
2ψ method with ﬁve psi angles from 45° to −45° and eval-
uated peak of di ﬀraction of 151.88° is used. Elastic strain theory is
assumed following Hook ’s law using Young ’s modulus equal to
199.9 GPa and Poisson ’s ratio equal to 0.29 [ 18]. Electro-polishing in
incremental steps is selected to obtain residual stress pro ﬁles.
A closed loop servo-hydraulic testing machine (Instron 8516) with a
dynamic load of plus/minus 100 kN has been used for mechanical fa-
tigue testing. A 4-point bending fatigue life analysis con ﬁguration has
been used, with a dedicated tooling developed with adjustable sup-
porting and loading points, to adapt the uniaxial fatigue machine is not
required. Just able to perform bending fatigue tests to specimens of
dimensions 6 × 6 x 100 mm for each cutting technology.
2.2. Experimental procedure and operating parameters
Specimens with dimension of 6*6*100 mm used for each cutting
technology were obtained using: i) a high-pressure tri-axial waterjet
machine (Byjet L2030) in AWJ machining, ii) a Charmilles Robo ﬁl 440
machine equipped with a CnZn50 coated brass wire electrode of
0.25 mm diameter for WEDM and iii) a ﬁve axis CNC machining center
DMG-Sauer DMU 125 P for conventional and ultrasonic milling. For
both milling tests a cemented carbide tool was selected. No special tools
with cutting edges are available in the market for UAM, so a Fraisa
P8720505 solid carbide S10 (geometry of the tool: shoulder end mill,
2 mm corner radius bull-nose type, helix angle 40° and primary rake
angle 5°).Cutting conditions for each technology are presented in

procedure could be seen on [ 14].

3.1. Roughness
Fig. 4 shows surface roughness maps of a specimen for each of all
cutting technologies employed. Surface roughness was measured at the
center of specimens at three di ﬀerent points. Arithmetic mean value
(Sa) and ten-point height parameter (S10z) were selected [ 19]. These
two parameters are similar to traditional Ra and Rz parameters when
extracting a pro ﬁle. As a result, Fig. 5, shows the comparison between
the measurement of the mean and standard deviation of workpiece
surface roughness (Sa, S10z).
Results show that UVAM has the smoothest surface with the lowest
Sa of 0.2 μm compared to conventional milled surface which had a Sa of
0.8 μm. Non-conventional methods showed the highest Sa values,
1.6 μm for AWJ surface and 3.4 μm for WEDM surface. Similar trends
could be observed for S10z parameter even though the standard de-
viation for this process is higher, indicating greater variation from in-
dividual peaks and valleys, especially for the WEDM surface.
UVAM workpieces appear to exhibit signi ﬁcant advantage on sur-
face roughness. Additionally, the possibility to sculpt micro-textures at
the external surface of the milling parts could be of highly interest,
further research on the correct selection of vibration parameters, tool
geometry and cutting conditioning for this purpose will be of interest.

3.3. Residual stress
X-ray di ﬀraction has been used to measure residual stresses. In this
case, residual stresses are a ﬀected due to sample ageing precipitates in
the lattice and due to grain size. Measured results show strong inﬂ uence
of microstructure alterations resulting in some cases in double di ﬀrac-
tion peaks or distorted peaks which have been ﬁnally discarded when
further evaluating residual stresses. The error bar in the results re-
presents the deviation from the di ﬀraction peak curve ﬁtting and in-
dicates when microstructure has a ﬀected the measurement.
Surface residual stresses are compressive for milled, AWJ and
UVAM surfaces while WEDM surface is in tensile residual stress state.
Both AWJ and WEDM surfaces show similar stress state across ( Fig. 8)
and along ( Fig. 9) the sample, while the two milled surfaces show a
diﬀerence in the two measured directions. This is most likely due to
how strain is built up during machining which di ﬀers between two
directions. This di ﬀerence between directions is approximately
300 MPa for UVAM surface and 700 MPA for milled surface.
Machining operations show great in ﬂuence on measured residual
stress proﬁles, as can be seen in Fig. 9. Tensile residual stress pro ﬁle for
WEDM surfaces di ﬀers from others. WEDM has a rather high surface
tensile stress state, which decreases slightly with depth. Results further
indicate that WEDM process has a great in ﬂuence on stress state with a
rather large depth of in ﬂuence.
AWJ sample has a compressive residual stress state in surface region
which is rather shallow. This relatively low penetration depth, of
50 μm, for AWJ could be the reason for the relatively low fatigue limit
of AWJ sample. Sample below this depth has a stress-free state.
Milled and UVAM samples show similar residual stress pro ﬁles
characterized by a high compressive residual stress state in surface

3.4. Four-point bending fatigue test
WEDM surface resulted to have the lowest fatigue life, followed by
AWJ. Milled surfaces showed much better performance ( Fig. 10) where
UVAM resulted the highest fatigue strength, as predicted before due to
its higher hardness. As it was expected surface topography has great
inﬂuence on fatigue strength of the component. Fig. 10 shows that
distribution of amplitude function of surface roughness (Sa, Sz) is in-
versely proportional to fatigue life of a component [ 15].
Diﬀerence in fatigue strength between milled and UVAM samples
might be connected to friction in the contact to the bars during fatigue
testing since UVAM surface show much lower roughness in comparison.
AWJ sample relatively low fatigue limit could be due to problems re-
lated to abrasive particles that stick to the surface and initiate failure
even though this sample has a relatively high compressive surface re-
sidual stress.
Fig. 11 shows the irregularities on the surface of the sample in the
case of AWJ which is closely related with the cutting mechanism. In the
case of WEDM crater marks have been found on the surface analysis of
SEM micro-graph of the workpiece material as it can be shown in
Fig. 10 . However, these irregularities could be mitigated by setting of
less productive process parameters WEDM [ 21].
Fig. 12 shows the SEM images of the specimens to observe the
fracture produced during fatigue testing. Di ﬀerences were found in the
directionality of the fracture between the samples machined with AWJ
and WEDM, and the milled ones. In the case of AWJ and WEDM failure
appears on the surface and in the case of milled specimens, fracture has
edge direction.
Fig. 13 evidence the correlation between fatigue and the presence of
residual stresses. AWJ sample indicates a somewhat higher surface fa-
tigue strength compared to WEDM, which could be expected due to the
compressive residual stress state in the surface region. Milled and
UVAM samples have similar residual stress conditions but surface fa-
tigue for UVAM sample is better. Di ﬀerent surface roughness between
surfaces might have in ﬂuenced during the fatigue test and in the con-
tact with support bars.
UVAM process has shown slighter improvement in fatigue test, they
can be explained by compression generated during the milling due to
the axial movement, giving higher hardness and balance residual stress
across and along the sample. In case of conventional milling samples
residual stresses are lower across the sample and roughness is higher
due to tool trajectory resulting in lower fatigue cycle. Samples obtained
by AWJ and WEDM shows worst fatigue life results, as it has been re-
ported [ 22] that lower roughness results in longer fatigue life, but that
for roughness values in the range 2.5 – 5 μm Ra it is primarily dependent
on workpiece residual stress and surface microstructure, rather than
roughness. In WEDM samples craters and traction residuals stress due to
the heat generated during the process and have negative e ﬀect on the
fatigue life. The AWJ cutting mechanism by not producing heat results
in a state of non-tractive stresses, even though, shows erosion cracks
which are formed by the impact of the accelerating solid particles
within high-velocity waterjets, causing stress concentration that lead to
a smaller number of cycles until their end of fatigue life.
4. Conclusions
Diﬀerences
in surface integrity and fatigue of non-conventional
machined surfaces compared to conventional milling have been pre-
sented. Following conclusions could be extracted from experimental
investigations.
Regarding surface fatigue, low surface roughness results in high
surface fatigue strength. WEDM samples show tensile stresses and the
lowest fatigue strength while UVAM sample shows the highest fatigue
strength and the highest compressive residual stresses.
Machining methods shows great in ﬂuence on the residual stresses.
Milled, UVAM and AWJ samples have a compressive residual state in
surface region while WEDM samples have a tensile stress state. Surface
residual stresses show big di ﬀerence for milled surface between parallel
and perpendicular directions to feed. This di ﬀerence is less pronounced
for UVAM samples. AWJ sample has a compressive stress state only in
outer surface region of 40 μm and below this depth stresses become
tensile. UVAM and conventionally milled samples show similar pro ﬁles.
