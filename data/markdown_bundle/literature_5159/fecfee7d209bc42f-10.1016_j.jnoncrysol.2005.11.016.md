Abstract
We have studied the inﬂuence of the arc melting procedure of Nb-containing Zr-based bulk metallic glasses (BMGs) on their thermal
and mechanical properties. We found that the strength and plastic strain to failure, determined at room temperature, increases by the
addition of a few percentage of Nb into the Zr-based BMGs. High-resolution transmission-electron microscopy results show that the
addition of 2 at.% Nb introduces the formation of as-cast nanocrystals. At the same time, thermal analyses indicate an increase in glass
forming ability with the small addition of Nb. Contrary to the reported results in other amorphous alloys, we found that the plastic strain
increases further after heat-induced structural relaxation in the Zr-based BMGs.

1. Introduction
Materials without a long-range crystalline order have
been found to exhibit unique optical, magnetic, and mechani-
cal properties [1–5]. In the early 1990s, with the develop-
ment of techniques to make nanostructured materials, a
substantial increase in the strength has been observed in a
number of nanocrystal-reinforced amorphous-matrix com-
posites prepared by isothermal annealing of amorphous
ribbons [6–9]. Following up on these eﬀorts, nanocrystal-
containing Zr-based amorphous alloys with increased tensile
strengths were successfully produced in bulk sizes larger
than 4 mm in diameter [10–13] (nanocrystal-containing
bulk metallic glass composites (nano-BMGCs)). Studies of
the relationship between the microstructure of the nano-
BMGCs and their compressive mechanical properties have
also found that both the strength and the plastic strain to
failure increase with increasing volume fraction of up to
about 30 vol.% of 1–3-nm-sized nanocrystals [14,15].
Such bulk nano-BMGCs have been synthesized either by
casting or by the heat-induced crystallization of nanocrys-
tal-forming amorphous alloys [14,15]. In the case of heat-
induced nanocrystallization, structural relaxation of the
amorphous alloy occurs ﬁrst. It has been reported in Fe-,
Co-, and Ni-based amorphous alloys that such structural
relaxation results in brittleness [16]. However, it is yet
unclear if the material becomes brittle before showing
increased mechanical properties in the nano-BMGCs.
Thus, it is necessary to further investigate the relationship
between the mechanical properties and the structural relax-
ation.
Previously it has been shown that the addition of Nb to
Zr–Cu–Al amorphous alloy ribbons can induce nanocrys-
tallization [7]. Therefore, the (Zr
58Ni13.6Cu18Al10.4)100/C0x-
Nbx (x = 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 at.%) alloy was selected for the
systematic studies of the as-cast bulk nano-BMGCs, and
the relationship between mechanical properties and struc-
tural relaxation.

2. Experimental procedures
To bring attention to the subtleties of the preparation of
Nb-containing BMGs, we ﬁrst examined the properties of a
well known composition, Zr
55Ni5Cu20Al10Nb5, and dem-
onstrate the inﬂuence of variation in the preparation route
of the master-alloy on the resultant BMG properties.
Zr55Ni5Cu20Al10Nb5 master-alloy ingots (about 20 g each)
were prepared by arc melting high purity elements, using
two diﬀerent procedures, i.e., a one-step versus a two-step
master-alloy preparation procedure followed by suction
casting the alloy into a copper mould to form BMG rods.
The raw elemental Nb component was in the same shape of
a u = 2.8 mm diameter wire. The one-step procedure is an
often used method of preparing master-alloy ingots [17–
22], i.e., arc melting all of the constituent elements into a
master-alloy at once. In the two-step procedure [23], which
has been commonly used in industry for preparing conven-
tional alloys, binary ingots of Zr–Nb are fabricated ﬁrst,
followed by re-arc-melting it into master-alloy ingot with
the remaining elemental components of Ni, Cu, and Al.
For each method, the master-alloy ingots were arc melted
several times for a total time of about 8 min prior to suc-
tion casting into BMG rods. Between each melt, the button
was ﬂipped to ensure complete mixing between the elemen-
tal components.
The amorphous structure of the BMG rods was con-
ﬁrmed by X-ray diﬀraction. The microstructure was exam-
ined using a ﬁeld emission high resolution transmission
electron microscope (HRTEM). The glass transition ( T
g)
and crystallization ( Tx) temperatures of the samples were
measured in a Perkin–Elmer Diamond diﬀerential scanning
calorimeter (DSC), and the liquidus temperatures ( T
l) were
determined using a Netzsch STA 409 diﬀerential thermal
analyser (DTA). Quasistatic compression tests were per-
formed at room temperature using a MTS servohydraulic
load frame, operated under a displacement control at a
strain rate of 10
/C04 s/C01. Compression samples (length to
diameter ratio, L/u =2 ; u = 3 mm and L = 6 mm) were
centerless ground and polished. The engineering stress
and strain data are presented.

4. Discussion
It is valid to ask what is the reason that the thermal and
mechanical properties between the two types of BMGs,
whose master-alloys have the same nominal composition
but were prepared by a one-step or a two-step method,
are so diﬀerent as was shown in Fig. 1 . Nb, one of the
higher melting temperature ( T
m) elements ( Tm = 2742 K),
is often added to Zr- or Cu-based BMGs to investigate
its inﬂuence on the BMGs’ glass forming ability, corrosion
resistance, and mechanical properties [17–22,25]. However,
the melting temperatures of the Zr- and Cu-based BMGs
are typically below 1200 K. Such a large discrepancy in
the melting temperature of the constituent phases makes
the melting process diﬃcult, and often results in an incom-
plete melting of the refractory element. Furthermore, the
refractory components have higher densities (8.6 g/cm
3
for Nb) as compared to the Zr-BMGs (6.5 g/cm 3) that
could cause gravitational segregation of the heavier refrac-
tory elements during processing. Finally, limited solubility

Only after the master-alloy is properly prepared, with no
unmelted Nb cast into the BMG rod, it is meaningful to
discuss the inﬂuence of Nb addition on the thermal proper-
ties and glass forming ability ( Fig. 2), and further elaborate
on the relationship between the microstructure and
mechanical properties ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). From the observa-
tion described previously, adding a small amount of Nb,
e.g., 1–2 at.%, to Zr–Ni–Cu–Al metallic glasses results in
a wider supercooled liquid region DT
x, larger Trg, and c
(recall Fig. 3). Higher values of these three parameters cor-
respond to a better glass-forming ability BMG.
Studies of the mechanisms of crystallization, related to
the microstructural evolution of nanocrystal forming
amorphous alloys, suggest [11,26] that the nucleation in
amorphous alloys occurs heterogeneously at quenched-in
nuclei or quenched-in medium range order (MRO)
domains. The size of these domains can be as large as a
few nanometers ( /C241 nm). If the number of quenched-in
nanoscale particles (clusters) is large enough to inﬂuence
the mechanical properties of the BMG, we may refer to
the latter as nanocrystal-reinforced amorphous matrix
composites. The average size of such nanocrystals is typi-
cally from 1 to 3 nm, which is much smaller than the thick-
ness of shear bands ( /C2420 nm). Such small particles cannot
absorb the shear bands. However, the converse may be
possible. Thus, it is hypothesized that instead, the particles
interact with and impede the movement of already formed
shear bands to increase the viscosity and, thereby, provide
opportunity for a higher number of shear bands to form. It
is expected that the greater the number of shear bands that
are generated, the larger the sustained plastic deformation
of the BMG.
Structural relaxation takes the BMGs from an initially
metastable amorphous state, with a random atomic
arrangement frozen in at a high temperature, to a stable
amorphous state at a lower temperature. Consequently,
no additional free volume, which is commonly used to
account for the deformation dominated by the initiation
and propagation of shear bands in BMGs [1,2], can be fur-
ther created during structural relaxation. Many studies of
the relationship between mechanic properties and struc-
tural relaxation in BMGs have been reported and most
of them showed an increase of material brittleness [16].
However, as shown above in our composition family,
structural relaxation led to an increased plastic strain to
failure. Reasons for the increased plastic strain to failure
may stem not only from structural relaxation in the
BMG, but also from stress relaxation upon annealing. That
is, substantial residual stresses in amorphous alloy samples
introduced during quenching would be relieved and thus
could also contribute to the observed improvement in
plasticity.
5. Conclusions
We have fabricated a series of Nb-containing (Zr
58-
Ni13.6Cu18Al10.4)100/C0xNbx BMGs by a two-step master-
alloy preparation process. We have shown that the use of
the two-step process for BMG fabrication results in a more
reliable and proper investigations of the BMG proper-
ties than the use of the conventional one-step process.
The Nb-containing (Zr
58Ni13.6Cu18Al10.4)100/C0xNbx BMGs
exhibit greater glass forming ability, and the strength and
plastic strain to failure have been found to increase when
a few percentage of Nb has been added into the Zr-based
BMGs. The plastic strain has also been found to further
increase after structural relaxation of the BMGs.
