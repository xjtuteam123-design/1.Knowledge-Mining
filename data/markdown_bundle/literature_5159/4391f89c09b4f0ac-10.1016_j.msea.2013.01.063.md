abstract
Cu–Al–Ni high temperature shape memory alloy (HTSMA) strips were successfully prepared from rapid
solidiﬁed water atomized Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed powders via hot densiﬁcation rolling of unsheathed
sintered powder preforms. Finished heat-treated Cu–Al–Ni alloy strips had ﬁne-grained structure,
average grain size approximately 16
mm, and exhibited a combination of high strength and high
ductility. It has been demonstrated that the redistribution of nano-sized alumina particles, present on
the surface as well as inside the starting water atomized Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed powder particles, due to
plastic deformation of starting powder particles during hot densiﬁcation rolling resulted in the ﬁne
grained microstructure in the ﬁnished SMA strips. The ﬁnished SMA strips were almost fully
martensitic in nature, consisting of a mixture of
b0
1 and g0
1 martensite. The average fracture strength
and fracture strain of the ﬁnished SMA strips were 810 MPa and 12%, respectively, and the fractured
specimens exhibited primarily micro-void coalescence type ductile nature of fracture. Finished Cu–Al–
Ni SMA strips exhibited high characteristic transformation temperatures and an almost 100% one-way
shape recovery was obtained in the specimens up to 4% applied deformation pre-strain. The retained
two-way shape memory recovery increased with increasing applied training pre-strain, achieving a
maximum value of 16.25% at 5% applied training pre-strain.

1. Introduction
Shape memory alloys (SMAs) are a special class of multi-
functional materials having the ability to undergo shape change
and then recovering its original shape under the inﬂuence of
external stimuli, which could be mechanical, thermal, electrical,
or magnetic in nature [1,2]. In recent years, a considerable
amount of attention has been paid toward the development of
high temperature shape memory alloys (HTSMAs), which can be
used as actuators, sensors, fasteners, and vibration dampers in
elevated temperature environments, such as near aircraft and
automobile engines, oil and gas drilling equipments, high tem-
perature chemical industry for process control, and some aero-
space applications [2–4]. Amongst a variety of HTSMAs, Cu–Al–Ni
SMAs have been considered a potential cost effective material for
applications in the temperature range of 373 to 523 K, because of
their high thermal stability above 373 K together with the relative
ease of processing as compared to Ni–Ti-based alloys [3–8].
However, the conventionally cast Cu–Al–Ni SMAs suffer from
the problem of severe brittleness, wherein the material failure
occurs via intergranular fracture due to a combination of high
degree of order, high elastic anisotropy ratio ( /C24 14) and large
grain size (1–3 mm) [4,8–10]. These factors enhance the difﬁculty
of relaxation of stress concentration at grain boundaries, leading
to the ease of fracture. As a result, these alloys exhibit poor
mechanical properties (fracture stress /C24 150 MPa and fracture
strain /C24 0.5%). Several attempts were made to improve the
mechanical properties, especially ductility, of the conventionally
cast Cu–Al–Ni SMAs by grain reﬁning via different techniques,
including thermo-mechanical treatment and additions of small
amounts of quaternary elements, such as Zr, Ti, Co, and V [11–19].
Although these efforts resulted in an improvement in the
mechanical properties, such as strength and ductility, the
mechanical properties were still unsatisfactory for most of the
intended commercial applications.
Powder metallurgy (PM) processing is well-known for its
capability of producing not only ﬁne-grained materials but also
near-net shape products on commercial scale. The most fascinating
aspect of PM processing is that the desired microstructure and
properties can be obtained by designing the process through a

SMAs via powder metallurgy route, wherein rapid solidiﬁed gas

material [20–23]. Since most of the commercial applications
require products in the form of wire, rod, or strip, near-full density
Cu–Al–Ni SMA rods/strips were prepared via ‘‘cold-compaction–
sintering–hot swaging’’, ‘‘hot pressing–sintering–hot rolling’’, and
‘‘hot isostatic pressing–hot rolling’’. These near-full density Cu–Al–
Ni SMA rods/strips had grain size in the range of 20–100 mm, and
exhibited mechanical properties far superior than those prepared
by conventional casting technique. It must be emphasized that the
mechanical properties of the PM products are governed by not only
the ﬁnal density but also the interparticle bonding between the
powder particles. Therefore, to achieve good mechanical properties
in the PM products, it is necessary to ensure that the powder
consolidation methods result in good interparticle metallurgical
bonding. Mechanical working of sintered powder preforms is an
efﬁcient way of achieving good interparticle bonding between the
powder particles, and rolling, forging, extrusion, or swaging of
powder preforms can be used to achieve the above stated goals.
However, it would be worth mentioning that hot swaging and hot
extrusion require canning/encapsulation of powders under
vacuum. Obviously, such a canning or encapsulation of powders
not only makes the process complex but also imposes limitations
on the bulk processing.
Rolling is a versatile and an industrially attractive method of
inducing plastic deformation in the metals. It provides an oppor-
tunity to prepare various types of products such as strips, sheets,
rods, and channels etc. Rolling can also be utilized to prepare
near-full density products from the powders, wherein powder
particles are deformed during densiﬁcation and better interpar-
ticle bonding is achieved. Recently, Vajpai et al. [24–26] success-
fully prepared full density Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips via hot densiﬁcation
rolling of sintered powder preforms prepared from argon ato-
mized pre-alloyed powders as well as elemental powder mixture,
wherein near-full density was achieved primarily by hot rolling of
sintered powder preforms under controlled atmosphere. Since hot
densiﬁcation rolling under protective atmosphere does not
require any canning or encapsulation of sintered powder pre-
forms during hot rolling, it is a relatively simple and industrially
more attractive method. The ﬁnished Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips,
prepared from argon atomized pre-alloyed powders, had an
average grain size in the range of 26–90 mm. On the other hand,
the use of short time milled elemental powder mixture to prepare
Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips resulted in much smaller grain size, of the
order of 6 mm, as compared to that of argon atomized pre-alloyed
powders. It was demonstrated that the presence of nano-sized
alumina particles in the starting powder and their re-distribution
during processing plays a crucial role in determining the micro-
structure of the ﬁnished Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips.
It is interesting to note that water atomized pre-alloyed powders,
which would inherently contain high amounts of oxides including
alumina, have not been used for developing Cu–Al–Ni SMAs. Since
water atomization is the most commonly used technique of pre-
paring low cost metal powders in high volumes, preparation of Cu–

2. Experimental procedure
In the present work, water atomized Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed
powder (average particles size approximately 65 mm; nominal
composition (wt%): Cu ¼81.4, Al ¼14.1, Ni ¼4.5), supplied by
Padmasree Enterprises, Hyderabad, India, was used as starting
material. Details of the microstructural characteristics of the
argon atomized powders, and the microstructure and properties
of the SMA strips prepared from these powders, are provided
elsewhere [24,25]. The pre-alloyed powders were cold compacted
at 500 MPa pressure to prepare rectangular ‘‘green’’ preforms
having dimensions 30 mm ( L) /C2 18 mm ( W) /C2 6m m ( T). Subse-
quently, the green preforms were sintered under the hydrogen
atmosphere at 1273 K for 60 min, followed by hot rolling at
1273 K. A total thickness reduction of approximately 85% was
given to the sintered powder preforms in 3 rolling passes,
wherein preforms were annealed for 30 min at 1273 K between
two consecutive hot rolling passes. The hot rolling was carried out
in a specially designed two-high rolling mill interlinked with the
pre-heating furnace in such a manner that the heated sintered
compacts remained under protective atmosphere right up to the
nip of the rolling mill. The hot rolled strips were immediately
placed in a bed of graphite to minimize oxidation of hot rolled
strip not only on the surface but also inside the porous strip.
A two-high rolling mill, having roll diameter 127 mm and rotating
at 80 rpm, was used in the present work. The rolls were not
heated prior to hot rolling. The hot rolled strips were solutionized
at 1223 K for 60 min under inert gas atmosphere (argon). Subse-
quently, the solutionized strips were quenched in water at room
temperature.
The microstructural characterization and elemental composi-
tional analysis was carried out by Scanning Electron Microscopy
(SEM) equipped with Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy
(EDS). To study microstructural features of hot rolled SMA strips

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural characteristics of Cu–Al–Ni
pre-alloyed powders
Fig. 2 shows typical SEM micrographs of the as received water
atomized powder particles (average particle size /C24 65 mm). It can
be seen that the water atomized Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed powders
are highly irregular in shape and are also folded. It is evident that
the morphology of the water atomized Cu–Al–Ni powders is quite
different as compared to the argon atomized pre-alloyed Cu–Al–
Ni powder particles, which are typically spherical in shape. The
difference in the morphology of Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed powders
can be attributed to the different atomization conditions. During
atomization process, the stream of molten metal/alloy is frag-
mented
in to small irregular shaped liquid droplets by the shock of
atomizing media, i.e. gas/water, and the ﬁnal shape of the
atomized powder particles depends on the cooling rate of these
fragmented liquid droplets during ﬂight [27]. In case of gas
atomization, the liquid metal droplets cool down slowly and
surface tension turns these droplets in to sphere before they are
solidiﬁed. On the other hand, water atomized powders are
typically irregular in shape because liquid droplets cool rapidly
and solidify before the surface tension turns them in to sphere.
Fig. 3 shows typical SEM micrographs of the cross-section of
as received water atomized pre-alloyed powder particles
together with elemental distribution maps of a selected area
on the cross-section. In case of water atomized powders, it can
be seen that Cu and Ni are uniformly distributed in the matrix
(Fig. 3 a). However, it can be noted that the amount of aluminum
and oxygen is higher in some particular areas on the cross-
section of the particles, which suggests that these areas contain
higher amounts of alumina as compared to other areas. Further-
more, it is interesting to note the presence of a circular depres-
sion, having higher amounts of aluminum and oxygen content,
inside the powder particle. It appears that the circular depres-
sions have originated as a result of formation of porosity due to
the entrapment of water vapors during water atomization, and
the entrapped water vapors formed alumina, resulting in higher
Al and oxygen content inside the pores. It can also be observed
(Fig. 3 b) that the region near the surface of the water atomized
powder particle also contains higher amounts of aluminum and
oxygen, suggesting the presence of relatively higher amounts of
alumina near the surface. The presence of relatively higher
amounts of oxides near the surface, i.e. Aluminum oxide in the
present case, is a typical characteristic of water atomized metal/
alloy powders. The average chemical composition of the powder
particle cross-section, as estimated by EDS analysis, is provided
in Table 1 . The high amounts of oxygen on the cross-section of
water atomized pre-alloyed powders further indicate the pre-
sence of alumina particles inside the water atomized powders. It
would be worth mentioning that the inert gas atomized powders
contain a very small amount of alumina only on the surface
[22,24].
3.2. Sintering and hot rolling of powder preforms
The water atomized Cu–Al–Ni powders were cold compacted to
prepare ‘‘green’’ powder preforms. Subsequently, the green Cu–Al–
Ni powder preforms were sintered at 1273 K for 60 min under
protective atmosphere. It was observed that sintering did not bring
about any signiﬁcant change in the dimensions of the powder
preforms. The relative density of the sintered powder preforms
was found to be approximately 50% with respect to that of near
fully dense ﬁnished Cu–Al–Ni alloy strips prepared from same
powder. It was found that the density of the powder preforms did
not increase signiﬁcantly as a result of sintering. However, the
strength of the powder preforms increased signiﬁcantly. It would
be worth mentioning that the objective of sintering was to impart
enough strength to the preform for safe handling during subse-
quent hot rolling. Thereafter, the sintered porous preforms were
hot rolled to prepare near full density strips in three rolling passes,
achieving 65%, 80%, and 85% total thickness deformation in the
successive passes. Fig. 4 shows the variation in the relative density
of the sintered powder preforms, with increasing thickness defor-
mation during hot rolling. It can be seen that the relative density
increased continuously with increasing thickness deformation, and
near full density was achieved after approximately 85% thickness
deformation. Fig. 5 shows a typical photograph of the Cu–Al–Ni
SMA strip hot rolled to approximately 85% thickness deformation.
It can be seen that no severe surface cracks were formed in the hot

3.5. Mechanical properties
Fig. 12 shows a typical stress–strain curve of the ﬁnished
martensitic Cu–Al–Ni strips prepared from water atomized pre-
alloyed powder. It can be seen that the stress–strain curve
consists of two regions, i.e. an initial region (Stage-I) of higher
slope followed by a second region (Stage-II) of lower slope. The
transition stress, Sy, corresponds to the stress necessary for the
initiation of homogeneous growth of favorably oriented marten-
site variants at temperatures below Ms, and the slope of stage II is
a measure of the ease of growth of favorably oriented martensite
variants [30]. The average fracture stress and fracture strain of the

3.6. Shape memory properties
Fig. 14 shows the shape of a specimen of martensitic Cu–Al–Ni
SMA strips at room temperature in initial, deformed, partially
recovered, and fully recovered states. It can be seen that deformed
SMA specimen returned to its original shape after reaching a
temperature higher than Af. Fig. 15 shows the one-way shape
recovery with increasing temperature at 4% applied pre-strain. It
can be seen that the one-way shape recovery was very small up to
417 K (144 1C), which is very close to Ms, followed by extremely
rapid shape recovery with an increase in temperature, and almost
100% shape recovery was achieved at 441 K (168 1C), which is
close to Af. Fig. 16 shows the effect of number of training cycles on
the two-way shape memory (TWSM) strain at 3, 4 and 5% applied
pre-strain. It can be seen that the two-way shape memory strain
increased rapidly in the initial approximately 5 training cycles,
and then reached a saturation value. Moreover, it can also be
noted that the retained TWSM strain increased with increasing
applied training pre-strain. A similar nature of two-way shape
memory strain evolution with increasing number of training
cycles has also been reported for polycrystalline Ni–Ti, Cu–Zn–
Al and Cu–Al–Ni alloys [24–26,31–35]. The retained two-way
shape memory recovery was found to be approximately 9.25%,
11.25% and 16.25% after 15 training cycles at 3, 4 and 5% applied
training pre-strain, respectively.
The evolution of two-way shape memory effect in SMAs is
considered a consequence of generation of dislocations in the
SMAs during thermo-mechanical training cycles. These disloca-
tions generate micro-stresses in the material, leading to the
formation of some preferential variants of martensite via biasing
the nucleation and growth of martensite. As a result, dislocations
generated during the initial period of training procedure lead to
the creation and simultaneous strengthening of oriented stress
ﬁelds. It was envisaged that the stress ﬁeld created during ﬁrst
few cycles suppresses further introduction of dislocations during
subsequent training procedure, leading to stable two-way shape
memory strains after ﬁrst few cycles. However, a partial con-
tribution to the two-way shape memory effect can also be
attributed to a possible stabilization of some amount of retained
martensite during training [36]. In the present work, the max-
imum value of two-way shape memory strain was found approxi-

4. Conclusions
Fine-grained Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips, having equiaxed grains of
average size approximately 16 mm together with high strength
and high ductility, were successfully prepared from water ato-
mized pre-alloyed powders by hot densiﬁcation rolling of
unsheathed sintered powder preforms. The average grain size of
the ﬁnished Cu–Al–Ni SMA strips, approximately 16 mm, was
signiﬁcantly smaller than the average particle size, approximately
64 mm, of the starting water atomized pre-alloyed powder. The
formation of extremely small size equiaxed grains was attributed
to the nature of distribution of nano-sized alumina particles in the
water atomized Cu–Al–Ni pre-alloyed powders and their redis-
