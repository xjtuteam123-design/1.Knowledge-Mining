Abstract
Purpose – To characterise the high temperature oxide scales for some plasma sprayed NiCrAlY coated Ni- and Fe-based superalloys.
Design/methodology/approach – Ni-22Cr-10Al-1Y metallic coatings were deposited on two Ni-based superalloys; Superni 601 and Superni 718 and
one Fe-based superalloy; Superfer 800H by the shrouded plasma spray process. Oxidation studies were conducted on uncoated as well as plasma spray
coated superalloys in air at 900 8C under cyclic conditions for 50 cycles. Each cycle consisted of 1 h heating followed by 20 min of cooling in air. The
thermogravimetric technique was used to approximate the kinetics of oxidation. X-ray diffraction, SEM/EDAX and EPMA techniques were used to
analyse the oxide scales.
Findings – All of the coated, as well as the uncoated, superalloys followed an alnost-parabolic rate of oxidation. The NiCrAlY coating was found to be
successful in maintaining its continuous contact with the superalloy substrates in all the cases. The oxide scales formed on the exposed NiCrAlY coated
superalloys were found to be intact and spallation-free. The main phases analysed for the coated superalloys were oxides of nickel, chromium and
aluminium and spinel of nickel and chromium, which are expected to be useful for developing oxidation resistance at high temperatures.
Practical implications – The coated superalloys showed remarkable cyclic oxidation resistance under simulated laboratory conditions. However, it is
suggested that these coated superalloys also should be tested in actual industrial environments of boilers and gas turbines, etc. so as to obtain more
practical and reliable oxidation data.
Originality/value – The knowledge of the reaction kinetics and the nature of the surface oxide scales formed during oxidation is important for
evaluating the alloys for their use and degradation characteristics in high temperature applications such as steam boilers, furnace equipment, hea t
exchangers and piping in chemical industry, reformer, bafﬂe plates/tubes in fertilizer plants, jet engines, pump bodies and parts.

Introduction
Iron- and nickel-based superalloys are the commercial alloys
commonly used for high temperature applications, e.g.
manufacture of compone n t su s e di na g g r e s s i v e
environments of gas turbines, steam boilers and other
energy conversion plants. The superior mechanical
performance and good corrosion resistance at high
temperatures of the superalloys, especially nickel-based
superalloys, make them useful materials for hot components
(such as blades or vanes) in industrial gas turbines and other
energy conversion systems. However, the presence of
combustion gases constitutes an extreme environment and
hot corrosion is inevitable when alloys are used at high
temperatures for long periods (Goebel et al. , 1973). T o
improve the oxidation/corrosion resistance of superalloys and
to increase the service life of superalloy components at high
temperatures, one of the available approaches is coating the
alloy with a protective layer, using various surface treatment
techniques. Many coating techniques have been suggested,
such as pack cementation, plasma spraying and chemical
vapour deposition. However, among the various coating
methods, the advanced plasma spray technique has many
advantages, such as high productivity for thick coating ﬁlms of
more than 100
mm and good applicability for a wide range of
coating materials including ceramic powder. Furthermore,
the process does not cause degradation of the mechanical
properties of the alloy substrate (Gill and Tucker, 1986;
Nicoll et al. , 1986).
With no promising turbine materials beyond the coated
nickel-based superalloys apparent in the foreseeable future,
continued progress will likely be made by further reﬁnement
of control of thermally grown oxide adherence and by more
cost effective manufacturing processes for contemporary types
of coatings (Goward, 1998). Manufacture of overlay coatings
by EBPVD dominated through the 1970s. Low-pressure

issue (Evans and T aylor, 2001). The objective of the present
work was to study the oxidation behaviour of two Ni-based
and one Fe-based superalloys with and without plasma spray

9008C in air. Knowledge of the reaction kinetics and the
nature of the surface scales formed during oxidation are
important for evaluating the alloys for their use and
degradation characteristics in high temperature applications

for high temperature applications such as steam boilers,
furnace equipment, heat exchangers and piping in chemical
industry, reformer, bafﬂe plates/tubes in fertilizer plants, jet
engines, pump bodies and parts.
Experimental procedure
The substrate superalloys were obtained in rolled sheet form.
The chemical compositions (wt%) of the substrate superalloys
have been detailed in T able I. The specimens with dimensions
of approximately 20 mm £ 15 mm £ 5 mm were cut from the
superalloy sheets. The specimens were polished and grit
blasted with alumina powder (grit 60) before being plasma
sprayed. A 40 kW Miller thermal plasma spray apparatus was
used to deposit the coating of NiCrAlY (Praxair NI-343) alloy
powder on the given superalloys. Argon was used as the
powder carrying and shielding gas. All of the process
parameters, including the spray distance, were constant
throughout coating process. The parameters included an arc
current of 700 A, arc voltage of 35 V , powder ﬂow rate of
3.2 rev min
2 1, the spraying distance of 90-110 mm, and the
plasma arc gas (Argon) and carrier gas (Argon) pressures of
59 and 40 psi, respectively. The details of the coating powder
and porosity (2.75-4.5 per cent) measurements of as-sprayed
NiCrAlY coatings are reported in earlier publications (Singh
et al. , 2005a, b). The detailed characterisation of these
coatings has been published elsewhere (Singh et al. , 2005b).
The average thickness of the coating, as measured from cross-
sectional back scattered electron images (BSEI) of the
specimens, was found to be 228
mm.
Cyclic oxidation studies were performed in static air for 50
cycles; each cycle consisted of 1 h heating at 900 8C in a silicon
carbide tube furnace, followed by 20 min cooling at room
temperature. The methodologies for oxidation testing and
analysis can be found elsewhere (Singh et al. , 2005a, b).
SEM/EDAX analysis was carried out with scanning electron
microscope (JEOL (JSM-5800)) with an EDAX attachment
by Oxford Instruments (Model-6841), made in England. The
equipment could indicate directly the phases (oxides) present,
along with their compositions, based on built-in EDAX
software, which is a patented product of Oxford ISIS300.
Results
Visual examination of oxide scales
The colour of the oxide scale formed on the Ni-based
superalloys Superni 601 and 718 after air oxidation for 50
cycles at 900 8C was dark grey with some brownish spots,
while the scale for oxidised Superfer 800H showed increasing
dominance of the brownish spots on the dark grey
background with the progress of the study. Lustrous scales
were formed in all the cases up to the mid cycles of study,
which eventually became progressively more dull with an
increasing number of cycles. The scales of the Superni 601
and Superfer 800H showed no spalling and remained intact
with the substrates, whereas in the scale of Superni 718, a
very small pit was observed at the end of seventh cycle, which
showed virtually no growth afterwards.
All of the plasma spray NiCrAlY coated superalloys
indicated the formation of smooth continuous scales
without the presence of cracks, when subjected to cyclic
oxidation for 50 cycles at 900 8C. The colour of the oxide
scales for all the coated superalloys at the end of the study was
observed to be greenish grey, close to dark green. The colour
of scale changed from grey with minor green tinges, to
greenish grey, with the progress of oxidation. The scales were
found to be lustrous, with no tendency for spalling. In
general, the coating was found to be in good contact with the
superalloy substrates over most of the surface area of the
specimens during the course of cyclic oxidation studies,
except at some edges. The development of minor superﬁcial
cracks in the coating at or near the edges was observed after
the very ﬁrst cycle in all the cases. This cracking resulted in a
minor spalling of coating from one or two edges in the form of
tiny ﬂakes during the subsequent cycles. However, this
spalling was marginal.

Cross-sectional analysis
The scale for NiCrAlY coated oxidised Superni 601; Figure 7,
as revealed by SEM/EDAX analysis, showed the outermost
layer of the scale to consist mainly of the oxides of chromium
and aluminium, along with small amounts of oxides of nickel
and yttrium. Aluminium showed ﬂuctuations in composition
along the thickness of the scale. Alternate layers rich in Ni and
Al, respectively, were evident. The nickel-rich area, as
represented by point 3, seems to be in an unoxidised state

EPMA analysis
BSEI and X-ray mappings of the oxide scale for Superni 601
after oxidation in air for 50 cycles at 900 8C, as shown in
Figure 8, revealed a dense scale, which was mainly rich in
nickel and chromium, along with some small amounts of iron
and aluminium. Manganese could also be seen in the scale in
lesser amounts. A corresponding analysis for the Superni 718
subjected to cyclic oxidation at 900 8C in air, Figure 9,
indicated a dense scale, which again was basically a nickel-rich
band where iron and chromium also were present. The scale
also contained titanium and aluminium. Some “islands”,
consisting purely of aluminium, perhaps as inclusions, were
seen in the scale. Similar analysis for the oxidised Superfer
800H showed iron dominating in continuous scale, which
contained substantial amounts of nickel, Figure 10.
Chromium and manganese have shown their co-presence in
the form of two dense bands; one at the scale/substrate
interface and the other at the outermost layer of the scale.
Titanium exhibited a similar tendency.
Analogous analyses for the NiCrAlY coated Superni 601, as
shown in Figure 11, revealed a scale with an outer layer
consisting mainly of aluminium and nickel, with some
diffused iron in the form of clusters. Yttrium was absent in
this outermost layer. In the intermediate portion of the scale
Ni, Cr, Al and Y co-existed. Diffusion of iron from the
substrate superalloy throughout the scale also was evident,
which was more prominent near the substrate/coating
interface. EPMA analysis for the oxidised NiCrAlY coated
Superni 718, Figure 12, indicated that the scale contained
mainly nickel, chromium, aluminium, and there was a
presence of some small clusters of iron in top layers of the
scale. The iron seemed to have diffused into the scale from the
substrate. Yttrium was seen in the form of clusters throughout
the scale. The analysis for the oxidised Superfer 800H case
revealed a lamellar scale consisting of nickel-rich areas, which
also contained chromium, Figure 13. Aluminium and yttrium
were present at the area boundaries in the scale and, alongside
these area boundaries, oxygen also had penetrated indicated
by the formation of oxides.

the EPMA analysis for the superalloys, Figures 8-10, that
chromium showed a tendency to form protective layers in
the respective oxide scales. The chromium layers, in turn,
may have blocked the diffusion of any species to reach the
substrate superalloys, and hence provided oxidation
resistance to the superalloys under study.
It was observed that, in general, the superalloys followed
a parabolic law of oxidation with slight deviations. Hussain
et al. (1994) also reported parabolic rate behaviour for an
Fe-based superalloy Incoloy 800H, similar to Superfer
800H, as did Delaunay et al. (2000) for Inconel 718
superalloy (akin to Superni 718) at 900 8C and Zhao et al.
(2005) for a new Ni-based superalloy at 950 8C. The small
deviations from the parabolic rate were observed by Levy
et al. (1989) during their studies on the oxidation and hot
corrosion of some Ni-base advanced superalloys at 704-
1,0938C. Greene and Finfrock (2001) also reported some
scatter in the mass gain per unit area versus time of
oxidation data for Inconel 718.
The XRD results for the oxidised superalloys were well
supported by the surface EDAX and EPMA analysis in the
present study. So far as the literature is concerned, Deb et al.
(1996) reported the formation of phases like NiO and Cr
2O3
during their oxidation studies on a cast Ni-base superalloy,
while Li et al. (2003) observed NiCr
2O4 phase also for a single
crystal Ni-base superalloy oxidised at 900 8C in air. Cr 2O3 and
Al2O3 phases were indicated by Bai et al. (2004) for another
Ni-base superalloy IN-738LC.
It was observed that all the plasma-sprayed NiCrAlY coated
superalloys generally follow a parabolic rate law of oxidation.
This indicates that the scales formed tend to act as diffusion
barriers. The minor deviations from the parabolic rate, as
shown by the coated specimens, conﬁrmed with the ﬁndings
of Choi et al. (2002), who attributed this scatter in data to the
formation and rapid growth of inhomogeneous oxides during
the oxidation process.

protective oxides such as NiO, Cr
2O3 and Al 2O3 in the scales
of NiCrAlY coated specimens. Choi et al. (2002) reported
similar phases for oxidised air plasma sprayed NiCrAlY
coatings. EDAX and EPMA analyses further conﬁrmed the

alloys by blocking the diffusion of reacting species towards the
substrate alloys, as has been suggested by Stroosnijder et al.
(1994) and T oma et al. (1999). The presence of the spinel
NiCr

resistance as these spinel phases usually have the much
smaller diffusion coefﬁcients of the cations and anions, rather
than those in their parent oxides (Chatterjee et al. , 2001).

elements of the substrate al loys. This indicated that
the coating was successful in acting as a reservoir for the

substrates. This was further conﬁrmed by the cross-sectional

In general, the surface scales for the NiCrAlY coatings
showed excellent spallation resistance during cyclic oxidation
in air, which indicates further that the effectiveness of the
coatings under study (as also for the cycle-oxidation
behaviour of the alloys) was dictated mainly by scale
spallation resistance, as per the opinion of Stott (1992).
Therefore, it can be inferred that the necessary protection was
provided by the coating to the substrate superalloys despite
the fact that the coated superalloys exhibited high overall
weight gains, compared to their uncoated counterparts.
Further, the NiCrAlY coatings subjected to cyclic oxidation
in air showed relatively high weight gains in the early cycles of
the exposure. These initial high oxidation rates in general might
be attributed to the rapid formation of oxides at the coating area
boundaries and within open pores, due to the penetration of the
oxidizing species along area boundaries and open pores during
the early cycles of exposure. Once the oxides had formed at
places of porosity and area boundaries, the coatings became
dense and the diffusion of oxidizing species to the internal
portions of the coatings slowed. The growth of the oxides then
became limited mainly to the surface of the specimens. This
made the weight gains and hence the oxidation rate stabilise
during subsequent exposure cycles.

in Figure 13 for the NiCrAlY coated Superfer 800H, and the

within the pores was most probably mainly aluminium oxide
co-existing with yttrium oxide. Identical ﬁndings were
reported by Niranatlumpong et al. (2000) and Choi et al.
(2002). The aluminium oxide has been reported to be a
protective oxide that blocks diffusion paths to protect the base

Development of some superﬁcial minor cracks near or along
the edges of the coated specimens during oxidation
experimentation was observed for all the coated cases,
which led to minor spalling of coatings. However, spallation
of the coatings was restricted to the edges only. Cracking and

shocks due to differences in the heat expansion coefﬁcients of
the oxides, coatings and the substrate (Rapp et al. , 1981).
Furthermore, the stress concentration factor at sharp edges of
the specimens also may have exacerbated cracking and
spalling. It is pertinent to mention here that the edges of the
specimens were kept sharp deliberately, to add one more
severe condition to the accelerated testing of the coatings. In
commercial applications, sharp edges are always avoided to
eliminate stress concentration.
Conclusions
. The Ni- and Fe-base superalloys under investigation,
Superni 601, 718 and Superfer 800H, showed very good
oxidation resistance when exposed to cyclic oxidation for
50 cycles at 900 8C in air, which could be attributed
partially to presence of chromia layers in their oxide scales.
. T h es h r o u d e dp l a s m as p r a yp r o c e s sp r o v i d e st h e
possibility of developing coatings of NiCrAlY powder on
the Ni-based superalloys Superni 601, 718 and Superfer
800H. The plasma sprayed NiCrAlY coatings when
subjected to cyclic oxidation in air at 900 8C, showed very
good adherence to the substrate superalloys under
investigation. The oxide scales also were found to be
intact and spallation-free in all the coated cases.
. The rate of oxidation was observed to be high during the
early cycles of the study for the NiCrAlY coated superalloys,
and this was attributed to the interconnected network of
pores and area boundaries present in coating structure.
. During cyclic oxidation in air, the NiCrAlY coated
superalloys exhibited higher weight gains than did their
uncoated counterpart superalloys. However, in all the
coated cases, the oxidation behaviour was observed to be
parabolic. Moreover, internal oxidation of the substrate
superalloys was not observed.
