ABSTRACT 
It is well known that yield strength is an important mechanical property of construction materials. 
In construction projects, engineers need to consider the effect of temperature on steel to meet fire 
safety design requirements, making it necessary to establish a temperature-related yield criterion 
for steel. The von Mises yield criterion is widely used for isotropic materials because of its good 
agreement with experimental results. However, the classical von Mises yield criterion does not 
consider the effect of temperature. Therefore, this study aims to establish a new temperature- 
dependent generalized von Mises (TGM) yield criterion for construction-grade steels. According 
to the energy-based theory, the effect of temperature on isotropic metallic materials is investi -
gated and the TGM yield function is further derived based on the classical von Mises yield cri -
terion. Further, it is proved by derivation that the classical von Mises yield criterion agrees with 
the new TGM yield criterion developed herein when the temperature is fixed, which indicates that 
the classical von Mises yield criterion is a special case of the TGM yield criterion. Then, the 
theoretical results of temperature-dependent yield strength calculated by the TGM yield criterion 
are compared with the experimental results, based on high-temperature tensile test data from five 
steels (two stainless steels and three high-strength alloy steels). The theoretical results are 
consistent with the experimental results, which verifies the reasonableness of the TGM yield 
criterion. This study may provide a reference for scholars and engineers in studying and designing 
steel structures for fire safety. 
1. Introduction 
The yield strength of materials is important in construction engineering when assessing the load-bearing capacity of structures 
[1 – 5] . Therefore, many scholars have studied the yielding behavior of materials [6 – 8] and have obtained many useful results [9 – 12] , 
some of which have been further applied to engineering practice [13,14] . A yield criterion is needed in the design of building structures 
to determine the yield strength of materials to prevent loss of life and minimize property damage, and it can be used for plastic analysis 
of structures [15] . To obtain the yield function, yielding under combined stress regimes can be expected to be related to some com -
bination of principal stresses. Unfortunately, there is not yet a theoretical way to correlate yielding for triaxial or biaxial states of stress 
with yielding in a uniaxial tensile test. In these circumstances, the yield criteria are essentially empirical relationships, which must, 
nevertheless, be consistent with experimental data [16] . Therefore, the range of applicable conditions of some yield criteria is limited,

Steel is widely used in construction work [5,18], so its yield behavior has attracted much attention [19]. Studies have shown that 
the yield strength of steels is very sensitive to temperature [20,21]. As the working conditions of steels are becoming more complex, 
many of them are required to serve at high or low temperatures. Therefore, it is necessary to consider the effect of temperature on 
yielding. Previous research indicates that the yield and plastic deformation of crystalline materials such as many metals are caused by 
dislocation slip [22], and the resistance of lattice atoms to dislocation slip will decrease with the increase of temperature. An atomic 
dislocation is a lattice line defect that defines the boundary between slipped and non-slipped portions of the crystal. The Peierls- 
Nabarro (PN) stress refers to the critical shear stress required to move a single dislocation in an ideal crystal, and it is 
temperature-dependent. Since the PN stress depends on the short-range stress field of the dislocation core, it is sensitive to the thermal 
energy in the lattice and, hence, the test temperature [23]. At low temperatures, where thermal enhancement of dislocation motion is 
limited, the PN stress is large. In crystals containing wide dislocations, however, the increase in PN stress with decreasing temperature 
is insignificant, since the PN stress is negligible ab initio. Accordingly, there is limited yield strength-temperature dependence in 
face-centered-cubic crystalline metals (e.g., aluminum, copper, and austenitic stainless-steel alloys). The situation is quite different in 
crystals that contain narrow dislocations: although the PN stress may be small at elevated temperatures, it increases rapidly with 
decreasing temperature and represents a large component of the yield strength in the low-temperature regime. The yield strengths of 
several body-centered-cubic crystalline steels (e.g., carbon steels and ferritic stainless steels) increase sharply with decreasing tem -
perature. The PN stress decreases rapidly with increasing temperature, thereby enhancing plastic deformation processes in these steels 
at high temperatures. In general, the strength of many metals will decrease with rising temperature. The magnitude of this change is 
closely related to their chemical composition (e.g., iron, chromium, molybdenum, copper, nickel, etc.), microstructure (ferrite, 
austenite, and duplex), and crystal type (body-centered-cubic crystalline or face-centered-cubic crystalline) [24]. Due to these factors, 
the temperature-dependent yield behavior of different metals also varies, even significantly, which poses difficulties for the study 
thereof. 
Johnson et al. [25], based on the shear properties of metallic glass at different temperatures, proposed that the increase in the 
internal energy of the material at high temperatures will lead to the yielding of the material by the material dislocation system crossing 
the mechanically defined yield stress barrier based on room-temperature conditions, as manifest by the decrease in the yield strength of 
the material at elevated temperatures. Gao et al. [26] found that either increasing stress or increasing temperature can cause 
crystal-boundary melting, reflecting the fact that the occurrence of either force-yielding or heat-melting of materials is due to the 
collective motion of atoms, which requires sufficient energy to overcome the bonding forces between atoms. From the energy point of 
view, either applying stress or increasing the temperature increases the energy stored per unit volume of a material, and they have a 
similar effect on yielding. Li W.G. [20] and Li Y. [27] assumed that the yielding of metallic materials is caused by the combined effect of 
thermal energy and the work done by mechanical deformation, then established a model to calculate the yield strength at different 
temperatures, verifying the reasonableness of the assumption using allied experimental data. 
In summary, scholars have investigated the yielding behavior of construction steels from the perspectives of the phenomenon of 
change in yield strength with temperature, microscopy, and energy, and some useful results have been obtained. However, a 
reasonable yield criterion for construction steels considering the effect of temperature has yet to be established: the widely used classic 
von Mises yield criterion is based on a fixed temperature (generally room temperature) and does not consider the effect of temperature 
variation, and related studies remain rare. Therefore, in the present study, the effect of temperature on the von Mises yield criterion is 
investigated from the perspective of energy theory, and a temperature-dependent generalized von Mises (TGM) yield criterion is 
developed for construction steels, and its rationality verified experimentally. This study may become a reference for scholars studying 
the fire-safety of steel structures and engineers who design steel structures to meet codified fire-safety requirements.

2. Effect of temperature on steel yielding 
2.1. Yielding behavior of materials 
Most materials will undergo deformation when subjected to stress. If the deformation cannot be fully recovered after unloading, 
this irrecoverable deformation is defined as plastic deformation. The criterion for determining whether a material will undergo plastic 
deformation under a given load is called the yield criterion [28] . When the stress on the material reaches the yield stress, plastic 
deformation occurs [29] . 
For homogeneous isotropic materials ( e.g. , most metals), the yield properties are usually measured in uniaxial tensile or 
compression tests ( i.e ., unidirectional stress states, with zero stress in other directions). When the tensile (compressive) stress is smaller 
than the material yield stress, the ratio of stress to strain is constant, showing a linear relationship, and the material is in an elastic state. 
When the stress reaches the yield strength (also known as the limit of proportionality), there will be irrecoverable deformation if the 
loading continues, and the ratio of stress to strain is no longer constant, showing a non-linear relationship, and the material enters a 
plastic state. The strength corresponding to a residual plastic strain of 0.2% is generally used as the yield strength (also called the 
nominal yield strength) in engineering practice to make full use of the strength of the steel, as shown in Fig. 1 . 
For the yielding of the material in the unidirectional stress state, the stress-strain curve of the uniaxial tensile (compression) test is 
used to determine the yielding stress point, which can be used as a criterion for yielding under uniaxial stress. In engineering practice, 
the unidirectional stress state only occurs in a very small number of members, while the majority of members are in a more complex 
three-dimensional stress state. In the three-dimensional stress state, the stress state at a point is the result of the actions of multiple 
stress components, and it is one-sided or even wrong to select only the stress component in one direction when assessing whether the 
material yields or not. A comprehensive analysis of the effect of each stress component on material yielding is required before the 
material yield criterion can be reasonably determined. In addition to stress conditions, some other factors ( e.g. , strain, temperature, 
time, etc .) also affect material yielding. The yielding of materials in complex stress states is generally considered as determined by the 
combination of stress, strain, time and temperature [28] , expressed as Eq. (1) .

3. Development of the TGM yield criterion 
3.1. Derivation of the TGM yield criterion 
The energy-based method is among the more important methods used in the study of mechanics [32] : because energy covers both 
mechanical deformation energy and thermal energy, it can be used as a bridge between force and heating, and has certain advantages 
in thermodynamic studies. In view of this, the energy-based method is used to study the TGM yield criterion for construction-grade 
steels. 
3.1.1. Two hypothetical conditions 
The special state of stress, which is uniform in all directions, is created by equi-triaxial compression or tension, when the principal 
stresses are identical in magnitude and sign, and Mohr ’ s circle is reduced to a point. Experiments have shown that materials can be 
hydrostatically compressed well beyond their ultimate strength in uniaxial compression without failure, and very large amounts of 
strain energy can be stored in material. The equi-triaxial tension or compression reduces only the volume of specimens, without 
changing their shape. For this particular state of stress, the theories build on the assumption that yielding depends only on deviatoric 
stress and the volumetric component of stress has no role in material yielding, leading to the conclusion: no shear stress implies no 
distortion, that in turn implies no yielding [16] . Therefore, the classic von Mises yield criterion contains two hypotheses: (1) the 
hydrostatic pressure does not affect the material yielding but only the deviatoric stress affects the yielding; (2) the material starts to 
yield when the deviatoric stress on the material reaches the value corresponding to the yield function f = 0. From the energy point of 
view, these two hypotheses can be described as: (1) the volume deformation energy does not affect the material yielding but only the 
shape deformation energy (distortion energy) affects the yielding; (2) the material yields when the shape deformation energy of the 
material reaches the energy corresponding to the yield function f = 0. 
To establish the TGM yield criterion, these two assumptions are further supplemented and refined: ①① Similar to the mechanical 
strain energy, the thermal energy corresponding to temperature is divided into two parts: volume deformation energy and shape 
deformation energy (distortion energy), and only the distortion energy affects yielding. ②② The material yields when the total stored 
distortion energy reaches the yield energy (it is inherent to the material and does not change with temperature or stress state.), which is 
equal to the sum of the thermal and mechanical distortion energies.

6. Summary and conclusions 
After analyzing the effect of temperature on the yield behavior of construction steels, the hypothetical conditions of the classic von 
Mises yield criterion are supplemented and refined based on the form of energy. Then, a new TGM yield criterion for construction steels 
(isotropic metallic materials) considering the temperature variation is established based on the energy theory. Finally, the 
temperature-dependent experimental results of two stainless steels and three high-strength alloy steels are compared with the theo -
retical results to verify the reasonableness of the TGM yield criterion. The following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) The projection of the three-dimensional yield surface of the metallic material in the π -plane is circular at a fixed temperature, 
which is in accordance with the classic von Mises yield criterion. As the temperature increases, the yield surface gradually 
shrinks and becomes smaller, and the classic von Mises yield criterion cannot describe this behavior as it does not consider 
thermal effects; 
(2) The effect of temperature on the von Mises yield criterion is analyzed based on energy theory. A new TGM yield criterion 
considering temperature variation is thereby developed. The results prove that the classic von Mises yield criterion is equivalent 
to the TGM yield criterion when the temperature is fixed, which indicates that the classic von Mises yield criterion is a special 
case of the TGM yield criterion; 
(3) The theoretical results predicted using the TGM yield criterion are compared with the experimental results pertaining to five 
steels at different temperatures. The theoretical results agree well with the experimental results, suggesting that the TGM yield 
criterion is reasonable.
