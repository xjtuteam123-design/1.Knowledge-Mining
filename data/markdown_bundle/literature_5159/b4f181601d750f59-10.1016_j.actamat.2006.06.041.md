Abstract
Rafting in Ni-based superalloys is investigated by examining the dependence of elastic energy on the shape and orientation of c0 par-
ticles. The precipitation misﬁt strain and the plastic strain occurring in the matrix are taken into account. The elastic energies associated
with spherical or disc-shaped particles with diﬀerent orientations are evaluated for some combinations of transformation strain and
matrix plastic strain. The anisotropy in elastic constants as well as the diﬀerence in elastic constant between the particles and matrix
are taken into account. It is found that the elastic energy becomes minimum when c
0 particles adopt a certain shape and direction with
respect to the elongation axis Æ001 æ. These results are in agreement with the rafted morphologies observed in crept superalloys. The onset
of the shearing of rafted c0 particles, leading to accelerated creep of the alloy, is also discussed by examining the stress state of rafted c0
particles.

1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are biphasic compounds, consist-
ing of a face-centred cubic c matrix and c0 precipitates.
The c0 precipitates having the L1 2 structure are stronger
than the c matrix at high temperatures. Thus, the creep
of c–c0 alloys is retarded by the c0 precipitate particles.
Rafting, which occurs during creep, also increases the creep
resistance of the c–c0 alloys. These factors give the c–c0
alloys unique high-temperature properties.
The morphology of the c0 precipitates strongly depends
on the thermomechanical history of an alloy. After a stan-
dard heat treatment (e.g. 1250 /C176 C/3 h/water quenched
+1100 /C176 C/1 h/air cooling + 850 /C176 C/24 h/air cooling for
CMSX-4 alloy), the microstructure consists of cuboidal c
0
particles almost periodically arranged along the Æ100 æ crys-
tallographic directions [1]. During a creep test, the c0 parti-
cles coarsen along a preferential crystallographic direction
[2]. The direction and shape of the precipitates depend,
among other parameters, on both the signs of the misﬁt
strain between the c and c
0 phases and the applied creep
stress [3,4].
Several theories have been proposed to explain rafting
or a shape change of particles occurring under an external
applied stress [5–9]. However, the rafting of c0 particles can
also occur under no external stress, if an alloy is pre-
strained and subsequently annealed [10,11]. This means
that the rafting is primarily caused by the residual stress
brought about by matrix plastic deformation, which should
have a similar eﬀect to that induced by the early stages of
creep. The residual stress and its associated elastic energy
can relax during further annealing at high temperature by
a change in the morphology of the c
0 particles. The present
paper proposes that this relaxation of the residual stress is

4. Discussion
The earliest work discussing the shape change of a c0
particle is probably by Pineau [5]. Pineau considered only
the misﬁt due to precipitation in addition to the inhomoge-
neity eﬀect on the basis of isotropic elasticity, when a stress
is applied. Two terms used by Pineau ( ninh and nint to
account for the inhomogeneity eﬀect and interaction
between inhomogeneities and applied stress, respectively)
play no role in the present context. A more sophisticated
theory was advanced by Johnson and Laughlin by taking
into account elastic anisotropy [6]. However, this theory
did not consider the role of plastic strain in the matrix.
As mentioned in Section 1, the main concern in the present
study is the eﬀect of plastic strain in the matrix and elastic

Buﬃere and Ignat proposed a dislocation-based
approach to understand rafting in a c–c
0 alloy [12]. Even
though we have used the inclusion theory of a misﬁtting
particle, it must be understood that a particle is sur-
rounded by dislocations [14]. Instead of calculating the
elastic state from a discrete dislocation method, we have
used the inclusion theory which gives a solution in closed
form. The assumed misﬁt strain produced by plastic
deformation corresponds to smearing out the dislocations
on the c/c
0 interfaces. The energy calculation, which is
essential to discuss shape change, is also simpler. Indeed,
using the inclusion theory, the ambiguity concerning the
argument in the logarithmic term to describe the energy
of a dislocation is avoided. We also point out that all
the surfaces of a particle are surrounded by dislocations,
since there is no reason why dislocations are present only
on particular interfaces. Even when a shape change
occurs, dislocations can never be annihilated from the
interfaces, except when two neighbouring c

combine to form a larger particle. This applies particu-
larly when the volume fraction of particles is large. In this
case, the dislocations on interface A of a particle annihi-

A further important point underlining the role of matrix
plastic strain in the shape change of the c
0 particles is to be
mentioned. When this plastic deformation occurs, the c0
particles are surrounded by Orowan dislocation loops.
The diﬀusion along the cores of dislocations is faster than
interfacial or bulk diﬀusion. Note that since the c/c
0 inter-
face is coherent, the interfacial diﬀusivity must be almost
equal to the bulk diﬀusivity. In other words, the disloca-
tions left around a c
0 particle after matrix plastic deforma-
tion enhance a change in the particle shape by providing
fast diﬀusion paths.
The shape change proposed in the present study is diﬀer-
ent from that studied for stress relaxation in a dispersion-
strengthened alloy [22]. When a second-phase particle is
stressed, an energy decrease can be achieved by the forma-
tion of misﬁt caused by diﬀusion. Suppose a second phase
is under tension along a certain direction, say the longitu-
dinal direction, and under compression along its lateral
directions. If diﬀusion occurs so that a positive misﬁt along
the longitudinal direction and negative misﬁts along the
lateral directions are induced, the potential energy of the
above stresses decreases. However, this formation of misﬁt
induces a coherency loss at the matrix/particle interface.
Thus, it can occur in such a material as a dispersion-
strengthened alloy, in which the interfaces are incoherent
from the beginning. Contrary to this, in the c–c
0 alloy,

We also discuss why the dislocation loops surrounding

the case of Eq. (3). Therefore, the symmetric uniaxial

That is, a network of the dislocations is formed at the
c/c

segments combine and have a combined Burgers vectors.
This is because the energy associated with dislocations can
be reduced by some combinations. This network structure
makes the movement, involving climb, of dislocations
diﬃcult.
Finally, we discuss the origin of the high strength of a
c–c
0 alloy and the reason for the breakdown of retarded
creep in the alloy. First, it must be noticed that c0 precip-
itates endure high stress, making Ni superalloys excellent
high creep strength materials. This is simply because c0
particles do not contain dislocations. If they do, anti-
phase boundaries exist in the particles, increasing the
energy. This implies that, when c
0 particles are formed
by a precipitation process, they avoid pre-existing lattice
dislocations. When the stress produced by the matrix plas-
tic deformation becomes large enough, almost equal to
the theoretical strength of the c
0 phase, the c0 particles
can undergo plastic deformation. This is the start of
shearing of the c
0 particles. The stress state for this pro-
cess is estimated below.
The data reported by Reed et al. [21] show that the plas-
tic deformation of rafted c0 particles occurs when the over-
all creep strain is about 1.1%. The corresponding plastic
strain in the matrix can be calculated to be approximately
5.0% from Eq. (11). Using Eq. (8), the stress due to plastic

5. Conclusions
In order to understand the rafting that occurs in a Ni
superalloy, the plastic strain has been introduced into the
c matrix and the elastic energy change has been calculated
when the shape of c
0 precipitates changes during anneal-
ing. The inclusion theory with anisotropic elasticity has
been used to evaluate the stress state and the associated
elastic energy caused by a change in the shape of the c
0 pre-
cipitates. The energetic analysis shows that rafting occurs
depending on the sign and magnitude of both precipitation
misﬁt and plastic strains. The raft shapes and directions
predicted by this analysis agree with previous experimental
observations. Using an estimation of the stress state after
the rafting, the onset of the accelerated creep due to shear-
ing of c
0 particles is discussed.
