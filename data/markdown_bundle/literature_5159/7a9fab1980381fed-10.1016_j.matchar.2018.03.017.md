Abstract 
The uniaxial mechanical properties of the high entropy alloy (HEA) 
Fe40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 doped with 1.1 at. % C have been determined for both the as -
cast and recrystallized state s at temperatures from 77-973 K. Very high yield strength s 
were obtained at 77K of 9 28 MPa for the as -cast HEA and 1037 MPa for the 
recrystallized HEA. The yield strength decreased sharply to 293 K and more slowly to 
973K, where it was ~200 MPa [11] in both conditions. The lattice fr iction stress, which 
results from the increase of the Peierls barrier, was the major contributor to the large 
yield strength at 77 K. On the other hand , the grain boundaries lose the ir strengthening 
ability at a high temperature, which largely account s for the sharp drop in yield strength 
of the fine-grained (5 µm) recrystallized HEA at 973 K . The elongation to failure was 
highest at 293 K and 673 K with the lowest values at 77 K. Transmission electron 
microscopy was used to study the effects of temperature on the deformation mechanisms 
of the HEAs, especially the temperature dependence of the microband-induced plasticity 
effect. At temperatures ranging from 77 K to 673 K microbands form in the as-cast HEA, 
while dislocation clusters were observed in the recrystallized HEA over this temperature

1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new class of materials that are developed based on 
maximization of the configurational entropy [1–6]. In previous studies , we designed a 
series of low -cost, carbon-doped Fe 40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEAs with excellent room 
temperature mechanical properties, e.g., the ultimate tensile strength of 5 µm grain-sized 
HEA is over 1 GPa with an elongation of 27 %, which outperforms most advanced steels 
[7–9]. A novel microband -induced plasticity (MBIP) effect wa s found in the carbon -
doped HEAs, leading to a disruption of the strength and ductility trade -off relationship 
[7]. 
Microbands have also been observed in aluminum alloys [10,11], lightweight 
austenitic steels [12–16], duplex steels [17,18], a two-phase f.c.c./b.c.t. C -doped 
Fe36Ni18Mn33Al13 alloy [19], and in a FeCoCrNiMo2.3 HEA [20]. Hughes [11] proposed 
that the formation of microbands in Al-Mg alloys resulted from grain subdivision. During 
deformation, the grain subdivide s into several misoriented domains to accommodate the 
plastic strain with microbands forming at the domain boundaries. Later, Welsch et al. [14] 
studied the dislocation substructure in lightweigh t steels at different tensile strains and 
suggested that the microbands developed from the slip bands filled with dislocations via 
planar slip. Recent observations by Wang et al. [8] showed that microbands were not 
present in the 1.1 at. % carbon-doped FeNiMnAlCr HEA when the grain size was 
reduced to 5 µm, since grain subdivision was inhibited by both the grain boundaries and 
precipitates, indicating that grain subdivision is critical for the formation of microbands. 
Microband-induced plasticity ( MBIP), twinning -induced plasticity (TWIP) and 
transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) are attractive strain hardening mechanism s that

have been found in HEAs. The TWIP and TRIP effects are extremely sensi tive to the 
testing temperature [21–26]. Deformation twin ning was observed at a n early stage of 
deformation in a f.c.c. CrCoFeMnNi HEA tested at 77 K [24], while for the room 
temperature testing, twinning occurred only close to the fracture strain [27]. Cryogenic 
testing also promoted the formation of deformation -induced martensite in Fe -Mn based 
high entropy steels, leading to both high strength and high ductility [26]. However, no 
study has been performed to investigate the influence of temperature on the formation of 
microbands. 
Therefore, in this study, tensile and compression tests were conducted over the 
temperature range 77 - 973 K on a 1.1 at. % carbon-doped Fe40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEA 
in both the as-cast and recrystallized states. These two states provide large -grained (118 
µm) and fine-grained (5 µm) material, respectively, and, thus, enable the effects of grains 
size on the deformation behavior to be studied. The temperature-dependence of the yield 
strength is analyzed in terms of the underlying strengthening mechanisms. The 
dislocation substructures of the mechanically tested specimens were examined to clarify 
the influence of temperature on the MBIP effect. 
 
2. Experimental 
~65g ingots of the 1.1 at. % carbon-doped Fe40.4Ni113Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEA were produced 
by using arc -melting: detailed procedures can be found in [5]. Some ingots were cold-
rolled to a reduction in thickness of 70 % with a reduction of ~5 % per pass, followed by 
annealing at 1273 K for 1 h.

A FEI XL -30 field emission gun (FEG) scanning electron microscope (SEM) in 
backscattered electron (BSE) mode and a FEI Tecnai F20 FEG transmission electron 
microscope (TEM) were employed to examine the microstructures. The operating voltage 
was 15 kV for the SEM and 200 kV for the TEM. The sample preparation procedures for 
the SEM and TEM are described elsewhere [5]. The melting point of the HEA w as 
measured using a NETZSCH 404 C differential scanning calorimeter (DSC). 
Flat dog-bone-type specimens with a gauge length of 10 mm, width of ~2.6 mm 
and thickness of ~1.2 mm were produced for tensile tests. Specimens were polished to a 
mirror surface usin g silicon carbide papers and finished with 0.3 µm alumina powder. 
Tensile tests were condu cted at temperatures of 77K, 293 K, 673 K, and 973 K with an 
initial strain rate of 5 × 10-4 s-1. Two specimens were tested at 77 K, and three specimens 
were tested a t the other temperatures. For the cryogenic tensile tests, the specimen and 
grips were cooled down using liquid nitrogen for about 10 -15 min before the start of the 
test and kept immersed in a liquid nitrogen bath during the test. A furnace was used to 
heat the specimen and grips during the high temperature tensile tests at 673 K and 973 K. 
Since fracture occurred at low strains at 77 K and 973 K during tensile testing, 
compression tests were also performed at these temperatures at an initial strain rat e of 5 
× 10-4 s-1 on 3 mm diameter , 6 mm high cylinders. Two specimens were tested for each 
condition. The strain was measured from the length of specimens before and after 
mechanical testing using an optical microscope. The s econdary electron (SE) imaging 
mode was employed in the SEM to examine the fracture surfaces of the tensile -tested 
specimens, and the TEM was employed to characterize the dislocation substructures of 
the fractured tensile specimens.

3. Results 
The BSE images in Fig. 1 show the micr ostructures of the as-cast and recrystallized 
HEA. The HEA is single f.c.c. phase in the as -cast state with a grain size of 118 µm (see 
Fig. 1a) ; the dark points in Fig. 1a were Al -rich nitride particles based on previous 
analysis [7]. After the thermo-mechanical treatment, the HEA had recrystallized to a 
much finer grain size of 5 µm, as shown in Fig. 1b. Precipitates were also formed during 
the annealing. In our earlier work , we, showed using TEM, that both M23C6 and M 7C3 
precipitates with an average size of 141 nm and volume fraction of 13.4 % are present 
[8]. 
DSC measurements were performed to determine the melting temperature of the 
HEA. Fig. 2 shows the DSC curves of the HEA in the as -cast state. The start temperature 
of the endothermic melting peak was the melting point. Based on the DSC curve in Fig.2, 
the melting temperature (Tm) for the HEA is 1465 K. 
Fig. 3 shows typical engineering stress -strain curves for the as-cast and 
recrystallized HEA obtained under tension at four testing temperatures. The data for 
293 K is from reference [8]. The yield strength and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) 
decreased with increasing temperature for the HEA in both states. No serrations are 
present in the stress-strain curves of tests at any temperature. There is no strain hardening 
at the highest test temperature of 973 K for both HEAs. At this temperature, the 
recrystallized HEA shows work softening due to the grain boundary sliding, similar to the 
CoCrFeMnNi HEA when tested at 1073 K [24]. The tensile strength for the recrystallized 
HEA reached its maximum value after a few percent strain at 973 K, and then the 
engineering stress continued to decrease until fracture occurred at 40 % strain.

4. Discussion 
4.1 Yield strength 
As shown in Fig. 4, the yield strength of both the as-cast and the recrystallized HEAs 
increases with decreasing testing temperature, a feature that has been observed before in 
f.c.c. HEAs [24,28]. In order to understand such a change in yield strength ( σy), the 
strengthening mechanisms can be analyzed individually, i.e.: 
σy = σfr + σgb + σppt (1) 
where σfr is the lattice friction stress, σgb andσppt are the strengthening contributed from 
grain boundaries and the carbide precipitate s, respectively. The strengthening from the 
solid solution , including both interstitial and substitutional strengthening , is commonly 
folded into the lattice friction part. It should be noted that precipitate strengthening is 
only present in the recrystallized HEA, since the HEA in the as-cast state is single f.c.c. 
phase. Our previous work showed that the main strengthening effect in the recrystallized 
HEA results from grain boundaries, whereas the precipitate strengthening is relative 
small [8].

In the testing temperature range from 77 K to 673 K t he strengthening from the 
carbides (σppt) is largely independent of temperature since the testing temperatures is less 
than half the homologous melting point . T he strengthening from the grain boundar ies 
(σgb) has also been found to be insensitive to temperature for many f.c.c.-structured 
HEAs at less than half the homologous melting point [28]. Thus, the variation of yield 
strength in this temperature region mostly arises from the change in the temperat ure-
dependent lattice friction stress ( σfr ). By considering the Peierls -Nabarro stress, the 
lattice friction stress in the HEAs can be expressed as [28]: 
σfr=σp(0) ∗ exp( −T
C ) (2) 
where σp(0) is the Peierls stress at 0 K, T is the temperature, and C is a constant related 
to ω0 b⁄ (ω0 is the dislocation width at 0 K and b is the magnitude of the Burgers vector). 
The equation implies that the lattice stress increase with decreasing temperature. 
Wu et al. [28] obtained a good match by applying this equation to describe the 
variation of yield strength in f.c.c. equiatomic multicomponent alloys (from three to five 
components), yielding a value of ω0 = b for multicomponent alloys, and ω0 = 1.5b for 
pure Ni. The f.c.c. multicomponent alloys exhibit narrower dislocations than those of the 
pure f.c.c. metals, producing a much stronger temperature dependence of the lattice 
friction stress. 
In the temperatures range from 77 K to 673 K , the yield strength of the 
recrystallized HEA is higher than that of the as -cast HEA. When the testing temperature 
was increased to 973 K (0.66 Tm), however, the recrystallized HEA shows a similar yield 
strength to that of the as -cast HEA. Our earlier work investigated the effectiveness of 
underlying strengthening mechanism s for HEAs at room temperature [8]. The higher

4.2 Dislocation substructure and the temperature dependence of the MBIP effect 
In the as-cast HEA, microbands were o bserved at high strains after testing at 77 K , 
293 K, and 673 K, while no microbands were present after testing 973 K. Microbands 
originate from the slip of dislocation, and grain subdivision is essential for the formation 
of microbands. The lack of microbands at 973 K is because dislocation climb can take 
place due to the thermal activation, and grain subdivision is no longer occurs during 
deformation. Thus, an irregular substructure of tangled dislocations is formed. Also, the 
annihilation of dislocations is greater at such a high testing temperature (0.66 Tm) and 
tangled dislocations with much lower densities are present as shown in Fig. 5d1. 
MBIP produces similar strain hardening effects to twinning-induced plasticity 
(TWIP) and transformation -induced plasticity (TRIP) . The high strain harden ing can 
delay the occurrence of necking during tensile deformation, resulting in enhance d 
ductility. Both TWIP and TRIP effects show strong temperature dependence s [40–42, 45, 
46]. When the testing temperature is higher than 473 K, almost no deformation -induced 
martensitic phase transformation or twinning occurs, and dislocation slip is observed 
instead [30–32]. The stacking fault energy (SFE) increases with increasing temperature

[33,34], and as a result, the SFE is too high at 473 K to allow the occurrence of the TRIP 
and TWIP effect s [31]. Conversely, the occurrence of twining and martensite is 
remarkably enhanced at temperatures lower than room temperature, le ading to very high 
strain hardening and ductility [35,36]. 
As shown in Fig. 9, the MBIP effect extends over a wider range of temperature s 
(up to 673 K) than that of TWIP and TRIP effects, which can be ascribed to the entirely 
different formation mechanism of microbands. First of all, the SFE does not play a 
dominate role in the formation of microbands, since the MBIP effect has been seen in 
f.c.c. alloys with both high [14–16] and low [7,8,20] SFE. In addition, although the 
temperature increased to 673 K, the flow stress is similar to that at the room temperature 
of 293 K, which suggests th at the effect of thermal ly-assisted deformation is limited at 
673 K. Thus, grain subdivision is still necessary to accommodate the deformation, 
leading to the formation of microbands. The large temperature region for the MBIP effect 
offers more possibilities in the design of structural materials. 
In the recrystallized HEA, dislocation clusters are observed from 77 K to 673 K , 
as shown in Fig. 5. The transformation from a regular structure of microbands to irregular 
structure of dislocation clusters has been discussed in our previous work [8]. It is due to 
the inhibition of the grain subdivision resulting from grain boundaries and precipitates. 
When the testing temperature increases to 973 K, the recrystallized HEA shows similar 
tangled dislocations to the as-cast HEA due to thermally-activated deformation. 
The excellent mechanical properties of the carbon-doped HEA over a wide range 
of temperatures can expand their engineering applications. Of particular prominence are 
applications for HEAs in cryogenic environments. The C-doped Fe40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6

4.3 Ductility 
When the temperature was increased from 293 K to 673 K, the ductility for both the as-
cast and recrystallized HEA changes very little, since the strain hardening mechanisms 
remain the same for both alloys. When the testing temperature was increased to 973 K, 
microbands no longer form , resulting in a lack of work hardening. This means that any 
plastic flow instability will lead to failure, which is the cause of the sharp decrease of 
ductility in the as -cast HEA. The recrystallized HEA , however, shows larger tensile 
elongation than that of the as-cast HEA at 973 K, see Fig. 4c. This is because of the grain 
boundary sliding that occurs in the fine-grained (5 µm) HEA at 973 K [39]. 
When the temperature was decreased to 77 K, the as -cast HEA is very brittle, 
whereas the recrystallized HEA shows several percent elongation to failure , as shown in 
Fig. 4c. As noted earlier, the lattice friction stress exhibits a substantial increase at 77 K, 
which strongly impedes the mo vement of dislocations. This led to the as -cast HEA 
becoming very brittle at 77 K and fracture occurred at yielding during the tensile test. It is 
worth noting that, in contrast to the as -cast HEA, the recrystallized HEA exhibits 11% 
tensile elongation at 77 K. Due to the formation of carbides, the carbon content in the 
f.c.c. matrix decreased from 1.1 at. % in the as-cast HEA to 0.8 at. % in the recrystallized 
HEA, which caused a 67 MPa reduction in the lattice friction stress at 293 K [8]. 
Consequently, the lattice friction stress is lower for the recrystallized HEA at 77 K, 
resulting in lower resistance to the dislocation slip. More importantly, it has been found

5. Conclusions 
The mechanical properties and dislocation substructure evolution for a s-cast and 
recrystallized 1.1 at. % C-doped Fe40.4Ni113Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEAs in the temperature range 
of 77 - 973 K were investigated. The main conclusions are: 
1. The yield strength of both as -cast and recrystallized HEAs decreases sharply with 
increasing testing temperatur e. The strong temperature dependence of yield 
strength from 77 K to 973 K is ascribed to the thermally-induced variation of the 
lattice friction stress. However, at 973 K, the weakness of the grain boundar ies 
leads to a further decrease in the yield strength for the recrystallized HEA. 
2. For the tensile tested as-cast HEA, very brittle behavior is observed at 77 K since 
dislocation movement is inhibited, whereas microbands are found at both 293 K 
and 673 K, and tangled dislocations are present at 973 K. 
3. For the tensile-tested recrystallized HEA, dislocations clusters were observed 
from 77- 673 K, while at 973 K only tangled dislocation were present.
