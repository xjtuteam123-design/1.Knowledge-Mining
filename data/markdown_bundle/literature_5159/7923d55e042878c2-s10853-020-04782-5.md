ABSTRACT
A diffusion-barrier-contained Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating was prepared by
ﬁrstly composite electroplating and then aluminising. The TEM study of the
microstructure showed that Hf serves as a solid solution in (Ni,Pt)Al lattice and
forms HfO 2 in the Hf-rich zone. This HfO 2 is formed in situ during alumini-
sation under low oxygen pressure. It acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent the
inter-diffusion during cyclic oxidation. In comparison with (Ni,Pt)Al coating,
the oxide scale on Hf-dope (Ni,Pt)Al is more adherent. Moreover, the surface
rumpling extent is much relieved due to slower Al depletion rate and higher
creep resistance by Hf addition.
Introduction
Single-crystal superalloy possesses attractive creep
and fatigue strength. It is extensively used as struc-
tural materials in key hot end components, such as
blades and vanes of modern aeroplane engines and
marine turbine engines [ 1, 2]. However, due to the
inability to form protective oxide scale, the service
life of blades and vanes is restricted by lack of high-
temperature oxidation and corrosion resistance [ 3].
The Pt-modiﬁed B2 structured b-NiAl, normally
(Ni,Pt)Al, is a primary alumina former, which is a
widely used coating on Ni-based superalloy to
improve high-temperature oxidation and corrosion
performance [4, 5]. When (Ni,Pt)Al coating is applied
as bond coat (BC) beneath the thermal barrier coating
(TBC), the adhesion of the thermally grown oxide
(TGO) scale is of particular importance. This is
because failure of TBCs is often initiated at the TGO/
BC interface [ 6–10].
In order to enhance the adhesion of oxide scale
formed on b-NiAl, a small amount of Hf was added
in the coating. It remarkably reduces oxide growth
rate and promotes the formation of a more adherent
oxide scale [ 11, 12]. According to our previous work
[13], an addition of 0.05 at.% Hf into (Ni,Pt)Al coating
can decrease the alumina scale parabolic growth rate
by a factor of ten. The same results have been

The work of Wang [ 15] reported that a rapid oxide

optimised in the coating and usually controlled
below 0.1 at.% [ 17].
Another challenge in (Ni,Pt)Al coating is inter-
diffusion of elements between substrate and coating.
This causes the formation of a thick inter-diffusion
zone (IDZ) and a secondary reaction zone (SRZ)
[18, 19]. They are detrimental to the mechanical
properties of the substrates [ 20]. In addition, it will
also lead to an unnecessary aluminium loss and
decrease the service life of the coatings [ 21, 22].
To prevent or mitigate inter-diffusion, various
diffusion barriers, such as TiN, AlN, SiC, Al–O–N,
Ni–Cr–O, Al 2O3, Ir, Re and W [ 23–29], have been
investigated. However, for coatings prepared by
aluminising, Ni atoms from the substrate are
required to participate in reactions for coating
growth. Hence, the pre-existing aforementioned dif-
fusion barrier will obstruct the coating preparation.
Therefore, a suitable diffusion barrier for diffusion
coatings should be formed in situ at the interface
during coating preparation.
Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating was prepared by
composite electroplating of Pt ? Hf and then alu-
minising in our previous work [ 13]. A Hf-rich zone
formed between the outer (Ni,Pt)Al coating and
inter-diffusion zone, which served as a diffusion
barrier during high-temperature exposure. In addi-
tion, a trace of Hf diffused into the outer (Ni,Pt)Al
coating and signiﬁcantly improved the oxidation
performance. Theoretically, this is an ‘ ‘ideal’ ’ diffu-
sion coating because it possesses superior oxidation
resistance and diffusion barrier effect simultaneously.
However, its cyclic oxidation performance remains
unclear. The microstructure of this Hf-rich zone and
its mechanisms of inhibition diffusion are still not
well understood neither. Therefore, the current work
is endeavouring to investigate the microstructure of
the Hf-rich zone as well as the outer Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)
Al zone by TEM. The diffusion barrier effect of Hf-
rich zone during the coating preparation and cyclic
oxidation are analysed and discussed. Besides, the
mechanism of Hf on mitigating surface rumpling is
also discussed by analysing the residual stress and
the creep resistance of the coating.
Experimental
Coating preparation
A home-made nickel-based single-crystal superalloy
was used as the substrate material, of which the
nominal composition is shown in Table 1. Cylindrical
specimens with dimension of U 15 9 2 mm were
machined along the [001]-orientated crystal axis. The
substrate was grounded down to a ﬁnal 400# SiC
sandpaper and then blasted humidly using alumina
grit (300 mesh in size). Prior to electroplating, pre-
treatments were conducted to degrease contamina-
tions introduced during the cutting and grounding
process. The pre-treatments include boiling in NaOH
solution (50 g L -1) for 10 min and cleaning in acetone
and ethanol ultrasonically for 15 min, respectively.
A Pt layer with 5 lm in thickness was electroplated
with acid solution composing mainly of K 2-
[Pt(NO2)2SO4]. Pt ? Hf composite plating layer with
the same thickness was electroplated by adding
micro-sized metallic hafnium (0.1–10 lm in diameter)
particles into the Pt-plating solution. As the com-
mercially available hafnium particle is 99.5 wt% and
500 mesh in size, the hafnium particles were grinded
in an agate container ﬁlled with agate balls for about
1 week. In order to dilute Pt content in the surface,
annealing treatment (600 /C176C for 2 h and 1050 /C176C for
1 h with the vacuum lower than \ 6 9 10-3 Pa) was
conducted prior to aluminising (naming ‘ ‘prior
annealing’ ’ hereafter). Aluminising treatment was
conducted at 1060 /C176C for 6 h with the samples
hanging above the aluminising pack. Powder of Fe-
49 wt% Al was used as the Al source and NH 4Cl
(2.0 wt%) was used as the activator for aluminising.

Cyclic oxidation test
Cyclic oxidation performance was conducted in static
air at 1100 /C176C by a horizontal tube furnace equipped
with an automatic lift. A complete cycle includes
maintaining coating specimens inside the furnace for
50 min and then taking out from the furnace with air
cooling for 10 min. The net weight of the coated
samples was traced by an electronic balance (BP211D,
Sartorius, Germany) with sensitivity of 0.01 mg after
given cycles.

Figure 5a shows the mass change curve of Hf-doped
(Ni,Pt)Al coating in comparison with simple (Ni,P-
t)Al coating during cyclic oxidation at 1100 /C176C. It can
be noticed from the inserted ﬁgure that the ﬁrst mass

contrast, the ﬁrst mass loss for Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al
coating appears after 420 cycles, but a continuous
mass gain follows until the end of the test.
The XRD patterns after cyclic oxidation for 500
cycles are shown in Fig. 5b. Exclusive a-Al2O3 forms
both for Hf-dope (Ni,Pt)Al coating and (Ni,Pt)Al
coating. But the phase constitute for the inner coating

degenerated c/c0 phase peaks remain for (Ni,Pt)Al
coating. This indicates that more Al is consumed in
(Ni,Pt)Al coating after experiencing the same
exposure.
Figure 6 shows the surface and cross-sectional

In order to distinguish different phases clearly in

Discussion
Formation and microstructure characters
of Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating
As described in ‘ ‘ Experimental’ ’ section, the Hf-
doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating was prepared by Pt ? Hf
composite electroplating and aluminising. The Hf
particles added in the electroplating solution are
metallic. But HfO 2 was found in the Hf-rich zone
according to Fig. 4. Considering the processes of
coating preparation, the metallic Hf particles may
possibly be oxidised in three ways: ﬁrstly, it may be
oxidised during the long-time and high-speed
grinding or during its immersion in the acidulous
electroplating solution with sustained current ﬂow;
another possibility is the oxidation occurring during
the aluminising process. However, the XRD patterns
of the Hf particles after grinding and electroplating
show that only the diffraction peaks of the metallic

metallic Hf is oxidised during aluminisation.
In the aluminising process, the sample chamber
was ﬁrstly vacuumed using a mechanical pump and
then ﬁlled with Ar gas before heat to the aluminising
temperature. Thus, a low content of oxygen may still
be residual. Metals, like Ni, Al and Cr, are inertial
under such low oxygen pressure even at high tem-
perature [ 32]. However, a relatively high chemical
afﬁnity exists between Hf and O, which entitles Hf to
be oxidised under low oxygen pressure [ 33–35]. As a
result, HfO2 forms in situ between the outer (Ni,Pt)Al
coating and inter-diffusion zone during aluminising
(zone II in Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, a small amount of Hf
atoms diffuses outwardly along with Ni, Pt and other
atoms (atoms contained in the substrate, such as Cr,
Co) to the outer coating (zone I in Fig. 1a). They serve
as solid solution in NiAl lattice. However, the diffu-
sivity and solubility of hafnium in the b-NiAl are low
[36]. Thus, very limited hafnium could transport to
the outer (Ni,Pt)Al zone, which avoids excessive Hf
containing in (Ni,Pt)Al coating.
When a third atom is doped in NiAl phase, the
NiAl lattice will be distorted. According to the ﬁrst
principle predication [ 37, 38], Hf trends to substitute
Al, while Pt prefers to replace Ni in the B2 structured
NiAl lattice. The atomic radius of Hf and Al is 1.55 A ˚
and 1.25 A˚, respectively [ 39]. When one position of
Al is replaced by Hf in NiAl lattice, a strong distor-
tion will be generated. In comparison, the radius
difference between Pt and Ni is much smaller, which
causes less lattice distortion accordingly [ 39, 40].
The d-spacing in (110) face of the (Ni,Pt)Al is about
1.47% larger in comparison with the stoichiometric
B2 structured b-NiAl, while for the malposed zone,
an elongation of 6.37% is observed in d-spacing due
to substitution of Hf in Al position. Nevertheless, the
concentration of Hf in the outer coating is low. Hf
atoms cannot distribute homogenously in every NiAl
lattice. As a result, lattice with Hf atoms doping
become larger. When the X-ray passes through these
lattices, the diffraction spot may deviate slightly from
the regular diffraction spot. And the decentralised
diffraction patterns are generated, as in Fig. 1c.
Diffusion barrier effect of the Hf-rich zone
on the coating formation process and cyclic
oxidation exposure
Diffusion barrier effect of Hf-rich zone on the coating
formation process
The diffusion barrier effect of Hf was once reported
by He [ 41]. They found that the inter-diffusion zone
(IDZ) disappeared and coating became much thinner
when a rehafnising step was conducted before alu-
minising. They ascribed this phenomenon to the
formation of Hf-rich phases such as Hf 2Ni7 during
the rehafnising step. Haynes et al. [ 42] also tried to
prevent the inter-diffusion by introducing a Hf-rich
layer via magnetron sputter. However, little diffusion
blocking effect was observed with the Hf ﬁlm thinner
than 1 lm. When the Hf ﬁlm was thickened, catas-
trophic oxide spallation take place because of the
formation of HfO 2 in the oxide scale. Moreover, the
inter-diffusion behaviour is not affected when Hf
only distributes in outer coating [ 15].
For the current work, the Pt- and Pt ? Hf-plating
layer was annealed in vacuum to dilute the content of
Pt on the surface. During this prior annealing pro-
cess, elements from the substrate diffused outwardly
and Pt/Hf diffused in the opposite direction. The
surface composition of Pt- and Pt ? Hf-plating layer
after annealing is listed in Table 2. It can be noted
from Table 2 that a signiﬁcant difference exists in the
content of Ni and Pt between the two layers. The
content of elements from substrate like Ni, Co, Cr and
Ti is lower, while Pt content is higher in Pt ? Hf-
plating layer.
However, there is almost no difference in the
thickness (Fig. 1a) and the surface composition
(Table 2) of the two coatings. Figure 10 shows the

Effect of HfO 2 belt on the cyclic oxidation
Theoretically, inter-diffusion will also occur at high-
temperature exposure due to the difference compo-
sition of the coating and substrate. It leads to a phase
transformation from b to c0, which begins in the IDZ
and then gradually shifts into the outer coating [ 27].
However, a strip of b phase reserves at the top of IDZ
for Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating after cyclic oxidation
at 1100 /C176C for 200 cycles (Fig. 6c). In contrast, only c0/
c remains for (Ni,Pt)Al coating (Fig. 6d).
The above difference in coating’s evolution may
stem from the different diffusion behaviour of Ni and
Al between coating and substrate. A schematic is
illustrated in Fig. 11 to explain the phase evolution
process of the two coatings. Firstly, topologically
close-packed (TCP) phase precipitates in the IDZ for
both (Ni,Pt)Al and Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating after
aluminising (stage I in Fig. 11). When the coatings are
exposed to high-temperature and oxidative environ-
ment, Al is consumed by not only outwardly diffus-
ing to form oxide scale, but also inwardly diffusing to
the substrate. As a result, phase transition from b to c0
occurs under the oxide scale as well as in IDZ. A
interface of c0/b generates hence as illustrated at
stage II in Fig. 11. With the prolongation of oxidation
time, the Al content in IDZ is too low to sustain a
stable b phase. And the interface of c0/b moves
towards the coating gradually. For Hf-doped (Ni,P-
t)Al coating, the existence of the HfO 2 belt near the
original surface effectively blocks the diffusion both
of Al and Ni. This slows down the inter-diffusion
process between the coating and substrate and more
Al reserves in the IDZ. Therefore, the interface of c0/b
moves more slowly in Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating
(stage II in Fig. 11). When Al is further depleted,
interface of c0/b moves across the original surface of
the substrate for (Ni,Pt)Al coating. Instead, the
interface of c0/b in Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al still stays
beneath the original surface and a substantial of b
phase remains as shown in Fig. 6d and illustrated in
Fig. 11 (stage III).

widely studied by Tolpygo [ 43, 45–48], which mainly

and so on. The primary factors that drive surface

the TGO and thermal expansion mismatch strains
between the substrate/BC/TGO system [ 47, 49].
When the B2 structured NiAl coatings are exposed to
high-temperature oxidative atmosphere, phase tran-

transformation. For the Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating,
the addition of Hf effectively lowers the oxide growth
rate and blocks the inter-diffusion. Consequently, the
phase transformation process is slowed down.

Conclusion
The microstructure of the Hf-rich zone and the outer
(Ni,Pt)Al zone of Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating is
analysed by TEM in the current work. The diffusion
barrier effect of the Hf-rich zone during coating
preparation and cyclic oxidation exposure are dis-
cussed. The mechanism why Hf addition could alle-
viate surface rumpling is proposed. Based on the
experimental results and discussion, the following
conclusion can be drawn:
1. Hf serves as a solid solution in (Ni,Pt)Al coating
and forms HfO 2 in the Hf-rich zone after
aluminising;
2. Metallic Hf particles in the Pt ? Hf composite
plating layer partly inhibit the diffusion of Ni and
Pt during prior annealing, but it does not restrain
the diffusion of Al during aluminising;
3. Phase transformation from b to c0 in the outer
(Ni,Pt)Al zone for Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating is
retarded due to low oxidation rate and more
adherent oxide scale;
4. Phase transformation from b to c0 in the IDZ is
slowed down in Hf-doped (Ni,Pt)Al coating
owing to the diffusion barrier effect of Hf-rich
zone;
5. The lower surface rumpling in Hf-doped (Ni,P-
t)Al coating than that in (Ni,Pt)Al coating is
attributed to its less residual stress accumulation
and higher creep resistance.
