abstract
The as-cast and heat-treated microstructures and high temperature creep properties have been in-
vestigated in four experimental Ni-based single crystal superalloys containing various levels of Hf ad-
dition (0–0.4 wt%) and B addition (0 –0.02 wt%). The experimental results indicated that the creep rupture
life showed an improvement with individual addition of Hf, but it was decreased with individual addition
of B. The elemental partitioning ratio and interfacial dislocation spacing of γ/γ′ were obviously changed
with individual Hf or B additions. Meanwhile, the formation of secondary phases, such as the blocky MC
carbide, script-like shape M
3B2 phases, was observed in the creep samples, which was also closely related
to the high temperature creep behaviors. The high volume fraction of residual ( γþ γ′) eutectics was
mainly attributed to the signi ﬁcant decrease of creep rupture life for the present experimental alloy
containing both Hf and B additions. This study is helpful to better understand Hf and B's role of
strengthening mechanism and to optimize Hf and B additions in single crystal superalloys.

1. Introduction
Single crystal (SX) superalloys are considered to be major ma-
terials for advanced areo-engine turbine blades. Advanced Ni-
based SX superalloys typically contain signi ﬁcant levels of re-
fractory alloying additions such as Re, W, Ta and Mo in order to
achieve a remarkable creep resistance at elevated temperature.
However, during solidi ﬁcation of complex airfoil shapes of hollow
turbine blades made by these superalloys, it is challenging to
maintain uniform thermal gradients to yield a monolithic single
crystal around variable cross-sections such as platforms and
shrouds [1]. Therefore, there is a high incidence of grain boundary
defects during the single crystal casting process and as a result,
manufacturing cost is high due to poor eligibility rates. In SX su-
peralloys, minor elements such as C, B and Hf have generally been
excluded, since their additions could introduce potential detri-
mental effects (e.g. lower incipient melting temperatures or crack
initiation from carbides) [2]. Nowadays, in some alloys, minor
elements were re-introduced in order to render the superalloys
more tolerant to problems related to grain boundary defects with
the objective of reducing the number of rejected blades [3,4].
Previous work has largely concentrated on grain boundary
strengthening effect of these three minor elements (C, B and Hf)
on the mechanical properties of grain boundary. Signi ﬁcant im-
provement of tolerance to high angle grain boundaries with minor
element additions of C, B and Hf have been observed in these al-
loys [3,4]. However, it is also important to thoroughly examine the
inﬂuence of these elements on the response to heat treatment,
microstructures and mechanical properties compared with the
base alloy from which they are evolved. Creep is often identi ﬁed as
the main cause of failures in gas turbine components, and time
dependent strain occurring at high temperature and low stress is a
key design criterion for superalloys [5]. Early work suggested that
the creep strength of high temperature low stress could be ob-
viously in ﬂuenced by additions of altogether C, B with Hf or in-
dependent C. Chen reported that shorter creep life was obtained at
1050 °C/152 MPa due to detrimental in ﬂuence of eutectic regions
that contained large, irregular and limited rafted
γ′ by altogether C,
B and Hf additions in alloy RR2072 [2]. Liu et al. reported that
longer creep rupture lives were obtained at 1038 °C/172 MPa with
the addition of 0.015 wt% and 0.05 wt% C, which was due to the
combined effects of the bene ﬁcial effect of the M 6C carbides and
the reduction of casting pores with increasing C content [6].
However, to this day, evolution of the microstructures in SX su-
peralloys with independent and interactive Hf and B effects and
the relationship between microstructures and mechanical prop-
erties are still unclear. Nevertheless, similar to the role in casting
polycrystalline superalloys, Hf and B additions may also promote

2. Experimental procedures
The current investigated alloy is a second generation SX su-
peralloy DD1 1, which was developed by the Beijing Institute of
Aeronautical Materials (BIAM), China. Four experimental alloys
with compositions listed in Table 1 were directionally solidi ﬁed

3.4. Creep properties
Fig. 7 a illustrates the creep strain curves of four alloys under
110 0°C/130 MPa. Fig. 7 b displays corresponding strain rate as a

similar to each other. All the creep curves exhibited distinctive

obtained through the comparison of the secondary stage creep

HfB. Table 7 lists the creep rupture life ( tR), the rupture strain ( εR)

4. Discussion
Hf and B are usually added to Ni-based SX superalloys to im-
prove the castability and the tolerance of low angle grain bound-
ary and [4,11 ]. However, there is no report regarding Hf or B in-
ﬂuencing the microstructures and creep properties of the matrix of
SX superalloys, to the current knowledge of the authors. A large
number of previous studies demonstrated that creep properties of
high temperature and low stress of SX superalloys mainly depend
on the γ′ size and volume fraction, γ channel width [12], γ/γ′
lattice misﬁt [13], solid-solution strengthening effect [14], residual
eutectics and carbides as well as borides [15,16]. The relationships
of these associated microstructural changes and the corresponding
creep properties of the investigated alloys are discussed as follows.
4.1. Strengthening behaviors
4.1.1. Effect of γ′ volume fraction, γ′ size and γ channel width
As precipitation strengthening alloys, γ′ precipitates coherently
embedded in the γ matrix are the primary strengthening phase in
Ni-based superalloys, and their creep properties are mainly de-
pendent on the volume fraction and size of γ′ precipitates. Mur-
akumo and co-authors reported that the creep life was longest in
alloy with about 65 –70% volume fraction of γ′ precipitates in both
900 °C/392 MPa and 1 100 °C/137 MPa [12]. The previous work on
CMSX-2 has shown that the creep behavior can be optimized by
choosing a precipitation heat treatment leading to cubical γ′ pre-
cipitates with a mean cube edge of approximately 0.45 mm [17].
Meanwhile, for the case where precipitates are stable in shape and
resist shearing, the Orowan resistance to bowing of dislocations
through the narrow matrix channels must be overcome. It can be

[21] and Carroll et al. [10] investigated the fourth generation SX
superalloys, and suggested that the more negative lattice mis ﬁt,
the ﬁner the interfacial dislocation networks were, and resulted in
the smaller the minimum creep rate during creep at high tem-
perature and low stress. Wang et al. [22] also found that more
negative lattice mis ﬁt, denser γ/γ′ interfacial dislocation networks
and higher microstructure stability, thus it can maintain a mini-
mum creep rate in the steady state stage in the SX superalloy.
In this study, with Hf addition, the elemental partitioning ratios
of Re, Mo and Cr were signi ﬁcantly increased, while Al, W, Co and
Ta were of no signi ﬁcant impact ( Fig. 6 ). The concentration of Re,
Mo and Cr in the
γ matrix was increased, and Re, Mo and Cr can
increase the lattice constant of γ phase, so that γ/γ′ lattice mis ﬁt
was more negative, giving rise to denser γ/γ′ interfacial dislocation
networks. In alloy B, the concentration of Re, Mo and Cr in the γ
matrix is slightly lower, and γ/γ′ lattice mis ﬁt decreases, resulting
in wider γ/γ′ interface average dislocation spacing. It is obvious
that, in alloys Hf and B, with the decrease of interfacial dislocation
spacing, creep rupture life linearly increases ( Fig. 8 ), this study is
consistent with previous report [21]. In alloy HfB, the concentra-
tion of Re, Cr and Mo in the γ matrix was between that of alloys B
and base alloy due to the combined effect of Hf and B. However,
the interfacial dislocation spacing of alloy HfB was not between
that of alloy B and base alloy. It was slightly wider than that of
alloy B, but the creep rupture life of alloy HfB was signi ﬁcantly
lower than that of the alloy B. Therefore, other factors should be
taken into account to explain the abnormality of the creep prop-
erties in alloy HfB.
4.1.3. Effect of solid-solution strengthening
In Ni-base SX superalloy, the precipitation strengthening of γ′ is
exploited to its maximum [12,17]. Thus, the solid-solution
strengthening of the soft γ matrix is of great interests to further
enhance the mechanical properties. Re, W, Cr and Mo are all ef-
fective solid-solution hardening elements [14]. The Re effect in Ni-
based superalloys can be explained by Re offering the strongest
solid-solution hardening ef ﬁciency in γ matrix by the reduction of
stacking fault energy and diffusivity as well as the highest parti-
tioning coef ﬁcient to the matrix [23]. With increasing the Cr
content, Re partitioned preferentially to the γ matrix, which in
turn resulted in more complete rafting structure and increased the
stress rupture life at 1 100 °C/140 MPa signi ﬁcantly [24]. Mo addi-
tion can increase the content of Re, Mo and Cr in the γ matrix, and
the time of forming the rafting structure is signi ﬁcantly enhanced,
increasing the stress rupture life [25]. It should be noted that, the
impact on creep properties of Re is largest at the same change of
concentration, due to Re has the most pronounced preference to
partition to the γ matrix of all elements normally alloyed to nickel-
base superalloys [23,26].

4.2. Fracture behaviors
In the tertiary creep regime, damage develops in the form of
cavities nucleating and growing from casting porosity and
secondary phases, such as TCP phase, carbides and borides [29].

creating local stress concentrations in the surrounding material
that can be relieved by decohesion of the matrix/particle interface
or by introducing more damage and providing additional sites for
crack formation and growth. Wilson et al. [15] suggested that the
large undissolved eutectics in the interdendritic regions degraded
the creep properties of CMSX-4. Kong et al. [16] presented that
large, irregular and partially rafted

blocky carbide particles are not detrimental to the creep behavior
[5]. Generally, Hf is a strong MC carbide forming element, and it
can control the content and morphology of MC carbides in Ni-
based superalloys. Chen et al. [2] found that Hf promoted forma-
tion of MC carbides, while improving the MC/matrix interfacial
energy, leading to more blocky morphology, which was less prone

5. Conclusions
In the present study, the effect of Hf and B on the as-cast, heat-
treated microstructures and creep properties under 1 100 °C/
130 MPa was investigated in four Ni-based SX superalloys con-
taining various levels of Hf addition (0 –0.4 wt%) and B addition (0 –
0.02 wt%). The conclusions can be summarized as follows:
(1) Hf addition enhanced the volume fraction of ( γþ γ′) eutectics
and MC carbides, and B addition increased the volume fraction
of ( γþγ′) eutectics and promoted the precipitation of the
script-like M 3B2 boride in as-cast and as heat-treated alloys.
Signiﬁcant increase in volume fraction of the ( γþγ′) eutectics
was observed in as-cast and as heat-treated alloys with both
additions of Hf and B.
(2) The volume fraction and size of γ′ precipitates as well as γ
channel width were not changed by Hf and B additions in four
alloys.
(3) Hf addition improved the creep properties due to the in-
creasing of γ/γ′ misﬁt and the solution-strengthening effect.
The small quantity of residual eutectics and blocky carbides
have negligible effect on the creep rupture life.
(4) B addition decreased the creep properties due to the de-
creasing of γ/γ′ misﬁt and the solution-strengthening effect.
The weakening effect of script-like M 3B2 phases has an ob-
vious impact on the decreasing of creep rupture life.
(5) When both Hf and B were added into the alloy, the creep
rupture properties were decreased signi ﬁcantly due to the
high volume fraction of residual ( γþγ′) eutectics, and the alloy
with both Hf and B additions did not comply with the linear
relationship of average interfacial dislocation spacing and
average creep rupture life.
