Microstructure and mechanical properties of directionally solidiﬁed high-Nb
containing Ti–Al alloys

abstract
Two high-Nb containing Ti–Al alloys, Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn were fabricated using direc-
tional solidiﬁcation. Their microstructures and mechanical properties at both room and high tempera-
tures were studied. Results showed that the addition of 1% Sn promoted the formation of laths and
contributed remarkably to the enhancement in room-temperature strength and high temperature ductil-
ity of Ti–Al alloy. The alloys exhibited the feature of quasi-cleavage fracture at room temperature and
they experienced signiﬁcant plastic deformation at high temperatures.

1. Introduction
In recent years, extensive investigations have been carried out
on Ti–Al–Nb ternary alloys, which are classiﬁed into two categories
dependent on the atomic contents of Al and Nb included in the al-
loys. The ﬁrst type is the ‘‘second generation” c-TiAl alloys with Al
content ranging from 42 to 49 at.% and Nb content ranging from 4
to 10 at.% [1–5]. Due to an improvement of high temperature
strength, creep properties and oxidation resistance, they have a po-
tential of application in aviation gas turbines and automotive en-
gines at higher temperatures than conventional TiAl alloys [6].
The second category is the Ti–Al–Nb alloys with Al concentrations
between 12 and 26 at.% and Nb concentrations between 22 and
38 at.%. They may have a mixed structure consisting of ordered
hcp
a2 phase (Ti 3Al), orthorhombic O phase (Ti 2AlNb), and/or bcc
B2 phase. Representative compositions receiving extensive atten-
tions are Ti–25Al–17Nb, Ti–22Al–33Nb, Ti–25Al–25Nb, and Ti–
23Al–27Nb. They are being developed for applications at elevated
temperatures due to their excellent creep resistance associated
with the signiﬁcant amount of O phase contained in these alloys
[7,8]. They are also being considered for replacing Ti–6Al–4V as
biomedical implant alloy [9]. Nevertheless, the above-mentioned
Ti–Al–Nb alloys exhibit fatal drawbacks as they display limited
ductility, and therefore are quite brittle at room temperature.
Unfortunately, almost no reports have been available up to date
concerning the ternary Ti–Al–Nb alloys with moderate content of
Al and Nb. The alloy composition relies on the concept that some
of the remaining pure Ti as the tough phase will undoubtedly en-
hance the ductility and fracture toughness of Ti–Al–Nb alloys. On
the other hand, previous investigations have demonstrated that
the fully lamellar structured
c-TiAl based alloys possess reasonable
yield strength and tensile elongation when the lamellar boundary
orientation is aligned parallel to the tensile direction by means of
directional solidiﬁcation process [10–13]. Moreover, it has been
shown that the room-temperature ductility and strength of Ti–
48 at.% Al alloy fabricated by arc melting can be improved by small
amount of Sn addition up to 1.0 at.% [14].
Hence, the objectives of this work are twofold: (1) to examine
the feasibility of directional solidiﬁcation used for processing of
Sn-doped Ti–Al–Nb alloys with moderate Al and Nb concentra-
tions, i.e. Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn and (2) to prelimi-
narily study the microstructure and mechanical properties of
aforementioned alloys.
2. Experimental procedures
The alloys used in this research were produced from 99.99 wt.%
Ti, 99.99 wt.% Al, 99.9 wt.% Nb and 99.9 wt.% Sn. Master ingots for
directional solidiﬁcation with nominal chemical compositions of
Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn, in atomic percent, were
ﬁrstly prepared in a vacuum electromagnetic induction furnace
with a water-cooled copper crucible. The resulting ingots were
re-melted and turned at least ﬁve times or more with the electro-
magnetic stirring effect to promote homogeneity of the
composition.
The ingots were re-melted in a vacuum induction furnace using
CaO crucible. Directional solidiﬁcation (DS) was carried out using
Ti–16Al–8Nb alloy as a seed material at and the water-cooled stage
was pulled downward at a withdrawal rate of 60 mm/h in a labo-
ratory scale vacuum furnace as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1 .
The furnace temperature was heated to the range of 1750–
1820 /C176C maintaining the vacuum at 1.0 Pa level. It is worth noting

3. Results
3.1. Microstructural characterization
Fig. 2 presents a pair of SEM backscattered-electron (BSE)
micrographs showing the microstructures of longitudinal sections
for the DS Ti–16Al–8Nb ( Fig. 2a) and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn (Fig. 2 b) al-
loys. The results obtained were somewhat unexpected but actually
present a vigorous case for the inﬂuence of trace impurity levels
and processing parameters on the microstructure of DS Ti–Al al-
loys. Both the two alloys consisted of a uniform basket-weave type
microstructure. Nevertheless, the addition of Sn obviously reduced
the inter-lamellar spacing, which might be attributed to the inhibi-
tion of lath-interface-located small-sized Nb
3Sn dispersoids on the
transverse growth of laths. In both cases, almost the lath orienta-
tion was not parallel to the withdrawal direction. In contrast, the
majority of the laths trends to be inclined with an angle of approx-
imately 45/C176to the growth direction. This misalignment of lamellar
orientation has been also reported in the DS Ti–Al [15,16], Ti–Al–
Mo(B) [10], Ti–Al–X (X = Mo, Re, W) [11] and Ti–Al–Si [13] systems
containing higher Al in the range between 43 and 55 at.%.
It is generally known that the thermal gradient, G and with-
drawal rate, R are the two key parameters controlling the direc-
tional solidiﬁcation process. In general case, high thermal
gradient and low withdrawal rate are beneﬁcial to the formation
of DS microstructures. However, it is necessary to achieve matched
states between the two parameters. Moreover, it has been demon-
strated that the moving rate of the solid/liquid interface during DS
process is synchronous with the withdrawal velocity only when
the latter takes a relatively low value. Conversely, a lagging effect
exists in case of higher withdrawal speed [17]. Accordingly, for a
given axial heat transfer condition, withdrawal velocity is an
important factor affecting the microstructure and the shape of
the solidiﬁcation front. This can be easily understood in the follow-
ing way. The withdrawal movement of the solidifying sample will
make the front moving downward. Simultaneously, the thermal
gradient will drive the front to go upward. In this case, if the
ascending effect is more signiﬁcant than descending, the front will
be convex which is favorable for directional solidiﬁcation. Other-
wise, in case of high withdrawal speed, the front will take the
shape of concave, and therefore, the favorable condition for DS
are lost [18]. As a result, misaligned microstructure along the
growth direction is obtained. In the present study, comparatively
lower temperature gradient and higher withdrawal velocity
(60 mm/h) were employed for the DS process due to the limita-
tions of the apparatus. In case of DS Ti–43Al–3Si, a thermal gradi-
ent as high as 400 /C176C/cm was employed and well-aligned lamellar
microstructure was obtained in Luo et al.’s recent investigation
[13]. On the other hand, the thermal gradient will inevitably
change during processing. For instance, initially there is a high
thermal gradient since the water cooled chill plate is used, result-
ing in columnar growth of relevant phases. However, the thermal
gradient deceases away from the chill plate and equiaxed growth
of grains occurs.

3.2. Mechanical properties and fractographs
Fig. 5 shows the true-stress versus true strain curves at room
and high temperature for the directionally solidiﬁed alloys with
the tensile axis aligned parallel to the growth direction, where
the ultimate strength and tensile elongation are also presented. It
is evident that both the Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn alloys
displayed little ductility, and splitting type fracture immediately
after the stress reached the maximum without obvious yielding
process at room temperature (curves a and d). It is worth noting
that the same result was true for the former alloy even at 650 /C176C
(curve b). Nevertheless, the stress increased with increasing strain
after yielding, resulting in working hardening for the Ti–16Al–Nb–
1Sn alloy tested at 650 /C176C (curvee). The stress–strain curves c and f
shows great plastic deformation for the two alloys tested at 750 /C176C.
In particular, obvious necking occurred for the Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn
alloy prior to ﬁnal fracture. In general, the ultimate strength de-
creased with increasing test temperature and the converse trends
held for the tensile elongation. Furthermore, the addition of Sn ex-
erted a remarkable enhancement in tensile strength and elonga-
tion of Ti–16Al–8Nb alloy at room temperature and 650 /C176C. For
example, the average strength of the Ti–16Al–8Nb alloy was
362 MPa whereas the addition of 1 at.% Sn increased the strength
to 456 MPa at room temperature and the tensile elongation was
enhanced from 0.3% to 0.5% and from 0.8% to 2.9% at room temper-
ature and 650 /C176C, respectively. The elongation enhancement effect
was much more signiﬁcant, i.e. from 5.9% to 14.3% 750 /C176C whereas

attributed to the intrinsic lattice structures of Ti 3Al (ordered hex-

tility of Ti–Al based alloys via microalloying should be
concentrated on improving the inhomogeneous distribution of
electron cloud around Ti atoms in the lattice to increase the mobil-
ity of dislocations. Accordingly, it is documented that the microal-
loying elements should occupy the substitutional positions on Al
sublattice and have greater electronegativities and smaller atomic
radii than Ti and Al, respectively. Ti, Al and Sn atoms have a respec-
tive electronegativity value of 1.5, 1.5, and 1.96 with a correspond-
ing atomic radius of 0.239, 0.236 and 0.225 nm [21,22].I ti s
obvious that Sn satisﬁes the aforementioned conditions, and there-
fore, contributes to the improvement in the inhomogeneous distri-
bution of electron cloud around Ti atoms. As a result, the Sn-
microalloyed Ti–Ti
3Al–Nb alloy in the present study exhibited
more signiﬁcantly enhanced ductility at room and high tempera-
tures than Ti–Ti
3Al–Nb alloys without Sn addition. In particular,
signiﬁcant plastic deformation was observed at high temperatures
as indicated by the fracture surfaces.
Fig. 6 illustrates the fracture surfaces for the two alloys after
tensile tests. At room temperature, the fracture surfaces were quite
ﬂat and exhibited the intragranular and cleavage fracture features
for Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–16Al–8Nb–1Sn alloys, respectively. Some
secondary cracks were also observed on the fracture surfaces. In
general, the two alloys showed the brittle features. However, the
facture surfaces were obviously much rougher and the alloys
exhibited faceted fracture with some ductile dimples at high tem-
peratures. In particular, some cavities were formed during the
necking process and tearing striations were present on the fracture
surfaces of Ti–16Al–8nb–1Sn alloy at 650 /C176C and 750/C176C, which
indicated that the alloy has undergone remarkable plastic defor-
mation prior to ﬁnal fracture. Again, the addition of Sn has indeed
contributed to a prominent enhancement in the ductility of ternary
Ti–Al–Nb alloy.
4. Conclusions
In the present study, the microstructure and mechanical prop-
erties of directionally solidiﬁed ingots of Ti–16Al–8Nb and Ti–
16Al–8Nb–1Sn alloys were investigated. It shows that both the
two alloys displayed a uniform basket-weave type microstructure
whereas the lath orientation was not parallel to the withdrawal
direction due to the limited thermal gradient and comparatively
high withdrawal velocity. The Sn addition reduced the inter-lamel-
lar spacing, promoted the formation of laths and remarkably en-
hanced the ductility of ternary Ti–Al–Nb alloy at high
temperature without signiﬁcant deterioration of strength. The
fracture surfaces exhibited the brittle and ductile features of the al-
loys at room and high temperatures, respectively.
