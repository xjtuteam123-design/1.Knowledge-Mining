 Abstract 
 The microstructural and texture evoluti ons of as-cast AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 eutectic high 
entropy alloy (EHEA) have been investigated in the course of thermomechanical processing at 
the temperature range of 25-500°C. Interestingly, c ompared with other conventional casting 
structures, significant strength-ductility ratio ha s been achieved at room temperature. In 
addition, the volume fractions of the constituent p hases: soft FCC (face-centered cubic), and 
the hard BCC (body-centered cubic) phases, do not s ignificantly change from room to 
elevated deformation temperatures. In fact, the strength and ductility have not been d ecreased 
at higher temperatures which represent the mechanic al stability of the alloy in the examined 
temperature range. From room temperature up to 300 ° C, the dendrites have been stretched 
and broken with a slight deviation from the load di rection, whereas at higher temperature of 
500 ° C the dendrites have been rotated relative to the d irection of load before fracture. Texture 
examination reveals the formation of a random texture in the initial and deformed states due to 
simultaneous contribution of different influencing factors such as stretching of dendrites 
during deformation, the dendrite morphology changes , and the presence of hard and soft 
phases and their interaction with each other. 
 
Keywords: 
Eutectic high entropy alloy; Cast structure; Therma l stability; Thermomechanical 
processing; Texture evolution

 Owing to the limited resources, the de velopment of new materials has drawn much 
attention as the mainly upfront challenge of the ne xt generation. Conventional alloys are 
based on one or two main elements with relatively s mall amount of alloying elements for 
modification of the evolved microstructure [1]. How ever, the high entropy alloys (HEAs) are 
a new class of materials which are commonly defined as alloys containing four or more 
elements with equimolar or near-equimolar compositi ons; with atomic fraction between 5 and 
35 at.% for each element forming a single or dual p hase structure despite multiple elements 
[2, 3]. There are mainly four core effects that dis tinguish this category of alloys from the 
conventional ones: ( i) high-entropy effect ( ii ) sluggish diffusion ( iii ) severe lattice distortion 
and ( iv ) cocktail effect [3]. Previous researches have shown promising properti es of HEAs 
such as significant mechanical properties, noticeab le corrosion and wear resistance, thermal 
stability at elevated temperatures [4, 5], and slug gish diffusion kinetics [6]. These specified 
properties categorize HEAs as a suitable candidate for high temperature applications. 
The balance between the strength and ductility is c onsidered as a main challenge to 
schedule the processing routes of HEAs [7]. In this regard, single phase solid solution FCC 
structured HEAs show merely good ductility [8, 9 ] while single-phase solid solution BCC 
structured HEAs possess high strength [10 ]. Therefore a proper combination of the constituent 
phases (BCC and FCC phase fractions) can significan tly enhance mechanical properties [11 ]. 
However, simply introducing a combination of FCC an d BCC phases, without a proper 
structural design, could not solve the problem [12 ]. Besides, the problems resulting from 
casting e.g. segregation and shrinkage cavities may cause deterioration and heterogeneity in 
mechanical properties [13, 14 ]. To overcome this problem, the eutectic HEAs alloys have been 
developed which benefit from acceptable cast-abili ty [3]. Since the eutectic reaction is an

isothermal transformation, there is no solidificati on temperature range and both the 
segregation and shrinkage cavity can be alleviated [7]. The concept of eutectic high entropy 
alloy (EHEA) was first introduced by Lu et al. by t he composition of AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 , 
consisting of both FCC and BCC phases holding eutec tic structure. High density of mobile

the BCC phase can pin the dislocations, resulting i n further strengthening of the material. 
Briefly, the FCC (L1 2) phase act as a soft phase and is deformed via dis location planar slip 
and stacking faults (SFs) formation, whereas the BC C (B2) phase acts as a hard phase 
reinforced by nano-precipitates [15-17]. Studies sh ow that the hardness of the B2 phase (~12 
GPa) is nearly three times higher than that of the L12 phase (~4 GPa) in the as-cast EHEA 
[18]. 
As is realized, the room temperature deformation me chanisms of both ordered FCC and 
BCC phases in the EHEA has been investigated. Howev er, the deformation behavior of the 
alloy at high temperatures is not clear and needs m ore systematic investigation. In line to 
previous efforts for instance [7] , it is speculated that the constituent ordered FCC (L1 2) and 
BCC (B2) phases would exhibit high thermal stabilit y up to 600°C. In addition, the 
development of deformation texture in initial cast structure should be considered as one of the 
main influencing factor. Having all into the consideration, the current study emphasizes on the 
deformation behavior of a five component EHEA at te mperature range of 25-500 °C. This 
investigation has been supported by flow behavior a nalysis and in-details microstructural

2. Material and experimental procedures 
 The experimented material was received in vacuum arc remelted condition by the 
nominal composition Al-Co-Cr-Fe-2.1Ni (in aspect ra tio of at. %). The chemical composition 
of the alloy is listed in Table 1. The specimens were cut through the electro-discharg e 
machining (EDM) from as-remelted material with thei r axes parallel to the cooling direction 
according to the ASTME8 (for room temperature tests ) [19] and ASTME29 (for high 
temperature tests) [20] standards by the gauge leng th of 25mm. Tensile tests at room and 
elevated temperatures (25 °C, 300 °C, and 500 °C) were carried out using a GOTECH AI-7000 
universal testing machine coupled with a programmable resistance furnace. The specimens for 
high-temperature deformation were initially pre-hea ted to the specified temperature and 
soaked isothermally for 10 min. Tensile tests were then performed under the strain rate of 
0.001s -1 up to fracture. After the fracture, the specimens w ere immediately quenched in water 
to preserve the deformed microstructures. 
For microstructural examination, the specimens were ground and polished and then 
etched in a solution of 10CuSO4+50mlHCl+50mlH2O. Th e obtained micrographs were taken 
near the fracture surface of the tensile tested spe cimens employing a CamScan MV2300 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM). X-ray diffracti on analysis (XRD) using a Cu K α 
radiation in the 2 θ range of 30° to 90° was used to characterize the phases existing in the 
initial microstructure. The local composition of t he constituent phases was also studied 
assessed through energy dispersion spectroscopy (ED S). Microstructure examinations were 
also performed through Electron backscatter diffrac tion (EBSD) in a high-resolution SEM

 3. Results and discussions 
3.1. Initial microstructure 
 Fig 1a shows the XRD pattern of the initial microstructur e verifying the existence of 
two constituent FCC and BCC phases with L1 2 and B2 structures, respectively [7]. The 
corresponding EBSD phase map in Fig 1b also reveals that the matrix is composed of both 
FCC and BCC phases. The volume fractions of FCC and BCC phases were measured to be 
77 % and23%, respectively. The BCC phase is mostly located in dendritic regions. In 
conventional casting structures, the formation of d endrites is irregular and does not follow a 
specific pattern. However, due to controlled solidi fication in this alloy, a specific distribution 
and arrangement of dendrites in the structure can b e formed [21, 22]. Formation of such 
unique distribution and arrangement of dendrites st ems from the eutectic solidification of the 
experimented high entropy alloy in which the solidi fication occurs at a specific temperature 
rather than a temperature range [7, 23] . 
Grain boundary map of the initial microstructure in Fig. 1c shows a dual-phase structure 
and dendritic morphology as it has been demonstrated by the phase maps. As can be seen, the 
initial microstructure consists of high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs), which are shown 
with blue lines in Fig. 1c . It should be noted that the HAGBs are mostly dist ributed in the 
places where the BCC phase is located. The grain si ze of the FCC and BCC phase has been

3.2 Tensile flow behavior 
 The typical stress-logarithmic plasti c strain curves of the investigated EHEA 
obtained through tensile testing at room temperature up to 500 °C under the strain rate of 10 -3 
s-1 are depicted in Fig 3a . The room temperature result demonstrates a relative ly high yield 
strength (YS) of 520 MPa and ultimate tensile stren gth (UTS) of 1212 MPa along with 27% 
elongation, which is comprehensively greater in com parison to conventional high entropy 
alloys or other cast materials [21, 28]. Fig. 3b compares the strength and ductility of eutectic 
alloys with other common and advanced alloys. This alloy, despite being cast, has a 
significant strength-ductility ratio and can show s alient properties compared to other 
common alloys [29, 30]. It can also be seen that the YS has not considerably changed with an 
increase in the deformation temperature. Hence, thi s is an indication of the thermal stability 
in this temperature range [3]. Demonstration of suc h significant mechanical properties 
stemming from a cast structure is due to the eutect ic solidification of the alloy, which occurs 
at a certain solidification temperature resulting i n minimization of cast defects such as 
separation and shrinkage cavities during solidifica tion [7]. Moreover, by increasing the 
temperature, both elongation and UTS of the materia l decrease to 26% and 1180 MPa at 300 
°C and 25% and 1020 MPa at 500 °C as shown in Fig. 3a . Although ductility changes are 
negligible in this alloy, the gradual descending tr end in elongation is contrary to other 
researches reporting the increase in elongation wit h the rise of deformation temperature 
[31] .In conventional alloys, dynamic recrystallization a nd subsequent grain growth occur, 
resulting in lower strength based on the Hall-Petch relationship but higher ductility [31, 32]. 
However, in this alloy, no considerable decrease is observed, and this is proof of the thermal 
stability of this alloy in the examined temperature range. It was previously reported that this

behavior could be observed from room temperature up to 600 ℃, while afterward, the 
thermal stability of material vanishes with respect to temperature increment [7, 16]. At 
elevated temperature deformation of this EHEA, prec ipitation and dynamic recrystallization 
(DRX) may take place simultaneously. The recrystall ization process can be suppressed via 
precipitation since the driving force of DRX is hig her than that for the formation of fine 
precipitates [16]. It should be noted that an incre ment in temperature eases the movement of 
dislocations and results in higher elongation with respect to loss of strength. However, a 
slight change in yield stress, UTS, and elongation of the utilized as-cast alloy is an indication 
of its high thermal stability up to 500 ℃. 
The strain hardening is expected to be influenced b y the stability of constituent phases. Thus, 
the strain hardening rate of the material has been illustrated in Fig. 3c, in which three 
different regions can be defined in all three sampl es . In the first region, a continuous decline 
is observed. This decline for the sample deformed a t elevated temperature can be linked to 
the occurrence of dynamic recovery [22]. In the second region, the work hardening rate of all 
samples increases, which is attributed to twinning related deformation mechanisms [23,27]. 
In the third region, a continuous decline is observ ed stemming from the necking and failure 
of the samples. Noticeably, neglectable deviation i n the work hardening level of all samples 
is another supporting fact of the thermal stability resulting in similar mechanical properties 
among all three samples [25]. Therefore, it can be concluded that utilized EHAE possesses 
high thermal stability up to 500 ℃. These findings are in good agreement with the st udy in 
which reported the thermal stability of this alloy up to 600 ℃ [15 ].

3.3. Microstructure evolution during deformation 
 EBSD phase maps of samples deformed at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 4 . 
Accordingly, volume fractions of the phases and mic rostructure development after 
deformation can be analyzed. At room temperature an d 300 °C, the breakage of dendrites and 
their segregation are well-observed. although with increasing temperature, breaking dendrites 
are more severe. At deformation temperature of 500 °C, structural development becomes 
more complex, and the behavior of the matrix and de ndrites changes dramatically. This effect 
is since at higher temperatures, the conditions for breaking dendrites are better, and dendrites 
experience more acute conditions [33]. In Fig. 4d , the volume fractions of the phases in all 
conditions are plotted. It should be noted that the volume fractions slightly vary with the 
change in the deformation temperature. However, the constancy of phase volume fractions 
during high temperature deformation of this alloy i s another proof of the thermal stability in 
the examined temperature range. 
For a more detailed characterization, grain boundar y maps of the deformed samples are 
demonstrated in Fig. 5 . By applying the tensile test at room temperature, the dendrites have 
been broken (indicated by yellow ovals) and stretch ed parallel to the loading direction ( Fig. 
5a ). Moreover, due to the accumulation of dislocation s in the dendritic boundaries, the 
fraction of the LAGBs has been increased, and conse quently, the fraction of the HAGBs has 
been decreased [34].

small angle grain boundaries, even the subgrains [38]. However, the formation of dynamically 
recrystallized grains has not occurred in this alloy, and this could be due to thermal stability at 
examined temperatures.

4. Conclusion 
The room and high temperature deformation behavior of AlCoCrFeNi2.1 EHEA was assessed 
through conducting tensile tests at 25 °C up to 500 °C. The excellent combination of high 
strength, high ductility at various conditions and observed thermal stability were phenomenal; 
therefore the evolved microstructures and textures were precisely studied. The following 
conclusion can be drawn based on the results shown:

1. The high-angle grain boundaries in initial structur e were mostly arranged at the FCC/BCC 
interphase regions representing the coarse structur e of each constituent phases. In addition, 
owing to the controlled solidification and the evol ved lamellar structure, there was almost 
no LAGBs in the initial structure. 
2. The volume fraction of the constituent phases in in vestigated temperature range remained 
relatively unchanged. As a result, there was no dec rease in strength and ductility of the 
material. In this regard, the increase in the harde ning capacity at higher temperatures was 
linked to the formation of deformation nano-twins d uring plastic deformation of the soft 
phase (FCC). However, the evolution of the misorien tation maps during tensile deformation 
showed that the strain accommodation was mainly took place in the hard phase (B2). 
3. The dendrites in deformed microstructures at room t emperature and 300°C were stretched 
and broken with a slight deviation toward the load direction. However, at the 500 0C, due to 
the activity of more slip systems and the complexit y of deformation, the dendrites were 
rotated relative to the loading direction. 
4. The random texture of the initial microstructure, w hich was attributed to the alloy 
characteristics such as casting structure and prese nce of ordered phases, was eliminated 
through high temperature deformation and the presen ce of specified texture components 
were evident.
