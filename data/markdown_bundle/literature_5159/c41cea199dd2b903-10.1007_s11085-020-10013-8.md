Abstract
The present investigation focuses on the thermal oxidation of two, Nb- and Ta-
alloyed (Ni,Co)-based superalloys having in their microstructures high contents of 
hard phases and considered potential candidates as a tool for friction stir welding 
process. The alloys were produced by investment casting using high-purity elements 
in induction furnace under vacuum atmosphere. The alloys were oxidized pseudo-
isothermally up to 1000  h in muffle furnace under laboratory air, and the mass 
change was monitored manually using an analytical balance. External oxidation 
products growing on the surface of the oxidized samples were mainly Cr2O3 and 
 Al2O3. At 800 and 900 °C, internal oxidation products were also observed and con-
sist of Al2O3 and AlN. From a kinetic point of view, both superalloys presented the 
same behavior at 800 and 900 °C with k p values typical of alumina formers. How -
ever, Ta-alloyed material exhibited superior oxidation resistance at 1000 °C when 
compared to the alloy containing Nb.
Keywords (Co,Ni)-based superalloys · Friction stir welding tools · High-
temperature oxidation · Alumina forming · Parabolic behavior
Introduction
Friction stir welding (FSW) is a technique consisting of joining pieces in solid 
state which may avoid common welding defects associated with fusion welding 
processes [1 , 2]. The welding is performed thanks to a non-consumable rotating 
tool of W–Re alloys or polycrystalline cubic boron nitride (PCBN), for exam-
ple. The FSW tool is designed with a shoulder and pin that heats up and stirs 
the material to be weld through severe plastic deformation, resulting in enhanced

behavior against high shear and impact forces, high wear and elevated oxidation

(Co–Ni)-based superalloys with γ/γ′ dominant microstructure have been consid-
ered good candidates for application as FSW tool, because they exhibit high mechan-
ical strength and toughness. In contrast to W–Re alloys or PCBN, FSW tools based 
on Co–Ni alloys can be manufactured at low cost and easily machined. Co–Al–W-
based alloys having γ/γ′ microstructure, similar to that of Ni-based superalloys, may 
have melting temperatures up to 100 °C higher than these latter. However, Co-based 
superalloys are known to present lower corrosion/oxidation resistance compared to 
Ni-based superalloys which is the main restriction for their use in harsh environment 
at elevated temperatures [5–7].
Investigations focused on the effects of alloying additions on phase equilibria 
in the Co–Al–W system showed that Ta additions substantially increase γ′ solvus 
temperature which is estimated to be at least 60 °C higher than those of Ni-based 
superalloys [3–7]. Costa et  al. reported that a Ta-alloyed Co–Ni-based superalloy 
presented higher yield stress values above 800 °C compared with its Nb-modified 
version [8]. Other studies reported that the γ′ phase may be stabilized through 
small additions of either Mo, Ta, Nb or V [9–12]. In addition, it has been observed 
that γ′ solvus temperature and the yield stress may be increased by Ni additions to 
Co–Al–Mo–Ta/Nb alloys [6–13].
The oxidation behavior of the γ/γ′ Co-based alloys may be enhanced by the addi-
tion of B [14] at 800 and 900  °C when added to alloys based on Co–9Al–8/9W 
(at %) system. Klein et al. [15] have also investigated the effect of different amounts 
of Cr (4; 8 at.%) in a Co–9Al–9W–0.04B alloy on the oxidation resistance at 800 °C. 
The authors have observed that Cr addition enhanced the oxidation behavior of the 
alloy by limiting the excessive formation of Co oxides and favoring the growth of 
protective oxides such as Cr2O3 and Al2O3. The effect of Ni and Si additions on the 
oxidation behavior of a Co–9Al–9W–0.12B (at.%) alloy at 800 (Ni; Si), 900 (Ni; 
Si) and 1000 °C (Si) was investigated by Klein et al. [16]. The formation of a pro-
tective inner Al2O3 layer is inhibited for Ni-containing alloys, and lower oxidation 
resistance is observed in comparison with Ni-free alloys. On the other hand, Si addi-
tions improve the oxidation resistance at temperatures above 900 °C. Yan et al. [17] 
have evaluated the oxidation resistance of different alloys having as compositional 
base Co–7Al–(3–7)W (at.%). They were modified by the addition of Ni (6; 20%), 
Cr (10%), Fe (20%), Si (1%), Ta (2%), Mo (2%), Ti (2%) and V (2; 4%). Similar to 
the observations by [16, 18–20], these authors also showed that Cr addition strongly 
improves the oxidation resistance of the Co-based superalloys.
During the oxidation process, Al is usually depleted at the oxide/alloy interface 
to promote the formation of a protective Al2O3 oxide layer, which is desirable to 
slow down the growth rates [6]. According to Klein et al. [14], the oxidation γ/γ′ 
Co-based superalloys with 9 at.% of Al lead to the formation of a region beneath 
the oxide scale with a γ/Co 3W microstructure, where the Co3W phase presented a 
needlelike morphology. These precipitates are not suitable since they are detrimen-
tal to the mechanical properties of the alloy.

Since the current understanding of oxidation behavior of Co–Ni–Al–W alloys 
with high Al contents is restricted, this work aims for evaluating the effects of alloy-
ing elements Ta and Nb, on the oxidation behavior of two new Co–Ni-based alloys 
in view of their utilization as friction stir welding tools [21]. Pseudo-isothermal oxi-
dation tests were carried out at 800, 900 and 1000 °C in laboratory air up to 1000 h, 
and the characterization of the oxide layer compositions, crystal structure, kinetics 
and morphology was performed.
Materials and Methods
Bars with dimensions of Ø 12 mm × 70 mm of two Co–Ni-based superalloys, with 
the same amount (at.%) of either Nb or Ta, were induction melted and investment 
cast under vacuum. The bars were prepared using high-purity elements (min. 99.8%) 
and their chemical compositions determined by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy 
(XRF) as given in Table  1. Since no calibration standards were used for boron and 
carbon, their contents in Table  1 are the nominal ones. In the present study, the two 
alloys were designated by Nb alloy and Ta alloy since the main difference between 
both is related to the presence of either Ta or Nb in their compositions. It is worth 
nothing that the amount in at.% of either Nb or Ta is the same in both alloys. Con-
sidering that these alloys are expected to operate under a maximum temperature 
close to 1000 °C in FSW applications, the as-cast bars were heat-treated at this tem-
perature for 48 h and air cooled in an attempt to produce stable microstructures as 
well as to remove residual stresses associated with the casting process.
The oxidation tests were performed at 800, 900 and 1000 °C up to 1000 h under 
laboratory air using a muffle furnace. Disk specimens of Ø = 7 mm by 2 mm were 
removed from the heat-treated bars by electrical discharge machining (EDM). In 
order to obtain reliable results, a batch composed of three samples of each alloy was 
used for each oxidation temperature. Prior to the oxidation tests, the surface of the 
samples was ground using SiC paper down to grit P1200 and subsequently polished 
with 0.5 µm alumina suspension, cleaned ultrasonically in water and in acetone and 
then dried with hot air. The specimens were placed vertically in alumina crucibles, 
introduced in preheated muffle furnaces and frequently removed to monitor the mass 
change over the exposure time. The mass change induced by oxidation was meas-
ured using an analytical balance with 0.1 mg precision.

deposition (PVD) and then electroplated with a Ni layer of approximately 20 µm to

Discontinuous Thermogravimetric Measurements
Figure 2a–f gives the specific mass change (mass variation over the area) for the oxi-
dation of Nb and Ta alloys under laboratory air at 800 (a, b), 900 (c, d) and 1000 °C 
(e, f). At temperatures of 800 and 900 °C, a quite similar behavior is observed for 
the oxidation of both alloys (Fig.  2a–d). At these temperatures, the kinetic curves 
presented a parabola-like behavior with very low mass gains, maximum values 
being near 0.35 and 1.25 mg cm −2 at 800 and 900 °C, respectively. The oxidation 
curves at 1000 °C exhibited a completely different behavior when compared to those 
of oxidation at 800 and 900 °C. At 1000 °C, the kinetic curves clearly showed the 
superiority of Ta alloy compared to Nb alloy in terms of their sensitivity to ther -
mal shocks. For both alloys, a negative slope of mass change was observed after a 
slight increase not exceeding 1 mg cm −2 at the first moments of exposure. For the 
Nb alloy (Fig.  2e), after 400 h of exposure, samples exhibited an exacerbated mass 
loss, reaching maximum values near 20–40 mg cm −2. However, for the Ta alloy, a 
linear and low negative slope was observed after the first 60 h, without recording 
negative mass changes for all samples tested, as shown in Fig. 2f.

Characterization of Oxidation Products
The macroscopic aspect of the samples after oxidation tests is presented in Fig.  3. 
Oxidized samples of Ta alloy at 800 and 900 °C are characterized by dark gray col-
oration which tend to be dark greenish blue at 1000 °C, samples of Nb alloy present-
ing similar aspect. It can be observed that the samples conserved their integrity after 
1000 h of exposure, except that one of the Nb alloys oxidized at 1000 °C, for which 
exacerbated oxide spalling is clearly distinguished at the edges of the oxidized disks. 
These regions were likely more susceptible to thermo-mechanical stress induced by 
heating and cooling during mass monitoring.
The XRD results of the phases formed on the Nb and Ta alloy surface after expo-
sure at 800, 900 and 1000 °C for 1000 h are given in Table  4. For both alloys, and 
taking into consideration the three temperatures, no selective oxidation was observed 
at any condition of exposure. XRD data have shown that the oxidation products con-
sist mainly of Cr2O3 and α-Al2O3. Other oxidation products such as complex oxides 
of the spinel family (NiCr2O4, CoCr2O4) or rutile type (Cr(Ta,Nb)O 4) were also 
detected. Furthermore, the XRD data of both alloys oxidized at 800 °C and 900 °C 
have also indicated the presence of cubic AlN nitride.

Discussion
In this work, long-duration oxidation behavior in laboratory air was investigated 
for two high-aluminum (Ni–Co) superalloys: one containing Nb and the other con-
taining Ta with the same amount in at.%. Both alloys have similar microstructures 
and are composed of γ/γ′-matrix, B2-CoAl, µ-Co 7W6, Cr-rich Laves phase and MC 
carbides. The high Al content led to an elevated volume fraction of B2-CoAl pre-
cipitates, which is in agreement with several studies [6, 11, 13]. It is intentional and 
desired that these alloys have high amount of hard phases for the intended applica-
tion, i.e., withstand harsh working conditions during friction stir welding process.

The kinetic results showed a similar behavior of both Ta and Nb alloys in high-
temperature oxidation under air at 800 and 900 °C. The kinetic curves adopt a para-
bolic behavior, particularly at 900 °C, which indicates that the growth of oxide layer 
is controlled by solid-state diffusion and that a steady-state regime is established 
between the substrate and the oxide scale. Even though the formation of Cr-rich 
oxides was observed, values of kp ranging from 1.16 × 10 −14 to 2.17 × 10 −13 g−2 
 cm−4 s−1 were calculated for temperatures of 800–900 °C, respectively, which are 
similar to those for alumina forming alloys, as reported in the literature [23, 24]. 
For the sake of comparison, typical values of k p for the oxidation of some Ni- and 
Co-based alloys in dry air were reported in an Arrhenius plot, as illustrated in Fig.  9 
[25–32]. The oxidation kinetics of the alloys investigated in the present work are 
higher than typical alumina formers such as Kanthal AF and lower than classical 
chromia formers Ni-based superalloys such as RR 1000, Udimet 720, Inconel 718 
Astroloy and Waspaloy that are widely used as turbine blade. The parabolic con-
stants from this work are also lower than those determined by Rybicki and Smialek 
[31] for the oxidation of Beta-NiAl at 800 and 900 °C. This behavior is mainly asso-
ciated with the formation of transient aluminas θ and δ having fast growth rates. 
The transient aluminas are formed during short exposure durations and transform for 
longer durations to α-Al2O3 having slow growth rate. This is in good agreement with 
XRD results from this work that showed only the presence of the stable α-Al 2O3. In 
spite of no selective oxidation, considering the high amount of aluminum in these 
alloys, the parabolic constants values were somehow expected in view of the mul-
tilayered scale formation with the growth of Al2O3 beneath the Cr-rich oxide scale. 
These findings are of particular interest since all Co-based γ/γ′ alloys that contain 
lower amount of aluminum (typically up to 10  at.% approximately) are character -
ized by low oxidation resistance as reported in previous works [9–21]. According to

As mentioned previously, the behavior of both Nb and Ta alloys in oxidation was 
quite similar up to 900 °C in terms of oxidation products and kinetics, while superior 
resistance was observed for the Ta alloy at 1000 °C. In fact, this later alloy exhibited 
lower mass loss and enhanced adherence of the oxide scale, while the Nb alloy suf-
fered exacerbated spalling. Selective oxidation of Al is observed for the Ta alloy at 
1000 °C with the formation of protective alumina scale, which explain the very low 
mass changes recorded and the low depletion of the subsurface region that main-
tained high amount of Al. Surprisingly, the Cr content near the subsurface region is 
high, even with the formation of chromium-rich oxides during oxidation. The con-
centration profiles measured on the cross sections near the subsurface regions from 
Fig. 8a, b do not exhibit significant gradient compared to the base metal. It is likely 
that the Cr released by the dissolution of the Cr-rich Laves phase in the depleted 
region is responsible for maintaining its elevated content, regardless of the forma-
tion of the external oxide layer.
If one considers the external oxide layer mainly composed of Cr2O3, the forma-
tion of Al2O3 beneath this layer is expected from a thermodynamic point of view. 
 Cr2O3 is less stable compared to Al2O3, and the Gibbs free energies of formation at 
900 °C reported to one mole of oxygen are approximately − 552 and − 868 kJ mol−1 
for Cr2O3 and Al2O3, respectively. The corresponding equilibrium pressures of O2 
associated with the formation of these oxide are 2.6  ×  10 −25 and 2  ×  10−39  atm, 
respectively, for Cr2O3 and Al2O3, which explain the stratification observed in the 
oxidized samples.

changes beneath the oxidation front. When increasing the temperature and time 
of exposure, a depleted region free of B2-CoAl, γ′, Laves, MC and μ phases was 
observed and it is mainly composed by γ phase, as observed for both Nb and Ta 
alloys. It is in this region that internal oxidation and precipitation of AlN takes place.

It should be pointed out that these values are for the sake of illustration, and accurate 
calculations imply the consideration of Al and Cr chemical activities in the alloys, 
which change indeed the values of the corresponding N2 equilibrium pressures for 
these reactions. In addition, the formation of nitride precipitates depends on the 
permeability of nitrogen in the alloys and the exposure conditions (isothermal or 
cyclic). The oxidation tests in this work were performed in such way that the sam-
ples were frequently removed from the furnace for mass monitoring, which can lead 
to defects formation in the protective oxide scales and cause internal infiltration of 
nitrogen and subsequent attack of the substrate.
The idea of introducing Nb in the alloy was primarily obtained based on cost 
optimization (Nb is cheaper than Ta). Taking into consideration the fact that Nb and 
Ta are chemically identical, one may presuppose that similar effect on alloy proper -
ties would be obtained, especially considering the low contents of these elements 
in the alloys as well as their similar microstructures. However, experimental results 
from oxidation tests undoubtedly demonstrated the beneficial effect of Ta addition 
on the oxide scale adherence. Vojtech et al. [33] also mentioned that γ-TiAl alloys 
containing Ta exhibited better oxidation resistance during cyclic tests compared to 
those with Nb, i.e., enhancement of oxide scale adherence. These authors suggested 
that Ta may modify bonding energy in the oxide scale, inducing a reduction in the 
coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between the oxide scale and the 
alloy. In addition, a modification of the diffusivity of the other alloying elements by 
the Nb or Ta may be responsible for affecting the oxidation behavior of the alloys 
investigated in this work. The formation of an important concentration of pores in 
the depleted region, as observed in the case of the Nb alloy oxidized at 1000  °C 
(Figs. 4f, 6f), can be correlated with a high diffusivity of elements in the bulk. In 
contrast, the presence of Ta may induce sluggish diffusion of the alloying elements 
and thus enhance the oxidation behavior of the alloy since no voids were observed in 
the oxidized Ta alloy.
Conclusions
The oxidation behavior of two (Co–Ni)-based alloys of high aluminum contents, 
modified by Nb or Ta, was investigated at 800, 900 and 1000 °C in view of evaluat-
ing their applicability as friction stir welding (FSW) tools. Both alloys presented 
elevated oxidation resistance, especially at 800 and 900 °C, thanks to the formation 
of highly protective alumina and chromia oxide layers during oxidation. The main 
conclusions from this work can be drawn as follows:

1. The oxidation behavior of Nb and Ta alloys at 800 and 900 °C is very similar in 
terms of kinetic and the formed oxidation products. The oxidation kinetic of both 
alloys is parabolic, and rate constants determined from the mass change curves 
are akin to those of alumina forming alloys.
2. The parabolic rate constants determined at 800 and 900 °C were successfully used 
for fitting the experimental data and can be used by extrapolation to predict the 
behavior for longer periods of exposure.
3. At 1000 °C, Ta alloy exhibited superior oxidation resistance compared to Nb 
alloy. This later presented poor oxidation resistance mainly due to low adherence 
of the oxidation product and their spalling during cooling. Ta alloy, on the other 
hand, presented elevated oxidation resistance by the formation of slow-growing 
and adherent oxide scale.
4. Both alloys presented internal oxidation and precipitation of nitride at 800 and 
900 °C as well as simultaneous oxidation of elements, except for the Ta alloy 
oxidized at 1000 °C that seems to present selective oxidation of Al without being 
depleted of γ′ beneath the oxide scale.
