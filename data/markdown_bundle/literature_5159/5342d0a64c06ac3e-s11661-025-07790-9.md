ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
A Density Functional Theory Study of Phase Stability
and Stacking Fault Energy in Co–Re Alloys
MARTIN BA¨KER
Co–Re alloys are a promising class of high-temperature alloys that exhibit a phase transition
between a high-temperature fcc phase and a low-temperature hcp phase. To understand the
transition between these phases and also the strength of the material, it is important to study the
stacking fault energy since a low stacking fault energy facilitates the transformation and
deformation by twinning, but also increases creep strength by splitting of partial dislocations. In
this paper, the inﬂuence of Cr and Re on the phase stability and on the interplanar distance of
the crystal is investigated, and the stacking fault energy in the fcc and hcp phase of pure Co and
of a Co-18.75Re-25Cr alloy is studied. It is shown that the stacking fault energy is
approximately determined by the energy diﬀerence between the hcp and fcc phase in all cases.
For the alloy, the energy strongly ﬂuctuates due to the formation or breakage of bonds during
slip.

I. INTRODUCTION
COBALT–RHENIUM alloys are a promising class
of materials for high-temperature applications beyond
1400 K. [1,2] The alloys contain Rhenium to increase the
melting temperature and Chromium to improve oxida-
tion resistance. The alloys can be hardened by precip-
itating tantalum carbides.
[3] Cobalt has a hexagonally
close-packed (hcp) structure at low temperatures and a
face-centred cubic structure (fcc) at high temperatures.
Since the solubility of Ta and C is very low in the hcp
phase, but high in the fcc phase, it is necessary that the
alloy has the fcc structure during the heat treatment at
high temperatures, but the hcp structure at service
temperature.
Although the fcc-hcp transition in Co is not fully
understood,
[4] it is known that both Rhenium and
Chromium stabilize the hcp phase. [5]. Mukherji et al. [2]
have shown that there is no fcc phase present in a
Co-17Re-23Cr alloy at temperatures of 1100 /C14 C. To
facilitate precipitation hardening with TaC, it is thus
desirable to design the alloy so that it has the fcc
structure during heat treatment, but the hcp structure at
service temperature.
Stacking faults are important for the transition
between the fcc and hcp phase because the transforma-
tion between the phases occurs via shearing with
Shockley partial dislocations.
[6] Furthermore, the stack-
ing fault energy also has an important inﬂuence on the
high-temperature strength because it governs the possi-
bility of cross slip of dislocations and also of twinning in
the hcp structure.
In this paper, density functional theory calculations at
0 K of the stacking fault energy are performed for pure
Co and for a Co–Re alloy with a realistic concentration
of 18.75 at. pct Re and 25 at. pct Cr, close to that
investigated in Mukherji et al.
[2] We also study the
inﬂuence of the Co and Re concentration on the phase
stability and on the interplanar distance of close-packed
planes in the alloys.

the fcc and 8 in the hcp structure with 16 atoms per
plane. The same geometry was also used for the
composition Co-18.75Re-25Cr, see Figure 1. This com-

B. Calculation Details
The software VASP with the PAW-PBE potentials
was used for all calculations.
[12–16] For each element, the
pseudopotential with maximum number of electrons
was chosen. The energy cutoﬀ was set to 520 eV.
Smearing with a width of 0.07 eV and a ﬁrst-order
Methfessel–Paxton scheme was used. The precision
parameter was set to ‘‘accurate’’, and real space projec-
tion operators were calculated to a precision of 10
/C0 4.
The convergence criterion for the electronic loop was set
to 10 /C0 3 meV, the ionic loop during relaxation was
stopped when the energy change was below 10 /C0 1 meV.
All calculations were spin-polarized.
For each conﬁguration, 5 DFT calculations with
varying scale factor (usually between 0.99 and 1.01)
were performed at a ﬁxed lattice volume with a
moderate k-point spacing of 0.2 A ˚ /C0 1. Ionic positions
and cell shape were allowed to relax. Afterwards, the
energies were ﬁtted with a Birch–Murnaghan state
equation
[17] and the minimum of the ﬁt was used to
determine the optimal scale factor. For this ﬁnal
calculation, a ﬁner k-point spacing of 0.1 A˚ /C0 1 was used.
This method has the advantage of being reasonably
eﬃcient and providing higher accuracy than a direct
relaxation of the cell volume with the VASP parameter
ISIF set to 3.

As a reference, the energy of pure Co in the fcc and
hcp phase was calculated. The energy diﬀerence per
atom between Co in the hcp and the fcc phase is /C0 17:6
meV, showing that the hcp phase is stable at 0 K.

B. Inﬂuence of Cr and Re on Phase Stability
To study the inﬂuence of Cr and Re on the phase
stability of Co, SQS structures in a concentration range
between 6.25 and 25 pct were created for a 144 atom fcc
and 128 atom hcp cell. Figure 3 shows the calculated
energy diﬀerences for the Co–Re and Co–Cr system. It can
be seen that both elements stabilize the hcp phase by
increasing the energy diﬀerence per atom. For large Cr
concentrations, the stabilization eﬀect is reduced. This is
probably caused by Cr–Cr nearest neighbour repulsion,
which is stronger in the hcp phase than in the fcc phase.
[19]
In all Co-X SQS cells, the mean vertical distance
(perpendicular to the close-packed planes) was mea-

increases by 0.0023 A ˚ /at. pct in the fcc and 0.0016 A ˚ /
at. pct in the hcp lattice. For Cr, the distance becomes
slightly smaller with increasing Cr content with values of
/C0 0:00025 A˚ /at. pct in the fcc and /C0 0:00050 A˚ /at. pct in
the hcp lattice.

D. Stacking Faults in Co-18.75Re-25Cr
In Sect. 3.1, it was shown that the stacking fault
energy in pure Co can be estimated from the diﬀerence
between the energy of Co in the fcc and hcp phase.
Using the same logic, the expected energy of a stacking
fault plane can be calculated from the 27 meV energy
diﬀerence of the fcc and hcp SQS cells. The resulting
value is 80 mJ/m
2. However, diﬀerent from pure Co, in
the SQS cell the bond structure changes when a plane is
slipped because some nearest or next-nearest neighbour
bonds will be broken and others formed. Therefore, it
has to be expected that there will be strong ﬂuctuations
in the energy due to the formation or breakage of
favourable or unfavourable bonds.
As explained in Sect. 2.1, stacking faults were added
to the two SQS cells in every possible vertical position,
resulting in 8 diﬀerent conﬁgurations for the hcp and 9
for the fcc cell. For each conﬁguration, the energy
diﬀerence to the reference SQS cell without a stacking
fault was calculated. Table II shows the mean and the
range (maximum and minimum) of the calculated
stacking fault energies. For the extrinsic stacking fault
in the hcp conﬁguration, the energy given is that of the
full stacking fault including the intrinsic 1 fault at the
top. The mean value of all stacking fault energies is
larger than for pure Co. This should be expected due to
the additional stabilization of the hcp phase from Cr
and Re. If the stacking fault energy is divided by the
number of layers involved in the fault, the mean
absolute energy of all faults is between 75.6 mJ/m
2 (fcc
intrinsic) and 95.2 mJ/m 2, close to the value expected
from the diﬀerence of the hcp and fcc phase. It should be
noted that for the hcp intrinsic 1 and extrinsic stacking
fault, atom planes away from the stacking fault also had
to be slipped so that the number of bonds between
diﬀerent elements changes also in these regions.
Although this aﬀects the energy of a single conﬁgura-
tion, the results show that the eﬀect averages out and the
ﬂuctuations in the values are not larger than for the
stacking faults that do not have this problem.
As before, the energy per atom involved in a stacking
fault can also be calculated. Figure 5 shows the energy
per atom for all stacking faults in the two SQS cells. The
range of the (absolute) energy per atom in the stacking
fault lies between 13.8 and 48.8 meV with a mean value
that is close to the expected value of 27 meV.
Although the mean energy per atom (and the mean
stacking fault energy) agrees well with the prediction
from the stabilization eﬀect of Cr and Re, the large
ﬂuctuations also need to be explained.
There are two eﬀects playing a role here: the number
of Cr and Re atoms in the fault planes and the breakage
or formation of favourable or unfavourable bonds. To
analyse the ﬁrst possibility, the number of Cr and Re
atoms in the fault planes was calculated for each
conﬁguration. However, there was found to be no clear
correlation between this and the stacking fault energy.
This suggests that the energy ﬂuctuations are dominated
by the bond changes occurring when atomic planes are
shifted.
To show that changing bonds indeed have a strong
inﬂuence on the stacking fault energy, additional sim-
ulations were performed for the hcp intrinsic 2 conﬁg-
uration, replacing only two Co with either Cr or Re in
the two stacking fault planes, either at a distance of 3.5
A˚ (next-nearest neighbour) or 5.5 A ˚ (distant). The
results were compared with a reference hcp conﬁgura-
tion without a stacking fault and with the two atoms at a
distance of 4.3 A˚ . The comparison of the stacking fault
energies thus shows how the formation of a next-nearest
neighbour bond (which has been found to be attractive

that is comparable to that of pure Co. This explains why
we observe strong ﬂuctuations in the values for the SQS

correlation of the element concentration and the stack-

neighbouring bond shows, the eﬀect of the alloying
element is completely obscured here with a stacking
fault energy that is almost identical to that of pure Co.
In principle, a more detailed analysis of this eﬀect
could be performed by considering the formation of
breakage of all types of bonds (nearest-neighbour,
next-nearest neighbour, etc.). However, for a precise
calculation, clusters of 3 or possibly 4 atoms would also
have to be accounted for in a cluster expansion
calculation. To do so would require a large eﬀort
because clusters would have to be calculated not only in
hcp and fcc structures, but also in mixed structures with
adjacent fcc and hcp planes since this will also inﬂuence
the interaction energies. This calculation is beyond the
scope of this work.
IV. DISCUSSION
In this section, possible consequences of the calcu-
lated stacking fault energies are discussed. In fcc
crystals, a low stacking fault energy increases the creep
strength because partial dislocations can split further
apart. In principle, this eﬀect will also occur in hcp
crystals, but a low stacking fault energy increases the
tendency for deformation by twinning. Since these two
eﬀects counteract each other, the inﬂuence of the larger
stacking fault energy in the Co-18.75Re-25Cr hcp cell on
the creep strength is not easy to evaluate. Experimen-
tally, it has been found that a retained fcc phase at
application temperature is detrimental for the creep
strength of Co–Re alloys.
[6]
However, the large ﬂuctuations of the stacking fault
energy in the full alloy can be expected to strongly
inﬂuence this picture. If we consider the simple case of a
dislocation splitting into two Shockley partials, there
will be local regions where the stacking fault energy is
large and the separation distance of the dislocation is
therefore small, impeding the separation even in regions
of low stacking fault energy due to the increase in
dislocation length. Similarly, deformation by twinning
will probably be impeded by local regions of high
stacking fault energy. Disordering eﬀects like this were
studied in Shih et al.
[20] who showed that partial
dislocations do not split apart as far as expected when
the stacking fault energy is small or negative due to the
interaction of the dislocation with diﬀerent alloying
atoms. Furthermore, it was shown in Pei et al.
[21] that
there is a decoupling between the strengthening eﬀects
from Shockley partial dislocations and low stacking
fault energy in fcc medium entropy alloys.
In addition, it should also be noted that the current
study used SQS cells. In general, nearest- or next-nearest
interactions can be strong;
[22] for example, the energy
diﬀerence between the two Re-containing conﬁgurations
from Table III is 236 meV. Therefore, during heat
treatment of the alloy, short-range order eﬀects will
occur, favouring conﬁgurations with low interaction
energies of the elements. Thus, more favourable bonds
will be broken than formed during slip. Therefore, it can
be expected that in the actual alloy, the stacking fault
energy increases and that ﬂuctuations might be reduced
by this eﬀect. This eﬀect could be studied in the future
using cluster expansions as described at the end of the
previous section.

temperatures close to the phase transition point,
phonons should be expected to play an important role.
Both these eﬀects should be studied in the future.
