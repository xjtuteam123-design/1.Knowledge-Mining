Microstructure and mechanical properties of Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites 
prepared by hot-pressing sintering 
 
Yuan Gao, Zong-de Liu, Qi Wang, and Yong-tian Wang 
Key Laboratory of Condition Monitoring and Control for Power Plant Equipment of Ministry of Education, North China Electric Power University, Beijing 102206, China 
(Received: 9 November 2017; revised: 11 February 2018 accepted: 13 February 2018) 
 
Abstract: Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites (V(Nb)/V(Mo) = 1) with 15vol% or 30vol% of ZrB2 were fabricated by hot-pressing sintering at 2000°C. 
The phases, microstructure, and mechanical properties were then investigated. The composites contain Nb−Mo solid solution (denoted as 
(Nb, Mo)ss hereafter), ZrB, MoB, and NbB phases. Compressive strength test results suggest that the strength of Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites 
increases with increasing ZrB2 content; Nb–Mo–30vol%ZrB2 had the highest compressive strength (1905.1 MPa). The improvement in the 
compressive strength of the Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites is mainly attributed to the secondary phase strengthening of the stiffer ZrB phase, sol-
id-solution strengthening of the (Nb, Mo)ss matrix as well as fine-grain strengthening. The fracture toughness decreases with increasing ZrB2 
content. Finally, the fracture modes of the Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites are also discussed in detail. 
Keywords: metal–matrix composites; microstructure; mechanical properties; sintering 
 
 
 
1. Introduction 
Niobium-based alloys are promising refractory alloys 
for high-temperature structural applications [1]. However, 
the strength of niobium decreases substantially above 
900°C [2–3]. As a result, various approaches have been 
used to improve the strength of niobium, including 
solid-solution strengthening using V, Ta, and W, composite 
strengthening with intermetallic compounds, such as Nb
3Al, 
Nb3Ir, and Nb5Si3 [4–11], and composite strengthening us-
ing carbides [11–13]. However, few studies have focused on 
composite strengthening using borides. Sun et al. [14] re-
ported on the microstructure and mechanical properties of 
ZrB
2–25vol%Nb composite. The results suggested that, 
when compared with pure ZrB2, the fracture toughness and 
strength of ZrB 2–25vol%Nb composite improved signifi-
cantly by the addition of Nb and the increase in strength is 
attributed to the excellent interface bonding between ZrB
2 
and Nb. Sun et al. [14] argued that ZrB2 particles can be re-
garded as a potentially strengthening phase for the compos-
ite strengthening of niobium because of the good interface 
bonding between ZrB
2 and Nb. However, the high melting 
point (>3000°C) and the strong covalent bonding make ZrB2 
difficult to be densified; furthermore, it is difficult for ZrB2 
to react with Nb at low temperatures [15]. Wang et al. [16] 
reported the synthesis of ZrB2–Mo composites with various 
volume fractions of Mo (0vol%–10vol%) by hot-pressing at 
1950°C. The results suggested that the addition of Mo could 
improve the densification behavior and reduce the densifica-
tion temperature of ZrB
2. Tan et al . [2] have studied the 
high-temperature mechanical behavior of Nb–Mo–ZrC al-
loys. The results suggested that the compressive strength of 
Nb–ZrC alloys improved significantly with the addition of 
Mo at room temperature and high temperature. The im-
provement of the strength of Nb–Mo–ZrC alloy was mainly 
attributed to the solid-solution strengthening of the matrix, 
with maximum effect at n(Nb)/n(Mo) of 1. As a result, the 
addition of Mo is considered to improve the strength and 
reduce the reaction temperature of Nb–ZrB
2 composites. 
In this study, Nb–Mo–(0vol%, 15vol%, 30vol%)ZrB 2 
(V(Nb)/V(Mo) = 1) were fabricated by hot-pressing sintering 
at 2000°C for 2 h under 30 MPa in Ar atmosphere. The mi-
crostructure and fracture surface were examined by scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM) and XRD. The compres-

2. Experimental 
The nominal compositions of the composites investigated 
in this study are listed in Table 1. Commercially available 
ZrB
2 particles with mean size of 25 μm (purity >99.9%), Nb 
and Mo with mean size of 18–30 μm (purity >99.95%) were 
supplied by Beijing Gold Crown New Material Technology 
Co. Ltd, China. The starting materials were proportionally 
weighed and mixed according to Table 1 and then pulver-
ized in a planetary ball mill in stainless steel ball milling 
containers at 200 r/min for 12 h using stainless steel balls. 
After mixing, the powders were dried in a drying oven at 
110°C for 3 h. Next, the mixed powders were put into molds, 
compressed at 29.4 kN, and dr ied in a vacuum furnace at 
280°C for 40 min. Finally, the semifinished products were 
put into graphite dies and sintered at 2000 °C for 2 h in Ar 
atmosphere under uniaxial pressure of 30 MPa followed by 
furnace cooling. The obtained Nb–Mo–ZrB
2 composites 
with 15vol% ZrB 2 and 30vol% ZrB 2 were denoted as 
NM–15Z and NM–30Z, respectively. For comparison, 
Nb−Mo alloy without ZrB
2 (named NM) was also prepared 
under the same conditions. X-ray diffraction was conducted 
by using a Rigaku D/max-Ra, Tokyo diffractometer with 
Cu K
α radiation, between 2θ of 10° and 90° and scanning 
rate of 8°/min. Back-scattered electron images of the micro-
structures were acquired with a Zeiss S-360. Chemical 
compositions were obtained with an energy-dispersive spec-
trometer. The Vickers hardness was measured using a Mi-
cro-Vickers FM-300 tester by applying a 9.8 N load for 30 s. 
Compression tests were conducted at room temperature us-
ing s hydraulic universal test er (SHT 4305). The initial 
strain rate was 1.4 × 10
−4 s−1 and the compressive deforma-
tion was recorded up to 40% or until the specimen fractured. 
The bulk density of the Nb −Mo−ZrB
2 composites was 
measured using the Archimedes method with alcohol as the 
immersing medium. Fracture toughness was determined by 
three-point bending using single-edge-notched beam (SENB) 
specimens and an electromechanical universal testing ma-
chine (C45.105). Fracture toughness specimens with 3 mm × 
4 mm cross-section and 36 mm span were prepared and the 
notch was 0.15 mm wide and 1.5 mm deep. The relative 
density was determined by dividing the bulk density by the 
theoretical density based on the rule of mixing. The volume 
fraction of secondary phase particles in the composites was 
measured by point counting using the recorded SEM images 
and an image analyzer.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Phase constitution 
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of the Nb–Mo–ZrB 2 
composites. As the XRD patterns show, the main phase in 
all three samples is (Nb, Mo)ss. In addition, diffraction 
peaks for MoB, NbB, and ZrB were also observed both in 
the NM–15Z and in the NM–30Z. Furthermore, compared 
with NM–15Z and NM–30Z, the phase constitution is the 
same with increasing amounts of ZrB
2; that is to say, the 
main phases of NM–15Z and NM–30Z are (Nb, Mo)ss and 
ZrB, whereas the main phase of NM is only (Nb, Mo)ss.

3.2. Microstructure 
Fig. 2 shows SEM images of the polished surfaces of the 
hot-pressed Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites. Energy dispersive 
spectrometer (EDS) results in Table 2 shows that the dark 
gray section in Fig. 2(a) is Nb-rich (Nb, Mo)ss and the light 
gray section is Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss. The difference between 
light and dark gray is mainly due to the different percent-
ages of Nb concentration. The darker sections have more Nb. 
According to the Mo–Nb binary alloy phase diagram [17], 
the reaction L ↔ L
1 + (Nb, Mo)ss ((2550 ± 10)°C) leads to 
the formation of Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss. Owing to the inhomo-
geneous diffusion between Nb and Mo during furnace cooling, 
Nb and Mo do not substitute each other uniformly [18]. As a 
result, Nb-rich (Nb, Mo)ss and Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss can be 
distinguished easily by energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) 
maps, as shown in Fig. 3.

As shown in Fig. 2(b), the microstructure of NM–15Z is 
different from NM because of the introduction of ZrB2 par-
ticles. Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss is irregularly shaped with many 
edges and ZrB is distributed in the (Nb, Mo)ss matrix as the 
reinforcing phase. The interface between ZrB and (Nb, 
Mo)ss is well bonded and without microcracks. In the work 
of Sun et al . [14] and Wang et al . [16], NbB and MoB 
formed according to Eqs. (2) and (3) and strengthened the 
interfacial bonding strength between the ZrB/(Nb, Mo)ss 
interfaces and facilitated the densification of the composites 
and improved the mechanical properties. The NbB and MoB 
phases in this study are difficult to be distinguished in Fig. 2(b) 
because they are incorporated in (Nb, Mo)ss or ZrB. However, 
based on the XRD diffraction peaks in Fig. 1, it is obvious 
that NbB and MoB phases formed after hot-pressing sintering. 
Furthermore, the EDX maps in Fig. 4 also show that Nb, 
Mo, and B have overlapping. 
Fig. 2(c) shows the microstructure of NM–30Z. The most 
obvious difference between NM–30Z and NM–15Z is the 
shape of the Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss and ZrB phases. In addi-
tion, based on Fig. 2, the image analysis shows that the 
volume of ZrB particles is about 5.8% and 11.5% in the 
NM–15Z and NM−30Z samples, respectively. Figs. 2(c) and 
5 show that Mo-rich (Nb, Mo)ss in NM–30Z is surrounded by 
Nb-rich (Nb, Mo)ss and has a round shape. The ZrB phase is 
also round and is distributed with Nb-rich (Nb, Mo)ss.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
The bulk densities of Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites are listed 
in Table 3. Using the rule of mixing and assuming that the 
true densities are 6.09 g/cm3 for ZrB 2 [14], 8.57 g/cm 3 for 
Nb [14], and 10.20g/cm3 for Mo [16], the theoretical densi-
ties of the Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites in this study are 9.385 
g/cm3 for NM, 8.891 g/cm 3 for NM–15Z, and 8.397 g/cm 3 
for NM–30Z. Based on the true densities, the relative densi-
ties are 93.00% for NM, 93.82% for NM–15Z, and 93.80% 
for NM–30Z. The three samples have almost equal relative 
densities and the relative densities are all lower than ex-
pected. According to a literature survey, ZrB
2 without addi-
tives has been densified by hot-pressing sintering at higher 
than 2000° C at 20–30 MPa or at reduced temperatures 
(1790–1840°C) and at much higher pressures (800–1500 
MPa) [15]. The low relative densities in this study are due to 
the reduced temperature of 2000°C and low pressure of 30 
MPa during hot-pressing sintering.

The compressive stress–strain curves of Nb–Mo–ZrB 2 
composites are shown in Fig. 6. It is obvious that NM ex-
hibits plasticity, whereas NM–15Z and NM–30Z are brittle. 
Both Nb and Mo are ductile and soft metals [18], whereas 
ZrB
2 ceramics are brittle and have fracture toughness in a 
range of 2.4–3.5 MPa·m1/2 [19–22]. The introduction of hard 
brittle ZrB2 particles not only improves the strength but also 
leads to the brittleness of NM–15Z and NM–30Z. As shown 
in Table 4, the compressive strength of NM–15Z and 
NM–30Z is 1802.5 MPa and 1905.1 MPa, respectively, and 
both are much higher than that of NM (1380.2 MPa), 
Nb–Mo–20at%ZrC (1168 MPa), and Nb–Mo–40at%ZrC 
(1705 MPa) [2]. It is reasonable to argue that ZrB
2 is a rein-
forcing phase in Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites based on secon-
dary phase dispersion strengthening. The yield strength in-
creases with increasing ZrB
2 content and the NM–30Z has 
compressive strength of 1864.4 MPa, which is higher than 
that of NM–15Z (1670.5 MPa). This is attributed to the in-
creased distribution of ZrB phase in NM–30Z more than 
that in NM–15Z. The distributed ZrB along grain bounda-
ries acts as pinning centers that slow the slipping of disloca-
tions and improves the mechanical properties. Generally, the

As shown in Table 4, the fracture toughness is 6.52 
MPa·m1/2 for NM–15Z and decreases to 5.88 MPa·m 1/2 for 
NM–30Z, suggesting that the fracture toughness decreases 
with increasing ZrB 2 content. The fracture surfaces of 
Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites are shown in Fig. 7. Trans-
granular fracture is observed in NM and both transgranular 
and intergranular fractures are observed in NM–15Z and 
NM–30Z; the transgranular and intergranular fractures are 
respectively progressing in (Nb, Mo)ss and ZrB particles. 
The (Nb, Mo)ss in Fig. 7(a) shows smooth and flat fracture 
surfaces with river-like patterns and tiny avulsion edges. 
This implies that the (Nb, Mo)ss phase undergoes plastic 
stretching. The fracturing of Nb–Mo–ZrB
2 composites 
consists of the interface separation between the (Nb, Mo)ss 
and ZrB particles, where the brittle fracturing of the ZrB 
particles is marked by the arrow in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c). 
However, the fracture toughness of NM (main phase is 
(Nb, Mo)ss) is lower than that of NM–15Z and NM–30Z 
(main phase is (Nb, Mo)ss and the brittle secondary phase 
ZrB). There are two factors that negatively affect fracture 
toughness. One is the low relative density and the other is 
the brittle fracture of the secondary phase [25]. As for NM, 
the relative density (93.00%) is lower than that of 
NM–15Z (93.82%) and NM–30Z (93.80%); the pores and 
holes in the samples initiate cracks during compressive 
strength testing. As a result, the fracture toughness of NM 
is the lowest. Comparing NM–15Z with NM–30Z, the ef-
fect of the relative density is neglected and the brittle 
fracture of the secondary phase ZrB is the main reason 
that decreases the fracture toughness. As reported by

4. Conclusions 
(1) (Nb, Mo)ss, ZrB, NbB, and MoB are observed in the 
Nb–Mo–ZrB2 composites and the shape of (Nb, Mo)ss 
changes from irregular with many edges to roundness and 
more ZrB particles tend to be distributed along the grain 
boundaries with increasing ZrB2 from 15vol% to 30vol%. 
(2) The compressive strength, yield strength, and hard-
ness increase w ith increasing ZrB 2 content and the maxi-
mum values are 1905.1 MPa, 1864.4 MPa and HV 859.8, 
respectively, whereas the fracture toughness decreases with 
increasing ZrB
2 content and the minimum value is 5.88 
MPa·m1/2 when comparing NM–15Z with NM–30Z. 
(3) ZrB2 is a reinforcing phase in Nb–Mo–ZrB2 compos-
ites because of secondary phase strengthening and fine-grain 
strengthening. The optimum content of ZrB
2 as reinforcing 
phase is higher than 30vol%.
