ABSTRACT 
It is always a great challenge to manufacture defect-free and high-performance pure tungsten by laser additive 
manufacturing until now. In this work, using nanopowder as precursors, the pure tungsten green parts were 
successfully manufactured by indirect additive manufacturing technology, powder extrusion printing. The sub -
sequent debinding-sintering process of the green parts has been optimized to obtain high-relative-density, fine- 
grain and defect-free tungsten samples. The relative density of the sample increased with the increase of sintering 
temperature. The microstructures of pure tungsten prepared by direct and indirect additive manufacturing 
techniques were studied comparatively, including laser powder bed fusion and powder extrusion printing. The 
formation of microcracks in pure tungsten samples is mainly due to the high residual stress provided by laser 
powder bed fusion and the high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature characteristics of tungsten. The sintering 
temperature of the pure tungsten samples with the highest compressive strength (1290 ± 10 MPa) was deter -
mined to be 2000 
◦
C. Compared with other sintered samples, defect-free samples sintered at 2000 
◦
C had finer 
grains (9.8 μ m) while maintaining high relative density (99.1 ± 0.4%). The higher strength of the sintered 
samples at 2000 
◦
C is related to the fine-grain strengthening and high relative density compared to samples 
obtained from other direct additive manufacturing technologies and sintered at other temperatures. The indirect 
additive manufacturing technique based on the use of nanopowder precursors developed in this work provides a 
new technical approach for the additive manufacturing of high-performance tungsten and/or other refractory 
metals. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten (W), as a type of refractory metal, has many favorable 
physical properties such as high melting point (3420 
◦
C), high density 
(19.35 g/cm
3
), high hardness, low vapor pressure, low thermal expan -
sion coefficient, excellent radiation-shielding properties and excellent 
thermal conductivity [ 1 – 3 ]. Based on this unique combination of those 
properties, tungsten has been used in a wide range of applications in 
engineering, military, medical and electronics fields, such as plasma- 
facing material for future nuclear fusion reactors, collimators and 
high-performance rocket nozzles [ 4 – 6 ]. At present, tungsten parts are 
usually prepared by powder metallurgy (PM), chemical vapor 
deposition (CVD) and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [ 7 – 9 ]. However, due 
to the high brittleness and high hardness of tungsten, it is difficult to 
obtain tungsten parts with complex structures by PM and subsequent 
machining, which further limits the application of tungsten. 
Additive manufacturing, as a near-net-shape shaping technology, is 
capable of producing parts with complex structures, thus bringing about 
the possibility to overcome the shortcomings of traditional methods 
[ 10 , 11 ]. Direct additive technology uses a high-energy source (laser or 
electron beam) to directly act on metal powder to melt, solidify and 
shape, including laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) [ 12 ], electron beam 
selective melting (EBM) [ 13 ] and laser melting deposition (LMD) [ 14 ].

with the addition of Nb. The samples with a relative density of 98% and 
microcracks were obtained. It was found that the formation of micro -

reduction in the number and discrete distribution of nanopores and the 
solid solution strengthening of Nb in W. Li et al. [ 33 ] introduced the 
secondary-phase nanoparticle ZrC into the tungsten matrix during the 
LPBF process. The addition of ZrC inhibits the formation of tungsten 
microcracks by achieving grain refinement and concentration decre -
ment of the oxygen impurities with the formation of ZrO

relative density pure tungsten and tungsten alloys can be achieved 
through direct additive manufacturing, but there were coarse tungsten 
grains and microstructural anisotropy in the sample. Therefore, if the 
advantages of powder metallurgy (preparation of high relative density 
and defect-free pure tungsten parts) and additive manufacturing 
(preparation of complex structures) can be combined, this would greatly 
promote the manufacturing of defect-free and fine-grained tungsten 
parts with complex structures. Based on this, a type of indirect AM 
technology, powder extrusion printing (PEP), combining additive 
manufacturing technology and powder metallurgy technology is 
developed to manufacture pure tungsten parts. The PEP technology uses 
a nozzle to extrude a slurry of metal powder and multi-component 
binder according to a specific scanning strategy to form green parts. 
Green parts require subsequent debinding and sintering to obtain high- 
performance sintered samples. 
In present work, the tungsten nanopowder prepared by the wet 
chemical method was innovatively used as the precursor powder, and 
the pure tungsten green parts were successfully prepared by using the 
PEP technology. The densification and microstructure differences of the 
pure tungsten samples sintered at different temperatures were investi -
gated in detail. The difference in microstructure of pure tungsten pre -
pared by LPBF and PEP was studied. The insights into the 
microstructural evolution and sintering mechanism of pure tungsten 
samples were further proposed.

further study the effect of powder particle size on the microstructure of 
sintered samples. Fig. 7 a and b show the SEM images of pure tungsten

impurities at the grain boundaries of tungsten samples prepared by 
LPBF, which would greatly increase the DBTT of the tungsten samples

localized heating/cooling cycles and high thermal gradients brought 
about by LPBF can induce high residual stresses in metal samples [ 30 ]. 
Due to the combined effect of high residual stress provided by LPBF and 
high DBTT and low grain boundary strength due to tungsten oxide im -
purities at grain boundaries, microcracks would form in pure tungsten 
samples prepared by LPBF. The maximum Von Mises residual stress 
exceeds the yield stress of the material, which can be taken as the critical 
stress for crack initiation and propagation. Thus, the size of the crack- 
affected zone in pure tungsten samples manufactured by LPBF co -
incides with the region where the maximum Von Mises residual stress 
[ 28 ]. The formation of network cracks mainly depends on the magnitude 
of the local longitudinal and transverse components of residual stress 
[ 28 ]. Therefore, PEP technology can be used to prepare pure tungsten 
samples with no defects and high relative density, which can make up 
for the shortcomings of the laser additive manufacturing to prepare pure 
tungsten samples with high melting points. 
The above results illustrate that the sintering temperature and the 
powder particle size have a significant effect on the microstructure of the 
sintered samples. Fig. 8 shows the schematic diagram of microstructural 
evolution of the tungsten samples fabricated by PEP during debinding 
and sintering process. As the temperature increased, the binder in the as- 
debinded samples gradually decomposed, leaving pores between the 
tungsten particles. During this process, tungsten particles begin to con -
tact and form sintered necks through atomic diffusion, thus forming a 
continuous network of tungsten in the sample. The formation of sintered 
necks transforms the inter-particle interface into the grain interface. The 
samples before debinding depend on the bonding between the binder 
and the tungsten particles to ensure shape integrity. With the decom -
position of the binder, the formation of sintered necks began to be the 
dominating factor for maintaining the shape of samples. Due to the 
unchanged particle shape and the presence of a large number of pores, 
the relative density of the entire sintered sample is low and no shrinkage 
occurs. With the further increase of sintering temperature, the intensi -
fication of atomic diffusion makes the sintered neck grow further. The 
migration of grain boundaries at the sintered necks into the grains leads 
to the growth of tungsten grain [ 39 , 43 , 44 ]. The reduction in the distance 
between the tungsten particles and the reduction in the number and size 
of pores left by the binder indicates that the sample begins to densify. As 
the degree of densification increases, the size of the sintered samples 
begins to shrink, the relative density increases and the grains grow. The 
sintering process is driven by the reduction in surface free energy be -
tween pores and powders [ 39 , 45 , 46 ]. That is, the higher the surface 
energy of the powder, the more conducive to the occurrence of sintering 
densification determined by the grain boundary diffusion mechanism 
[ 39 ]. Compared to micron-sized powders, nanopowders are character -
ized by high surface energies, which means that the nanopowder par -
ticles have better sintering activation than that of micron-sized powders. 
The high surface energies of the nanopowders can facilitate the diffusion 
of atoms and promotes the formation and growth of sintered necks 
during the sintering process. And the samples using nanopowders would 
form a higher number of sintered necks at lower sintering temperatures 
than that of the samples using micron-sized powders. It means that more 
grain boundaries are formed in the sintered samples using nanopowders, 
which can enhance the grain boundary diffusion mechanism and pro -
mote the densification process. Therefore, the samples using nano -
powders with high surface energies can have a higher degree of 
densification than that of the samples using micron-sized powders at the 
same sintering temperature. 
5. Conclusion 
In this work, tungsten nanopowder was innovatively used in indirect 
additive manufacturing (PEP) and successfully manufactured pure 
tungsten green parts. A high-relative-density, fine-grain and defect-free 
pure tungsten part have been successfully prepared by determining the 
appropriate debinding and sintering process. The relative density of the 
sample increases with the increase of the sintering temperature and 
reaches more than 99.0% at 2000 – 2300
◦
C. The reason for the high 
relative density of the sample is that the nanopowder with high sintering 
activation can promote densification during the sintering process. 
Compared with other sintered samples, pure tungsten samples sintered 
at 2000 
◦
C have fine grains (9.8 μ m) while maintaining high relative 
density, and possess the best combination of relative density and grain 
size. The pure tungsten sample sintered at 2000
◦
C has the highest 
compressive strength of 1290 ± 10 MPa, which is higher than that of the 
tungsten samples (1138 ± 8 MPa) sintered at 2300 
◦
C and tungsten 
samples prepared by direct additive manufacturing, which can be 
attributed to fine grain and high relative density.
