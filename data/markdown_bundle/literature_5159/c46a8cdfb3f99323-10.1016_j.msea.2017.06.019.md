ABSTRACT
Alloy element carbon was added into a lamellar γ-TiAl alloy with a composition of Ti-47Al-2Nb-2Cr. The volume
fraction of B2 phase decreased with the increase of carbon content. Most of carbon atoms were in solid solution
state in the C-containing TiAl alloys and some ﬁne carbide were dispersedly distributed in the lamellar matrix in
case of 0.5 at% carbon additions. For the TiAl alloys containing carbon, the lattice parameters (i.e., a and c) and
unit cell volume of α2 phase increased larger than those of γ phase due to higher solid solubility of carbon in the
α2 phase. Incorporations of carbon re ﬁned the interlamellar spacing of the TiAl based alloys by promoting the
nucleation of γ phase and suppressing the thickening of γ lath. The room temperature tensile properties of the
TiAl based alloy were improved by the additions of carbon and the alloy with 0.2 at% C showed the highest
tensile strength. The trans-lamellar was the major fracture mode. Besides, more excellent high temperature
ductility was acquired for the C-doped TiAl alloys because parts of the lamellae displayed the features of ductile
fracture. Presence of nano-twins in the C-containing TiAl alloys contributed to the higher high-temperature
strength.
1. Introduction
Advanced intermetallic γ-TiAl based alloys have great application
potential in aerospace and automobile industries because of low den-
sity, high speci ﬁc strength, excellent oxidation and creep resistance as
well as high strength and modulus retention at high temperature [1–3].
They have been regarded as structural high-temperature materials for
turbine blades in aircraft engines and turbocharger wheels in auto-
mobile combustion engines [4–7]. However, the room temperature
brittleness of TiAl alloys restricts their applications in wide range and
the service temperature limit of the current engineering TiAl alloys is
about 750 °C largely because of insu ﬃcient strength and creep re-
sistance at temperatures beyond [1]. Recently many researchers have
been interested in the e ﬀects of alloying elements on the micro-
structures of TiAl based alloys aiming to improve their mechanical and
technical properties [8–10]. Numerous studies have reported that the
TiAl alloys containing carbon additions exhibit more excellent room-
and elevated-temperature properties [11–13].
As the radius of C atom is much smaller than those of Ti atom
(0.145 nm) and Al atom (0.143 nm), the C atoms usually occupy the
interstitial sites of L1
0 (γ-TiAl) and D0 19 (α2-Ti3Al) lattices in the TiAl
based alloys. The TiAl based alloys doped with carbon can show a
substantial increase in strength, which result from the solid solution
hardening and precipitation of ﬁne carbide [2,12,13]. Solid solution
eﬀects of carbon atoms on the TiAl alloys have been comprehensively
studied by Worth et al. [14]. They found that the TiAl alloys showed a
signiﬁcant reduction in the minimum creep rate and the creep re-
sistance increased when the carbon atoms were in solid solution state.
Some academics have pointed that C atoms in solid solution acted as
weak obstacle and were not very e ﬃcient to hinder dislocation gliding
[3,8] These weak obstacles could be easily overcome in the presence of
thermal activation. For higher concentration of carbon additions, C-
containing precipitates formed in the TiAl alloys. These precipitates
were either Ti
3AlC-phase (P-phase) or Ti 2AlC-phase (H-phase) de-
pending on the C concentration or subsequent treatments [7,12,15,16].
Formation of ﬁne Ti 3AlC perovskite precipitate could act as strong
obstacle with long-range stress ﬁeld. The dislocations could not go over
these strong obstacles with the aid of thermal activation. Then a de-
crease of dislocation mobility corresponds to a higher creep resistance.
Ti2AlC hexagon phase could also precipitate in the C-doped TiAl alloys
under certain conditions. Nevertheless, the formation of the coarse
Ti
2AlC precipitate was less e ﬃcient in hardening TiAl alloys compared
to the Ti 3AlC phase [13].
In the present paper, a lamellar γ-TiAl alloy was micro-alloyed with
diﬀerent contents of carbon. The microstructures and mechanical
properties of C-containing TiAl alloys were compared with the

2. Experimental details
Three materials based on the Ti-47Al-2Nb-2Cr alloy were in-
vestigated within the present study and the importance of the alloy
system (Ti-47Al-2Nb-2Cr) was clari ﬁed in our previous paper [17]. The
chemical compositions of the alloys were summarized in Table 1. The
three alloys were called TNC, TNC-0.2C and TNC-0.5C, respectively. All
the alloys were ﬁrst prepared by vacuum consumable melting tech-
nology and then re-melted in a vacuum cold crucible inducting melting
furnace under argon atmosphere for uniform composition.
The samples for microstructure investigation and phase constituents
analysis were cut from the ingots by wire electrical discharge machine.
After grinding and polishing, the microstructure observations of the
samples were taken by Quanta 200FEG scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) in back-scattered electron (BSE) mode. The phase identi ﬁcation
of all the alloys were further con ﬁrmed by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD,
Model D/maxrB, Japan) with Cu K α radiation ( λ = 0.154056 nm), and
the scanning degree range of 2 θ was 20–90°. Detailed investigations of
the microstructures were carried out through transmission electron
microscopy (TEM). The thin foils for TEM observations were ﬁrst
manually ground to 80 µm and then twin-jet electron-polished in a
solution of 60 vol% methanol, 35 vol% butanol and 5 vol% perchloric
at −30 °C and 15 V.
The room- and high-temperature tensile specimens were cut from
the ingots by electric discharge wire-cutting and the gauge sizes were
6 mm × 2 mm × 18 mm and 5 mm × 2 mm × 20 mm, respectively.
The room-temperature tensile tests were carried out on the 5569
Instron test machine with a strain rate of 0.5 mm/min. The high-tem-
perature tensile tests were conducted on the Instron-5500R machine
with a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min. The heating rate was 10 K/min and
the specimens were maintained at the temperature for 5 min after
reaching the operating temperature. In order to avoid oxidation of
fracture surface, the high-temperature specimens were quenched into
water instantly after tensile testing.

3.1. Microstructure observations
Eﬀects of C additions on the microstructures of all the alloys are
investigated by SEM observations in BSE mode as shown in Fig. 1 .A s
shown in Fig. 1(a), alloy TNC exhibits near lamellar (NL) micro-

2+γ)
lamellae as well as blocky (B2+ γ) phase. The chemical compositions of
the marked areas in Fig. 1 (a) have been detected through energy dis-
persive spectrometer (EDS) and the results are listed in Fig. 1(b). It can
be seen that the chemical compositions of the ( α2+γ) lamellae are
basically consistent with the nominal compositions, the blocky γ phase
is rich in Al and much more β-stablizers (Cr, Nb) are involved in the B2
phase. The volume fraction of the B2 phase in the alloy TNC is about
0.65%, which is measured through the computerized image analysis. By
contrast, the microstructures of the TiAl alloys containing carbon show
no signi ﬁcant changes and mainly contain ( α2+γ) lamellae. However,
the volume fraction of the B2 phase decreases dramatically in the C-
doped TiAl alloys and the B2 phase almost disappears in case of TNC-
0.5C alloy. Reduction in the volume fraction of the B2 phase is related
to the additions of the strong α-stablizer C. The room-temperature B2
phase is originated from the high-temperature β phase. Incorporation of
carbon can promote the phase transformation of β→α, leading to the
decrease of residual high-temperature β phase. Then the amount of B2
phase in the C-containing TiAl alloy is reduced when the temperature is
cooled to room temperature. In addition, some bright particles were
found in the microstructure of alloy TNC-0.5C at higher ampli ﬁcation.
As shown in Fig. 2(a), these particles are ﬁne and dispersedly dis-
tributed in the lamellae matrix. The chemical compositions of the
particles are detected. As sizes of these particles are very small, the
elements of the particles can only be determined qualitatively and the
quantitative analysis of the chemical compositions is not accurate (due
to the resolution restriction of EDS investigation). The EDS results of the
particle are presented in Fig. 2 (b) and the principle elements of the
particle are Ti, Al and C. So it can be concluded that these particles are
carbide.
In order to further deﬁ ne the compositions and crystal structure of
the carbide in the TNC-0.5C alloy, the particles are investigated by
TEM. Scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) is conducted
and the high angle annular dark ﬁled (HAADF) image of a particle is
presented in Fig. 3(a). The lens-shaped particle is identi ﬁed as carbide
mainly composed of Ti and C, poor in Al, whereas the existence of Cr
and Nb are hardly detected in the particle as shown in Fig. 3(b) –(f).
Fig. 3(g) shows the selected area di ﬀraction patterns (SADPs) of the
carbide indicating the carbide with hexagon structure. Considering the
compositions and crystal structure, the carbide is deduced to be Ti
2AlC.

It has been proved that the formation of the lamellar structure re-
sults from the precipitation of γ lamellae in the hexagonal α matrix,
following the reaction →+→+α αγα γ2 [19]. The nucleation for the
γ phase in α matrix involves: (1) a crystal structure change from h.c.p.
to f.c.c.; (2) a chemical composition change through atom transfer.
As shown in Fig. 8(a), the microstructure is composed of hexagonal
α phase before the nucleation of γ phase and the stacking sequence is
ABABAB…in the hexagonal α matrix. During continuous cooling from α
phase ﬁeld, a perfect a/3 1120 dislocation dissociates into two
Shockley partials bordering a stacking fault
( →+ +aaS F a/3 1120 /3 1010 /3 0110 ). Such a stacking fault lo-
cally creates an f.c.c. type stacking sequences in the hexagonal matrix
with either ABC or ACB depending on the sign of the Shockley partials
(as shown in Fig. 8(b)). Repeating this mechanism every two basal
planes of hexagonal α matrix brings the crystal structure change
(h.c.p.→f.c.c.) to completion. As the close-packed planes and directions
of f.c.c. and h.c.p. are strictly parallel to each other, planar and highly
coherent interface are formed [19]. Both the formation of a local f.c.c.
stacking sequence and the creation of highly coherent interface be-
tween this f.c.c. stacking zone and the hexagonal matrix lower the
nucleation barrier to the precipitation of the γ phase. Thereby, these
f.c.c. stacking zones in the hexagonal matrix are expected to be nu-
cleation sites of γ lamellae.
Due to di ﬀerent chemical compositions in α and γ phases, a che-
mical composition change through atom transfer is also necessary for
the nucleation of γ phase. If the atom transfer is carried out through
classical di ﬀusion process, interface energy of a local f.c.c. stacking
zone and α matrix is the driving force of atom di ﬀusion. However, the
interface between a local f.c.c. stacking zone and α matrix is planar and
highly coherent, implying low interface energy. Di ﬀusion along the
interface is restricted. According to the previous studies reported by
Denquin et al. and Howe et al. [19,21], modi ﬁcation of chemical
composition can proceed by terrace-ledge-kink. As shown in Fig. 8(c),
the terrace bordering the ledge is ﬂat and the kink is the favorable site
to the atomic attachment [21]. The chemical composition change is
completed by Ti and Al atoms attachment to the kink.
The role of carbon in re ﬁning microstructure can be explained on
the basis of the lamellar transformation mechanism as discussed above.
Carbon atoms segregated at prior α grain boundaries can lower the
stacking fault energy, thus leading to the increase in the frequency of
stacking fault. As these stacking fault zones are the nucleation sites of γ
phase, carbon additions e ﬀectively increase the nucleation rate of γ
phase. On the other hand, the solid solubility of carbon atoms in α
phase is much higher than that in the γ phase, and carbon atoms are
expelled from the γ precipitate during the solid state phase transfor-
mation of
→+α αγ . By segregating at α/γ interface locations, carbon
atoms destroy the absorption sites of Ti or Al atoms in the kink, which
retard the thickening of γ lath. If carbon content is high enough and
carbide forms at the interfaces, the growth of γ lath is further restricted
by the carbide. The combined e ﬀect of enhancing nucleation rate and
decreasing thickening rate leads to ﬁner lamellae in the C-containing
TiAl alloys.
3.4. Room temperature tensile performances and fracture surface analysis
The room temperature tensile performances of Ti-47Al-2Nb-2Cr-xC
(x = 0, 0.2, 0.5) alloys are displayed in Table 3. It can be seen that the
ultimate strength and elongation are improved with carbon additions
and TNC-0.2C alloy shows the highest ultimate strength and elongation.
The improvement of the tensile strength of the TiAl alloys with carbon
additions can mainly be attributed to the re ﬁnement of interlamellar
spacing. More semi-coherent interfaces are involved in the TiAl alloys
with ﬁner lamellae, which could act as the obstacle to the dislocations
movements. Dislocations are piled up at the semi-coherent interface
and higher applied stress is needed to drive dislocations motion during
the tensile. Then the TiAl alloys containing carbon show higher tensile
strength. Furthermore, volume fraction of B2 phase is another factor
inﬂuencing the tensile strength of TiAl-based alloys at room tempera-
ture. Researchers have pointed that the presence of B2 phase could
usually act as the cracking source and damage the tensile properties of
TiAl-based alloys [22–24]. As a result, the reduction or disappearance
of B2 phase in the C-doped TiAl alloys also contributes to the higher
tensile strength at room temperature.

alloys. In the previous studies, the lamellar TiAl alloys present two

Moreover, the fracture surfaces of the sample alloys display some
other features. As shown in Fig. 11 , river-like patterns are observed in
the fracture surfaces of the three alloys, which are the unique char-
acteristics of cleavage fracture. Cleavage fracture is the trans-lamellar
fracture which occurs along a certain crystallographic plane at an ex-
tremely rapid rate. Cleavage facet is the crystallographic plane with the
lowest surface energy, usually is the low index plane. But the crystal-
lographic planes with low indexes are generally not parallel in the
polycrystalline metals. The cleavage steps are formed on the fracture
when the cracks propagate across the grains. Actually, the river-like
patterns is the top view of the cleavage steps. The “river” initiates from
the grain boundary, ﬂows in the grain and ends at the grain boundary of
the same grain. So the source of the “river” is usually considered to be
the crack origin and the path of the “river” is the same as direction of
crack propagation. It can be concluded that the cracks initiate in the
interior of the specimens. In addition, secondary cracking along la-
mellar interface after room temperature tensile can be occasionally seen
in the TNC-0.2C alloy (as shown in Fig. 11 (d)). In terms of crack
branching, the secondary cracks propagation along the lamellar inter-
faces is seeking the path of least resistance, which means that lamellar
structure of TNC-0.2C alloy provides stronger resistance against trans-
lamellar fracture. As a result, this alloy shows higher tensile strength in
comparison with the other two alloys under investigation.

the environment of more than 700 °C. So the high-temperature per-
formances of TiAl alloys are critical parameters, which determine the
operational security of high-temperature components. In this section
the TiAl alloys are deformed under the uniaxial tensile stress at 800 °C.
The in ﬂuences of carbon additions on the high temperature tensile
properties and deformation microstructures are investigated.

4. Conclusions
This paper was conducted to investigate the in ﬂuences of carbon
additions on the microstructure and tensile properties of a lamellar γ-
TiAl alloy. The following results were obtained.
(1) The volume fraction of B2 phase decreases with carbon additions
and the B2 phase almost disappears at 0.5 at% C. The C atoms are in
solid solution state at low concentration and some carbide particles
exist in the alloy TNC-0.5C.
(2) The lattice parameters of α2 phase increases more signi ﬁcantly than
those of γ phase because of higher solid solubility limit in α2 phase.
Carbon segregation can promote the nucleation of γ phase.
(3) TiAl alloys containing carbon additions show better room tem-
perature tensile performances compared with C-free TiAl alloys.
Trans-lamellar is the major fracture mode, while inter-lamellar
fracture can also be found in the alloys TNC and TNC-0.5C. Inter-
lamellar secondary cracking is occasionally seen in the alloy TNC-
0.2C.
(4) The high-temperature fracture morphologies of three alloy show
characteristics of trans-lamellar fracture and inter-lamellar sec-
ondary cracking. A few cleavage facets can be found in the alloys
TNC and TNC-0.5C. The deformation twins are easier to form in the
C-containing TiAl alloys during high-temperature deformation. The
twins can act as internal obstacles to dislocations motions, thus
leading to more excellent high-temperature tensile properties
compared with the reference TiAl alloy without C.
