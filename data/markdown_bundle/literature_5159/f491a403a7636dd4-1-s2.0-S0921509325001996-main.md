A B S T R A C T
The mechanical properties and microstructural evolution of solution-treated and aged Al 0.3CoCrFeNi high-
entropy alloys (HEAs) are systematically explored. The solution-treated alloy exhibits a single face-centered
cubic (FCC) phase, whereas the aged alloy displays striped B 2 precipitate bands rich in Al and Ni. Uniaxial
compression tests are conducted in a strain rate range of 10 -3–2700 s−1 and a temperature range of 173–693 K
for both types of the alloys. At a constant temperature or strain rate, yield strength increases with increasing
strain rate or decreasing temperature. The aged alloy demonstrates a slightly higher yield strength due to the
presence of B2 precipitates giving rise to precipitate strengthening and enhanced deformation twinning. The
investigation reveals multiple deformation mechanisms in both alloys, including dislocations, stacking faults,
immobile Lomer-Cottrell locks, kink bands and deformation twins. Dislocations are along the {111} slip planes
in the solution-treated alloy in contrast with tangled dislocations in the aged alloy. Besides, B 2 precipitates,
lower temperatures or higher strain rate all facilitate the activation of deformation twinning. Additionally, a
modified Johnson-Cook-Cowper Symonds (JC-CS) constitutive model is developed, effectively capturing the
plastic flow behavior of both alloy types across a broad range of strain rates and temperatures.
1. Introduction
Dynamic response of structural materials plays a pivotal role in
various engineering applications, such as aerospace, defense technolo-
gies, and automobile industry [1–4]. With the growing demands for
high strain-rate service conditions, structural materials are expected
to maintain high strength and toughness at high strain rates. The
family of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [5–9] with a single face-centered
cubic (FCC) crystal structure and a mix of transitional elements ex-
hibit outstanding mechanical properties, including superior strength
and remarkable ductility [10–12], high radiation resistance [13,14],
excellent creep resistance [15], high corrosion resistance [16,17] and
an extraordinary capacity for strain-rate hardening [18,19].
Generally, single-phase FCC-type HEAs exhibit a trade-off between
strength and ductility, which seriously restricts their practical utilities.

In FCC HEAs, the trade-off effect can be mitigated via different meth-
ods, such as precipitation hardening [20–23], twinning-induced plas-
ticity (TWIP) [24,25] and hetero-deformation-induced (HDI) strength-
ening [26,27].
The addition of Al to FCC HEAs can facilitate the formation of
abundant secondary phases, such as the body-centered cubic (BCC)
phase, and ordered B2, L21 and L12 phases [11,28,29]. FCC-based
HEAs with low stacking fault energy (SFE), such as Al𝑥CoCrFeNi (𝑥=
0–0.3) alloys, are highly susceptible to generate deformation twins
and various precipitates at different temperatures [30,31]. Different
thermo-mechanical processes can induce a coherent ordered L12 phase,
hard B2 precipitates or the 𝜎-phase in a well known FCC-based alloy,
Al0.3CoCr FeNi [32–34]. It is well expected that this alloy bears great po-
tential as an advanced functional material for demanding engineering
applications in extreme service environments.

Extensive research has been conducted on the precipitation be-
havior, mechanical properties, and plastic deformation mechanisms of
Al0.3CoCr FeNi HEA under quasi-static (10−4–10−1 s−1) loading [32,
35–38]. The ordered precipitates, particularly L12 and B2, play a cru-
cial role in optimizing the mechanical properties of HEAs [39,40].
A cryo-rolled Al0.3CoCr FeNi alloy, characterized by a heterogeneous
microstructure composed of deformed grains, recrystallized fine grains,
and B2 precipitates, achieves an impressive combination of strength and
ductility, with a yield strength of 911 MPa and a fracture elongation of
23% [41]. Notably, the tensile yield strength of Al0.3CoCr FeNi can be
significantly enhanced from 160 MPa to over 1.8 GPa through grain
refinement, dislocation strengthening, and precipitate hardening [33].
The local stress partitioning between the FCC matrix and the B2 phase
in Al0.3CoCr FeNi under tension is elucidated through in situ electron
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis. Furthermore, crystal plasticity
finite element modeling (CPFEM) is employed to better understand this
phenomenon [42].
In contrast to quasi-static loading, deformation of materials at high
strain rates represents a complex interplay of various factors, includ-
ing strain hardening, strain rate strengthening, thermal softening, and
phonon drag effect [7,43,44]. However, the studies on high-strain-rate
loading remain relatively scarce [45–47]. Under dynamic compression
with split Hopkinson pressure bar systems, precipitate-enhanced twin-
ning contributes to extraordinary dynamic work hardening (exceeding
2200 MPa) and notable strain rate sensitivity in Al0.3CoCr FeNi [45].
Under higher-strain-rate plate impact loading, grain size and hetero-
geneous microstructures in Al0.3CoCr FeNi can enhance spall strength,
while L12 precipitates can surpass deformation twinning under ultra-
high strain rate (over 105 s−1) tension [47]. Moreover, Al0.3CoCr FeNi
subjected to ballistic impact [48] and dynamic shear testing using
hat-shaped samples [49] demonstrates superior ballistic performance
and remarkable resistance to shear localization, respectively. Under
dynamic loading conditions, materials may experience a transition in
plastic deformation mechanisms, resulting in a significant increase in
flow stress with rising strain rates [50–52]. Therefore, it is essential
to develop appropriate constitutive models to effectively describe and
predict the yield strength and strain hardening behavior of alloys across
a wide range of strain rates and temperatures.
In this work, the mechanical properties and microstructural evo-
lution of the solution-treated and aged Al0.3CoCr FeNi HEAs under
uniaxial compression are systematically investigated over a wide range
of strain rates and temperatures. The effects of strain rate, tempera-
ture and microstructure on mechanical response are thoroughly ex-
plored, alongside the discussions of deformation mechanisms. For
both alloys, yield strength increases with higher strain rates or lower
temperatures at a fixed temperature or strain rate, respectively. The
aged Al0.3CoCr FeNi alloy, featuring a FCC-B2 duplex microstructure,
demonstrates slightly higher yield strength compared to the single-FCC
solution-treated alloy. Multiple deformation mechanisms, including
dislocations, stacking faults, immobile Lomer-Cottrell locks, kink bands
and deformation twins are revealed through postmortem characteriza-
tions. The morphology of dislocations varies between these alloys. B2
precipitates, lower temperature, and higher strain rate all promote the
activation of deformation twins. Moreover, a modified Johnson-Cook-
Cowper Symonds (JC-CS) constitutive model is adopted to describe the
dynamic flow behavior of the HEAs.

the striker bar and the incident bar to weaken the Pochhammer–Chree

1.5 mm in thickness, depending on the desired loading strain rate.
The cylindrical specimen is placed between the incident bar and the
transmission bar, and vaseline is spread onto all the contact surfaces
to reduce friction. The SHPB system uses high-pressure nitrogen to
accelerate the striker bar. The striker bar then impacts the incident
bar, inducing an incident wave, a reflected wave and a transmitted
wave, and plastic deformation in the specimen. The incident wave, the
reflected wave and the transmission wave are recorded with the strain
gauges (4) on the incident and transmission bars, as shown in Fig. 3c. At
least three repeat tests are conducted for each experimental condition

The true stress–strain curves of the ST and AG alloys at various
strain rates and ambient temperature are depicted in Fig. 4a and b,

4. Discussion
4.1. Deformation mechanism
The elevated aging temperature (1073 K) provides adequate energy
for the migration of Al and Ni elements, which exhibit a greater propen-
sity to be incorporated into the BCC phase [2,69]. The enrichment of Al
and Ni contributes to the formation of the BCC structure and may also
promote the emergence of a minor (Al, Ni)-rich B2 ordered phase in the
AG alloy (Fig. 2d, e). This phenomenon is attributed to reduced atomic
packing efficiency, as well as the highly negative mixing enthalpy of the
Al-Ni atomic pair [36,70,71]. Generally, BCC phases exhibit superior
strength compared to the FCC matrix, due to the varying number of
available slip systems [72,73]. Quasi-static compression tests demon-
strate that yield strength of the AG alloy (∼291 MPa), which consists
of FCC matrix and B2 precipitates, surpasses that of single-phase ST
alloy (∼202 MPa). In the AG samples, nano-precipitate bands located at
the grain boundaries can impede dislocation motion (Fig. 7d), leading
to precipitate strengthening [74]. Additionally, the blocking and piling
up of dislocations at phase boundaries also contribute to the relatively
higher yield strength of the AG alloy when subjected to high strain rate
loading (Fig. 4).

Low stacking fault energy FCC-type HEAs, such as CoCrFeNi, CoCr-
FeMNNi, and Al0.1CoCr FeNi exhibit a heightened susceptibility to
{111}⟨112⟩ deformation twinning [75,76], especially under high strain
rate conditions [10,67,77]. In our study, deformation twinning is pro-
moted in the AG alloy (Fig. 6e) with a FCC-B2 duplex microstructure,
compared to the single FCC-phase ST alloy. The harder B2 phases
within the FCC matrix can function as an Eshelby-type inclusion [78],
effectively blocking mobile dislocations to trigger twinning, which is
consistent with TEM observations (Fig. 7d). Furthermore, the promo-
tion of deformation twinning can be attributed to the Kurdjumov-
Sachs (KS) orientation relationship between the FCC and B2 phases,
which contributes to an increased local stress level during the dynamic
deformation process [79,80]. Additionally, density functional theory
calculations [81,82] suggest that the segregation of Al and Ni normally
leads to a high local SFE, resulting in an increased dislocation density,
as illustrated in Fig. 7d. The actual contents of Al and Ni elements with
high SFE in the FCC matrix are lower than the nominal compositions
caused by elemental partitioning (Fig. 2d, e), resulting lower SFE
of the matrix and thus promoting twin formation (Fig. 6e) [83,84].
The influence of the B2 phase in promoting twinning within the
Al0.3CoCr FeNi alloy is also supported by previous molecular dynamics
simulations [38].
In Al0.3CoCr FeNi HEAs, significant temperature-dependent varia-
tions in yield strength and work hardening capacity are observed
(Fig. 4c and d). Yield strength of∼515 MPa and 530 MPa are achieved
for the ST and AG samples at cryogenic temperature (173 K), repre-
senting a strength enhancement by 63% and 38% compared to room
temperature (298 K), respectively. The AG sample exhibits a higher
density of shock-induced deformation twins after dynamic loading at
cryogenic temperature, while deformation twins in the ST alloy are only
observed at 173 K. In addition to the alloy composition, temperature
is a crucial factor that affects the competitive deformation mechanisms
between dislocation slip and deformation twinning [85–87]. Extensive
twinning is anticipated at low temperatures, as the critical stress for
dislocation slip sharply increases while the twinning stress remains
nearly constant with decreasing temperature [88,89]. The formation
of deformation twins introduces additional interfaces and reduces the
mean free path of dislocations (dynamic Hall–Petch effect) [86,90].
These additional twin boundaries impede dislocation movement and
lead to the accumulation of high-density dislocations, resulting in
dislocation proliferation [91]. Consequently, twin-related deformation
behavior can further enhance the strength and strain-hardening ability
of the Al0.3CoCr FeNi HEA at cryogenic temperatures, indicating its
potential for superior performance under extreme conditions. A similar
phenomenon is observed in other FCC HEAs/MEAs such as CrMnFe-
CoNi [86,92] and CrCoNi [85,87], for which strong strain hardening
induced by deformation twinning at low temperatures significantly
enhances both the uniform plasticity and fracture toughness of the
alloys.

In order to validate the obtained constitutive models, two additional
sets of experiments are conducted, and the results are compared with fi-
nite element method (FEM) simulations of SHPB. The FEM simulations,
along with the supplementary SHPB tests for the ST and AG samples,
are carried out at 2000 s−1 and 223 K. Detailed information regarding
the FEM simulation are provided in Appendix A.3.

stress histories in the incident bar (SHI) and the transmission bar
(SHT) from simulations (Fig. A.4) in Appendix A.3. The simulated stress

support to the validity of the constitutive models.
5. Conclusions
Mechanical response and deformation mechanisms of solution-
treated (ST) and aged (AG) Al0.3CoCr FeNi alloys are investigated over
wide strain rates and temperatures. The main conclusions are list as
follows:
• The aged alloy contains striped B2 precipitate bands, which are
enriched in Al and Ni, but depleted in Co, Cr and Fe, in contrast
with the single FCC phase solution-treated alloy.
• Compared to the solution-treated alloy, the aged alloy exhibits a
slightly higher yield strength due to precipitation strengthening
and precipitate-enhanced deformation twinning. For both the
alloys, an increase in yield strength is observed with an increase
in strain rate or a decrease in temperature.
• Microstructural characterizations of both the solution-treated and
aged alloys reveal multiple deformation mechanisms, including
dislocation slip, stacking faults, immobile L-C locks, kink bands
and deformation twins. The morphology of dislocations is dif-
ferent for these two alloys. The B2 precipitate, low temperature
and high strain rate all facilitate the activation of deformation
twinning.
• Modified JC–CS constitutive models are established considering
the effects of temperature and strain rate on plastic flow in the
solution-treated and aged alloys.
