Hot tensile characteristics and microstructure 
of a Cu-0.65Cr-0.08Zr alloy for fusion reactor applications 
G. Piatti and D. Boerman 
European Community Commission, Joint Research Centre, Ispra site, Institute for Advanced Materials, I-21020 lspra, 
Varese, Italy 
Received 23 April 1991; accepted 13 May 1991 
Tensile tests were carried out at different temperatures covering a wide temperature range from 20 to 750°C on a heat 
treatable Cu-0.6 wt% Cr-0.08 wt% Zr alloy of interest for nuclear fusion reactor application as primary heat sink material. 
The alloy shows good strength properties up to 500’ C. Just above this temperature, a drop in the 0.2% yield strength, a 
reliable indicator of a softening process, occurs. A loss in ductility is observed at intermediate temperatures with a minimum 
(elongation to rupture = 5.5%) at 550 o C. This embrittlement, according to microstructural investigations (optical microscopy 
and scanning electron microscopy) on several deformed specimens, is due to an intergranular cracking process, which is 
typical of several copper alloys when hot deformed. 
1. Introduction 
A combination of high strength, high softening resis- 
tance and high thermal conductivity at elevated temper- 
atures can be achieved in wrought copper alloys, con- 
taining low-solubility elements such as chromium and 
zirconium in low concentration (generally less then 1 
wt%), by thermal decomposition from a supersaturated 
cy-copper solution (precipitation hardening treatment 
often referred to as age hardening) resulting in a fine 
scale dispersion of a complex second phase in a copper 
matrix [l-5]. Further improvement in strength can be 
obtained by additional cold working before or after or 
before and after ageing. There are several applications 
for these alloys in different industrial fields. A new use 
as heat sink material in the impurity control system of 
near-term experimental fusion reactors has been sug- 
gested [6-91. However, the response to crucial (heat and 
irradiation) conditions in fusion devices is difficult to 
evaluate reliably because of the scarcity of tempera- 
ture-mechanical property data in both unirradiated and 
irradiated conditions [6-81. 
The present work deals with high temperature tensile 
characteristics (in unirradiated conditions) of the heat 
treatable Cu-0.65 wt’% Cr-0.08 wt% Zr alloy. This 
material has been considered in a NET (Next European 
Torus) divertor design for coolant tubes [lO,ll] and has 
also been used in some JET (Joint European Torus) 
components [12]. Tensile tests were carried out at differ- 
ent temperatures in secondary vacuum over a large 
temperature range (20-750 o C). Factors influencing the 
deformation and fracture behaviour were studied by 
optical and scanning electron microscopy @EM) and 
energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDS). The final pur- 
pose was to establish a correlation between tensile char- 
acteristics and microstructure.

3. Experimental procedure 
Round tensile specimens (4 mm diameter and 20 mm 
gauge length) were machined from as-received rods. No 
porosity or other defects were detected in the specimens 
by careful non-destructive X-ray testing. A series of 
tensile tests was performed in vacuum (< 10W3 Pa) at 
various temperatures from 20 to 750°C at a single 
strain rate of 8.3 X 10e4 s-l. Two specimens were 
tested for each condition. All specimens were pulled to 
fracture by an Instron electromechanical-assisted ma- 
chine programmed to run at constant extension rate. 
The temperature variation along the gauge length of the 
specimen was maintained at f lo C. After loading the 
tensile specimens into the furnace at a heating time 
depending on test temperature, a delay of 30 min was 
necessary to ensure that uniform test temperature was 
achieved. 
Longitudinal sections, cut from the halves of all 
broken specimens, were prepared for metallographic 
observations by mechanical grinding and polishing and 
chemical etching (NH,OH-H,O,-water solution). 
Fracture surfaces of all broken specimens were investi- 
gated by SEM (Philips 505) to assess the fracture mode. 
Lastly, microhardness tests (50 g load) were performed 
using a digital eyepiece hardness tester (Leitz Miniload) 
on longitudinal sections of several broken specimens. 
Three different regions (grip, uniform elongation and 
fracture) of every section were subjected to hardness 
indentation.

Microscopic features of fracture surfaces of selected 
broken specimens revealed by SEM are reported in figs. 
8 and 9. The fully ductile fracture appearance delin- 
eated in optical observations on specimens deformed at 
moderate temperature from 2O“C (fig 6a) to 200°C is 
confirmed in the SEM fracture of fig. 8. The fracture 
zone seen in fig. 8 contains dimples of various sizes due 
to the presence in the copper matrix of different popu- 
lations of void nucleating impurity particles. Some of 
the cavities (fig. 8b) are clearly associated with the 
round and rod-shaped particles (< 3 pm mean size) 
described previously (see fig. lb). The dimples are deep 
with much plastic deformation on the ridges. 
At a test temperature of 250°C the fracture path 
exhibits a significant change (fig. 9a). There is the 
appearance of a mixed mechanism of fracture (dimples 
coalescence plus intergranular separation). The inter- 
granular fracture path is incomplete and the remaining 
material fractures by voids coalescence and there are 
plastic deformations. Secondary cracks are also present. 
Intergranular areas are covered with myriads of ex- 
tremely fine and shallow microvoids and dimples as 
shown at high magnification in fig. 9b. The above 
microstructural aspects also characterize the fracture 
surfaces of specimens deformed at higher temperatures 
up to 550” C (minimum ductility temperature). The 
incidence of grain boundary increases with temperature 
from 250 to 450° C (fig. SC). However, as can be ob- 
served from the figure, the areas characterized by 
coalescence of dimples do not disappear and there is 
also evidence of plastic deformation striations near to 
these areas (fig. 9d). At 600°C and higher, the fracture 
mode changes gradually and the fracture surfaces ap- 
pear macroscopically brittle (fig. 9e) and microscopi- 
cally ductile (fig. 9f). The fracture areas are smooth and 
rounded because of the effects of surfaces tension and 
diffusivity at elevated temperatures. Figs. 9e and f refer 
to the test temperature of 700 o C which is equivalent to 
a high value of the homologous temperature, T/T, = 
0.73, where T, is the melting point in degrees Kelvin. 
According to the above experimental findings the 
tensile behaviour of the present alloy is characterized by 
a loss of ductility (elongation to fracture) in the temper- 
ature range referred to as region II in fig. 4a. Grain 
boundary separation occurs in this region due to micro- 
voids coalescence at the interface of the grains as a 
result of decohesion of the precipitate/matrix inter- 
faces. However, this is not a matter of a catastrophic 
brittle separation because the intergranular path, as 
previously observed, is incomplete and the remaining 
material fractures by dimples coalescence. Above the 
minimum of ductility at 550°C, restauration mecha- 
nims (grain boundary movement and dynamic recrys- 
tallization) become operative and there is a sudden 
increase of ductility. The above explanations are in 
agreement with literature on hot deformed copper and 
copper alloys [21-24,301. 
Conclusions 
(1) The investigated solution treated, cold worked and 
aged Cu-0.6 wt% Cr-0.08 wt% Zr alloy shows good 
strength and good softening resistance up to 500 o C. 
Above this temperature coarsening and loss of 
coherency of the small Cu-rich precipitates and 
restauration of the matrix occur. 
(2) A trough in ductility is observed at intermediate 
temperatures with minimum at 550 o C. This embrit- 
tlement is due to a intergranular cracking process. 
Above 55O“C the ductility recovers because the 
intergranular cracking is suppressed by grain 
boundary migration and dynamic recrystallization. 
(3) The above mentioned ductility loss should not limit 
an eventual application of the alloy, because the 
minimum ductility values of the alloy for fusion 
components operating up to 350°C (elongation to 
fracture > 8%) are higher than that required for 
safe performance. However, the lack of data on 
irradiation effects does not permit to make a final 
conclusion.
