1. Introduction
The long-range ordered structure ( γ 0 precipitation phase) of
closely arranged atoms affects the motion and multiplication
of dislocations, making it one of the most effective methods
to strengthen materials by introducing ordered nanoprecipitates
into the materials. This strengthening strategy has been widely
applied to various metal materials, such as
martensitic alloys, [1] magnesium alloys, [2]
and nickel-based superalloys.[3] The volume
fraction of the ordered strengthening phase
(generally about 20– 60%[4– 6]) is a key factor
determining the strength of the alloys. [7– 9]
However, as the increase of the strengthen-
ing phase, the crystal structure of the alloy
becomes highly ordered, leading to severe
room temperature brittleness. In addition,
the alloying concept of conventional alloys
with a single main element intrinsically
limits the quantity of the ordered strength-
ening phase, making it dif ﬁcult to obtain
the optimal combination of strength and
ductility.[10]
The recent discovery of high-entropy
alloys (HEAs) offers a promising pathway
for the development of materials with
advanced structural applications. [11– 20]
Several strategies have been used to intro-
duce the precipitation phase into HEAs and
demonstrated to have the potential to
increase the strength of alloys without sacri ﬁcing ductility. He
et al. [21] demonstrated that proper thermomechanical treatment
and the addition of small quantities of Ti and Al can produce
dispersed coherent L1 2 nanoprecipitates in the face center cubic
(FCC) HEA alloy matrix. These nanoprecipitates can signiﬁcantly
increase the strength of the alloy. Fu et al. [22] designed a novel
structure for introducing an increased high density of

great signi ﬁcance for structural applications. However, low volume fractions
of L1 2 precipitates in conventional alloys (nearly lower than 60%) tend to
limit their practical usage, while the strengths of the alloys generally increase
with L1 2 precipitation contents. Herein, a novel high-entropy alloy (HEA)
Ni35Co35Fe10Al8Ti10B2 with ultrahigh concentration L1 2 precipitates is success-
fully designed aided by the calculation of phase diagrams (CALPHAD). The
volume fraction of L1 2 precipitates in this HEA is up to 75% and outperforms
that of most of traditional superalloys. The novel L1 2-strengthened
Ni35Co35Fe10Al8Ti10B2 has an ultrahigh tensile yield strength of /C25 1.45 GPa,

temperature. The desirable strength –ductility combination is superior to most of
conventional superalloys and reported HEAs, mainly due to the presence of
ultrahigh concentration L1 2 precipitates that act as dislocation obstacles and the
formation of numerous stacking faults and deformation twining. This work is
expected to provide guidance for developing new high-performance HEAs with
an excellent combination of strength and ductility.

2. Experimental Section
2.1. Ingredient Design Using CALPHAD
In this work, thermodynamic calculations were performed using
Pandat software with the PanCo2016 database. By changing the
Al and Ti elements content, the proportion of L1 2 phase is main-
tained above 70% at 750 °C and above 50% up to 1100 °C. The
starting point of alloy optimization is Ni 3.5Co3.5Fe1 because Ni
and Co have good high-temperature stability and dissolve more
alloying elements while still maintaining γ austenite.[24,25] As
shown in Figure 1 , we add equal atomic ratios of Al and Ti ele-
ments to Ni3.5Co3.5Fe1 and notice that with the increase in Al and
Ti, the volume fraction of the L1 2 phase improves signi ﬁcantly.
The best combination of L1 2 phase stability and volume fraction
was obtained at 10 at% Al and Ti. However, the addition of a large
amount of Al will cause large lattice distortion and promote the
transformation of the lattice structure from FCC to body center
cubic (BCC), resulting in the loss of ductility at room tempera-
ture.[26,27] Therefore, we limit the Al content within a certain
range, which leads to a higher Ti/Al ratio, where the value of
the antiphase boundary energy is higher at the higher, leading
to an increase in the precipitation strength. [28] Excess Ti which
does not enter the Ni 3AlTi-type nanoprecipitated phase will pro-
mote the formation of brittle L2 1 particles, resulting in intergran-
ular fracture. To overcome this contradiction, we added a small
amount of B to the system. Obviously, the bene ﬁt of the addition
of B is multifaceted. First, B has the effect of strengthening the
grain boundary of the Ni 3Al-type ordered phase, which can effec-
tively overcome room temperature brittleness and change the
fracture mode at ambient temperature from intergranular to
transgranular fracture.[29– 31] Second, the extremely negative mix-
ing enthalpy of B and Ti offset the high mixing entropy of the
system, and the excess Ti tends to enrich the grain boundary
to form Ti – B intermetallic compounds, which increase alloy
strength considerably. According to this, we optimized
Ni35Co35Fe10Al8Ti10B2 (hereinafter referred to as ATB2).
2.2. Alloys Preparation
The high-entropy superalloy with the nominal composition of
Ni35Co35Fe10Al8Ti10B2 was designed based on thermodynamic
calculations, and was prepared by the arc-melting method in a
water-cooled copper crucible under a highly pure Ar atmosphere,
using Ni, Co, Fe, Ti, Al, and B with purities >99.9 wt%. All the
ingots were melted at least 6 times to ensure uniform composi-
tion. The resulting cast ingots were button shaped with a diame-
ter of approximately 30 mm and a height of approximately
10 mm. Then, the as-cast samples were homogenized at
1423 K for 2 h followed by water quenching to room temperature.
Cold rolling was carried out with a total thickness reduction of
75%. Subsequently, the cold-rolled sheet was partial recrystal-
lized at 1273 K for 5 min and quenched in water. Finally, these
samples were isothermally aged at 1023 K for 8 h, and water-
quenched to room temperature.
2.3. Mechanical Properties Tests
Flat dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with a gauge length of
5 mm, width of 2 mm, and thickness of 1.5 mm along the rolling
direction were used for the tests. They were fabricated using
electric discharge machining (EDM) after heat treatment, with
their longitudinal axes parallel to the rolling direction, and both
sides were ground with 1500-grit SiC paper prior to testing.
Tensile tests were calibrated at room temperature in air using
noncontact video extensometer (NCM-2D) at a nominal
engineering strain rate of 2 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1. Each test was performed
at least third times to ensure accuracy (representative results are
reported in this article). Fracture surfaces were examined using
Zeiss Supra 55 scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
2.4. Materials Characterization
Crystalline structure and microstructural morphology of samples
were identiﬁed using the X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8,
Cu K α radiation, λ = 0.15406 nm), optical microscope (OM,
Olympus), the SEM (Zeiss Supra 55), and ﬁeld emission trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM, the JEOL-JEM-2100F),
respectively. Specimens for OM and SEM observations were
prepared using standard metallographic techniques and etched
with a 20% aqua regia solution. TEM samples were prepared by
twin-jet electron polishing in a solution of 10% HClO 4 þ 90%
C2H5OH (volume fraction) at a cryogenic temperature of
243 K. Statistical analysis of the volume fraction and particle size
of L1 2 precipitates was performed using SEM morphology
images.

3.2. Phase Composition and Microstructure
The XRD pattern of ATB2 is shown in Figure 3a. The XRD pat-
tern reveals that the ATB2 is composed of FCC phase and L1 2
phase. As-cast and cold-rolled samples have a single-phase
FCC structure, and thermal processing samples exhibit FCC
and L1 2 phases. The Rietveld re ﬁnement result of the thermal
processed ATB2 is summarized in Figure S1, Supporting
Information, which could accurately detect the proportion of
FCC and L1 2 phases as 20.4% and 79.6%, respectively.
This result indicates the extremely high proportion of the
second phase, which is consistent with the simulation results
(Figure 1).
Figure 3b,e shows the corresponding OM micrographs of two
HEA samples in the as-cast and thermally processed states,
respectively. The alloy is mainly composed of dendrites of bright
FCC and dark L1 2, where the volume fraction of L1 2 is about
20%. The SEM microstructure of the two HEA samples is
shown in Figure 3c,d,f,g. Both these samples exhibit a signi ﬁcant
quantity of ﬁne precipitates. Compared with the as-cast samples,
the thermal processing samples behave more uniformly. In the
thermal processing samples, several hierarchical precipitates are
distributed in the matrix and these appear as squares under the
microscope. To describe the hierarchical precipitates more
clearly, two SEM images of the ATB2 are provided in Figure S2,
Supporting Information. It could be found that heterogeneous
L12 precipitates were successfully fabricated through a thermo-
mechanical process. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the
two precipitated phases with different scales are both L1 2
phases. The average sizes of the heterogeneous precipitates
are approximately 202.1 and 71.4 nm, respectively, and their
volume fractions are approximately 9.6% and 90.4%, respec-
tively, as measured by image analysis (Figure 3f ). This type
of heterogeneous precipitation was commonly observed in
Al- and Ti-doped HEAs.
3.3. Structures and Compositions of Nanoprecipitates
As shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, the TEM image
along with the [110] zone axis indicates that the thermal process-
ing ATB2 has micron-scale ultra ﬁne recrystallized grains,
discontinuous precipitates distributed at the grain boundaries,
and continuous precipitates dispersed within the grains.
The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping in the scan-
ning– TEM mode (STEM – EDS) reveals that the dispersion of Ni,
Co, Al, and Ti is continuously distributed in the grains, while a
large quantity of Ti and B is segregated in the intermetallic
precipitates.
The TEM BF phase of the heat-treated ATB2 microstructure is
shown in Figure 4a. The size of the recrystallized grains generally
ranges from nanocrystalline to ultra ﬁne, a large amount of pre-
cipitations can be found and a certain number of annealing twins
in these FCC grains can be clearly identi ﬁed (Figure S4,
Supporting Information). The appearance of these twins may
be due to the enrichment of Fe and Co in the FCC matrix, result-
ing in a low stacking fault (SF) energy of FCC matrix. [49]
Figure 4b shows a DF TEM image of ATB2, corresponding to
the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) inset of the [001]
zone axis in the lower right corner, indicating that the precipi-
tated phase has an ordered L1 2 structure. DF TEM images
corresponding to Figure 4b are shown in Figure 4c, revealing
a uniform distribution of high-density nanoprecipitates. More
importantly, the L1 2 nanoparticles are uniformly and coherently
precipitated in the FCC matrix, as con ﬁrmed by the high-
resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Figure 4d). The corresponding
lattice sketches are inserted in Figure 4d. In Figure 4e – h, the two
fast Fourier transforms (FFT) and two inverse fast Fourier
transforms (IFFT) were used to distinguish nanoprecipitation
and the matrix, and the difference between the extinction points
indicates the coherence relationship between disordered FCC
matrix and the ordered L1 2 precipitate.
3.4. Strengthening Mechanisms
The yield strength of ATB2 is up to 1.45 GPa, which is much
greater than for similar alloys. The main reasons are as follows:
ultrahigh-density L1 2-type nanoprecipitation with continuous
distribution; discontinuous distribution of Ti – B-type precipita-
tion pinning at grain boundaries; hierarchical distribution of
spatial precipitation morphology; the presence of submicron-
scale ultra ﬁne grains; and a certain number of nanotwins.
First and foremost, such a high-volume fraction of precipitates
is extremely rare compared to conventional superalloys and
precipitate-strengthened HEAs reported in recent years. [1,50]
Whether it is the coherent strain strengthening mechanism,
the Orowan mechanism, or the dislocation cutting mechanism,
the number of L1 2 phases plays a decisive role in precipitation-
strengthened HEAs. The effect of precipitation strengthening at
room temperature is positively correlated with the number of
second phases. For quantitative analysis, it is necessary to accu-
rately calculate the strengthening effect of the L1 2 precipitated
phase. In general, for precipitates with smaller precipitate sizes,
the shearing mechanism predominates, while for precipitates
with larger size that are incoherent with the matrix, the
Orowan mechanism predominates. Based on the precipitate
morphology characteristics of heat-treated ATB2, dislocation
cleavage will occur (Figure 6c,d).

ability (see Figure 2). The second is the formation of SFs, defor-
mation twins, and L – C locks during deformation. Figure 6a

We attribute the large number of SFs and twins after deforma-
tion and fracture to two aspects. On the one hand, the SF energy
of the FCC-structured alloy is low. On the other hand, while
designing the alloy, we added a large amount of Co to reduce
the SF energy of matrix, and the precipitation of a large number
of Ni3Al-type precipitates results in further reduction. In contrast
to alloys with higher SF energy where cross-slip and microstrip
are the main deformation modes, the dislocations in ATB2 tend
to decompose to form extended dislocations (incomplete disloca-
tions) that effectively prevent cross-slip. Furthermore, the two
disintegrated incomplete dislocations interact to form immov-
able L – C locks on the {100} slip plane. L – C locks improve the
deformation stability and work hardening ability of alloys owing
to the following: ﬁrst, L – C locks act as a strong obstacle in sup-
pressing dislocations during the deformation process. The dislo-
cations from other slip systems are pinned when they encounter
L– C locks, which aggravate the dislocation accumulation. The
stress concentration near the L – C locks can be clearly observed
in Figure S5, Supporting Information. Second, with the increase
in the deformation, more and more dislocations from the {111}
slip system are decomposed to generate incomplete dislocations
and pressure rod dislocations due to the obstruction of SFs,
which continuously improves the density and stability of L – C
locks. Finally, as the deformation proceeds, the number and
density of SFs will increase, and the spacing between SFs will
decrease. The high-density SFs network dynamically re ﬁnes sub-
micron grains, thereby reducing the mean free path of disloca-
tion motion (dynamic Hall – Petch effect).
Meanwhile, the in ﬂuence of the addition of B on the ductility
of the alloy should be taken into account. It is agreed that the
addition of B can increase the bonding force of the grain bound-
ary, effectively strengthen the grain boundary, and alleviate the
stress caused by the difference in concentration between the pre-
cipitate and the matrix, which in turn improves cracks and micro-
pores, thereby effectively improving the ductility of the alloy.[30,65]
At the same time, borides generally exist in granular form at the
grain boundaries, effectively hindering the grain boundary slip
and the connection and expansion of grain boundary micropores.
Therefore, it is obvious that ATB2 can maintain a ductility in
excess of 20% while possessing an ultrahigh strength of nearly
2 GPa, which is closely related to the doping of boron.

4. Conclusion
In conclusion, we utilize a computational thermodynamic model
to control the composition and quantity of second-phase forming
elements along with high-entropy design concepts, to create an
ultrahigh concentration L1 2 precipitation phase-strengthened
Ni35Co35Fe10Al10Ti8B2 HEA. Owing to the advantages of the
multimain element/high-entropy matrix, our novel high-entropy
superalloy has higher yield strength ( >1.45 GPa) and tensile
strength ( >1.85 GPa), lower density due to the presence of Al
and Ti, good ductility (>22%), and excellent work hardening abil-
ity provided by the dislocation network. The volume fraction of
precipitated phase in HEA can ostensibly be further improved
through composition design and heat treatment process optimi-
zation in the future. Similarly, this alloy design strategy can also
be effectively applied to other alloy systems of the same class to
achieve the required mechanical properties under complex and
extreme conditions, providing guidance for future research and
design of new high-strength and plastic alloys.
