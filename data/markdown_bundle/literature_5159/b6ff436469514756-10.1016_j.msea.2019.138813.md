Abstract 
In this study, Ti–4%Al–2%Fe–(1~4)%Mn alloys exhibiting transformation-induced 
plasticity (TRIP) were investigated using electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD). Fe, 
Al, and Mn are all low-cost elements with low melting points, making them suitable 
alloying elements for next-generation 
α + β titanium alloys with high strength and 
ductility. A pronounced solution temperature and Mn content dependence of the tensile 
behavior was observed. The solution temperature affected the β stability i.e., the kinetics 
of TRIP, because the Fe and Mn atoms (strong β stabilizers) were partitioned to the 
retained β phase during solution treatment in the α + β region. When the Mo equivalent 
content was between 8.4 and 10.4, the alloys exhibited an excellent combination of 
strength (>1.1GPa) and ductility (>25%). On the other hand, solution treatment in the β 
region bestowed the same alloys with brittleness, even though the Mo equivalence was 
similar. During deformation, the alloys exhibited a stress-induced martensitic 
transformation followed by martensite coalescence. The transformation could increase 
the strain hardenability, but the coalescence could not. Experiments revealed that the 
coalesced martensite grains were propositional in size to the prior β grains. Solution

1. Introduction 
The strengthening of Ti alloys is essential for com petitiveness in various industries like 
aerospace, marine, petrochemical, automotive, and s urgical implants [1-8]. For the new 
generation of structural alloys, high strength and high ductility are essential. One promising 
aspect is based on the new generation of steels tha t exhibit twinning-induced plasticity 
(TWIP) or transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP), as these steels possess an excellent 
combination of strength and ductility [9-11]. TRIP steels present a high strain hardening rate 
owing to the martensitic transformation from austenite to martensite. 
TRIP behavior can also occur in Ti alloys composed of metastable β phase; the application 
of a certain stress or strain acts as a driving for ce for the martensitic transformation from 
metastable β to αʹ- or α″ -martensite, which have hexagonal and orthorhombic structures, 
respectively [12]. The martensitic phase that forms is dependent on the alloying content, with 
αʹ-martensite forming at relatively low alloying conc entrations, and α″-martensite forming at 
very high alloying contents. When the alloying cont ent is very dilute, an acicular-type α′ -
martensite microstructure is formed. However, the microstructure changes to plate type as the 
alloying content increases. Some plate-type α′ -martensite has { 1011}c α′ -type internal twins 
that initiate from β {110} β [13]. 
There are two types of deformation-induced αʹ-martensite. Stress-induced αʹ-martensite 
(αʹsim ) nucleates upon the application of stress on the s ame nucleation sites as thermally 
induced martensite, whereas strain-induced αʹ-martensite nucleates at additional nucleation 
sites that form upon plastic deformation after yiel ding—such as transformed phases, twin 
boundaries, staking-fault bundles, and planar slip bands [14, 15]. When deformation-induced 
αʹ-martensitic transformations occur, the strain hardening rate rapidly increases and the stress 
concentration is delayed owing to the growth of a l arge number of dislocations around the αʹ-
martensite laths [16]. At the same time, various ty pes of twin systems are activated. Thus, 
dynamic Hall–Petch behavior occurs, resulting in a good balance of strength and ductility 
owing to the combined effect of TRIP/TWIP [17]. Acc ording to Zhang et al. [6] and 
Marteleur et al. [7], Ti–12Mo (wt%) alloys exhibit both high strength and ductility owing to a 
combination of {112}<111>/{332}<113> twinning and the β → αʺ martensitic transformation. 
Brozek et al. [8] reported the TRIP/TWIP behavior o f Ti–8.5Cr–1.5(Sn or Al) ternary alloys, 
which exhibited 3–4 times greater ductility than Ti –6Al–4V , and a 50% higher yield strength 
than Fe–22Mn–0.6C (TWIP steel). Additionally, Sadeg hpour et al. [18] designed a multi-

component alloy system of Ti–3Al–5Mo–7V–3Cr with TR IP/TWIP behavior that achieved a 
yield strength of 750 MPa, strain hardening rate of 1800 MPa, and uniform elongation of

Despite the success of manufacturing novel alloys w ith a good balance of strength and 
ductility, alloying with heavy elements degrades th e specific strength. Furthermore, since the 
melting point is high, large amounts of energy are required for the melting process, which 
may considerably lower the economic efficiency of t heir production. Based on these 
problems, a Ti–4Al–4Fe–0.25Si alloy was developed t o demonstrate improved mechanical 
properties as well as decreased manufacturing cost compared to Ti–6Al–4V alloys [19, 20]. 
When this alloy is heat treated in the α + β region (below 950 °C), the β phase stability 
increases due to the partitioning effect between the α and β phases of Fe (a β-stabilizer). Thus, 
metastable β phase is retained via the suppression of athermal αʹ(αʹath )-martensite 
transformation during water quenching [20]. In this condition, TRIP behavior occurs owing to 
the formation of stress- or strain-induced αʹ-martensite during deformation. From this 
research, it is worth noting that it is possible to design a TRIP-assisted low-cost lean Ti alloy 
by alloying low-cost substitutional elements such a s Al and Fe into the Ti base. Fe acts as a 
strong β-stabilizing element and it is cost effective, ther efore making it suitable for a 
constituent element of a low-cost TRIP Ti alloy. Ho wever, excessive Fe leads to an issue 
known as β-flecking, in which local segregation occurs inside the ingot during melting and 
casting due to the low partitioning coefficient of Fe in Ti [21]. 
To overcome this problem, Ti–4Al–2Fe (wt%) alloys w ere designed, with the Fe content 
limited to 2 wt%. As a result of this limitation, M n, another β stabilizing element, was added 
to increase β stability. Mn is a suitable alloying element for r eplacing Fe, as it is inexpensive 
and has a low melting point (1247 °C), making it su itable for low-cost alloy design. In 
addition, Mn and Fe have similar densities (Fe: 7.8 7 g/cm 3, Mn: 7.47 g/cm 3) and exhibit 
similar solution strengthening efficiencies (Fe: 46 MPa/wt%, Mn: 34 MPa/wt% [22]), 
suggesting that itwill be beneficial for the specific strength of the alloy. 
This study investigated TRIP-assisted Ti–Al–Fe–Mn a lloys made with low-melting-point 
and low-cost elements (Al, Fe, and Mn). Two final s olution temperatures were assessed 
(880 °C of double α + β phase region and 1050 °C of single β phase region) in order to study 
the effect of solution temperature on TRIP behavior . In addition, the mechanical properties 
and microstructural changes with Mn content were in vestigated to identify the deformation 
behaviors of this alloy system.

2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Materials manufacturing 
Ti (grade 1), Al, Fe, and Mn with a purity of 99.95 % were used for the manufacturing of 
the investigated alloys, and TiO 
2 with a purity of 99.9% was used to control the oxy gen 
content. Ti chips were supplied by Dosalloy (Incheon, Korea) and Al, Fe, Mn, and TiO 2 were 
supplied by ITASCO (Seoul, Korea). The alloying com ponents were weighed according to 
the designed composition and melted using vacuum ar c remelting (V AR) equipment. The 
repetitive melting (at least 4 times) was implement ed for a 250 g ingot to ensure uniform 
mixing of the alloying elements. The chemical compo sition of the resultant alloys was 
investigated by means of inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and 
nitrogen/oxygen analysis, and presented in the Tabl e 1. Thermomechanically controlled 
processing (TMCP) was utilized to produce fine equi axed microstructures from the melted 
specimens. A Deltaglaze coating supplied by Henkel (Dusseldorf, Germany) was sprayed 
onto the alloys to prevent oxidation during TMCP an d subsequent processing. The ingot 
breaks down during TMCP, which means that a hot forging process in the β phase region will 
break the coarse β grains of the as-melted specimen. Therefore, hot f orging was repeated 
eight times at 1075 °C, followed by hot caliber rol ling performed at 880 °C up to a true strain 
of approximately 1.3. After rolling, a final soluti on treatment was carried out at either 880 or 
1050 °C, followed by water quenching to form a metastable β phase.

Because the temperature of 880 °C is the two-phase region ( α + β) of the Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn

partitioning of the alloying elements. Figures 2a–2 d show the solution temperature and Mn

predicted compositions (solid lines) were attained through thermodynamic simulation using 
J-Matpro-v8, and the experimental compositions (sym bols) were measured by EDS. The Fe 
partitioning in the β phase is much stronger than the Mn partitioning. T he partitioning 
behavior measured by EDS is in good agreement with the thermodynamic simulation. The 
solution temperatures and Mn content ( x = 1–4 wt.%)-dependence of the [Mo] eq is calculated 
based on Eq. (1) and presented in Figure 2e. Due to the element partitioning, even with the 
same alloy composition, the alloy solution-treated at the two-phase region (880 °C) had a 
higher [Mo] eq than that solution-treated at the single phase region (1050 °C). In particular, the 
Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn alloy was able to form metastable β phase through the solution treatment at 
880 °C, facilitating the deformation-induced marten sitic transformation. On the other hand, 
as the Mn content increased, the fraction of α phase decreased, and the alloying elements 
were uniformly distributed across the coarse β phase. Therefore, a similar [Mo] eq was 
obtained for high Mn alloys (Ti–4Al–2Fe–(3–4)Mn) af ter solution treatment at both 880 and 
1050 °C. These results show that the stability of t he β phase can be controlled not only 
through the alloying components, but also by adjust ing the conditions of the final solution 
treatment.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1 Effect of solution temperature on microstructural and tensile properties 
Figure 3 shows the solution temperature dependence of the EBSD phase and inverse pole 
figure (IPF) maps of the alloys prior to deformatio n. The island-shaped primary 
α (αp) phase 
is indicated in black and αʹ-martensite phase is in gray; only the β phase in the remaining 
region is colored in the IPF map. When the solution temperature was 880 °C, the 
microstructure consisted of α and β phases; no αʹath -martensite was observed. The fraction of 
β phase in the Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn alloy was approximatel y 60%, while in the Ti–4Al–2Fe–
4Mn alloy (having the highest Mn content), the β phase fraction increased to approximately 
98%. The grain size of the β phase increased with increasing Mn content owing t o a decrease 
of the pinning effect caused by α. For Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn alloy solution-treated at 1050 °C, 
αʹath -martensite was formed upon water quenching owing t o the low [Mo] eq (= 6.3) of the β 
phase (no alloying elements partitioning). The Ti–4 Al–2Fe–(2–4)Mn alloys treated at 
1050 °C (Figures 3f–3h) exhibited a fully β phase microstructure with coarse grains. 
Figure 4a shows a typical stress–strain curve of a conventional TRIP Ti alloy that exhibits 
stress plateau or double yielding behavior [25, 26, 29, 31]. The tensile behavior of TRIP-
assisted alloys is normally classified into 4 stage s. Stage 1 is the initial elastic region. The 
parent phase undergoes an initial elastic deformati on. In stage 2, stress plateau behavior is 
observed caused by the αʹsim martensitic transformation. In this stage, the critical stress for the 
plateau behavior is related to the stability of the β phase. From stage 2, the strain hardening 
rate increases rapidly. This is synergic effect of the intersection between the αʹsim -martensite 
laths and high density of dislocation motion caused by the formation of αʹsim -martensite [25, 
30]. Stage 3 corresponds to the elastic behavior of the transformed αʹ-martensite [32]. The 
strain hardening rate then decreases in stage 4 via plastic deformation of the constituent 
phases [33]. 
Figures 4b and 4c show the tensile behavior of the investigated alloys after solution 
treatment at 880 and 1050 °C, respectively. The occ urrence of TRIP behavior corresponded 
well to [Mo] eq values between 8 and 12. Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn annealed a t 880 °C ([Mo] eq = 8.6) 
showed TRIP behavior, whereas the same alloy anneal ed at 1050 °C ([Mo] eq = 6.5) showed

3.2 Solution temperature dependence of microstructural behavior during deformation 
Figures 5a–h show how the crystal orientation of Ti–4Al–2Fe–2Mn alloy differed according 
to the solution temperature after tensile deformati on. Multi-variants of αʹsim -martensite were 
observed in Ti–4Al–2Fe–2Mn alloy strained to 5% reg ardless of the solution temperature 
(Figures 5a and 5c). As the tensile strain increase d, the αʹsim -martensite variants started to 
coalesce with neighboring variants; by fracture (Fi gures 5b and 5d), the microstructure 
contained large αʹsim -martensite grains with the same crystallographic o rientation. Lee et al. 
[26, 31] investigated the deformation mechanism of metastable Ti alloy (Ti–4Al–4Fe–025Si) 
containing αʹsim -martensite, and reported that several variants of αʹsim -martensite were 
reoriented in the plastic region during deformation . In addition, the several αʹsim -martensite 
variants were aligned to one direction by this reor ientation, which affected the mechanical 
properties. Herein, traces of the reoriented αʹsim -martensite variants were clearly observed in 
the deformed microstructures (Figures 5b and 5d), i ndicating that the same phenomenon

occurs in Ti–4Al–2Fe– xMn alloys. Since the grain boundaries ( αʹ/αʹ and αʹ/β) could be 
effective barriers to dislocation motion, the small size of the αʹsim -martensite laths that 
transform in the beginning of the deformation could result in an increase of strain hardening. 
However, as the deformation continues, the effect g radually decreases because the large 
coalesced martensite grains reduce the area of thes e grain boundaries. A highly aligned 
orientation of coalesced martensite was observed af ter deformation of the sample solution-
treated at 1050 °C, as shown in Figures 5d and 5h. This explains the relatively low 
mechanical properties of this specimen. On the othe r hand, the specimen that was solution-
treated at 880 °C comprised relatively fine-grained β. Since the size of the coalesced 
martensite grains was proportional to the prior β grain size in the specimen solution-treated at 
880 °C (Figure 5b), the coalescence of martensite w as limited. In addition, the orientation of 
the aligned martensite was relatively distributed i n the 880 °C sample (Figure 5f) compared 
to that in the 1050 °C sample (Figure 5h). This fin e coalesced martensite could give rise to 
relatively high strain hardenability and delay uniform strain. 
For Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn alloy, the solution treatment in the β region resulted in full αʹath phase 
formation during water quenching owing to the low β stability. This resulted in an alloy with 
high strength and low ductility. In contrast, when the same alloys were subjected to solution-
treatment in the α + β region, metastable β and αp phases were observed, therefore increasing 
the β stability owing to partitioning of the alloying el ements between phases. The grain size 
of the metastable β phase was constrained owing to the pinning effect of the αp phase. During 
deformation, TRIP behavior (stress-induced martensi tic transformation) was observed, with 
the formation of several variants of αʹsim -martensite resulting in an excellent balance of 
strength and ductility. As the Mn content was incre ased (Ti–4Al–2Fe–(2–3)Mn), the 
metastable β phase showing the TRIP behavior was obtained regar dless of the solution 
temperatures, but the various solution treatment co nditions produced the significantly 
different mechanical properties.

4. Conclusions 
In this study, a new Ti–Al–Fe–Mn alloy system exhib iting TRIP behavior was developed 
using low-melting-point and low-cost elements. The microstructural behavior and 
deformation mechanism of this alloy system were ana lyzed. The main findings are 
summarized as follows. 
1. Thermodynamic simulations were used to design TRIP Ti alloys by predicting the 
β phase stability of two-phase Ti alloys. Solution t reatment in the α + β region 
could control the β stability and kinetics of the martensitic transfor mation owing 
to the element partitioning effect. The β stabilizers (Fe and Mn) were partitioned 
to the retained β phase, resulting in an increase of β stability during solution 
treatment. 
2. Ti–4Al–2Fe–1Mn solution-treated in the β region (1050 °C) had the lowest β 
stability ([Mo] eq = 6.3), resulting in an athermal martensitic trans formation. This 
alloy exhibited extremely high strength (1266 MPa) but brittleness (2.2%). 
3. When the [Mo] eq of the retained β phase in the Ti–4Al–2Fe–xMn alloys that were 
solution-treated in the α + β region (880 °C) was between 8.5 and 10.4, stress-
induced martensitic transformations occurred upon d eformation, resulting in 
extra strain hardenability. These alloys showed an excellent combination of 
strength (>1.1 GPa) and ductility (>25%). 
4. Experimental results revealed that the multi-varian ts of αʹsim-martensite reorient 
and align to one specific orientation (coalescence) as the amount of deformation 
increases. This coalescence reduces the ability to accommodate dislocations due

11 
to the reduction of grain boundary area. Since the size of the coalesced martensite 
grains is proportional to the size of the prior β grains, the alloys solution-treated 
in the β region showed relatively low strength and ductilit y, even though the 
[Mo] eq was β between 8.5 and 10.4. 
5. Ti–4Al–2Fe–4Mn has the highest β stability ([Mo] eq > 11.6). The stress-induced 
martensitic transformation was inhibited in this al loy regardless of the solution 
temperature, resulting in low strain hardening.
