ABSTRACT 
Developing additively manufactured alloys with high cryogenic performance can facilitate the development of 
geometrically complex components for cryogenic applications. In this study, we have implemented ultrasonic 
surface rolling process (USRP) on an additively manufactured CoCrNi-based medium entropy alloy to introduce a 
gradient nanostructure therein. The resulting alloy exhibits a substantial strength-ductility balance at 88 K, a 
relatively high ductility (24.6 %), 2.07 times higher than that at 293 K, as well as a yield strength as high as 1274 
MPa. The exceptional yield strength is attributed to the high-density dislocations induced by additive 
manufacturing and ultrasonic surface rolling process, while the origin of impressive ductility promotion lies in 
two aspects, i.e., enhanced hetero-deformation induced hardening effect and activated twinning/microband 
behaviors at matrix region at 88 K. The former mainly comes from suppressed dynamic recovery in gradient 
layer. The latter is resulted from the lowered stacking faulting energy and high flow stress. 
High/medium entropy alloys (HEAs/MEAs) have drawn extensive 
attentions in recent years [1] . Specially, MEAs with face-centered cubic 
(FCC) structure have displayed impressive facture toughness and 
ductility at cryogenic temperature [2] . However, the engineering 
application of FCC MEA is constrained by its low strength, necessitating 
further efforts to achieve a more optimal balance between strength and 
ductility at cryogenic temperature. Laser-aided powder bed fusion 
(LPBF), a leading additive manufacturing (AM) technology, provides a 
near-net-shaped way for geometrically complex parts [3] . More impor -
tantly, nanoscale cellular structures introduced by LPBF significantly 
improve materials ’ strength [4] . 
Further, another substructure, known as the gradient nanostructure, 
is considered to be an efficacious approach in mitigating the strength- 
ductility trade-off. For example, Pan et al. [5] fabricated a gradient 
cell nanostructure on the surface of a HEA, leading to an exceptional 
strength and ductility synergy, which mainly lies in the progressive 
formation of stacking faults (SFs) and deformation twins (DTs) in 
gradient nanostructure. Recently, AM combining surface modification 
techniques (SMTs) was reported to improve the strength-ductility bal -
ance [6] and fatigue resistance [7] of materials. Specifically, these im -
provements are derived from the beneficial effect of SMTs on the AMed 
materials, which mainly lies in two aspects, i.e., introducing gradient 
nanostructure and closing the defects adjacent to the surface. Therefore, 
the combination of AM with SMTs can present a pivotal approach for 
high-performance FCC MEA. However, AM-fabricated alloys with 
gradient nanostructures are rarely studied regarding their cryogenic 
properties. The aforementioned discussions shed light on the potential of 
exploring the cryogenic behavior of AM-fabricated FCC MEAs endowed 
with gradient nanostructures. 
Thus, a CoCrNi-based MEA with gradient nanostructure was fabri -
cated by LPBF and subsequent ultrasonic surface rolling process (USRP). 
USRP can bring high-quality surface and deep gradient microstructure 
[8] . Good strength-ductility balance at cryogenic temperature was 
achieved. Specifically, the ductility of this alloy demonstrates substan -
tial enhancement as temperature decreases, primarily resulting from the 
suppressed dynamic dislocation recovery, intensified 
hetero-deformation induced (HDI) hardening, and activated twin -
ning/microband behavior. Elaborated characterization techniques were 
conducted to support this conclusion.

Fig. 1 presents the microstructure of the sample subjected to USRP 
process along depth direction via EBSD and TEM techniques. The 
maximum depth from the surface is set as 550 μ m, exactly a half of the 
thickness of the USRP-treated sample. Fig. 1 a displays the inverse pole 
figure (IPF) map of the USRP-treated sample, and the composition dis -
tribution along depth is homogeneous (Supplementary Fig. S2). The 
grains with the distance above 300 μ m from the surface exhibit a 
columnar morphology with a large aspect ratio, attributed to the strong 
thermal gradient during AM process [10] . While in the gradient region, 
the grains are distorted, presenting a inclined morphology with a minor 
average aspect ratio (2.17 versus 3.03 for central grains), and the vari -

Fig. 1 b shows the grain boundary map with three different grain

boundary (HAGB) and twin boundary (TB), suggesting LAGB is domi -
nant in the gradient region due to the medium stacking fault energy 
(SFE) of this material. Fig. 1 c displays the kernel average misorientation 
(KAM) map, where a higher value is observed in the gradient region, and 
specific KAM value along depth is presented in Supplementary Fig. S3b. 
The corresponding microhardness along depth direction is plotted in 
Fig. 1 d, implying a gradient depth of 400 μ m. Notably, hardness is not

the matrix region, i.e., 550 μ m from the surface, only cellular structures

(range 0 – 400 μ m) and matrix region (range 400 – 550 μ m). The average 
microhardness of different conditions is presented in Fig. 4 c. When 
deformed at 293 K, the microhardness of the gradient region is nearly

Based on above results, the origin of a good strength-ductility bal -
ance in the USRP-treated sample at 88 K can be elucidated as follows. 
The reason for high YS is mainly attributed to the grain refinement and 
high-density dislocations in gradient layer, as well as the intrinsic high- 
density dislocations, which provide 182 MPa and 416 MPa for the whole 
YS (Supplementary Text 2). Moreover, the impressive improvement of 
ductility at 88 K can be primarily attributed to two key factors, and the 
schematic is presented in Fig. 5 . First, stronger HDI effect is enabled 
upon cryogenic deformation. The gradient region and matrix unaffected 
by USRP process can be regard as hard and soft domains, respectively. 
During deformation, geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) are 
generated at boundaries between two domains to reconcile the me -
chanical incompatibility induced by the strain partitioning [ 6 , 15 ], 
which can generate substantial HDI stress to provide extra strain hard -
ening. In our USRP-treated sample, the matrix region undergoes plastic 
deformation firstly due to its relatively low strength, evoking GNDs at 
interfaces to provide additional strain hardening. In the case of ambient 
deformation, the gradient layer with high-density pre-existing disloca -
tions is hard to produce more dislocations, owing to the accelerated 
dynamic recovery [ 5 , 16 ], which is reflected in the nearly unchanged 
microhardness in Fig. 4 c. Meanwhile, the matrix region starts strain 
hardening, shrinking the gap between soft and hard domains in terms of 
strength such that the efficiency of HDI stress is reduced until dislocation 
saturation and subsequent necking. However, upon cryogenic defor -
mation, the dynamic recovery in the gradient layer is effectively sup -
pressed [ 16 , 17 ], thus, the gradient region regains the strain hardening 
capacity, as proved by the substantial increment of microhardness in 
Fig. 4 c. With the increasing strain, the discrepancy of strength between 
hard gradient layer and soft matrix still keeps in a relatively high level, 
enough to activate strain partition and evoke GNDs at interfaces to offer 
extra strain hardening. Second, more TBs are found in the gradient layer 
at 10 % under 88 K, and denser dislocations accompanied with twins 
featuring a smaller average spacing (6.2 nm versus 17.9 nm for unde -
formed state) and higher volume fraction (57.1% versus 46.4%), 
compared with the undeformed state, are observed in Supplementary 
Figs. S4 and S7. Besides, the density of DTs is substantially increased 
within the gradient layer when fractured at 88 K (Supplementary 
Fig. S8). These scenarios indicate more dislocations, SFs, and DTs are 
generated in the gradient region during cryogenic deformation, helpful 
for the strain hardening. Finally, it is observed that SFs, DTs and MBs 
emerge in the matrix region deformed at 88 K, which is conducive for 
strain hardening capacity by means of reducing dislocation mean free 
path and the promotion of dislocation interlocking [18 – 20] . Thus, the 
appearance of multiple deformation substructures in the matrix provides 
additional strain hardening at high strain. In a word, the increase in the 
strain hardening, resultant from stronger HDI effect and extra defor -
mation substructures, delays the onset of necking instability and ac -
counts for the impressive strength-ductility balance at 88 K. The 
utilization of AM in conjunction with USRP presents a promising 
pathway towards manufacturing high-performance thin-walled parts for 
cryogenic applications.
