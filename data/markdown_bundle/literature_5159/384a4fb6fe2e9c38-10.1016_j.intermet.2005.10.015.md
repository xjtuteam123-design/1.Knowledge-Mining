Abstract
The addition of Nb to Fe3Al-base alloys has been shown to improve strength for temperatures below about 700 /C14 C, but thereafter the strength
gains are minimal. Changes of order and precipitation occur on annealing of quenched Fe eAleNb alloys at temperatures in the range of about
600e800 /C14 C, and these are responsible for the changes of mechanical properties. Factors responsible for the poor precipitate stability and loss
of strength are examined, which include the role of interface structure on destabilizing precipitates of the intermediate phase which forms,
promoting its transition to the stable Laves phase, as well as allowing rapid coarsening of Laves precipitates.

1. Introduction
There is considerable interest in the development of iron
aluminides for use at temperatures above 600 /C14 C. For many
iron aluminide base alloys the ﬂow strength falls rapidly at
such temperatures [1e7] and the creep stress drops to low
levels [8e13]. Even though some of these alloys allow
strength retention to higher temperatures, this is often at the
expense of ductility loss or reduced resistance to oxidation
e.g. [3e7]. As part of a wider study examining alloying addi-
tions for improving high-temperature strength, Nb has recently
been examined as a solution and precipitate hardener [14].I t
has been seen that high strength can be retained from room
temperature to about 500
/C14 C, but at higher temperatures
the strength falls rapidly to low values. The microstructural
changes occurring during such high-temperature testing, as
well as during annealing of quenched material [15], are exam-
ined here and compared with changes occurring in iron alloys
strengthened by similar intermetallic phases. Reasons for
structural instabilities are examined, and the conditions neces-
sary for a more stable strengthening precipitate/dispersion are
discussed.
2. Experimental
Results are presented here on a variety of Fe eAleNb al-
loys prepared by induction melting and drop casting into cyl-
inders of 20 e30 mm in diameter. Al contents between 15%
and 30% were examined (atomic percent is given throughout).
A Nb content of 5% was initially used (for the alloy with 20%
Al), but it was seen that the solid state solubility of Nb in these
alloys was much less, even at very high temperatures, and
hence subsequent alloys were prepared with the Nb content re-
duced to 2%. Materials were given an initial homogenising
and solutionising anneal at 1150 e1300
/C14 C for 1 h and water
quenched. Subsequent ageing treatments were carried out, in
air, for 1 h period at temperatures between 200 and 900
/C14 C,
and treatments terminated by air cool. Mechanical testing
was carried out by Vickers microhardness testing (100 g

3. Results
3.1. Mechanical testing of Fe e25Ale2Nb at
high temperatures
This material was tested both in the solutionised state (after
an anneal of 1 h at 1300
/C14 C, followed by a water quench) and
in the precipitated state, after a further anneal of 1 h at
900
/C14 C. These treatments were able to dissolve most, but
not all, of the Nb, and to precipitate out this solute as C14
Laves particles of size 0.2e0.5 mm [14]. The variation of yield
stress with test temperature is seen in Fig. 1, where this mate-
rial is also compared with a simple binary Fe e30Al alloy
[14,16], a solution hardened Fe e28Al alloy containing small
amounts of Cr and Si [14], and a mechanically alloyed
MA40 Fe e40Al alloy [14,17]. Fig. 1 shows that the present
Fee25Ale2Nb alloy has good strength from room temper-
ature to about 500 /C14 C, due to solution and precipitate hard-
ening, but that the ﬂow stress falls rapidly thereafter to very
low values for test temperatures of 700 e800 /C14 C and above.
During the range of loss of high-temperature strength, it is
clear that the initially solutionised material (labelled 25-2(I))
is stronger than the initially precipitated material (labelled
25-2(II)), which has been shown to be due to the ﬁne precip-
itates present [14]. Of importance for the present report,
strength is lost at the high temperatures (above 600
/C14 C) due
to the rapid formation and coarsening of precipitates during
the high-temperature testing [15].

quenched material

FeeAleNb alloys on annealing for 1 h at various temperatures

hardness increase from the quenched or slightly annealed state

alloys with higher Al content. The hardness of the quenched

range, similar to the trend of ﬂow stress as Al content changes.

4. Discussion
Nb was chosen as a potentially strong hardener for high
temperatures in view of its relatively low solubility at temper-
atures of interest (of the order of 0.2e1% at 700e900 /C14 C) and
its relatively low diffusivity [18]. Thus it was expected that
stable Nb-based particles might form to give good strength.
In order to analyse why the present Nb-based precipitates
give relatively poor hardening it is of interest to compare the
behaviour shown here with that of iron alloys (in comparison
with FeeAl alloys) strengthened by similar Laves phase pre-
cipitates [19e24]. Alloying Fe with elements such as Nb or
Ti allows age hardening to be achieved as Laves particles pre-
cipitate from solution. Further alloying additions, as for exam-
ple in Fe eTieSi alloys, allow an intermediate phase to form,
coherently and in much higher particle number density, before
eventually the stable Laves particles form.
Table 1 shows a comparison of the amount of age harden-
ing achieved on annealing such quenched iron alloys, and the
present quenched Fe eAleNb alloys, and also compares the
temperature where the age hardening maximum is observed.
The amount of hardening is related to the number of precipi-
tates forming, and the age hardening maximum is related to
the ease of destabilization of any coherent phase or to coarsen-
ing of the hardening particles. It may be seen that the iron al-
loys which precipitate directly Laves particles show relatively
little hardening (as compared with similar iron alloys that pre-
cipitate an initial transition phase), but have better thermal sta-
bility (age hardening peak at higher temperature), while those
that form ﬁrst particles of an intermediate phase show greater
hardening, but are destabilized more easily and age softening
sets in more quick.
For the earlier analysis showing relatively little hardening
due to particles in the Fe eAleNb alloys, and from this com-
parison with precipitation hardening steels, we can deﬁne
those factors unfavourable to the achievement of good
high-temperature strength in Fe eAleNb alloys. As summar-
ised in Table 2 , the Fe eAleNb alloys suffer from a rapid
transition of the metastable L2

Laves phase, and the Laves particles coarsen extremely rap-
idly (we could also argue that age hardening is not of greater

the needle morphology of the L2 1 phase particles, we can ar-

the high amount of elastic strain energy accumulated at the
interface (this may be a reason for the insufﬁcient nucleation

shown to follow a size cubed etime relationship, as expected
for growth by coarsening, with an activation energy that cor-
responds well to temperature dependencies of Nb diffusion
and solution. When comparing the experimental coarsening
kinetics (i.e. size cubed divided by annealing time) with
estimates based on classical coarsening theory (of Lifshitz-
Slyozov-Wagner), that is according to estimates of Nb solu-
bility and diffusivity in the Fe eAl lattice, and of the energy
of the incoherent particle ematrix interface, it is seen that the
experimental coarsening kinetics are faster than the theoreti-
cal estimates by 2 e3 orders of magnitude. While the reason
for this is not clear, it may be due to the morphology of the
incoherent matrix eLaves particle interface and the lobed or
ledge-edge morphology adopted by the particles. Thus,
coarsening driving forces are not directly related to the
average particle size, but instead to the radii at these corners,
and give much more rapid coarsening than hoped.
Finally, following from this discussion, a better alloy for
high-temperature strengthening should show a combination
of more frequent nucleation of ﬁne intermediate phase parti-
cles, better resistance to destabilization to the stable phase,
and better coarsening resistance of the stable phase particles.
As is clear from the discussion above, there are several fac-
tors that play an important role, including interface structure
and energy of both the intermediate phase particles and the
stable phase particles, and the solubility and diffusivity of
the relevant solute.
5. Conclusions
Nb in solution in Fe eAl alloys precipitates at temperatures
between 600 and 800
/C14 C in the form of metastable L2 1 nee-
dles, followed by the stable C14 Laves phase as globular par-
ticles. Changes of order at temperatures below the onset of
precipitation are responsible for the age hardening observed
at room temperature on quenched and subsequently annealed
materials. Strength at high temperatures falls at temperatures
above 600
/C14 C as precipitation and particle coarsening occur.
The rapid transformation of the intermediate, metastable
L21 needles to the stable C14 Laves globular particles, as well
as the rapid coarsening of these C14 particles, is believed to be
due, in large part, to the large misﬁt and elastic strain at the
L2
1ematrix interface and the incoherent, high energy, and
jagged C14 ematrix interface. Further alloy development for
improved high-temperature strength needs to consider the
selection of phases where these parameters do not have such
a strong impact on behaviour.
In conclusion, despite the presence of Nb in solution and as
precipitates, the improvement in the temperature to which high
strength can be retained, and the contribution to strength at
high temperatures (which was expected because of the rela-
tively low solubility and diffusivity of Nb in Fe(Al)) is disap-
pointingly small.
