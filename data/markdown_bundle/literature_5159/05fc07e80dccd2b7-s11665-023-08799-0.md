ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
A Cobalt-Free High-Entropy Alloy with Excellent
Mechanical Properties at Ambient and Cryogenic
Temperatures
Wei Liu, Dan Liu, Xuejiao Wang, Yucheng Wu, and Junwei Qiao
Submitted: 10 February 2023 / Revised: 7 September 2023 / Accepted: 17 September 2023 / Published online: 13 October 2023
A high-entropy alloy with an atomic composition of Fe 40Mn15Cr20Ni22Ti3 with outstanding strength–
ductility synergy induced by titanium additions was successfully developed. At room temperature, its
ultimate tensile strength can reach 1089 MPa and still maintain 21% elongation. Moreover, at low tem-
perature of 77 K, its ultimate tensile strength and elongation have been increased to 1285 MPa and 35%,
respectively. After careful analysis, it is found that the cause for the large increase in elongation is the
decrease of the stacking fault energy at low temperature, which leads to the deformation mechanism of the
alloy to change from dislocation wave slip to plane slip. In addition, the precipitation strengthening and
grain boundary strengthening induced by chrome-rich r precipitates are the main strengthening contri-
bution.
Keywords cryogenic temperatures, deformation mechanisms,
fractography, green industry, high-entropy alloys,
mechanical testing, stacking fault energy
1. Introduction
Materials are the foundation for human survival and
development, together with information and energy, and they
are known as the three pillars of contemporary civilization. The
progress of civilization and society cannot be separated from
the discovery and development of new materials. In the recent
20 years, one of biggest discoveries in the direction of metallic
materials is high-entropy alloy (HEA). Among these developed
HEAs with different compositions and structures, the CoCr-
FeMnNi HEA has attracted much attention because of its
outstanding ductility and fracture toughness properties at
ambient and cryogenic temperatures (Ref 1-3). Therefore,
many researchers expect this type of HEAs to serve as a
specialty material in extreme environments. But there are still
two major problems that need to be solved as engineering
application materials: 1. CoCrFeMnNi HEA is a single-phase
face-centered cubic (FCC) structure with low yield strength at
room temperature (Ref 4); 2. with the rapid development of
global new energy vehicles, the importance of cobalt is
increasing, and it is becoming more and more expensive. The
CoCrFeMnNi HEA, which need to consume a large amount of
cobalt, lacks sufﬁcient commercial value.
For the ﬁrst problem, experts have given many excellent
solutions, including solid solution strengthening, grain bound-
ary strengthening, second phase strengthening, and deformation
strengthening and so on (Ref 5-9). Nevertheless, due to the high
signiﬁcance of cobalt in the CoCrFeMnNi HEA system (Ref
10-12), the solutions that can solve the two major problems at
the same time are still very rare. The design of Co-free HEAs
with a good combination of strength and ductility is highly
desirable yet still very challenging.
Zhao et al. developed a cobalt-free HEA system in Fe-Ni-
Cr-Mn-Al-Ti through nanoprecipitation engineering (Ref 13). It
is reported that a series of Co-free non-equiatomic (Fe-

elaborate dual-phase structure of ( c + c’) successfully acquired
in the interior of the grains. The report has aroused signiﬁcant
attentions. Some scholars have obtained a large number of

2. Experimental
Ingots with an atomic nominal composition of
Fe40Mn15Cr20Ni22Ti3 (The actual components were obtained
by SEM-EDS analysis, Fe 42.02Mn14.64Cr19.44Ni20.67Ti3.23) were
prepared by arc melting. Moreover, we added extra 5 wt.% Mn
as compensation like other HEAs containing manganese to
countervail the loss of Mn during melting. Each material was
guaranteed to be at least 99.9 wt.% pure. Chemical uniformity
of qualiﬁed HEA ingot was also required, so each ingot was
turned over and smelted six times. The as-cast HEA obtained
by vacuum arc melting was ﬁrst homogenized at 1100 /C176 C for
5 h to reduce segregation and improve the microstructure
homogeneity of the alloy. The alloy was routinely cold -rolled
to a thickness reduction of 70%, recrystallized annealed at
1000 /C176 C for 5 min (marked as alloy A). After this treatment,
the fully recrystallized samples were annealed at 700 /C176 C for 3 h
(marked as alloy B).
The lattice parameters and phase structure were carefully
characterized by x-ray diffraction (XRD, Cu Ka radiation).
After grinding and polishing the samples, we chemically
corroded the samples with highly corrosive aqua regia for
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation. For TEM
observations, ﬁrstly, a sheet of 0.8 mm thick was mechanically
grounded into a 30-micron thick sheet with 2000 mesh
sandpaper; secondly, the sample is stamped into a 3 mm
diameter wafer; thirdly, the electrolysis double spray was used

3. Result
3.1 Effects of Aging on the Microstructure of HEAs
Figure 1 displays the XRD patterns of HEAs in two different
states. It is clear that alloy A, which undergoes only high
temperature heat treatment (homogenization annealing at
1100 /C176 C and recrystallization annealing at 1000 /C176 C), has a
single-phase FCC structure. After aging annealing at 700 /C176 C
for 3 h, a small amount of heterogeneous phase precipitates in
alloy B, which can be preliminarily speculated to be chromium-
rich sigma precipitates by referring to previous studies, such
precipitates often occur in CoCrFeMnNi HEAs (Ref 22-25). In
addition, according to some recent reports, a small amount of
precipitation-induced element addition can only promote the
formation of sigma phase, but not the formation of the
corresponding element enrichment phase (Ref 4, 26-28).
Figure 2 exhibits the SEM and EDS analysis results of alloy
A, from which it can be seen that alloy A has been completely
recrystallized. The fully recrystallized grain size is about 8 lm.
Furthermore, from EDS results, all elements are uniformly
distributed at the micron scale without any element segregation,
exhibiting a single-phase HEA. It can be seen that in addition to
the equiaxed crystal structure, the alloy also has a small amount
of circular titanium-rich second phase, which is mainly due to
the limited solubility of titanium in the CoCrFeMnNi alloy

3.2 Mechanical Properties
It can be found from Fig. 5 that alloy B after a series of heat
treatments is demonstrated to have excellent strength–ductility
synergy, showing high yield strength and ultimate tensile
strength of 667 MPa and 1089 MPa, respectively, while
maintaining an elongation of more than 20%. Due to the

engineering application prospect.

3.3 Microstructure Evolution of Alloy B upon Tension
Figure 7(a) presents the TEM characterization of alloy B after
tensile fracture at room temperature. Different from other
CoCrFeMnNi alloys with low or medium stacking fault energies,
this alloy does not have special deformation structures such as
stacking faults, nano-twins or phase transformation, but only has
high-density cross-slip dislocations. It is speculated that the
addition of titanium atoms greatly prevents other deformation
mechanisms. Further, it is found a small number of dislocations
with the plane slip along the direction (111) in Fig. 7(b).
Figure 7(c-d) reveals the TEM characterization of alloy B after
tensile fracture at a low temperature of 77 K, from which it can be
found that the plane slip replaces cross-slip as the dominant
deformation mode for the alloy. In some areas with severe plastic
deformation, the alloy opens up a new planar slip plane and forms
Taylor lattices or aggregates in the same plane to form a microband
structure. These tenuously dislocation structures can improve the
extensibility of the alloy as well as deformation twins.

The ﬁnal calculation results reveal that the minimum tensile

increases the SFE of the alloy, which makes that the alloy is
unable to produce transformation induced plasticity (TWIP)
and twinning induced plasticity (TRIP) effects, even at a low
temperature of 77 K.
The plane slip often has more obvious advantages over
wavy cross-slip for the following reasons. Firstly, compared
with the wave-slip dislocation structure at room temperature,
the plane-slip dislocation generated at 77 K can be more
homogeneously distributed on the slip plane, resulting in lower
strain and stress localization (Ref 79). Secondly, the planar slip
structure formed in FCC alloys can effectively suppress
dynamic recovery rate and reduce dislocation glide length
(Ref 80, 81), which results in a considerable improvement in
the storage capacity of the dislocation. As a result, the strength
and ductility of alloy B are improved simultaneously at a low
temperature of 77 K. Hong and Laird (Ref 82, 83) put forward

The existence of short-range order (SRO) can be used to
explain the plane slip of high SFE alloys at room temperature
(Ref 81-84). Alloys with ordered crystal structures tend to be
prone to SRO compared to almost completely disordered pure
metals. Cumulative dislocations have destructive effects on the
SRO, causing subsequent dislocations to slide on the same
plane without obstacle from the SRO. This process is known as
gliding surface softening. High-entropy alloys are characterized
by multiple principal elements, lattice distortion effect and slow
diffusion effect, such complex environment is very conducive
to the formation of short-range order (Ref 91-95). According to
Gibbs free energy, enthalpy dominates at lower temperatures.

5. Conclusions
1. A high-entropy alloy Fe 40Mn15Cr20Ni22Ti3 induced by
titanium additions was successfully developed after a ser-
ies of thermal–mechanical treatments. The alloy has a
yield strength of 677 MPa and an ultimate tensile
strength of 1089 MPa at room temperature, while main-
taining a ductility of more than 20%. In addition, the al-
loy achieves higher mechanical performance at a low
temperature of 77 K, with the yield strength up to about
800 MPa, tensile strength up to 1285 MPa, and ductility
up to 35 %. Compared with other HEAs, this alloy has
great application prospects.
2. The stacking fault energy of the alloy at room tempera-
ture is calculated to be 145.6 mJ/m
2 simply. It is found
that the true stress required for the formation of twins is
as high as 1733 MPa, which means that the alloy defor-
mation is almost impossible to produce deformation
twins, even at low temperature of 77 K.
3. At room temperature, the main deformation mechanism
of the alloy is dislocation cross-slip, with a small amount
of planar slip as a supplement. At low temperature of
77 K, the dislocation plane slip quickly becomes the
dominance. This is attributed to the combined effect of
temperature and SRO.
4. Finally, this work simply analyzed and calculated the
strengthening mechanisms. Grain boundary strengthening
and Orowan precipitation strengthening caused by
chrome-rich sigma precipitates are the main strengthening
mechanisms in alloy B.
