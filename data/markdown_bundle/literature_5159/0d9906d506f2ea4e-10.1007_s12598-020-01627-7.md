Thermal stability of bimodal grain structure in a cobalt-based
superalloy subjected to high-temperature exposure
Cheng-Lin Li* , Jeong Mok Oh, Seong-Woo Choi, Xin-Ming Mei,
Jae-Keun Hong, Jong-Taek Yeom, Qing-Song Mei, Zhen-Tao Yu,
Chan Hee Park*
Received: 23 July 2020 / Revised: 16 September 2020 / Accepted: 21 September 2020 / Published online: 12 January 2021
/C211GRINM Bohan (Beijing) Publishing Co., Ltd 2021
Abstract The present work investigates the thermal sta-
bility and mechanical properties of a Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni
(wt%) alloy with a bimodal grain (BG) structure. The BG
structure consisting of ﬁne grains (FGs) and coarse grains
(CGs) is thermally stable under high-temperature exposure
treatments of 760 /C176C for 100 h and 870 /C176C for
100–1000 h. The size of both FGs and CGs remains no
signiﬁcant changes after thermal exposure treatments. The
microstructural stability is associated with the slow kinetics
of grain growth and the pinning of carbides. The thermal
stability enables to maintain the BG structures, leading to
the same mechanical properties as the sample without
thermal exposure treatment. In particular, the BG alloy
samples after thermal exposure treatment exhibit superior
mechanical properties of both high strength and high
ductility compared to the unimodal grain (UG) structured
ones. The BG structure of the alloy samples after thermal
exposure is capable of avoiding severe loss of ductility and
retaining high strength. More speciﬁcally, the ductility of
the BG alloy samples after thermal exposure treatments of
870 /C176C for 500–1000 h is ten times higher (44.6% vs. 3.5%
and 52.6% vs. 5.0%) than that of the UG ones. The ﬁnding
in the present work may give new insights into high-tem-
perature applications of the Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni alloy and
other metallic materials with a BG structure.
Keywords Thermal stability; Bimodal grain structure;
High-temperature exposure; Cobalt-based superalloy
1 Introduction
Conventional nickel-based superalloys are the leading
high-temperature materials as they have high-temperature
mechanical properties and oxidation resistance [ 1, 2].
However, cobalt-based superalloys exhibit outstanding
strength and surface stability at high temperatures. In
comparison with nickel-based superalloys, cobalt-based
superalloys exhibit greater stability and higher sulﬁdation
resistance [3]. As a result, they are common materials for
the manufacturing of various components used in aero-
space applications such as gas turbines and aircraft turbines
[4, 5]. Their stability and high strength at elevated tem-
peratures, and good weldability and workability are bene-
ﬁcial in the design of turbine blades or combustion
chambers [ 3, 6]. These components are exposed to high
thermal and complex mechanical loadings requiring high-
temperature strength, good oxidation and sulﬁdation
resistance for prolonged times [ 7].
Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni (wt%) alloy is a cobalt-based
superalloy that exhibits high strength and excellent resis-
tance to corrosion and oxidation both at ambient and ele-
vated temperatures [ 8, 9]. It is commonly used in the
aerospace industry for gas turbine engines [ 10]. It has been
well known that Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni alloy exhibited a
substantial loss of ductility after aging at elevated tem-
perature for days or months due to the presence of carbides

[11, 12]. Recent studies [ 13, 14] revealed that low-tem-
perature short-time aging (600 /C176C for 4 h) improved the
ductility of this alloy because the presence of carbides
could suppress the c to e transformation and thus had a
contribution to the ductility improvement. Our previous
studies [ 15–18] reported that subjecting the Co–20Cr–
15W–10Ni alloy to cold-rolling and appropriate annealing
could create bimodal grain (BG) structures that consisted
of ﬁne grains (FGs with size ( d
fg) of 2–3 lm) and coarse
grains (CGs with size ( dcg) of 8–20 lm). The alloy with
BG structures exhibited both high strength and ductility at
room temperature [ 17, 18]. Furthermore, the resulting BG
structures were stable at 1100 /C176C till 1 h due to the pinning
of carbides [16]. Long-time annealing over 1 h resulted in a
loss of the grain bimodality, leading to the formation of a
unimodal grain (UG) structure [ 16]. This makes their
mechanical performance deteriorate.
BG structures have been investigated in many metals,
such as steels [19] and high entropy alloys [20]. Most of the
studies were concerned about their mechanical behaviors at
ambient temperature for simultaneously achieving high
strength and high ductility [ 21, 22]. Thermal stability that
characterizes the ability of the grain structure to resist an
apparent grain coarsening at elevated temperatures is very
critical for high-temperature applications. In general, the
thermal stability of metals can be enhanced by reducing the
driving force for grain growth or reducing grain boundary
energy [ 23]. There are common approaches to improve
thermal stability of metals such as Zener pinning or solute
segregation [ 24, 25], coherent twin boundaries [ 26] and
grain boundary engineering [ 27]. However, the applica-
tions of metals with a BG structure at elevated tempera-
tures are very challenging because BG structures consist of
both FGs and CGs, in which the grain boundary density or
energy is quite different. As such, their response to high-
temperature might be quite different due to the difference
in their grain boundary density and surface energy.
Therefore, it is very important to study the thermal stability
of BG structured materials and understand the response of
FGs and CGs to high-temperature. Unfortunately, it has
been rarely investigated for high-temperature metallic
materials so far.
The present work aims at investigating the thermal
stability and mechanical properties of a cobalt-based
superalloy with a BG structure. The initial BG structure
consists of FGs ( d
fg & 2.8 lm) and CGs ( dcg & 9.5 lm),
as well as many nano-carbides. Evolutions of grain struc-
tures and carbides of the BG structured alloy samples upon
high-temperature exposure experiments were investigated.
Understanding of the thermal stability and the resulting
mechanical properties of the BG structured Co–20Cr–
15W–10Ni alloy may give new insights into its high-tem-
perature applications.
2 Experimental
The material investigated in this work was a Co–20Cr–
15W–10Ni superalloy. The chemical composition (in wt%)
was Co (bal.), 20.58 Cr, 14.9 W, 10.81 Ni, 2.67 Fe, 1.72
Mn, 0.09 C. The material was fabricated by multi-pass
cold-rolling at room temperature and subsequently
annealed at 1000 /C176C for 30 min followed by water quench.
This process aims to attain a BG structure. Details of
processing procedures can be found elsewhere [ 15]. The
BG samples were then heat-treated at 760 /C176C for 100 h and
at 870 /C176C for 100–1000 h to investigate the thermal sta-
bility and, to evaluate the mechanical properties. Note that
the temperatures selected in this study were based on the
service temperature up to 760–870 /C176C in gas turbine
engines [28, 29]. The results can be also compared with the
data for HAYNES /C21025 alloy from Haynes International.
Microstructure characterizations were performed by
using backscattering electron (BSE) imaging and electron
backscattering diffraction (EBSD). BSE observations were
performed using a ﬁeld-emission scanning electron
microscope (FESEM, JEOL 7100F) operated at 20 kV to
observe the carbides. The size and volume fraction of
carbides were quantiﬁed based on the BSE images by using
an ImageJ program. EBSD observations were performed
using a FESEM (Hitachi SU6600) equipped with a TSL
EBSD system operated at 20 kV. EBSD maps were taken
with a working distance of 15 mm and a step size of
0.4 lm. The grain size and volume fraction of FGs and
CGs were calculated by using an OIM Analysis v8 pro-
gram. Fracture morphologies of the tensile tested samples
were observed using a FESEM (TESCAN MIRA III)
operated at 20 kV.
Tensile specimens with a dimension of 10 mm in gauge
length and 2.5 mm in gauge diameter were prepared.
Tensile tests were performed at room temperature by using
an Instron 5982 machine. A constant strain rate of
0.001 s
-1 was used. The tensile tests of the specimens
under each condition were carried out twice for average
values of the ultimate tensile strength (UTS), yield strength
(YS) and uniform elongation (uEl).

Based on the microstructural observations, the
microstructural stability of the BG structures in the alloy
samples can be mainly attributed to two aspects. First, the
global grain growth of this alloy at temperatures below
1000 /C176C is very slow due to the high activation energy
(600 kJ/C mol
-1) for grain growth (GB migration) [ 30, 31].
This energy is very high compared to the activation energy
for self-diffusion of pure cobalt (260 kJ /C mol-1 [32]) and
GB diffusion of pure cobalt (117 kJ /C mol-1 [33]). This
should be associated with the solute-drag effect as a con-
sequence of the high-content solute elements ( & 50%) in
the present alloy [ 34]. Second, the pinning of the carbides
also plays an important role in the microstructural stability.
The carbides in the samples are mainly observed in the FG
regions. Moreover, only a part of them located on the GBs
exhibited a coarsening from 500 nm to 1–2 lm and tended
to form continuous layers during thermal exposure. These
carbides in the size of 1–2 lm or in the form of a layer
(Figs. 3, 4) still have a strong pinning effect [ 35, 36].
However, there is no signiﬁcant change in the size and
morphology of the carbides pre-existing in the grain inte-
riors of FGs or CGs. Only a slight grain growth of CGs and
an increase in the vol
cg were observed because carbides are
nearly free in the interiors of the CGs.

Fracture surface observations were carried out on the
samples to understand their fracture behaviors and tensile
properties. Figure 6 shows the fracture morphologies of the
initial 870 /C176C/100 h and 870 /C176C/1000 h samples. All the
samples have a feature of a shallow cup-cone and an
appreciable amount of shear lip as marked in Fig. 6a–c.
These features are pieces of evidence of a ductile fracture
[38]. There is no evidence of grain boundary fracture or
river lines in the samples.
A high-magniﬁcation image in Fig. 6a’ reveals that the
surface of the initial sample has many dimples with parti-
cles at their bottoms (indicated by hollow arrows), typical
of ductile fracture. The dimples can be as small as 1 lmi n
diameter. The fracture surfaces of the 870 /C176C/100 h
(Fig. 6b’) and 870 /C176C/1000 h (Fig. 6c’) samples also con-
tain prominent dimples with carbide precipitations at their
bottoms. However, there is a remarkable increase in the
dimple size. Besides, coarse precipitates at the bottom of
large dimples are often crack (indicated by solid arrows).
The coarse carbides can be as large as 1–2 lm. Note that
the fracture surface of the 870 /C176C/100 h sample is quite
similar to that of the 870 /C176C/1000 h sample except the
carbide precipitations. As such, fracture surface analysis is
not sufﬁcient to understand why the ductility of the 870 /C176C/

100 h sample. A possible reason is that the 870 /C176C/100 h
sample had more carbides, which can suppress the trans-
formation of c to e and thus improving the ductility of the
Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni alloy as suggested by Ref. [ 14].
4 Conclusion
In the present work, we investigated the thermal stability
and mechanical properties of a Co–20Cr–15W–10Ni
superalloy subjected to high-temperature exposure. It is
demonstrated that the bimodal grain structure of the Co–
20Cr–15W–10Ni alloy is both thermally and mechanically
stable under high-temperature exposure of 760 and 870 /C176C
for 100–1000 h. The superior microstructural stability can
be attributed to the low kinetics of grain growth and the
pinning of carbides. The thermal exposure treatments of
760 /C176C for 100 h and 870 /C176C for 100–500 h had no sig-
niﬁcant inﬂuence on the strength of the samples due to the
stability of FGs and CGs. However, a decrease in their
ductility from 52% to 35% was observed due to the
occurrence of the slight coarsening of grain boundary
carbides. The bimodal grain structured alloy sample after
the thermal exposure of 870 /C176C for 1000 h exhibited
almost the same mechanical properties as the initial sam-
ple. The ductility of the alloy samples with a bimodal grain
structure after the thermal exposure treatments of 870 /C176C
for 500–1000 h is ten times higher (44.6% vs. 3.5% and
52.6% vs. 5.0%) than that of the alloy samples with a
unimodal grain structure. The bimodal grain structure is
capable of avoiding severe loss of ductility and retaining
high strength of the alloy after thermal exposure. The high
thermal stability of the bimodal structure can give new
insights into high-temperature applications for this alloy
and other metallic materials.
