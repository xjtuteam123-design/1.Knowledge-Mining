abstract
Commercial NiACrAMo based superalloys form ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo) precipitates at intermediate temper-
ature, which deteriorates their mechanical and corrosion properties, and whose kinetics varies signiﬁ-
cantly with alloy compositions. The aim of the present study is to understand the roles of Cr and Mo
solutes on ordering kinetics and transformation mechanisms in Ni ACrAMo alloys. On the basis of the
present study, it has been concluded that the Ni
2(Cr, Mo) phase evolves with a sluggish ordering kinetics
when its formation is governed only by the lattice diffusion of Cr, but its kinetics increases beyond a cer-
tain Mo/Cr ratio when lattice diffusion of Mo also starts contributing to its formation. This change has
been found to occur in alloys having Mo/Cr ratio = 1 and has been attributed to partial change in the nat-
ure of atomic bonding from covalent type to metallic type. This change in the nature of bonding also
reduced the necessary undercooling required below the disorder/order phase boundary for the precipita-
tion of the ordered phase. Micromechanisms of ordering transformations involved in the evolution of the
ordered phase have been described by a qualitative transformation model.

1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys such as C-4, C-22, C-276, Hastelloy S,
Haynes 242, Alloy 625 and Alloy 686, contain signiﬁcant amount
of Cr and Mo solutes which impart solid solution strengthening
and improve corrosion and oxidation resistance properties of these
alloys [1,2]. Such alloys combine properties which make them ide-
ally suited for many components of gas turbine engines such as
transition ducts, combustor cans, spray bars and ﬂame holders as
well as in afterburners, tail pipes and cabin heaters. The excep-
tional resistance to a wide variety of chemical environments,
including strong oxidizers such as ferric and cupric chlorides, chlo-
rine, hot contaminated solutions (organic and inorganic), formic
and acetic acids, acetic anhydride, and seawater and brine solu-
tions make them a candidate material for petrochemical industries.
However, formation of a long-range order (LRO) Ni 2(Cr, Mo) phase
(having an orthorhombic Pt 2Mo-type ordered structure) during
service deteriorates mechanical and corrosion properties
signiﬁcantly [3–7].
A considerable amount of effort has been made in the past to
understand the evolution mechanism of the ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo)
phase using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and
Monte-Carlo simulation studies. TEM studies have conclusively
established the involvement of a continuous ordering mode in
the formation of ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo) phase in Ni ACrAMo alloys
[8,9]. The LRO evolves via a short-range order (SRO) state charac-
terized by diffuse intensity maxima at characteristic {1 ½ 0} posi-
tions in the reciprocal space (termed as {1 ½ 0} SRO) [8,10]. The
transformation from SRO to LRO may take different order evolution
paths depending upon ageing temperatures [9]. For instance, in
NiA17MoA9.5Cr (at.%) alloys, the LRO forms by a continuous
ordering mechanism at T 6 700 /C176C, and, by a nucleation and
growth mechanism at temperatures above 700 /C176C [9]. In commer-
cial Ni-based alloys, the evolution kinetics of the Ni 2(Cr, Mo) phase
varies from one alloy to another due to different concentrations of
Cr and Mo solutes in them. For example, prolonged ageing is
required for the formation of this phase in Alloy 625 (containing
Cr = 21.73 and Mo = 8.82 (wt.%)) [11], while, it forms within a short
time in Haynes 242 (containing Cr = 9.3 and Mo = 16.4 (wt.%)) [12].
Due to signiﬁcant effect of the ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo) phase on
mechanical properties, interest has now shifted towards under-
standing its thermal stability with different Cr and Mo solutes in
these alloys. This can be seen in a recent 1st principle calculation

In Ni ACr alloys, Mo is added to impart solid solution strength-
ening as the atomic radius of Mo atoms is about 12% larger than
that of Ni or Cr atoms. Hardness of a Ni ACr alloy is therefore
expected to increase when Cr is replaced by Mo atoms. However,
continuous replacement of Cr by Mo atoms in stoichiometric
Ni2Cr alloys decreased the hardness beyond a certain composition.
The aim of the present study is, therefore, to identify the cause of
anomalous hardness and to understand the roles of Cr and Mo
solutes on ordering kinetics and transformation mechanisms in
NiACrAMo alloys. These studies have been carried out on ternary
NiACrAMo alloys of stoichiometric Ni 2(Cr1/C0 xMox) compositions
with varying Mo/Cr ratio employing differential scanning
calorimeter (DSC) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in
conjunction with hardness and resistivity measurements, which
has been recently shown as a promising tool for studying
short-range order and long-range order transformations [14].
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Material for study
Stoichiometric alloys of Ni 2(Cr1/C0 xMox) compositions with x =0 ,
0.25, 0.37 and 0.5 were prepared in an arc melting furnace in a high
purity argon atmosphere with a non-consumable tungsten elec-
trode and a water cooled hearth. Compositions of alloys were
selected on the basis of the Ni ACrAMo ternary phase diagram
[15] maintaining them in the single c phase ﬁeld (Fig. 1). Alloy but-
tons were homogenized at 1300 /C176C for 40 h under ﬂowing high
purity argon gas followed by furnace cooling. Composition of each
alloy was examined with energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrom-
eter attached to a scanning electron microscope on the basis of an
average of 10 points analysed across different regions of samples.
Compositions of all alloys were found in agreement with their
respective nominal compositions. Homogenized alloys were solu-
tion treated at 1150 /C176C for 2 h followed by water quenching.
Solution treated alloys were studied using Vicker’s microhardness,
resistivity measurement, DSC and TEM investigations.
Microhardness was determined by averaging hardness values mea-
sured at 20 different positions using a constant load of 1 kg.
2.2. Resistivity experiments
Resistivity measurements were carried out on about 20 mm
long samples with a square cross section (0.6 /C2 0.6 mm2)
machined out of homogenized alloys by electric-discharge machin-
ing (EDM). An in-house developed four probe resistivity measure-
ment set-up with heating arrangement was used for isochronal
heating of samples in solution treated state up to 900 /C176C. Details
of sample preparation and the resistivity instrument are given
elsewhere [14]. The effect of thermally induced potential at the
junctions of probes and sample was corrected by averaging the
voltage measured under current reversed conditions.
2.3. DSC experiments
A highly sensitive heat ﬂux differential scanning calorimeter
(DSC1/700) of Mettler-Toledo was used for phase transformation
studies on about 0.5 mm thick sample slices. For isochronal heat-
ing studies, samples were heated up to about 675 /C176C at varying
heating rates ranging from 2 /C176C/min to 10 /C176C/min. Two consecutive
heating cycles in identical experimental conditions were used for
every measurement to remove the instrumental effect from ther-
mograms. This was achieved by cooling the sample inside DSC at
a rate of 40 /C176C/min after completing the 1st heating run. The base-
line correction was carried out by subtracting the 2nd cycle ther-
mogram from that of the 1st cycle measured without disturbing
the sample or crucible positions inside the furnace. However, this
method of baseline correction was limited to cases where no reac-
tion occurred during the 2nd heating cycle.
2.4. TEM
Microstructural investigations were carried out on alloys in
both solution treated as well as aged conditions. Ageing treatments
at temperatures ranging from 500 /C176C to 760 /C176C for different periods
of time from few minutes to several hundred of hours were used to
produce varied microstructural conditions. Samples for TEM anal-
ysis were prepared by electrolytic jet thinning in a solution of 20%
perchloric acid and 80% ethanol maintained at /C0 40 /C176C. TEM exper-
iments were carried out using a Philips CM30 microscope operat-
ing at 300 kV and equipped with an EDX spectrometer. Standard
TEM techniques such as bright-ﬁeld (BF) and dark-ﬁeld (DF) imag-
ing and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) were employed.
Fuji image plates were used for recording diffraction patterns
employing long-time exposure to record weak and diffuse diffrac-
tion intensities.
An attempt was made to qualitatively understand the ordering
kinetics on the basis of intensity saturation of superlattice reﬂec-
tions (with ageing times) in diffraction patterns recorded with fol-
lowing precautions: (i) all diffraction patterns were recorded in
h100 i zone axes; (ii) a ﬁxed exposure time was used for recording
each pattern after ensuring a nearly same total electron current
density on the viewing screen; (iii) the same objective apertures
were used for all SAED patterns. Intensity proﬁles of superlattice
reﬂections in each diffraction pattern were ﬁrst determined along
a line passing through their pre-deﬁned centres. Thickness and
interaction volume corrections in each diffraction pattern were
then incorporated by proportionately correcting (enhanc-
ing/reducing) the intensities of respective transmitted beams to
match full-width at half-maximum of all diffraction patterns.
Diffraction intensities of superlattice reﬂections were then normal-
ized by their corrected transmitted intensity in each diffraction
patterns to quantify intensities at different ageing times.

3. Results
3.1. Microhardness study
Fig. 2 shows variation in microhardness with composition of
NiACrAMo alloys in the solution treated state. It is interesting to
note that the hardness ﬁrst increased linearly with composition
from Ni 2Cr to Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) alloys followed by a decrease in
Ni2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy. This was surprising because replacement of
Cr by Mo in Ni 2Cr alloys was expected to impart solid solution
strengthening due to relatively bigger atomic size of the latter as
observed in alloys with compositions up to Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37).
Softening of the alloy beyond Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) composition sug-
gested the inﬂuence of additional factors on the hardness of
Ni2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy, which is discussed later in Section 4.2.

microstructure with average grain size of about 0.5 mm (micro-

2Cr alloy

Resistivity results were consistent with diffuse neutron scattering

alloy [18]. Since resistivity and DSC results exhibited similar order-
ing characteristics in Ni 2Cr and Ni2(Cr0.75Mo0.25) alloys, similar SRO

be due to the presence of another SRO belonging to an ordered
structure. Das et al. [19] further characterized this SRO state by
the presence of diffuse intensity maxima at 1/3{1 1 3} positions,
which were also observed in the present case (see electron
diffraction patterns with h112 i and h013 i zone axes in
Fig. 6 (b) and (c)). Since 1/3{2 2 0} as well as 1/3{1 1 3} positions in
the reciprocal space are characteristic positions of the
Pt2Mo-type structure [11], it was not unreasonable to call it to be
a SRO of the Pt 2Mo-type order, which is referred to as ‘‘1/3{2 2 0}
SRO’’ in the present case. That diffuse intensity maxima due to
the 1/3{2 2 0} SRO was not a consequence of the streaking effect
of the existing {1 ½ 0} SRO was conﬁrmed by the electron diffrac-
tion evidences in h013 i zone axes in which reﬂections due to
{1 ½ 0} SRO were absent (see spots marked by arrows in
Figs. 6 (b) and (c)). Since 1/3{2 2 0} positions were close to {1 ½ 0}
positions, their overlap of intensities gave rise to a characteristic
nearly square-shaped intensity distribution in a h001 i zone axes
diffraction patterns ( Fig. 6 (b) and (c)). The qualiﬁcation of diffuse
reﬂections at 1/3{2 2 0} positions as SRO was also conﬁrmed on
the basis of resistivity change occurring during the intensiﬁcation

4. Discussion
On the basis of results presented above, it could be suggested
that Ni 2(Cr1/C0 x,Mox) alloys undergo ordering transformation via a
continuous mode through the {1 ½ 0} SRO state. The order devel-
ops with a gradual increase of 1/3{2 2 0} SRO at the expense of
{1 ½ 0} SRO until the formation of LRO. Likewise, at temperatures
above the critical ordering temperature (T C), the LRO phase trans-
formed into disordered phase through an intermediate SRO [14].
Increase of Mo in these alloys affected ordering kinetics as
evidenced by the accelerated ordering kinetics in Ni 2(Cr0.63,Mo0.37)
and Ni 2(Cr0.5,Mo0.5) alloys. Further, increase of the T C of the
Ni2(Cr, Mo) phase with the increase of Mo concentration suggested
a shift of phase ﬁelds of the ordered phase. This behaviour can be
explained more lucidly on the basis of stability of different phase
ﬁelds encountered by alloy during order evolution.
During order evolution, alloys encountered three phase ﬁelds,
namely, ordered phase (LRO), SRO and crystalline disordered phase
as depicted schematically in Fig. 10. These phase ﬁelds were sepa-
rated by phase boundaries. That a phase boundary, as shown by
broken lines, separating the disordered phase from SRO exists
was concluded on the basis of attainment of complete disorder in
splat-quenched state [25]. The SRO region was separated from
the LRO by an ‘‘ equilibrium phase boundary ’’, which represented
the limit above/below which the ordered phase must dissolve/-
form. However, due to the coherency strain of the ordered phase,
this limit would suppress to temperatures below the equilibrium
phase boundary and is called ‘‘ coherent phase boundary ’’ [26]. The
coherent phase boundary necessitates the requirement of under-
cooling for the formation of ordered phase. Below this boundary
ordered phase would form via a nucleation and growth mode. At
still lower temperatures continuous ordering would override the
nucleation and growth process due to the build-up of concentra-
tion waves of large amplitudes, which become unbound at the
instability limit [27]. This limit is called ‘‘ coherent instability bound-
ary’’ [28]. Below this boundary the order phase would evolve by a
continuous ordering mechanism.
The kinetics of ordering transformation in different phase ﬁelds
would be dependent upon the thermal history as well as composi-
tion of alloys [29], as they would decide the kinetics of transforma-
tion mechanisms. Usually, annealing at temperatures below the
coherent instability boundary would grow 1/3{2 2 0} SRO until
the formation of the LRO after a certain incubation period. This per-
iod would decrease with increase of Mo content as well as with the
ageing temperature. For instance, annealing of Ni 2Cr alloy for 500 h
at 500 /C176C produced very ﬁne ordered domains which could not be
resolved in a BF image ( Fig. 4 (b)). On the other hand, in
Ni2(Cr0.5,Mo0.5) alloys, nanometer size ordered domains appeared
within 2 h of ageing at 525 /C176C( Fig. 4 (d)) and the incubation time
reduced further (up to few minutes) at higher ageing temperatures
(Fig. 7 (e)). Due to large incubation times associated with the

4.1. Mechanisms of ordering transformations
In Ni2Cr and Ni2(Cr0.75,Mo0.25) alloys, SRO to LRO transformation
did not complete during isochronal heating and alloys remained
predominantly in the 1/3{2 2 0} SRO state up to T C. Earlier studies
have established that the T C of the Ni 2Cr alloy is 572 ± 5 /C176C [17],
above which SRO exists up to 650 /C176C [18]. This conﬁrmed that the
resistivity maximum which appeared at 571 /C176Ci nN i 2Cr alloy
(Fig. 3 (a)) corresponded to its TC and a decrease of resistivity
beyond it could be attributed to the formation of {1 ½ 0} SRO. For
temperatures, T < TC, the existing {1 ½ 0} SRO exhibited a tendency
to transform into LRO via the evolution of 1/3{2 2 0} SRO by a
mechanism involving atomic rearrangements as evidenced by
appearance of an exothermic peak in temperature regime II (see
Fig. 11 (a)). Likewise, atomic rearrangements could be associated
with the evolution of 1/3{2 2 0} SRO in Ni 2(Cr0.75Mo0.25) alloy,
though the addition of Mo shifted TC to a higher value (620 /C176C), sug-
gesting of increased stability of the ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo) phase with
Mo addition. The 1/3{2 2 0} SRO evolved via a 1st order continuous
mode as conﬁrmed by the appearance of exothermic peak in the
region II of Fig. 11 (a) and (b). For temperatures, T > T

SRO formed by a 2nd order transformation mode as evidenced
by the baseline shift at the end of exothermic peaks
(Fig. 11(a) and (b)), which coincided with the temperature exhibit-
ing resistivity maxima (i.e., at T = TC). During isochronal heating,
due to the sluggish ordering kinetics, the SRO (either 1/3{2 2 0} or
{1 ½ 0}) had barely reached its maximum attainable value which
caused the appearance of baseline shift at TC in consecutive heating
cycles. This was independently conﬁrmed by the calorimetric
experiment of a sample aged at 585 /C176C (marginally above TC) for
50 h, which was devoid of any baseline shift in the entire tempera-
ture range (see inset of Fig. 11(a)). The absence of baseline shift in
the aged alloy where the {1 ½ 0} SRO was allowed to reach its max-
imum value, thus, conﬁrmed that the formation of {1 ½ 0} SRO in
the solution treated samples occurred via a 2nd order transforma-
tion mode. Symmetry considerations of the {1 ½ 0} SRO structure
also supported its formation by a 2nd order transformation mode,
since N 2M2 structure responsible for the appearance of {1 ½ 0}
SRO [10,25,30] satisﬁes all the three Landau–Lifshitz-symmetry
rules of a 2nd order transformation [10,27].
A similar ordering tendency was observed in Ni 2(Cr0.63,Mo0.37)
and Ni 2(Cr0.5,Mo0.5) alloys for T < TC, though the 1/3{2 2 0} SRO
could transform into LRO due to faster ordering kinetics in these
alloys (see Fig. 11(c) for Ni 2(Cr0.5,Mo0.5) alloy). This was conﬁrmed
by the presence of sharp 1/3{2 2 0} reﬂections and near extinction
of {1 ½ 0} reﬂections in electron diffraction patterns of samples
arrested at the end of 1st exothermic peak ( Fig. 11d). Similar to cat-
egory I alloys, for T < TC, growth of the 1/3{2 2 0} SRO occurred via a
1st order continuous mode as evidenced by the appearance of an
exothermic peak in temperature regime II in Fig. 11 (c). However,
the 1/3{2 2 0} SRO to LRO transformation involved a 2nd order
mode as evidenced by a baseline shift immediately after the ﬁnish
of 1st exothermic peak (see temperature regime II in Fig. 11 (c)).
This analysis, thus, conﬁrmed that the temperature at a baseline
shift (marked by an arrow in Fig. 11(c)) corresponded to the forma-
tion of LRO which occurred initially via the evolution of 1/3 {2 2 0}
SRO involving a 1st order mechanism (continuous ordering) fol-
lowed by a 2nd order mode during later stages of transformation
pertaining to the elimination of {1 ½ 0} SRO. Resistivity behaviour
(regime II of Fig. 11 (c)) in conjunction with diffraction evidences,
where diffuse 1/3{2 2 0} reﬂections evolved until they became
sharp with near extinction of {1 ½ 0} reﬂections, also supported
this evolution mechanism and was consistent with a previous
report [14]. Thus, combined use of resistivity and DSC experiments
conﬁrmed the involvement of a 2nd order transformation mode
during the completion of continuous ordering reaction. The opera-

4.2. Effect of Mo addition on ordered phase boundaries
The dissolution temperature of the ordered phase is reported to
increase from 571 /C176Ci nN i 2Cr alloy to 672 /C176Ci nN i 2(Cr0.75Mo0.25)
alloy [32]. A further increase of the dissolution temperature from
720 /C176Ci nN i 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) to 775 /C176Ci nN i 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloys
(deduced on the basis of resistivity plots in Fig. 3 (b)) established
an increased stability of the ordered phase with increase of Mo.
This increased stability could be attributed to increased hybridiza-
tion strength of d–d orbitals with increase in Mo content in these
alloys, which has been demonstrated in a recent theoretical study
[13]. Increase of Mo-contents in Ni 2(Cr1/C0 xMox) alloys could also
affect the coherent phase boundary of the ordered phase provided
coherency free energy change by this effect. Coherent phase
boundary is a characteristic feature caused by the positive contri-
bution of coherency free energy to the total free energy and signi-
ﬁes the requirement of a certain amount of undercooling below an
equilibrium phase boundary for the nucleation process [26].
According to Karmazin et al. [17,32], an undercooling of about
37 /C176C below the equilibrium phase boundary is necessary for the
formation of ordered phase in binary Ni ACr and ternary
NiACrAMo alloys having varying amount of Ni and Cr for a ﬁxed
Cr/Mo ratio of 3. However, the present work suggested an under-
cooling of about 25 /C176Ci nN i 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy as evidenced by
the presence/absence of LRO at 750 /C176C/760 /C176C( Fig. 12 ). Therefore,
it was not unreasonable to conclude that replacement of Cr by
Mo in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy reduced the necessary undercooling
required for the formation of LRO. In other words, it could be con-
strued that the replacement of about half of Cr by Mo atoms in
Ni2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy brought the coherent phase boundary more
closer to the equilibrium phase boundary than that in Ni 2Cr and
Ni2(Cr0.75Mo0.25) alloys. For ordering transformations, the coher-
ency free energy varies as E /C2 g2, where E, is elastics modulus,
and g is order parameter. The elastic modulus is a measure of bond
strength between atomic species which can change with the
change of Mo amount in Ni 2(Cr1/C0 xMox) alloys. A recent study by
Hu et al. [13] has established that elastic modulus of Ni 2Cr alloys
increases with Mo addition up to Ni 2(Cr0.75Mo0.25), while reported
a decrease in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy. This difference has been attrib-
uted to partial change in the nature of atomic bonding from cova-
lent character in alloys up to Ni 2(Cr0.75Mo0.25) compositions to
metallic type in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy. Hu et al. [13] attributed this
change to the disappearance of the band gap at the Fermi level of
free electron density of states. The observed linear increase of
hardness from Ni 2Cr to Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) alloys and a decrease
beyond it ( Fig. 2 ) was consistent with this change in the nature
of atomic bonding from covalent to metallic type. This decrease
in the elastic modulus, in turn, lowered the elastic free energy con-
tribution, which manifested in shifting the coherent phase bound-
ary closer to equilibrium phase boundary in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy.

4.3. Kinetic interpretation
Attempts were made to identify atomic species controlling the
ordering reactions by comparing activation energies determined
by kinetic analysis with the reported literature. Usually in solid
state diffusion, activation energies for lattice diffusion of substitu-
tional solutes fall close to self-diffusion values of solvent atoms but
a variance is expected when there is a difference in the atomic
sizes. A larger misﬁt of solutes in a host lattice decreases the acti-
vation energy when the solute atom is large and vice versa [33,34].
For instance, nearly similar atomic radii of Cr and Ni atoms, the
activation energy for the self-diffusion of Ni is 275 kJ/mol [35]
while that of Cr in pure Ni is 272 kJ/mol [36]. However, activation
energy for the Cr diffusion increases to 290 kJ/mol in Ni A30at%Cr
[36]. This suggested of additional factors like atomic bonding
responsible for the higher activation energies in concentrated
alloys. Janotti et al. [33] and Kramer et al. [34] have reported that
increased bonding directionality increases activation energies for
solutes diffusion when solute and host atoms have similar radii.
On the basis of this argument it could be argued that increased
activation energy for Cr diffusion in Ni A30at%Cr alloys would have
resulted from the increased the bonding directionality between
solute-host atoms.
On the other hand, there is limited data available on the activa-
tion energy of Mo in Ni-alloys. The activation energy for the diffu-
sion of Mo in Ni is about 268 kJ/mol [37]. Due to the absence of
appropriate activation energy data of Mo diffusion in Ni ACr alloys,
the effect of Mo addition on its activation energy in Ni 2(Cr1/C0 xMox)
alloys was deduced on the basis of following understanding: (i) Mo
atoms substitute Cr atoms in the alloy; (ii) for a given Ni ACrAMo
alloy, NiAMo bonds are relatively stronger than Ni ACr bonds [13];
(iii) strengths Ni ACr bonds increases while that of Ni AMo bonds
decreases with further substitution of Cr by Mo [13]. It was,

247 kJ/mol in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloys suggested that Mo diffusion
promoted the formation ordered phase beyond Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37)

and increase that of Ni ACr bonds till they become nearly same at
Ni2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) composition [13]. In addition, the nature of bonding
also changes its character from covalent type (strong bond) in
Ni2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) alloy to metallic type in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy

strengths of Ni ACr and Ni AMo bonds promoted the diffusion of
both Cr as well as Mo in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloys.

Though, the activation energies for the formation as well as
growth (respectively 1st and 2nd peaks of Fig. 11 (c)) of ordered
domains in Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy were nearly same, activation
energy for growth in the Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) alloy (2nd peak of
Fig. 4 (c)) was signiﬁcantly lower than that for the formation
(1st peak of Fig. 4 (c)). Nearly similar activation energies for
the two processes in the Ni 2(Cr0.5Mo0.5) alloy conﬁrmed that the
lattice diffusion of both Cr and Mo solutes contributed during
the continuous ordering as well as growth stages. In contrast, in
the Ni 2(Cr0.63Mo0.37) alloy, signiﬁcantly lower activation energy
for the second peak suggested of little role of lattice diffusion of
Cr and Mo in Ni during the growth process. Since the investigated
alloy had quite a larger grain size, the effect of grain boundary
diffusion could also be ignored. Incidentally, this activation
energy value was close to the diffusion of Cr as well as Ni in pure
Cr [38]. The lower activation energy for the diffusion of solutes in
Cr could be attributed to its low energy required for the forma-
tion of vacancies [38]. On these lines it could be argued that
the diffusion of Mo in Cr would also result in such smaller value
of activation energy, which was supported by the fact that bigger
Mo atoms would require relatively smaller energy to reach at Cr
vacancy. Such a mechanism would lead to the enrichment of Mo
in the ordered Ni 2(Cr, Mo) domains, which has been shown in a
recent study [39]. However, a detailed analysis of such a mecha-
nism needs theoretical considerations, which is outside the scope
of the present study.
5. Conclusions
On the basis of the present work, following conclusions can be
made.
1. The order evolution may involve one or more of following steps
depending upon alloy composition, thermal history and ageing
temperature:
i. evolution of the SRO from the disordered phase;
ii. transformation of the SRO to LRO;
iii. growth of the LRO;
iv. dissolution of the LRO; and
v. evolution of SRO during the dissolution of LRO.
