ABSTRACT
Lightweight high-entropy alloys(LWHEAs) typically exhibit limited room-temperature ductility. Here, 
we show that Al-induced chemical ordering can be used to regulate deformation behavior in ac-cast 
Ti2ZrNbVAlx LWHEAs featuring dendritic segregation Although all alloys exhibit nominally single-
phase BCC structure, increasing Al content introduces nanoscale B2-type ordering from grain bound-
aries into grain interiors. An intermediate degree of ordering promotes dislocation storage capacity, 
and producing a pronounced strength-ductility synergy. In contrast, excessive intragranular order-
ing constrains dislocation motion, enforces slip localization, and triggers quasi-cleavage fracture. 
These results reveal that the spatial distribution and continuity of chemical ordering, rather than 
its mere presence, govern mechanical performance in BCC LWHEAs with heterogeneous dendritic 
microstructures.

1. Introduction
LWHEAs combine high strength [1–3], excellent ther-
mal stability [4–6], reduced density [7], and poten-
tial high-temperature performance [8–10]. yet their 
predominantly body-centered cubic (BCC) structures 
exhibit poor room-temperature ductility due to high 
Peierls stress, limited slip systems, and strain local-
ization [11–15]. Alloying strategies based on chemical 
complexity, especially introducing element pairs with 
strongly negative mixing enthalpy (1Hmix), effectively 
strengthen BCC HEAs by promoting chemical ordering 
[16–20], but excessive ordering often induces brittle frac-
ture and ductility loss [19,21–23]. Among them, Al is 
particularly influential, as its strong interactions with Ti, 
Zr, and Nb readily induce B2 or nanoscale ordering while

2. Results and discussion
The microstructural characteristics of the Ti2ZrNbV Alx
alloys are present in Figure 1. The XRD patterns of 
the Ti2ZrNbV Alx alloys indicate that all Al-containing 
compositions retain a single-phase BCC structure, with 
the (110) reflection being the dominant peak. This 
observation is further corroborated by the EBSD phase 
map shown in Figure 1(k), which confirms the absence 
of any secondary phases. Despite the overall BCC 
structure, pronounced elemental segregation is evident 
(Figure 1(b–d)). Specifically, Zr and Al are preferentially 
enriched in the intergranular regions, whereas Ti and 
Nb tend to concentrate within the grain interiors, as 
summarized in Table S1.
Figure 1(e–j) present the EBSD results of the Ti2ZrNb
V Alx alloys. The inverse pole figure (IPF) maps reveal 
that all the alloys exhibit fine equiaxed grain struc-
tures. The average grain size, summarized in Figure 1(l), 
shows a slight grain-refinement effect with increasing 
Al content. Owing to the limited spatial resolution of 
XRD and EBSD, nanoscale chemical ordering cannot 
be resolved by these techniques. Therefore, TEM was 
employed for more detailed microstructural character-
ization. As shown in Figure 2(a-II), the selected-area 
electron diffraction (SAED) pattern and the FFT pattern 
of the Al0 alloy exhibits only the fundamental reflections 
of a BCC structure, confirming the absence of chem-
ically ordered phases. Correspondingly, the elemental 
distribution in the Al0 alloy is relatively homogeneous 
(Figure 2(a-IV)). In contrast, upon Al addition, although 
the alloy retains an overall single BCC phase according to 
SAED, additional diffraction spots associated with a B2-
type ordered structure are clearly observed in the high-
resolution TEM image of the Al0.2 alloy (Figure 2(b-V)). 
Notably, these ordered domains preferentially emerge 
in the intergranular regions, whereas the grain interi-
ors remain largely in a locally disordered BCC state 
(Figure 2(b-II)). The superimposed inverse fast Fourier 
transform (IFFT) image reconstructed from both BCC 
and B2 reflections further corroborates this spatial het-
erogeneity (Figure 2(b-VII)). Specifically, well-defined 
ordered domains are concentrated along grain bound-
aries, while the intragranular regions exhibit only faint 
diffuse disks (Figure 2(b-II)), indicative of the formation 
of the ordered phase, together with sparsely distributed 
nanoscale ordered regions. However, some recent stud-
ies proposed different opinion that the extra diffraction 
spots would have other origins such as higher-order Laue 
zone(HOLZ) [35] or stacking faults [36]. More recently, 
Han et al. performed an in-situ cyclic loading experi-
ments in TEM and found that the relative intensity of 
diffuse spots reduced during cyclic loading. The results 
contradict with the presence of HOLZ and stacking fault 
which expected an increase of diffraction spots intensity 
[37]. Furthermore, L. W ang et al. suggested that, within 
the same material system, LCO corresponds to an early 
stage of nanocrystalline precipitation [22]. Accordingly, 
unlike the relatively clear superlattice reflections asso-
ciated with well-developed nanocrystalline precipitates, 
the diffraction features of LCO are typically weak and 
diffuse [38–41].
From a qualitative perspective, the superlattice spots 
in the Al0.6 sample are more distinct than those in the 
Al0.2 sample (Figure 2(b-VI and c-II)). With further 
increasing Al content, the Al0.6 alloy also develops a 
substantial number of B2 domains within the grain inte-
riors(Figure 2(c-III)). These observations reveal a hier-
archical evolution of chemical ordering, in which grain 
boundaries-owing to pre-existing chemical segregation, 
serve as preferential nucleation sites for B2-type ordering. 
Similarly, this can also be observed in the recrystallized 
homogeneous samples (Figure S2). Furthermore, in the 
Al0.6 sample, the ordered domains have acquired con-
tinuous spatiality throughout the entire crystal grain and 
have taken the dominant position in the microstructure. 
In addition, the area fractions of the ordered phases in 
the intergranular and intragranular regions were sepa-
rately quantified, as shown in Figure S3. With increasing 
Al content, the area fraction of the ordered phases in 
the intergranular regions shows a slight increase, whereas 
that in the intragranular regions increases significantly. 
This indicates a progressive enhancement of intragranu-
lar chemical ordering with increasing Al concentration.

Interestingly, no obvious compositional fluctuations 
are detected even in the Al0.6 alloy, despite its rela-
tively high-volume fraction of ordered regions. A sim-
ilar phenomenon has been reported previously [42], 
wherein local atomic rearrangements within a cubic lat-
tice rapidly evolve into nanoscale ordered domains with-
out the involvement of long-range elemental diffusion 
[43]. This behavior indicates that the ordering process is 
predominantly governed by short-range atomic shuffling 
rather than by classical diffusion-controlled phase sepa-
ration mechanisms [44,45]. With increasing Al content, 
both the degree and spatial extent of short-range order-
ing are significantly enhanced; gradually a long-range 
ordered phase is formed (as shown in the diffraction 
graph in Figure 2(c)). Consequently, the Al0.2 alloy main-
tains a single-phase matrix containing finely dispersed 
nanoscale ordered regions. This microstructural charac-
teristic is critical for preserving mechanical integrity, as it 
avoids the severe degradation of mechanical properties, 
particularly the catastrophic loss of ductility, commonly 
associated with the formation of coarse or unfavorably 
distributed ordered phases in refractory HEAs [7,46–48].
The pronounced increase in both the number density 
and size of B2 ordered phase domains with increasing 
Al content indicates a progressively enhanced thermo-
dynamic driving force for chemical ordering [32], in 
good agreement with the thermodynamic calculations.

The room-temperature tensile properties of the Ti2Zr
NbV Alx alloys are presented in Figure 3(a and b). The 
Al0 and Al0.2 alloys exhibit yield strengths of approxi-
mately 954 and 1049 MPa, respectively, while retaining 
considerable tensile ductility ( > 20%). This favorable 
strength–ductility combination can be attributed to the 
presence of a limited volume fraction of B2 ordered phase 
domains that are spatially confined primarily to grain 
boundaries. Increasing the Al content to 0.4 results in 
an approximately 20% increase in yield strength, accom-
panied by a concomitant ∼ 30% reduction in ductility, 
which is consistent with the increased density of ordered 
domains and their partial penetration into grain interi-
ors. With further increasing Al content to 0.6, the tensile 
elongation decreases sharply, reflecting the formation of 
extensive B2 ordered phase domains that severely impede 
dislocation motion and promote early brittle fracture. 
In addition, the lightweight alloy design leads to a sub-
stantial improvement in the specific yield strength of the 
alloys, as summarized in Table S4 and Figure S6.
The designed alloys exhibit an attractive strength-
ductility balance, in good agreement with their observed 
deformation behavior. Fractographic analysis (Fig-
ure 3(c)) reveals that the Al0 alloy is characterized 
by abundant dimples, indicative of homogeneous plas-
tic deformation and ductile fracture. As shown in 
Figure 3(d–f), with increasing Al content (x = 0.2–0.6), 
the fracture morphology progressively transitions toward 
a quasi-cleavage mode, characterized by a mixture of 
intergranular fracture and cleavage-like facets. Although 
the Al0.2 alloy still retains a notable work-harde-
ning capability, the Al0.4 alloy, where cleavage features 
become dominate, exhibits a substantially reduced work-
hardening capacity, ultimately leading to the onset of 
strain softening.

of an alloy is governed by multiple strengthening mech-
anisms, including intrinsic yield strength (1σ mix) [54], 
solid solution strengthening (1σ s) [46,55], grain bound-
ary strengthening (σ g) [56,57], dislocation strengthening 
(σ d) [58], and precipitation strengthening (σ p) [59,60]. 
Since the alloys investigated in this study are in the as-
cast condition with a relatively low initial dislocation 
density, the contribution from dislocation strengthening 
can be reasonably neglected. Accordingly, the calculated 
yield strength (σ cal) can be expressed as the sum of the 
remaining four contributions:

on the deformation mechanisms of the alloys, EBSD

fracture surfaces (Figure 4(a–f)). The Al0 and Al0.2

TEM analysis provides further insight into the defor-
mation behavior of the Al0.2 alloy. As shown in Figure 5(a 
and b), plastic deformation is predominantly accommo-
dated by planar slip, characterized by well-defined and 
spatially confined slip bands. Within these slip channels, 
dislocations interact and entangle, forming high-density 
dislocation walls [21], as highlighted by the yellow region 
in Figure 5(c). Such localized deformation behavior is 
commonly observed in BCC alloys [68,70], and is known 
to strongly influence their deformation ductility. With 
increasing strain, pronounced dislocation multiplication 
and frequent cross-slip events give rise to a complex, 
interconnected dislocation network between adjacent 
slip bands (Figure 5(d and e)) [22]. This microstruc-
tural evolution is consistent with the elevated GND 
densities revealed by EBSD analysis and demonstrates 
that the Al0.2 alloy accommodates plastic deformation 
through widespread dislocation activity. In particular, 
the nanoscale B2 domains act as strong obstacles of 
dislocation glide through an order-strengthening mech-
anism. When dislocations interact with these ordered 
regions, they must either shear the ordered lattice, lead-
ing to the formation of antiphase boundaries(APBs), 
or bypass the obstacles through local bowing or cross-
slip, both of which require a substantial increase in 
deformation energy [5,31]. More importantly, as recently 
highlighted by W ang et al., appropriately tailored local 
ordered domains can promote the multiplication and 
branching of planar-slip bands, so that dislocations are 
not confined to a few highly localized channels, but can 
progressively spread into previously undeformed regions, 
thereby enhancing deformation delocalization and sus-
taining the work-hardening rate to larger strains [22].
At moderate Al content(Al0.2), the limited fraction 
of ordered domains, which are primarily confined to 
grain boundaries, favor repeated dislocation accumu-
lation and multiplication within slip bands, thereby 
sustaining work hardening through dynamic disloca-
tion interactions. In contrast, for alloys with higher 
Al contents, ordered domains become increasingly dis-
tributed throughout the grain interiors, thereby impos-
ing stronger barriers to dislocation motion. Although 
this promotes rapid GND accumulation, it simultane-
ously confines plastic deformation to narrow regions. 
The inability to develop an extensive dislocation network, 
combined with the localized buildup of lattice curvature 
[71–73], leads to severe strain localization, which is par-
ticularly evident in the fracture behavior of the Al0.6

3. Conclusion
The influence of Al addition on chemical ordering 
and mechanical properties in lightweight Ti2ZrNbV Alx
alloys was systematically investigated. Although all alloys 
exhibit a nominally single-phase BCC structure, Al addi-
tion would induce pronounced chemical ordering. B2 
ordering domains preferentially form in the vicinity of 
grain boundaries, while intragranular ordering progres-
sively develops with increasing Al content. An interme-
diate Al concentration results in a favorable strength-
ductility synergy by providing effective barriers to dis-
location motion while maintaining distributed plastic 
deformation and sustained work hardening. In contrast, 
excessive Al leads to the formation of extensive ordered 
domains, which strongly restrict dislocation activity, pro-
mote strain localization, and ultimately cause brittle frac-
ture. The strengthening behavior cannot be explained 
by conventional solid-solution strengthening alone, but 
is instead dominated by Al-induced chemical order-
ing. These results demonstrate that controlled chemical 
ordering is an effective strategy for achieving a balanced 
combination of strength and ductility in lightweight BCC 
high-entropy alloys.
