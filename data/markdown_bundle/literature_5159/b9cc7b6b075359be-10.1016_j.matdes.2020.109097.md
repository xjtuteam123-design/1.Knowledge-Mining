It is known that Ni-based superalloys experience microstructural rafting during high temperature straining. This
paper presents a comparative study of the different rafting models by means ofﬁnite element modeling and ex-
perimentation investigation. It was found that uniaxial elastic loading does not alter the misﬁto rm i sﬁti s o t r o p y
of the structure, but causes repartitioning of the misﬁt into elastic strains in the γ and γ' phases. This causes an
elastic strain energy anisotropy and hydrostatic stress anisotropy among the vertical and horizontal γ phase
channels. Both anisotropies predict a raft structure that is contrary to the experimental observation. Through a
carefully designed pre-treatment at 750 °C and under a uniaxial stress of 750 MPa, an anisotropic dislocation
structure was created on the {001} γ'/γ interfaces without altering the cuboidal morphology of the γ' phase.
This allows the evaluation of the inﬂuence of dislocations on the rafting behavior of the alloy. It was found that

tions base don the anistropic dislocation strucutres. This demonstrates that an anisotropic dislocation structure
on the {001} γ'/γ interfaces is a direct cause of rafting. This is attributted to the fact that dislocations on γ'/γ in-
terfaces help to relax the local misﬁt strains, causing some to expand at the expense of others. At the same time,
the formation of the anisotropic dislocation structure is a direct result of the anisotropy of elastic strains induced
by the applied bia stess in during the pre-treatment. These explain the complex interplay of the elastic and plastic
models reported the literature.

1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys exhibit excellent mechanical
properties and oxidation resistance at high temperatures and, as a re-
sult, they are commonly used in high temperature load bearing applica-
tions, such as turbine blades and vanes [ 1–3]. The high temperature
mechanical strength of these alloys stems from their special microstruc-
ture, which contains an L12 ordered γ' phase in {001} cuboidal form em-
bedded in a continuous matrix of a disordered fccγ phase [4,5]. The two
phases are fully coherent with {001} interfaces, but owing to their dif-
ferent elemental enrichments [6–8], they have different lattice parame-
ters, causing lattice mis ﬁt at the {001} γ'/γ interfaces. A Ni-based
superalloy may have either a positive (the unit cell of γ' is larger than
that of γ) or negative (the unit cell of γ' is smaller than that of γ)l a t t i c e
misﬁt[ 9–11].
When used for turbine blades, superalloys are continuously sub-
jected to centrifugal forces at high temperatures in service, generally
700– 1100 °C. This causes creep damage and microstructural evolutions.
Microstructural changes in turn affect the mechanical properties and
creep life of the superalloys. Three major microstructural changes
occur during such processes, i.e., the formation of dislocation networks
(typically at above 700 °C) [12–16], precipitation of topologically close
packed phases (typically at 700 – 1100 °C) [ 17–21], and coarsening of
the γ' phase cuboids (typically at above 900 °C) [22–25]. The formation
and evolution of dislocation networks have been well studied in the lit-
erature. Dislocations in the alloys have been found to move, multiply
and form into networks on the {001}γ'/γ interfaces during thermal ex-
posure even under no external load [26,27]. This is largely driven by the
need to relax the lattice misﬁt energy at the interfaces. Dislocation net-
works are also found to form during high temperature straining under
external load, e.g., creep testing. In this case the networks formed are
generally denser with smaller cell dimensions and are anisotropic
among the three orthogonal {100} interfaces [ 28–30], apparently due
to the bias of the external load on the lattice mis ﬁts t r a i n s .
Coarsening of the γ' phase may occur in two forms, i.e., isotropic
coarsening and anisotropic coarsening, with the latter being commonly
referred to as rafting [31,32]. The isotropic coarsening generally occurs
during thermal exposure under no external loading. This is obviously
driven by the reduction of the total interface area, and via the mecha-
nism of Ostwald ripening [ 33,34]. Anisotropic coarsening (rafting) is a
more complex situation. It occurs under the bias of an anisotropic exter-
nal loading, such as uniaxial tension or compression. Coarsening of the
γ' phase has been found to occur at above 900 °C. Another important
factor for coarsening is the exposure time. The process is slow and it
takes typically ~10 h to form obviously rafted structures [ 22]. Higher
temperatures and higher magnitudes of bias stress provide faster kinet-
ics for the coarsening process [25,35].
Generally, two types of raft structures are observed experimentally,
i.e., plate-like raft and rod-like raft. The plate-like raft usually forms with
the plate normal (the unique direction of plate) aligned to the direction
of the external stress, thus it is also named the“N type” raft. The rod-like
raft is usually formed with its rod length direction parallel with the di-
rection of the external stress, thus named the “Pt y p e” raft. The type
selection of a raft structure is determined by the interplay between
the signs of the mis ﬁt and the external load, as summarized in Table 1
[36–38]. In addition to the uniaxial stress condition, the raft phenome-
non is also observed under biaxial loading conditions [ 39–41], which
can be considered as the subjoining of two uniaxial stress conditions.”
The phenomenon of rafting of Ni-based superalloys was ﬁrst ob-
served by Tien and Copley in 1971 [ 42]. Since then, extensive effort
has been made to characterize and understand this peculiar phenome-
non, via both experimentation and simulation. From experimental in-
vestigations, many facts have been established. For example, rafting of
the γ' phase occurs only after some initial creep deformation [35]. Cor-
respondences among the sign of the misﬁt, the mode of uniaxial stress,
and the morphology of a rafted structure have been clariﬁed, as summa-
rized in Table 1. Under the in ﬂuence of an applied stress, dislocations
can penetrate into narrow γ channels perpendicular to the dislocation
line, and multiply in their population [43]. In addition, some dedicated
two-stage experiments have been conducted, which include a pre-
straining stage at a lower temperature under stress to obtain anisotropic
distribution of dislocations followed by stress-free aging without ap-
plied stress [ 44–48]. These studies have con ﬁrmed that rafting can
occur during the stress-free aging without an applied load and it occurs
in the direction of the load applied in the pre-straining. This is attributed
to the anisotropic dislocation structures formed by the pre-straining.
However, some questions and uncertainties still remain, including:
(i) The formation of anisotropic dislocation distributions is often at-
tributed to the anisotropy of von Mises equivalent stressﬁeld be-
tween the vertical and horizontal channels. This stress is not a
shear stress that drives the dislocation movement. In addition,
the relationship between the speci ﬁc propagation manner of a
dislocation and the driving or resistance stress should also be
taken into consideration.
(ii) The two-stage experiments (pre-straining and stress-free aging)
attributed the occurrence of rafting during stress-free aging to
the inﬂuence of anisotropic dislocation structures formed during
the pre-straining process. However, conﬁrmation of such an an-
isotropic distribution of dislocations after the pre-treatment is
absent. Furthermore, there is also a lack of con ﬁrmation that
the morphology of the γ' cuboids has not changed after the
pre-treatment.
Due to the concurrent occurrence of several metallurgical processes
and the complex interplay of the multiple inﬂuencing factors, it is difﬁ-
cult to clarify the mechanisms and contributions of different factors to
rafting. For this much has been done by analytical and computational
simulations using various tools, such as ﬁnite element modeling in the
early days [43,49–53], phase ﬁeld simulations [54–58] and crystal plas-
ticity simulations [59–63] in more recent times, and analytical solutions
based on constitutive equations [64–81]. As a result, many theories and
models have been proposed in the literature to explain the mechanism
of this phenomenon. Some theories focus on explaining the causes and
driving forces for rafting whereas some others focus on explaining the
physical mechanisms of the process of rafting. Based on the concept of
elasticity, the various models may be grouped into three basic types:
the elastic models, the plastic models and the elastic-plastic models. For
the elastic models,t h ei nﬂuence of dislocation structures formed in the
γ-channels are ignored and the cause of rafting is attributed to the me-
chanical elastic stress-strain energy anisotropy caused by the interac-
tions among the applied stress, the lattice mis ﬁt and the elastic
moduli of the γ'a n dγ phases. The plastic models consider only the ma-
trix plasticity and attribute rafting to the interface mis ﬁte n e r g y

Despite the numerous studies reported in the literature, there lacks a
clear consensus on a commonly accepted, or acceptable, model. One
main challenge is the dif ﬁculty to delineate the in ﬂuences of the
often-concurrent dislocation activities (plasticity) and elastic energy

Some inconsistencies among the conclusions derived from the dif-
ferent simulations include:
(i) Inconsistency in the type of energy as the driving force for rafting.
In the literature, different types of energy are considered as the
driving force for rafting, including the elastic potential energy
(elastic strain energy), the interfacial energy, the chemical poten-
tial energy, and various combinations of them.
(ii) Inconsistency in the mechanics model of the system used. Some
simulations use simpliﬁed plane strain two-dimensional models,
allowing a uniform displacement in the third dimension,
whereas others use 3-dimensional models. Conclusions gener-
ated from two-dimensional models are unlikely to be able to ac-
curately predict the behavior of real alloys.
(iii) Inconsistency in the methods for denoting the strain and stress
states. Different assumptions, approximations and boundary
conditions, and different constitutive equations and theories
are used to describe and de ﬁne the often simpli ﬁed stress and
strain states for the highly complex situations in the γ channels
and γ' cuboids.
To date, there is no clear consensus on which of the models may be
valid and which may be not. It is also unclear what their relative contri-
butions are if they are operative concurrently in the process. This study
attempts to present a comparative investigation to clarify some uncer-
tainties between the elastic and plastic models by using both the FEM
simulation and experimentation methods.
2. Experiment
To verify the elastic model, the 3D- ﬁnite element modeling (FEM)
method was used to analyses the stress and strain ﬁelds within a nega-
tive misﬁt Ni-based single crystal γ'/γ system. The analysis focuses on
the elastic strain energy, the interfacial energy and the chemical poten-
tial energy. To create the initial isotropic misﬁt, an artiﬁcial differential
thermal expansion approach was used. The γ and γ'p h a s e sw e r e
given different thermal expansion coefﬁcients, with αγ =1 . 6 3×1 0−5
and αγ' =1 . 2 6×1 0 −5 [43], and thermal expansion mismatch was
used as an arti ﬁcial way to create the mis ﬁt condition of an alloy. For
the superalloy used in this study, the FEM alloy sample was heated to
850 °C to produce a (thermal) mis ﬁto f −0.32%. It needs to be stated
that this temperature is only used as an arti ﬁcial way to create the
desired misﬁt, and bears no implication to the temperature for rafting.
The difference in thermal expansion between the two phases created
a uniform nominal isotropic mis ﬁto f −0.32% on all {100} γ'/γ inter-
faces, i.e., it assimilated a negative misﬁt condition. For the FEM simula-
tion, a cubic volume of 3 × 3 × 3 of {100} γ and γ' cuboid unit cells was
simulated and data from the central unit cell were extracted for analysis,
so to eliminate the effect of free surface boundary conditions. The soft-
ware used for FEM analysis was ABAQUS, the mesh size was 0.05 μm
and the element type was C3D4.
To verify the plastic model, a two-step experiment was conducted.
The ﬁrst step was a pre-straining treatment under tension or compres-
sion at relatively low temperatures to encourage the formation of aniso-
tropic dislocations structures without causing rafting. The second step
involved thermal exposure aging at a higher temperature of the pre-
treated samples under no applied stress to verify the rafting behavior
under the in ﬂuence of anisotropic dislocation structures. Further, the
discussions are given on the mechanisms of dislocation propagation
within the matrix, the driving forces for and the resistances to their
movement to elucidate the mechanism for the formation of anisotropic
dislocation structures upon staining at elevated temperatures.

Another important note to make is the reconciliation between the
Elastic Model and the Plastic Model, or more precisely between the spe-

that anisotropies of the interfacial energy, the elastic strain energies in
the γ channels and γ' phase, and the chemical potential all can serve
as driving forces for rafting, but they cannot predict the experimental
observations summarized in Table 1.I nt h e Plastic model, rafting is at-
tributed by the interface elastic strain energy anisotropy caused by the
dislocation distribution anisotropy. These appear contradictor. It
seems that in a real process of high temperature straining, the elastic
strain energy anisotropy created by the applied uniaxial stress is quickly
overpowered by the formation of anisotropic dislocation structures and
become ineffective. This is invisible in FEM analysis.
On the other hand, due to the difference in elastic moduli of the two
phases, an uniaxial tensile stress causes an increase in magnitude of
elastic strains in the horizontal channel (perpendicular to the loading
direction) and decrease in the vertical channels (parallel to the loading
direction), as presented in Table 3. This elastic strain anisotropy as pre-
dicted in theElastic Modelcaused the formation of the observed disloca-
tion structure anisotropy, which is found to be effective in causing
rafting in accordance with experimental observations. In this regard,
this study disquali ﬁes the Elastic Model as an effective mechanism for
rafting, but does not dispose the validity of elastic stain energy anisot-
ropy as a driving force for rafting. TheElastic Modelis valid in predicting
the elastic stain anisotropy, which serves as a cause for the formation of
anisotropic dislocation structures, which in turn creates a misﬁte l a s t i c
strain energy anisotropy. The study supports the view that misﬁt elastic
strain energy anisotropy is a driving force for rafting.
5. Conclusions
This study investigated the mechanism of rafting phenomenon in
Ni-based single crystal superalloys, the main ﬁndings of this work are
summarized below:
About the Elastic Model
1) The mis ﬁt is accommodated by elastic lattice stains of the γ and γ'
phases. The actual partitioning of the misﬁt into the lattice stains is
determined by the Young's moduli of the two phases. Application
of a uniaxial stress alters the partitioning of the elastic strains in
the two phases in different channels, creating elastic strain anisot-
ropy, whereas the misﬁt remains unchanged.
2) The elastic strain anisotropy implies elastic strain energy anisotropy,
which may serve as the thermodynamic driving forces for rafting.
However, the predictions of these elastic strain energy anisotropies
on raft structure contradict the experimental observations.
3) The elastic strains in the γ phase channels may also contain hydro-
static stress components, which cause chemical potential anisotropy
for solubility of certain elements. Based on the FEM analysis of this
study, this criterion predicts a rafting structure conforming to the ex-
perimental observations in negative misﬁt alloys under tension, but
contradicts to the experimental observations in negative misﬁta l l o y
under compression.
About the Plastic Model:
4) Pre-straining at a properly chosen low aging temperature is able to
cause anisotropic distributions of dislocations on the {001} γ'/γ in-
terfaces without altering the morphology of the γ' phase cuboids.
The reason for the formation of an anisotropic dislocation structure
is the elastic strain anisotropy caused by the applied bias stress.
5) The anisotropic dislocation structures at theγ'/γ interfaces are effec-
tive in causing anisotropic rafting of the γ' phase cuboids. This is at-
tributed to the effect of dislocations in relaxing the lattice mis ﬁt,
resulting in net mis ﬁt anisotropy, elastic strain anisotropy and in
turn elastic stain energy anisotropy. This study supports the plastic
model.
Reconciliation of the Elastic Model and the Plastic model:
6) The Elastic Modeldoes not consider the associated changes to the mi-
crostructure of the alloy, such as dislocation structures formed and
the evolution of the γ' cuboidal morphology. However, it clearly de-
scribes the anisotropic elastic strain anisotropy among the {100}
channels due to the difference in elastic modulus between the two
phases, which causes the formation of anisotropic dislocation struc-
tures, which in turn cause rafting.”
