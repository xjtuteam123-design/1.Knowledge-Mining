Abstract
The effect of the growth condition on the microstructure and mechanical behaviour of the NiAl–Mo eutectic alloy was investigated. Samples
of the eutectic alloy were prepared using an arc furnace under argon atmosphere and then directionally solidiﬁed. The samples directionally
solidiﬁed showed regular and aligned eutectic microstructure. Compression tests were performed at room temperature and 1173 K. Vickers
hardness was determined at several temperatures, from room temperature to 1150 K. Mechanical properties of NiAl were also determined.
Results showed that when the growth rate is gradually increased from 8 to 50 mm/h the aligned microstructure presents higher ﬁbre density,
which leads to higher strength level at room temperature, while further increase in the growth rate causes the microstructure the loose regularity.
NiAl presents a well deﬁned mechanical behaviour transition, while the NiAl–Mo eutectic alloy softens almost linearly. The highest yield
strength value of NiAl–Mo was found to be about 1200 and 300 MPa at room temperature and 1173 K, respectively.

1. Introduction
The demand for high temperature structural materials is
the major motivation for the development of materials based
on intermetallic compounds, since some of these compounds
exhibit an interesting combination of high-temperature
strength and good oxidation resistance [1,2]. Nevertheless,
the intermetallic compounds are usually brittle, presenting
low fracture toughness at room temperature. This is the case
of the NiAl intermetallic compound, which has received
considerable attention [3–8]. When compared to conven-
tional superalloys, the NiAl B2-ordered compound shows
higher melting point, lower density and better oxidation re-
sistance [9]. In addition, it shows high thermal conductivity,
which is of paramount importance when cooling efﬁciency
is necessary. However, its poor fracture resistance at room
temperature, low strength at high temperatures and creep
resistance limit its applications.

The addition of refractory metals to the intermetallic com-
pounds may result in a eutectic reaction, which enables one
to produce in situ composites directly from the melt[10].A s
a result, this procedure generates a multi-phase microstruc-
ture, which presents reduced level of interface reaction be-
tween the reinforcing phase and the matrix, as the solid
phases grow near the thermodynamic equilibrium. Besides,
in this type of composite material produced by eutectic so-
lidiﬁcation, the inherent low ductility of the intermetallic
phases can be improved, which leads to a substantial im-
provement of mechanical properties [11,12].
Based on results of the Ni–Al–X (Ta, Cr, Nb, Mo and V)
eutectic systems, Whittenberger et al.[13] suggested that the
incorporation of a refractory metal to NiAl in the form of a
eutectic array allows one to increase the room temperature
fracture toughness, as well as to improve the creep strength
of the compound at high temperatures. Among these eu-
tectic systems, one of the most promising in situ compos-
ites is formed when NiAl is alloyed with Mo. According
to Kubaschewski [14], a eutectic reaction involving NiAl
and Mo phases occurs at 1873 K and at the composition of
10 at.% Mo. The eutectic reaction of the NiAl–Mo system
was also studied by Cline and Walter [15], who suggested

2. Experimental procedure
Ingots of 10–15 g were prepared using high purity el-
ements. Several Ni–Al–Mo alloys were arc melted us-
ing a non-consumable tungsten electrode furnace with a
water-cooled copper hearth under an argon atmosphere. In
order to improve chemical homogeneity, the ingots were
ﬂipped and remelted. The loss of mass due to the sample
preparation in the arc melting was checked and is lower
than 0.1%.
Directional solidiﬁcation experiments were performed us-
ing a vertical Bridgman type equipment with induction heat-
ing and Al
2O3 crucibles. Samples 6.0 cm long and 0.7 cm in
diameter were processed by slowly shifting the crucible from
the upper hot region to the lower cold region under argon
atmosphere. The growth rate varied from 8.0 to 50.0 mm/h
under a temperature gradient at the solid/liquid interface
near 10 K/mm. The temperature of the hot region was set at
1923 K and was controlled by an optical pyrometer.
After melting, the ingots were sectioned transverse and
longitudinally and, prepared for metallographic analysis.
Preparation involved grinding, mechanical polishing and
chemical etching with a solution 5%HF–30–65%HNO
3.
The metallographic analysis comprised the study of the
microstructures, regarding the growth conditions, utilizing
optical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped
with energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) and computer-
ized image analysis.The compression tests were performed
in a universal testing machine, in air, at room temperature
and 1173 K, using a strain rate of 2 × 10
−4 s−1 with the
compression axis parallel to the growth direction of the
crystal. The samples were square based (3 mm × 3 mm) by
6 mm in length. In the high temperature tests the specimens
were deformed up to 15%, while the room temperature tests
were conducted until the rupture of the specimens.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural study
A series of NiAl–Mo alloys were analysed in order to
study the features of the eutectic growth. Evaluation of
the microstructure of as-cast samples conﬁrmed that the
eutectic composition is close to 10 at.% Mo. Despite be-
ing far from the equilibrium condition, since melting in
an arc furnace completely limits solidiﬁcation control, the
microstructure of the as-cast samples exhibited some regu-
larity, with a growth direction dependent on the direction of

as a function of the composition.
The primary phase was found to be NiAl dendrites in
the hypoeutectic alloys ( Fig. 1a ) and /H9251Mo dendrites in
the hypereutectic alloys ( Fig. 1c). In these microstructures
the light grey phase is /H9251Mo while the dark one is NiAl.
The primary phase morphologies suggest that the dendrites
grow isotropically, without any preferential growth direc-
tion. This fact indicates that the NiAl–Mo eutectic growth
is dominated by atomic diffusion, since there is no barrier

for atomic transport between liquid and solid phases. This
allows one to conclude that both /H9251Mo and NiAl phases
lead to a tendency of non-faceted solid growth, which is
expected when /H9251Mo phase is concerned. However, as an
intermetallic phase, NiAl presents high entropy of fusion
which indicates susceptibility to faceted growth. Further-
more, when the structure of off-eutectic composition alloys
was observed, eutectic growth was also noted. One more
feature observed is that the primary phase is chieﬂy impor-
tant for the formation of the eutectic structure. When /H9251Mo
is the primary phase the eutectic structure is formed from its
interface. This means that the growth of the eutectic struc-
ture depends on the cooperative eutectic growth conditions,
i.e., the eutectic growth will happen when the melt near
the solid/liquid interface reaches both eutectic temperature
and eutectic composition. In opposition, when NiAl is the
primary phase, it will ﬁrst be surrounded by a layer of /H9251Mo
(Fig. 1a) and afterwards the eutectic structure will grow over
it. The results show that the/H9251Mo phase presents higher melt-
ing point and is the ﬁrst one to form nuclei. Consequently
it is the phase of preferential growth. In order to deter-
mine the transformation temperatures, a sample of eutectic
composition was submitted to differential thermal analysis,
using heating and cooling rates of 10 K/min. DTA results
conﬁrmed no peaks of primary phase formation, but only
one well-deﬁned peak, showing congruent melting, which
is a typical feature of the eutectic transformation. The onset
temperature of this eutectic transformation was found to be
1868 K, which is close to the results of Kubaschewski [14].
The observation of the directionally solidiﬁed (DS’ed)
samples resulted in interesting information about the devel-
opment of the eutectic microstructure, its morphology and
structure spacing. As shown in Fig. 2, the microstructure of
DS’ed NiAl–Mo eutectic samples consists of multiple eu-
tectic grains, with growth directions controlled by the heat
ﬂux. Each grain contains a well-aligned and equally spaced
two-phase eutectic structure.
Fig. 2. SEM micrograph showing the cross-section of a directionally
solidiﬁed sample at a growth rate of 15 mm/h.
An important feature of the eutectic microstructure is ob-
served at the ingot extremities, that are characterised by very
low regularity. At the extremities the cooperative growth did
not entirely occur, which results in a degenerated eutectic
microstructure, where the primary phases are poorly aligned
with the heat ﬂux direction, as seen in Fig. 3. On the other
hand, microstructure is rather regular in the central region,
showing no formation of primaries. This phenomenon had
been previously observed and depends on growth rate and
composition variations. Growth rate variation occurs at the
extremities of a DS’ed ingot and may result in the forma-
tion of a structure out of the cooperative growth zone. In a
Bridgman crystal growth equipment, as a result of the heat
transfer transient, the growth rate may differ from the sam-
ple translation velocity, particularly when considering a re-
gion close to the ingot extremities. The growth rate is high
at the ﬁrst stages of the process, it decreases as solidiﬁcation
proceeds, and increases again as the solidiﬁcation process
comes close to the end. In addition, composition may play
an important role in the formation of the eutectic microstruc-
ture. In the begin of the growth process, the sample may
present an off-eutectic composition, which causes primary
phase formation. As the growth process continues, solute
segregation to the liquid may occur and the melt can reach
the eutectic composition. As a result the eutectic alloy will
not show the expected microstructure at the extremity but
primary phase morphology with eutectic structure. As the
growth proceeds, the microstructure turns out to be regular.
As the growth rate is increased, the ﬁbre spacing ( λ)i s
reduced. Higher growth rates lead to a decrease of solute dif-
fusion in front of the solid/liquid interface, which produces
the spacing decrease. This result agrees with the classical
theory of eutectic growth presented by Jackson and Hunt
[16]. At low growth rates, one may observe that the eutectic
structure is very regular. Fig. 4a shows the microstructure
(longitudinal view) of a sample grown at V = 8.0 mm/h.
The ﬁbres are regularly aligned with the heat ﬂux direction
and no evidence of primary phase is seen.
Increasing the growth rate causes the reduction of the ﬁ-
bre spacing, as can be seen in Fig. 4b. At a growth rate of
35 mm/h, the eutectic microstructure is still very regular, as
presented by Fig. 4c . Further increase of the growth rate
produced a eutectic microstructure where the ﬁbres are not
regularly aligned and primary phase development was ﬁrst
observed. At a growth rate of 100 mm/h, the eutectic mi-
crostructure is replaced by a combination of eutectic colonies
and primary phase dendrites (Fig. 4d). At low growth rates,
the eutectic growth condition is near the equilibrium, which
allows perfect solute cross-diffusion near the solid/liquid in-
terface and the solidiﬁcation occurs within the eutectic co-
operative growth zone. Fig. 5 presents the microstructure
of an ingot which was submitted to a sudden change in
the growth rate. At ﬁrst, the growth rate was approximately
8.0 mm/h, then it was increased to more than 100 mm/h.
When the growth rate is low the resulting microstructure
consists of the NiAl matrix with Mo well-aligned ﬁbres,

4. Conclusions
The microstructures of several NiAl–Mo alloys have been
studied in order to determine the eutectic composition of
the system, which was determined to be NiAl–10Mo. After-
wards, the microstructure, compressive properties at elevated
and room temperature and hardness have been determined
for the eutectic alloy after being directionally solidiﬁed at
rates ranging from 8 to 50 mm/h. The higher the growth
rate, the ﬁner the ﬁbrous microstructure and higher the room
temperature compressive strength. At high temperatures, on
the other hand, the strengthening effect of the aligned mi-
crostructure is almost completely lost. On mechanical be-
haviour transition, while NiAl presents a sharp BDTT, the
aligned structure of NiAl–Mo softens almost linearly.
