Abstract
Carbon and titanium were added in two-phase (B2 +L12) Ni–Al–Fe alloys for carbides precipitation, and the additions of 0.2
wt.% C and 3 at.% Ti have been found to produce the most adequate high temperature structural material. Carbon doped alloys
showed more reﬁned microstructure than carbon free alloys. The carbides, which were formed in the as-cast microstructure
showing equiaxed dendrites, appeared to play a role in suppressing grain growth and coarsening of the second phases. The
NAF29-10 alloy, including no carbides, showed the lamellar type microstructure, while the NAF29-10-C alloy, including carbides,
showed a much more reﬁned mesh type microstructure. When the carbon free alloys were quenched into water, cracks occurred
at the grain boundaries probably due to martensitic transformation, however, these cracks were not observed in the carbon doped
alloys. In order to suppress quenching cracks in the carbon free alloys, the solutionizing treatment time was reduced. However,
cracks still formed as a severe intergranular mode at an early stage of elastic deformation. The reﬁned microstructure of carbon
doped alloys could compensate for elongation loss due to solution hardening and precipitation hardening. As a result, the carbon
doped alloys showed good room temperature ductility and also showed much higher yield strength than other alloys including no
carbides over the entire temperature range. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Intermetallic compounds have been the focus of con-
siderable research for potential structural materials in
the aerospace industry. In particular, nickel aluminides,
/p103/p37-Ni
3Al(L12 structure) and /p98-NiAl(B2 structure) have
excited a good deal of interest for their possibility of
application [1–4]. The /p103/p37-Ni
3Al alloy is attractive due to
the anomalous temperature dependence of ﬂow that
increases with increasing temperature [1,2] and en-
hancement of room temperature ductility by boron
addition [3,4]. However, the density of Ni
3Al is rela-
tively high as well as Ni-base superalloy, and problems,
such as creep strength and oxidation resistance, remain
to be solved. The NiAl alloy, which is another impor-
tant intermetallic compound in the Ni–Al system, has
attractive properties such as low density, high melting
temperature, good oxidation resistance, and high ther-
mal conductivity for high temperature materials. How-
ever, the unalloyed NiAl has two principal limitations
of poor ductility at room temperature and poor
strength at high temperature.
Recently, many researchers have found that mi-
crostructural modiﬁcations of /p98-NiAl including the
ductile /p103/p37phase result in remarkable enhancements of
hot workability and room temperature ductility [5–10].
These results, along with the fact that the /p98phase is
capable of undergoing thermoelastic transformation
from /p98to /p98/p37(L1
0 structure) in certain composition
ranges [11], open up a wider range of possibilities for
microstructural control and practical application.
In this study, Fe, which has been known as an
element improving room temperature ductility of the
/p98+ /p103/p37two-phase alloy [6,12,13], was added, and for
carbide precipitation carbon was added with the expec-
tation of better high temperature strength by effects of

2. Experimental details
The alloys used in this study were prepared from
starting materials of 99.97 wt.% Ni, 99.99 wt.% Al,
99.97 wt.% Fe, 99.99 wt.% Ti and 99.5 wt.% TiC
powder. Button ingots, approximately 30 g, were made
by vacuum arc melting under an argon atmosphere and
remelted more than ﬁve times to promote homogeneity.
The alloy compositions tested in this study are reported
in Table 1. Solutionizing treatments for 14, 6, and 1 h
were carried out at 1250 °C in an argon atmosphere,
and then the specimens were water quenched from this
temperature. The water quenched alloys were aged at
800 °C for 24 h for precipitation of Ni
3Al. Microstruc-
ture observations were performed using an optical mi-
croscope and a scanning electron microscope, etched in
the reagent consisting of 60 ml HCl, 15 ml CH
3COOH,
15 ml HNO 3 a n d1 5m lH2O. X-Ray diffraction, using
Cu–K/p97radiation, was performed to identify the phases
present, and the chemical compositions of each phase
were determined by WDS. Phase transformation behav-
iors in aging treatment were determined by DTA with a
10 °C/min heating rate in the range from room temper-
ature to 550 °C. Tensile tests were conducted using an
universal testing machine at a strain rate of 1 ×10
−4
s−1 in the temperature range of RT –800 °C. Tensile
specimens were prepared as plates with dimensions of 5
mm in gauge length and 2 ×1m m
2 in cross-section by
a electron discharge machine. Before testing, the speci-
mens were mechanically polished to the surface ﬁnish of
0.3 /p109m and then electro-polished in the 10% perchloric
acid–90% methanol bath at −40 °C. In high tempera-
ture tensile tests, if the elongation was over 100%, the
testing was stopped.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Heat-treatments using martensitic transformation
of the /p105phase
The heat treatments in this study are based on the
method applied by Russel [9], Khadkikar [8], Lee [10],
Ishida [18], etc. They carried out solutionizing treat-
ment at 1200 –1300 °C for formation of the /p98phase,
and then the samples were quenched to transform into
the /p98/p37-martensite. The /p98/p37-martensite alloys were aged at
750–850 °C, which is the region to form /p98+ /p103/p37two
phases in the phase diagram, to precipitate Ni
3Al along
martensite plates. As a result of these heat treatments, a
ﬁne two-phase lamellar structure showing high strength
and toughness could be obtained.
Fig. 1 shows the isothermal phase diagrams in Ni –
25Al–10Fe and Ni –29Al–10Fe alloys where 10 at.%
Fe was added for room temperature ductility. In the
case of the Ni –29Al–10Fe alloy, a two-phase structure
was formed through the same process as explained
above. The /p98-rich two-phase structure would be formed
since the fraction of /p98is more than that of /p103/p37as
conﬁrmed in the phase diagram at 800 °C. In the case
of Ni –25Al–10Fe alloy, when the /p98+ /p103/p37structure at
1250 °C was quenched the /p103phase remained as it was,
but the /p98phase was transformed to the /p98/p37-martensite,
which resulted in the ( /p98/p37-martensite + /p103) structure at
room temperature. When the quenched alloys were
aged at 800 °C, the /p103/p37phase was precipitated along
martensite plates and the /p103phase was transformed to
the /p103/p37phase. Consequently, Fig. 1 con ﬁrms that the
(lamellar /p98+ /p103/p37) structure is formed in Ni –25Al–10Fe
alloy, where the /p103/p37-rich two-phase structure is obtained
since the fraction of /p103/p37is more than that of /p98.

3.3. Formation of quenching cracks
Table 3(a) shows the results of tensile tests for each
alloy after heat treatments of 1250 °C/14 h /W.Q. +
800 °C/24 h /F.C. NAF25-10-C and NAF29-10-C al-
loys, including carbides, showed an elongation of more
than 1% at room temperature, while carbon free alloys
of NAF25-10 and NAF29-10 were fractured before
yielding. Moreover, carbon free alloys did not show
any plastic deformation and were fractured before
yielding at 800 °C. It can be understood that this
brittleness was not caused by intrinsic properties of the
alloys but by extrinsic factors for the reason that how
brittle the alloys are, they can be soft at elevated
temperature such as 800 °C. Many cracks were found
to occur along their grain boundaries in the carbon free
alloys. These cracks occurred when the carbon free
alloys were quenched due to martensitic transforma-
tion, and they would make the tensile specimens frac-
ture under tensile stress.
Fig. 5 shows scanning electron micrographs on frac-
ture surfaces of NAF29-10 and NAF29-10-C tensile
specimens. Severe intergranular fractures, due to crack
propagation, are observed in NAF29-10 alloy. How-
ever, transgranular dimple fractures are observed in
NAF29-10-C alloy exhibiting 3% tensile elongation.
The quenching cracks were not observed in carbide
precipitated alloys of NAF29-10-C and NAF25-10-C
alloys, and densely distributed carbides are expected to
they are thought to be TiC. The TiC carbide is known
to be thermally stable and the shape and number of
carbides would not be changed, particularly during heat
treatments.
