Abstract 
Microstructure of NiTiHf shape memory alloys can be engineered to have high strength and 
operate at high stress levels for a large temperatu re window. Nanoprecipitation is well-known 
method to improve the strength of materials but it can be employed to NiTiHf alloys to 
substantially alter their phase transformation char acteristics (martensite morphology, 
transformation strain, hysteresis and stress). The martensitic transformation of Ni-rich 
Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 was severely suppressed in the solution treated c ondition (900 °C-3h/ water 
quench) and after aging at low temperatures, while the transformation temperatures were greater 
than 100 °C after 650 °C-3h aging. Generation of na nosize precipitates (~20 nm in size) after 
three hours aging at 450 ºC and 550 ºC improved the strength of the material, resulting in a near 
perfect dimensional stability during isobaric thermal cycling at stress levels of greater than 1500 
MPa, with work output of 20–30 J cm – 3. Superelastic behavior with 4% recoverable strain was 
demonstrated at low temperatures (–20 to 40 °C) aft er aging at 450 °C-3h and at elevated 
temperatures (120 to160 °C) after aging at 550 °C-3 h, with stresses reaching 2 GPa without the 
onset of plastic deformation. A clear relationship between thermal treatments, microstructure, 
mechanical and shape memory properties will be shown. 
 
Introduction 
High temperature shape memory alloys (HTSMAs) are b ecoming a suitable candidate for a wide 
range of multifunctional applications in the aerosp ace, energy, and automotive industries due to 
the ability to exhibit superelasticity at high temp eratures and shape memory effect under high 
stress levels [1-5]. Among the NiTi-based HTSMAs, N iTiHf alloys have relatively lower cost 
compared to NiTi(Pd, Pt, Au) alloys making them esp ecially promising for large scale 
components. Similar to other NiTi-based alloys, tra nsformation temperatures (TTs) of NiTiHf 
alloys are highly composition dependent, with TTs i ncreasing significantly as the Hf 
concentration is raised above 10 at. % [6, 7]. 
It is also known that changes in the stoichiometry of NiTi significantly affect the material 
properties where Ni-rich NiTi alloys have a higher yield strength and lower TTs in compared 
with Ti-rich NiTi alloys [8, 9]. Furthermore, forma tion of Ni 
4Ti 3 precipitates, after suitable heat 
treatments, can significantly alter the TTs, shape memory and mechanical properties of Ni-rich 
NiTi alloys [10, 11]. In particular, formation of r elatively fine, coherent Ni 4Ti 3 precipitates

Similarly, Meng et al. [13] reported that the TTs a nd strength of Ni-rich NiTiHf can also be 
improved by forming Ni-rich precipitates after appr opriate heat treatments. Lately, several 
studies have illustrated that Ni-rich NiTiHf can be precipitation strengthened, resulting in

during thermal cycling under 500 MPa with maximum w ork output in the range of 15–20 J cm – 3 
were observed [3, 5, 18]. In these alloys, generati on of nanometer sized (~20 nm) precipitates 
also altered the martensite morphology, with the fi ne precipitates easily absorbed by large 
martensite variants during transformation while the growth of martensite plates was limited to 
the spaces between particles once the precipitates grew beyond a certain size. 
Detailed structural and microstructural analysis on Ni-rich NiTiHf alloys [4, 20, 21] confirmed 
that the precipitates have a face-centered orthorho mbic structure and are generally referred to as 
the “H-phase”. The H-phase precipitate is richer in Hf, leaner in Ti, and slightly richer in Ni 
compared to the nominal composition of the alloy [2 0, 21]. As the Ni content increases, the 
driving force for precipitation increases resulting in larger precipitates during furnace cooling, 
e.g., 15–35 nm to 200–750 nm as Ni content increase s from 50.7 at.% to 52 at.% in furnace 
cooled Ni-rich NiTi-20Hf alloys [4, 21]. The martensite twinning structures in Ni-rich NiTiHf is 
similar to those reported in (Ti+Hf)-rich NiTiHf al loys where the most frequently observed 
twinning modes are mixture of (011) B19’ type I and (001) B19’ compound twins [2, 21, 22]. 
However, the size of precipitates affect the distri bution of particular twin systems, with (011) B19’ 
type I twins predominant in samples with fine preci pitates, while (001) B19’ compound and 
(011) B19’ type I twins were evenly distributed in materials with large precipitates where 
martensite formation is constrained between particles [21]. 
There are even limited studies on the effects of qu aternary additions on shape memory properties 
of Ni-rich NiTiHf alloys. The addition of Cu and Nb to NiTiHf improved the ductility but 
decreased the transformation strain and temperature s [23-25]. In contrast, the addition of Pd 
seemed to further increase the strength of NiTiHf, resulting in near perfect dimensional stability 
with more than 3% actuation strain at 1000 MPa [26, 27]. Moreover, NiTiHfPd alloys achieved 
high damping capacity and excellent work output, in the range of 30–35 J cm -3, due to the high 
stresses that could be accommodated with little or no plastic deformation. 
With the exception of Coughlin et al. [28], most st udies on Ni-rich NiTiHf polycrystalline alloys 
have focused on the shape memory and material prope rties of slightly Ni-rich Ni 50.3 Ti 29.7 Hf 20 
alloys. The previous studies [4, 28] revealed that Ni-rich NiTiHf ally with Ni content higher than 
51 at. % are a suitable candidate for high strength applications. Thus, the present study 
investigates the effects of aging on the precipitat e characteristics and corresponding shape 
memory behavior of a Ni-rich Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 alloy. It has been reported that a furnace cooled 
Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 alloy contained large H-phase precipitates, on the order of 75–250 nm [4, 21]. In 
order to investigate the effects of thermal treatme nts on Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20, the material was

Experimental Procedures 
The Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 (at %) alloy was arc melted as a 100g button using high purity elemental 
constituents. The button was subsequently homogenized at 1000 °C for 48 h followed by furnace 
cooling. Samples were then solutionized at 900 ºC-3 h and water quenched. Finally, selected 
samples were aged for a duration of three hours at temperatures ranging from 400 to 750 °C 
followed by a water quench. 
 
Compression samples (4 × 4 × 8 mm 
3) were cut using an electro discharge machine. A Pe rkin 
Elmer Pyris 1 differential scanning calorimeter (DS C) was used to determine TTs at a 
heating/cooling rate of 10 °C/min. Microstructural analysis of select samples was conducted by 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a JEOL 2010F instrument operated at 200 kV. 
The thermo-mechanical experiments were conducted us ing an MTS Landmark servohydraulic 
test frame with custom compression grips. The level of applied force was measured by a 100 kN 
capable load cell, and the axial strain was measure d by an MTS high temperature extensometer 
with a total gauge length of 12mm. Heating of the s ample was achieved by conduction through 
the compression plates at a rate of 10 ºC/min and c ooling was achieved by flowing liquid 
nitrogen through copper coils wrapped around the gr ips to cool the system at a rate of 5 °C/min 
using a PID driven Omega temperature controller.

Discussion 
Transformation characteristics 
The martensitic transformation in Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 , as with most NiTi-based alloys, is dependent 
on thermal conditions and resulting microstructure. At low aging temperatures (400 – 500 °C), a 
very high density of fine H-phase precipitates with short interparticle distances were 
homogenously distributed throughout the structure a nd strongly suppressed the nucleation of 
martensite. Even moderate stresses (up to 500 MPa) were not sufficient to initiate the nucleation 
of preferred martensite variants and therefore did not result in shape memory behavior in 
material aged 450 °C-3h. At stresses greater than 5 00 MPa, the volume fraction of favorably 
oriented martensite variants increased gradually wi th stress, but still the transformation strain 
was low. On the other hand, TTs were shifted to hig her temperatures as H-phase precipitates 
became larger in size. Aging at 650 °C-3h resulted in large precipitates constraining martensite 
formation to the spaces between the precipitates. 
The strength of a material also strongly depends on the precipitate size, volume fraction, and 
inter-precipitate spacing. Fine coherent precipitat es with short interparticle distances formed in 
the alloy after aging at low temperatures, which ac t as potent obstacles against dislocation 
motion and significantly improve the strength of th e alloy. The precipitate size and spacing 
increase after aging at high temperatures (> 550 °C ) and the strengthening effect diminishes as it 
becomes easier for dislocations to bypass or loop a round the obstacles. The high strength levels 
observed in this alloy may also be attributed to changes in twinning morphology. 
Recoverable strains were determined as the differen ce between the total and irrecoverable 
strains, which were extracted from the load-biased strain-temperature data shown in Figure 3. 
Both the recoverable and irrecoverable strains as a function of bias-stress are summarized in 
Figure 5a. The recoverable strains increased with s tress and reached maximum values of 1.3% 
and 1.8% at 1500 MPa for the 450 °C-3h and 550 °C-3 h aged conditions, respectively. In

It is known that the transformation strain depends on the size and volume fraction of the 
precipitates in NiTiHf alloys [3, 22]. For Ni 
51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 , very fine precipitates with small 
interparticle spacing are formed after low temperat ure aging as shown by the TEM images in 
Figure 2. It should be noted that when the precipit ate size and interparticle distance is small, it is 
possible for the twin boundaries to bypass the particles resulting in large martensite plates, which 
have incorporated a large number of precipitates [2 , 21, 22, 32]. Under this type of 
microstructure, the recoverable strain increased as the aging temperature increased from 450 to 
550 °C, which can be attributed to a decrease in vo lume fraction of non-transformable 
precipitates with aging temperature, which was prev iously observed in NiTi alloys [12, 33, 34]. 
In addition, the lower transformation strain shown by the material aged at 450 °C with respect to 
550 °C can be due to presence of large density of p articles with very low interparticle distances 
in 450 °C aged condition which make the nucleation and growth of martensite difficult. As a 
result, martensitic transformation occurs under the assistance of high external stress (Fig. 3a) 
and, probably, only a fraction of martensite plates are able to grow in the favorable orientation 
which reducing the transformation strain.

significantly greater inter-precipitate distances. Under these conditions twins are unable to

Martensitic transformation behavior 
Transformation temperatures as a function of stress were determined from the isobaric thermal 
cycling responses shown in Figure 3 using the tange nt line method and were used to construct 
stress-temperature phase diagrams of the aged Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 samples as illustrated in Figure 6. 
The TTs were shifted to higher temperatures with in creasing stress and higher aging 
temperatures. It is significant to note that the A s was lower than the M s for all the aged samples, 
which can be attributed to the high elastic strain stored during the martensitic transformation [35-
37]. Also, the difference between M s and M f was greater after 450 °C-3h aging compared to the 
650 °C-3h aged condition. This indicates that a lar ger undercooling is needed to complete the 
forward transformation after 450 °C-3h aging and th e low strain-temperature slope during the 
forward transformation indicates that high elastic energy storage occurs during austenite to 
martensite transformation. During heating, stored e lastic energy helps the back transformation

“Ultra strength” behavior has been observed in nano materials including nanowires, nanopillars, 
nanolayers, and related materials [41]. Ultra stre ngth phenomena in nanoscale materials can be 
characterized by the overall stress levels of a sam ple reaching a significant fraction of its ideal 
strength, defined as the highest stress a perfect c rystal can sustain without any plastic 
deformation [41, 42]. The ideal strength of alloys is generally estimated as one tenth of their 
elastic modulus (E) [42-44]. Alternatively, alloys can be considered to have ultra-strength if their 
strength is in the GPa levels [44]. The deformation mechanisms of ultra-strength materials are 
related to the nucleation and movement of dislocati ons, dislocation-interface interactions, and 
deformation twinning [41, 44]. In the present study , the Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 alloy demonstrated ultra-
strength behavior by exhibiting near perfect dimens ional stability during thermal cycling at 
stresses above 1500 MPa. Moreover, full superelasti c recovery with about 4% total strain at low 
and high temperatures was observed at stress levels of about 2 GPa in samples aged for three 
hours at 450 °C and 550 °C.

Conclusions 
The present study focused on the effects of thermal treatments on the microstructure and shape 
memory properties of a high-strength Ni-rich Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 alloy. Important findings can be 
summarized as follows: 
1. Thermal treatments can be used to tailor the precip itate characteristics, microstructure, 
and shape memory behaviors of a Ni-rich Ni 51.2 Ti 28.8 Hf 20 alloy. Homogeneous 
distribution of fine H-phase precipitates (< 15 nm) with small interparticle distance was 
observed after aging at 450 ºC-3h, which improved t he strength of the matrix but 
suppressed the martensitic transformation. These na nosize precipitates and ones formed 
during aging at 550 °C-3h were absorbed by martensi te plates during propagation. 
However aging at 650 °C-3h, resulted in larger precipitates, ~40–70 nm, which restricted 
nucleation and propagation of martensite variants to spaces between the precipitates. 
 
2. Near perfect dimensional stability with negligible irrecoverable strain and low thermal 
hysteresis (< 30 °C) was demonstrated during therma l cycling at compressive stresses as 
high as 1700 MPa after aging at 450 °C-3h. Additionally, aging for three hours at 550 °C 
resulted in almost full recoverable strain at 1500 MPa with thermal hysteresis in the 
range of 30–40 °C. The M s increased above 100 °C, but poor dimensional stabi lity with 
noticeable irrecoverable strain and high thermal hysteresis was observed in samples aged 
at 650 °C-3h 
 
3. 
 The CC slope increased from 11.1 to 16.3 MPa/ °C w hen the aging temperature was 
raised from 450 to 650 °C for a constant aging time of three hours. 
 
4. Perfect superelastic behavior with 4% fully recover able strain was achieved at 
temperature ranges of –20 to 40 °C and 120 to160 °C for material aged at 450 °C-3h and 
550 °C-3h, respectively. 
 
5. In almost all aged conditions, A s was lower than M s during isobaric thermal cycling, 
resulting in high stored elastic strain during the forward transformation, which benefits 
the reverse transformation. 
 
6. A high work output, in the range of 25-30 J cm –3 was achieved, primarily due to the 
high stress levels that could be accommodated in th e aged material without plastic 
deformation.
