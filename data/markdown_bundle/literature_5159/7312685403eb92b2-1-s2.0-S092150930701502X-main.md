Materials Science and Engineering A 485 (2008) 92–98
Effect of carbon on the microstructures and mechanical
properties of as cast Nb-base alloy
Rengen Ding, Ian P. Jones∗, Huisheng Jiao
Department of Metallurgy and Materials, University of Birmingham, B15 2TT, UK
Received 17 May 2007; received in revised form 20 July 2007; accepted 24 July 2007
Abstract
Microstructure and mechanical properties of Nb35Ti6Al5Cr8V (at.%) containing different C contents (0, 1, 11.6 at.%) were investigated. The
microstructures were characterized using optical microscopy and scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM and TEM, respectively).
The alloy without C appeared single phase: Nb solid solution (Nbss). The carbide in 1% C alloy is fcc MC. The addition of 11.6% C resulted in
the formation of two types of particles: primary carbide ((Ti,Nb)
2C) and secondary carbide ((Ti,Nb)C). Compression testing was carried out at
room temperature, 1073, 1273 and 1473 K. The alloys showed good compression ductility at room temperature. The strengths at room and high
temperature increase with increasing C content, but the room temperature ductility decreases.

1. Introduction
In recent years, advanced structural materials for applications
at temperatures above the maximum operating temperature of
conventional high temperature Ni-base superalloys [1–5] have
been sought. Additionally, from the viewpoint of environmental
problems, an increase in the operating temperature of electric
power generators or jet turbines can lead to higher energy efﬁ-
ciency, thereby reducing the emission of harmful gases such
as CO
2 and NOx [3]. Under such situations, notwithstanding
the sensitivity of niobium alloys to oxygen, Nb-based refractory
alloys with a high melting point and a relatively low density have
been suggested as candidate materials for use at ultra-high tem-
perature above the maximum operating temperature of Ni-base
superalloys. However, the strength of Nb decreases substantially
at temperatures above 1200 K. In general, the strength of Nb at
elevated temperature is improved by various approaches, includ-
ing solid solution strengthening with Mo, Hf, Ta and W [6–8],
and dispersion or composite strengthening with intermetallic
compounds such as Nb
3Al and Nb5Si [9,10], as well as with a
carbide [11–14]. One of the major problems in the development

expence of losing some room temperature ductility.
This study, therefore, evaluates the mechanical properties of
Nb–Ti–Al alloys with different C contents at room tempera-
ture and high temperature. The microstructures have also been
investigated in detail to understand the effect of C.

3.3. Deformation microstructure
The alloys exhibited good room temperature compression
ductility. The 10C alloy, however, does not show as good ductil-
ity as the other alloys. SEM and TEM were used to examine the
microstructures of the room temperature compression samples.
Fig. 8 shows an SEM micrograph from a compression sample
of the 10C alloy, indicating that cracks initiated within the large
carbides thus leading to a reduction in the ductility of the 10C
alloy; no clear or long cracks were observed at the interface
between the carbides and the matrix. A high density of disloca-
tions can still be seen in the Nbss matrix (Fig. 9), which clearly
endured large-scale deformation. It is difﬁcult to quantify the
change in dislocation density between the deformed and unde-
formed alloys since in the undeformed alloys containing C a high
density of dislocations is already present, but undoubtedly the
density does rise. Dislocation structures in the deformed sam-
ples are being undertaken in order to understand the deformation
mechanisms.

deformed samples. An image of a needle-like carbide and its
SADP (left inset) is shown inFig. 10. Some defects are observed
inside the carbide. Some weak streaks in the diffraction pattern
suggest that the defects are planar. These defects are actually a
high density of microtwins. A higher resolution image from the

4. Discussion
The results obtained from the above study show that the
microstructure and mechanical behaviour of the alloys are
inﬂuenced by the addition of C. Microstructural observation
indicated that the addition of 1 at.% C resulted in the precipita-
tion of rod-like carbides and that the addition of 11.6 at.% C led
to the formation of primary carbide and secondary carbide. This
is because the solubility limit of carbon in Nb is about 0.55 at.%
at 1873–2453 K with almost no solubility at room temperature
[17]. Therefore, the small rod-like carbides in the 1C alloy and
the secondary carbides in the 10C alloy are formed in Nbss

during cooling after solidiﬁcation. The carbide reﬁnes the grain
size in the as cast samples. The reﬁnement by carbide is not sig-
niﬁcant for the low addition of C (1 at.%) because the carbide
precipitates from solid solution in the 1C alloy, and thus did
not provide a nucleation site for the grains. However, 11.6 at.%
carbon addition dramatically reﬁnes the grain size. 11.6 at.% is
larger than the eutectic composition of (Nb,Ti)-C. Primary car-
bide thus nucleates and provides a nucleation site for the Nb
grains.
It should be noted that two different carbides were observed
in the 10C alloy. This could not be explained by the simple Nb-
C binary phase diagram (Fig. 1) [18]. This difference could be
because the addition of other elements (Ti, Al, V , Cr), especially
35 at.%Ti, modiﬁes the phase diagram. Ti-C binary phase dia-
gram [18] shows the existence of two different carbides (Ti
2C
and TiC). Chen et al. [19] and Li et al. [20] determined that
the ordered Ti 2C formed in the Ti–V–Cr alloy system. The

The results show that the compressive yield strength of this
Nb–Ti–Al alloy was increased signiﬁcantly by C addition. The
addition of 1% C results in an improvement of 130 MPa in
yield strength at room temperature, and 250 MPa for 11.6% C.
The carbide formed has the following effects on the strength
of the alloy; ﬁrstly, the strength of a two-phase alloy generally
depends on the volume fraction, shape and distribution of the two
phases, and on the strength of each phase. Such reinforcement
strengthening is important for the 10C alloy because 11.6% C
addition produced about 20% volume fraction of carbide, but is
not important for the 1C alloy due to the small volume fraction of
the carbide (∼2%); secondly, carbide can act as hard particles
which might lead to an increase in dislocation density. How-
ever, because the primary carbides are so large this effect may
not be important for the 10C alloy; thirdly, the carbides reﬁne
the grain size which will lead to an increase in yield stress via
the Hall–Petch relationship, although the effect is small here;
fourthly, the carbides are nearly continuously precipitated at
grain boundaries (Fig. 3), which could restrain boundary sliding
and thus increase creep strength.
Despite the apparent advantages of the addition of carbon
there are some detrimental factors caused by the formation of
carbide which cannot be neglected. The carbide is a hard and
brittle phase, and is not capable of enduring a large deforma-
tion at room temperature. This results in the initiation of cracks
within the carbide ( Fig. 8) thus leading to a reduction in the
ductility (Fig. 7).
Here, it is worthy of mention that the compositions of the
carbides may inﬂuence their microplasticity. For example, some
defects such as dislocations and stacking faults were observed
in primary carbides (Nb/Ti: ∼1) in as-cast NbTiC alloys [16]
but not in the present alloys (Ti-rich carbide); it was easier
to ﬁnd secondary carbides (Nb/Ti: ∼1) with signiﬁcant twin
deformation in compressed NbTiC samples [16] compared to
secondary carbides (Ti-rich) in the present study. Ta addition
improved the plastic deformation of WC in WC–Co compos-
ite [22]. A detailed investigation of the inﬂuence of carbides on
microplasticity would clearly be of interest; this would include
deformation of and near the carbides.
5. Conclusions
Based on an investigation of the microstructure and mechan-
ical properties of Nb-base alloys with various C contents, the
following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) The alloy without C appeared single phase: Nb solid solu-
tion (Nbss). The carbide in the 1% C alloy is fcc MC. The
addition of 11.6% C resulted in the formation of primary car-
bide ((Ti,Nb)
2C) and secondary carbide ((Ti,Nb)C). With
increasing C content in the alloys, the volume fraction of
carbide increases.
(2) The room temperature and high temperature strengths
increase with increasing C content, whereas the addition
of 11.6% C decreases the room temperature ductility of
the alloy due to the breaking of primary carbide during
compression.
(3) Due to the difference in thermal expansion coefﬁcient
between carbide and Nbss, a high density of dislocations
was introduced into the Nbss during casting. After defor-
mation, an even higher density of dislocations was observed
in the Nbss with a high density of microtwins in the small
carbides and with cracks in the large (primary) carbides.
