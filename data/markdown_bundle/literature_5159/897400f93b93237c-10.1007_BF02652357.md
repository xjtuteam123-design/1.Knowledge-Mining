High-Temperature Behavior of Precious 
Metal Base Composites 
I.M. WOLFF and G. SAUTHOFF 
The high thermodynamic stability and exceptional mechanical properties of intermetallic compounds 
based on platinum group metals (PGMs) make them potentially exploitable as structural materials 
for elevated temperature service. In this study, the development of high-temperature strength was 
evaluated for a selection of eutectic composites based on Ru-A1, Ir-A1, Ru-Nb, Ir-Nb, and Ru-A1-Ni 
using compression tests. All the alloys tested were characterized by high strength at extreme tem- 
peratures, none more so than an Ir84Nb16 alloy which had a proof stress in excess of 600 MPa at 
1300 ~ In addition, several alloys showed significant compressional ductility at room temperature. 
Of particular interest is the synergy of soft constituent phases in evolving strengths greater than that 
anticipated by simple rules of mixture. The potential of secondary phases to advance both strength 
and room-temperature ductility makes these composites worthy of further investigation. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
A. Ductile-Phase Strengthening and Toughening 
STRATEGIES to ductilize or strengthen intermetallics 
usually involve alloying additions or an adjustment in com- 
position to take advantage of secondary phases. This often 
leads to a trade-off in the inherent density, melting point, 
and high-temperature strength advantages of the base alloy. 
Eutectic systems therefore enjoy some potential as alloys 
for sustained high-temperature use in that they are ther- 
modynamically stable, can be engineered to exhibit a high 
interface density, have inherent interphase compatibility (as 
opposed to metal matrix composites), and are often modi- 
fiable by alloying (for example, small additions of Mo, W, 
or V to NiA1-Cr change the eutectic morphology from 
round rods to faceted rods and platest~) or solidification 
processing to give directional structures.t2-6] Furthermore, 
eutectic structures can take advantage of strengthening or 
ductilizing phases intrinsic to the system and are particu- 
larly suitable vehicles for ductile phase toughening con- 
cerns. 
Ductile secondary phases have been shown to be effec- 
tive in enhancing toughness in a number of intermetallic 
systems by acting as obstacles to growing cracks tT-~~ or 
acting as compliant interface layers, t',~21 Interestingly 
enough, there is also evidence to support the idea that such 
a ductile phase can contribute to strengthening at the phase 
boundaries. This extends from the observation that many 
two-phase systems exhibit strengths in excess of that pre- 
dicted by a simple rule of mixtures. In coarse, two-phase 
systems, this has come to be associated in the first place 
with the constraint experienced by coexisting phases. In 
essence, such phases exhibit greater strengths and a modi- 
fied deformation response (relative to when they exist in 
isolation) owing to the constraint imposed on deformation 
by the surrounding hard phase, effectively increasing their 
yield strength, t131 Furthermore, soft particles embedded in a 
brittle intermetallic matrix can clearly strengthen the alloy 
in the same way as hard particles in a conventional disor- 
dered alloy, i.e., by acting as discontinuities in the path of 
dislocations (for example, Jung and Sauthofltt141). However, 
anomalous strengthening effects have also been associated 
with a critical interphase dimension. Kl6wer and Sauth- 
of~ 3,4] have found that under conditions of creep, there ex- 
ists an interlamellar spacing in (y + /3) Ni-Fe-A1 alloys 
below which the interfaces contribute more to the compos- 
ite strength than do the constituent phases. This can be un- 
derstood in terms of threshold stresses associated with 
strain incompatibilities at the interfaces. Eutectics have the 
advantage of allowing the attainment of such structures on 
a microstructural scale without recourse to thermomechan- 
ical processing. 
Depending on the nature of the interfaces, a fine multi- 
phase structure can also debilitate the resistance to plastic 
deformation. The development of a eutectic or eutectoidal 
morphology on the micrometer scale can lead to enhanced 
plasticity at temperatures approximately one-half the melt- 
ing point and to a loss of strength relative to structures with 
a coarser distribution of secondary phasesY 5j This phenom- 
enon, of course, forms the basis of superplasticity in many 
two-phase systems and has been exploited, for example, in 
the eutectic aluminum-palladium (A1-PdA14) system, tl61 The 
design of eutectic or eutectoidal composites for high-tem- 
perature service therefore requires a careful balance of the 
operative deformation parameters. 
Attention is given here to multiphase eutectic alloys that 
contain a metallic solid solution with one or more inter- 
metallic phases. The systems, based on Ru and Ir, are un- 
usual in that the solid solutions of the respective materials 
are both notoriously brittle, whereas, some of the interme- 
tallic constituents are singularly ductile within the context 
of intermetallics.

4. Ir-Nb 
The phase diagram of the Ir-Nb system contains a num- 
ber of phases with very high melting points. Specifically, 
it exhibits a eutectic point at 2400 ~ between Ir solid so- 
lution and Ir3Nb (L12). The solubility of Nb in Ir at the 
eutectic point constitutes the maximum (-16 at. pct). t411 
The volume fraction of Ir3Nb accordingly increases from a 
very small amount at solidification to approximately 40 pct 
at 1700 ~ under equilibrium. This necessitates substantial 
nucleation and growth in the Ir matrix and represents a 
potential dispersion-strengthening mechanism. 
Two further parameters were of interest to the present 
study. The first of these was the advent of an LI2 phase in 
the Ru-Nb and Ir-Nb alloys, which raises the possibility of 
a yield strength anomaly, exemplified in related compounds 
such as Ni3A1 and Cu3Au. The second is the susceptibility 
of certain LI2 phases to greater ductility by doping with 
boron. An interpretation based on improved grain-boundary 
cohesion by boron segregation has been at least partially 
successful in accounting for enhanced ductility in alloys 
prone to intergranular fracture. Boron additions have been 
shown to be effective in other systems, such as the previ- 
ously mentioned RuA1 compounds, and the intergranular 
nature of failure in Ir compounds led us to examine the 
possibility of improving the mechanical response by doping 
with B. It will be seen that the eutectic Irs4Nbl6 alloy sim- 
ilarly lends itself to modification by boron additions.

IV. DISCUSSION 
A. Strength of Multiphase Systems 
No coherent theory to describe the mechanism of inter- 
face strengthening at elevated temperatures exists. Aspects 
such as relative orientation, composition, microstructure, 
phase distribution, and size distribution have all been rec- 
ognized as playing a role, but it is difficult to systematically 
isolate their respective contributions. In the present study, 
contributions to the high-temperature behavior were ex- 
pected from variables such as the interlamellar spacing, pre- 
cipitation hardening, grain-boundary modification, and 
thermally activated yield phenomena, to name but a few. 
The apparent complexity is made even more difficult by the 
disparate behavior of seemingly closely related systems. 
Thus, predictability among intermetallic systems remains 
elusive. The essential concem with studies like this is to 
identify possible systems and mechanisms for achieving the 
high-strength and high-toughness duality central to struc- 
tural intermetallics development. To this end, several prom- 
ising concepts suggest themselves. 
The first is the synergistic effect of combining phases in 
a very fine composite system. The obvious advantage is that 
this allows the incorporation of soft phases, pursuant to 
improved toughness and ductility at room temperature. Al- 
though the number of high-temperature binary eutectics 
suitable for exploitation is limited, this does not rule out 
the identification of eutectic compositions in temary and 
higher systems. The Ru-Ni-A1 composition in this study 
represents one such example, whereby a very fine mixed 
eutectic, comprising a number of ductile phases, led to the 
development of strengths superior even to that exhibited by 
the simple binary Ru-RuA1 eutectic at intermediate tem- 
peratures, without any significant compromise in the room- 
temperature ductility in compression. 
The other important supposition is that strength might be 
aided by the precipitation of a particulate dispersoid, and 
the Ir84Nb~6 alloy in particular potentially presides over such 
a capability. The observation of high-temperature strength 
normally associated with less symmetric refractory com- 
pounds t431 in the essentially disordered cubic Ir84Nb~6 is un- 
usual, and precipitation strengthening is at least one of the 
mechanisms that might be invoked to account for this. No 
evidence was found for this in the present study and would 
require resorting to transmission electron microscopy to 
conclusively resolve such precipitates. While it is probable 
that the observed strength is primarily an inherent property 
of Ir, it is likely that even greater strengths can be devel- 
oped if the second-phase constituent is evolved. Signifi- 
cantly, the high strength observed was again accompanied 
by useful room-temperature ductility in compression. 
B. Strengthening Effects in the Ru-RuAl Type System 
The synergism of very fine composite constituents in de- 
veloping the mechanical properties was exemplified in this 
study by the Ru-RuAI type system, which was found to 
exhibit strengthening far in excess of that expected from 
the strength of its constituents. At least four mechanisms 
can be advanced to explain this potent effect. 
In the first place, it has been noted that Ru dissolves an 
increasingly greater amount of A1 at elevated temperatures 
(Figure 1). This arguably small solute constituent may be 
increasingly significant in solid solution strengthening at 
increasing temperatures, as its solubility range is extended. 
However, its role in this sense would be limited at room 
temperature, since the ruthenium solid solution essentially 
exists as a pure phase in the eutectic. 
Second, we note that the deformation mechanism of sin- 
gle-crystal Ru has been observed in other studies to incor- 
porate elements of basal slip, which can lead to enhanced 
slip processes and work hardening.t2~ Single crystals of Ru 
could reportedly be deformed up to 80 pct in compression 
and 20 pct in tension, when suitably oriented for basal slip. 
Furthermore, the crystals tended to rearrange themselves 
into orientations suitable for basal slip during loading, and 
cracks that formed in the process were reported to be stable 
against propagation. The strengthening observed in this 
study is therefore conceivably attributable to modified slip 
mechanisms and work-hardening effects, possibly as a re- 
sult of the eutectic Ru lamellae acting as small single crys- 
tals. 
There is also the apparent effect, mentioned previously, 
of constraint on a phase coexisting with another phase.t~31 
Even in the absence of applied deformation, the stress state 
of coexisting phases can differ from that in their isolated 
state. This can be attributed to the existence of internal 
stresses; for example, two phases in a stress-free condition 
accumulate interracial stresses as a result of different ex- 
pansion coefficients during cooling (or heating), which 
leave the phases in a condition of hydrostatic stress. Ray 
and Mondal t441 have shown by means of hardness measure- 
ments that such a residual stress increases the measured 
hardness of a material. This interphase constraint is also 
intimately linked to the toughness of a multiphase material 
in terms of the interface decohesionY 31 
Finally, it is recognized that soft particles can be effec- 
tive strengtheners in systems containing hard and brittle in- 
termetallic phases, and an increase in the interface density 
leads to a progressive strengthening in terms of a rule of 
mixtures, t~4,45j There is, however, an additional effect when 
the phase dimensions drop below a critical value. Klrwer 
and Sauthof~ 3,4] observed this effect during creep of a la- 
mellar Ni-40 at. pct Fe-18 at. pct A1 alloy. At an interla- 
mellar spacing of 2.2 /~m, a threshold stress inversely 
proportional to the lamellar spacing became evident. In the 
eutectic alloys under investigation, the interlamellar spacing 
was of the order of 0.6 to 0.8/~m. This represents a very 
small mean free path for dislocations, and thus, work hard- 
ening within the phases might be expected to play a sub- 
ordinate role to dislocation blocking at the interfaces. (Jung 
and Sauthofft~4~ have further observed that despite an in- 
crease in strength, an overall decrease in the creep resis- 
tance of Fe-NiA1 can occur owing to the NiA1 being 
off-stoichiometric. In the case of the Ru-RuA1 and Ir-IrA1 
systems, significant deviation from the stoichiometric com-

C. Strengthening Effects in the Ir-Nb Type System 
Several broadly defined deformation behaviors at ele- 
vated temperatures have been recognized in intermetallic 
compounds. The well-established yield-strength anomaly 
(whereby an increase in yield strength is observed at ele- 
vated temperatures as a result of thermally activated dis- 
location locks) represents one such behavior, but com- 
pounds can also be distinguished by either a negligible 
response of the yield strength to temperatures below about 
half the melting temperature T,,/2 or a rapid loss of strength 
below Tin~2. The latter behavior is usually associated with 
simple compounds such as the L10 and B2 structures, 
whereas temperature-independent behavior in this regime is 
common among the less symmetrical and complex com- 
pounds, t471 The offset yield strength of the Ir84Nb16 alloy at 
800 ~ was unchanged from that measured at room tem- 
perature and remained fairly constant up to much higher 
temperatures. In this regard, it exhibited behavior typical of 
systems in which thermally activated plastic deformation is 
negligible. 
Although no clear evidence of a thermally activated yield 
anomaly was found in the Ir-Nb, or any of the other alloys 
considered here, this does not preclude it from existing on 
a limited scale. Disparate flow stress behavior has been 
identified in numerous PGM-base compounds--those, like 
PtCu 3, that exhibit a positive temperature dependencet481 
and those like Pt3A1 that exhibit a dramatic increase of the 
flow stress with decreasing temperature549~ 
On the premise that strength should scale roughly with 
melting temperature, Gyurko et al.tSol have examined sev- 
eral Engel-Brewer intermetallics for anomalous yield be- 
havior, specifically the L12-strucmred Ir3Zr and Ir3Nb com- 
pounds. These compounds have very high melting points 
(2280 ~ and 2450 ~ respectively) but were surprisingly 
found to be inferior to Ni3A1 in the case of IraZr and only 
slightly better in the case of Ir3Nb, in terms of high-tem- 
perature strength. While Ir3Nb showed some evidence of 
anomalous yield behavior up to 800 ~ Ir3Zr showed no 
evidence whatsoever. They advance, as a basis for under- 
standing this behavior, the low ordering energy of Ni3A1 
that corresponds to a low antiphase boundary (APB) en- 
ergy, which in turn allows thermally activated dislocation 
locking. In contrast, the high ordering energy of Ir3Nb and 
Ir3Zr is hypothesized, in accordance with the model of Pai- 
dar et al., ~5~ to correspond to a high APB energy which 
regulates planar (and therefore glissile) dislocation config- 
urations. They note that the high ordering energy, while 
debilitating the yield strength, is the source of the strong 
bonding required for creep resistance. 
A yield anomaly such as that considered earlier would 
be unexpected in the disordered Irs4Nb16 phase. It might, 
however, be expected to play a role in the minor constitu- 
ent, the ordered L12 Ir3Nb, provided the phase makes up a 
significant part of the composite. In view of the sluggish 
kinetics of precipitation, the particle dimensions, and the 
relatively minor effect observed by Gyurko et al.,tsol the 
contribution of this effect in the present study can be dis- 
counted as marginal. 
More reasonably, the high-temperature strength of the as- 
cast Ir-Nb alloy can be understood as an inherent property. 
The exceptionally high resistance of Ir to plastic deforma- 
tion has been noted previously,t331 and in a study of the 
high-temperature tensile properties of Ir single crystals, 
Brookes et aL t321 found that the high critical resolved shear 
stress (CRSS) value did not fall off until testing at about 
half the melting point, i.e., about 1000 ~ They argued for 
a strong role for the impurity content, the drop in the CRSS 
corresponding to that point at which dislocation interactions 
with the impurities became unimportant. It has been re- 
ported that tramp impurity levels as low as 0.01 wt pct raise 
the softening temperature of work-hardened iridium to at 
least 1100 ~ I191 Clearly, the substitutional Nb in solution 
and the precipitation of any secondary phases considered in 
the Irs4Nb16 alloy would be expected to raise the CRSS. 
Considering that pure Ir exhibits a room-temperature tensile 
strength of 1103 to 1241 MPa, t521 the effect of the Nb is 
remarkable in that, if anything, it lowers the strength rela- 
tive to the unalloyed Ir. This could be explained in terms 
of a gettering effect. 
By contrast, the fine two-phase structure of the Ir-IrA1 
system developed strengths considerably greater than pure 
Ir at temperatures up to about 1000 ~ consistent with the 
arguments developed in Section B. There is, therefore, good 
reason to suppose that the controlled development of a sec- 
ond phase in the Ir-Nb alloys would have further strength 
benefits, at least in the lower temperature range. 
In summary, the properties observed for the Ir-Nb alloys 
are considered to essentially reflect the inherent strength of 
Ir. Owing to the sluggish precipitation kinetics at the tem- 
peratures of interest, the anticipated role of a precipitated 
dispersoid was difficult to verify on the optical level, al- 
though a significant increase in hardness on aging does sug- 
gest that greater strengths can be obtained. Transmission 
electron microscopy work is required to unambivalently es- 
tablish the existence of a suboptical precipitate. Additional 
tests to include specimens in which the second phase is 
more developed are anticipated. 
D. Morphological Stability of Eutectic Systems 
Decomposition of primary eutectic structures is not un- 
common (as discussed in References 7, 9, and 53) and is 
often associated with nonequilibrium compositions. This is 
clearly the case in the Ru65Nb35 alloy, where the eutectic 
decomposes at lower temperatures to other equilibrium 
phases. The driving force for coarsening, however, is a re- 
duction in the interracial area.t53J Although the slow diffu- 
sion kinetics in intermetallics mitigate against diffusion, 
accelerated transport can occur under the impetus of stress 
or when the structures are associated with boundaries or 
sub-boundaries. Kumar and MiracletgJ note that grooves as- 
sociated with these boundaries provide the curvature 
necessary for spheroidization. 
The localized association of the coarsening process with 
the primary dendrites observed in the Ru-RuA1 and Ir-IrA1 
systems suggests that the dendrites may well contribute to

the coarsening process by providing boundaries, nucleation 
sites, or solute reservoirs. The occurrence of halos around 
the primary dendrites is indicative of nonreciprocal nucle- 
ating characteristics, i.e., the/3 phase in each case is a less 
effective nucleating site for the Ru or Ir than vice versa. 
The poor nucleating characteristics of the primary/3 phases 
necessitate considerable undercooling to initiate growth of 
the coupled phase, resulting in solute enrichment and den- 
dritic growth of the halo phase around the primary phase 
prior to eutectic growth. [541 Significantly, the high degrees 
of undercooling lead to significantly finer eutectic struc- 
tures,t541 a feature further accentuated by the high cooling 
rates involved in the manufacturing procedure. At the same 
time, the halo width is expected to increase with the degree 
of undercooling and decrease with cooling rate. The very 
fine lamellar structures and/or coarsening observed may 
therefore not be typical of wholly eutectic structures with 
fewer perturbations within the matrix. 
Strain-assisted coarsening can also play a contributing 
role, even in the absence of mechanical deformation. Walter 
et al.,t55J for example, have shown the existence of networks 
of mismatch dislocations lying in the interface of NiAI-Cr 
eutectics. Thus, a degree of misfit strain might be expected 
at all such interfaces, leading to a driving force for diffusion 
and reaccommodation. 
Thermochemical stability is an unavoidable requirement 
for long-term high-temperature applications. The tendency 
of these eutectic structures to decompose is therefore prob- 
lematic. 
E. Role of Boron in the Ir-Nb Alloy 
Theories proffered to account for the boron ductilizing 
effect include (1) enhanced grain-boundary cohesion, per- 
mitting tensile stresses greater than the yield stress, which 
in turn allow global plastic flow; (2) a reduction in the 
contribution of the grain boundaries to the yield stress, im- 
plying a modified grain-boundary structure that allows slip 
accommodation and transmission across the boundaries at 
lower levels of stress accumulation; and (3) blocking of 
environmental hydrogen from entry to the grain bounda- 
ries. [56] These are all based on the observation that B seg- 
regates to the grain boundaries. 
The reportedly low solubility of B in Ir is borne out by 
the observation of an extensive grain-boundary phase in the 
B-doped Ir-Nb alloy, and this invokes an explanation of the 
modified mechanical properties observed based on modified 
intergranular properties. The amount of grain-boundary 
phase observed in the alloy is somewhat more than ex- 
pected from the amount of B alloyed. This may imply that 
the B precipitates as another intermetallic phase, or that the 
phase observed is simply incipient Ir3Nb, and that the B 
enhances diffusion. At any rate, it is clearly involved in 
modifying the grain-boundary composition and enhancing 
the ductility, but this is unfortunately accompanied by a 
debilitation of the high-temperature strength. The behavior 
of the Ru84Nb16 alloy therefore conforms to that of other 
alloys exhibiting the boron ductilizing effect. 
V. CONCLUSIONS 
The PGM-base eutectic composites typifying a range of 
potential strengthening mechanisms were investigated put- 
suant to evolving both high-temperature strength and room- 
temperature ductility. Expectations of good strength-ductil- 
ity combinations based on the incorporation of one or more 
ductile phases were met in a number of high melting point 
Ru- and Ir-base composites, evaluated in compression for 
temperatures up to 1300 ~ Some of the most important 
observations include the following. 
1. There is a development of strengths far in excess of that 
expected on the basis of a simple rule of mixtures. Sev- 
eral factors may contribute to this synergy; the most im- 
portant is considered to be the high interface density of 
the very fine eutectic structures. 
2. A singular high-temperature strength of the Ir-Nb sys- 
tem, which was found to have a proof stress of over 600 
MPa at 1300 ~ was seen. The high resistance to plastic 
deformation is probably an inherent feature of Ir but 
might be further developed by alloying and a subopti- 
caUy precipitated dispersoid. 
3. The good room-temperature ductility of certain of these 
composites, taken in conjunction with the development 
of high-temperature strength, ductile phase strengthen- 
ing concepts, should give impetus to further research 
into these systems.
