1. Introduction
Oxide dispersion-strengthened (ODS) steels are among the most 
important accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding materials. Due to their 
sufficiently high thermal stability (creep), superior strength, and 
excellent resistance to irradiation damage, they are ideal candidates for 
the II and III generations of light water reactors (LWRs), the IV gener -
ation of supercritical pressurized water reactors (SCPWs), and sodium- 
cooled fast reactors (SFRs) assemblies [ 1 , 2 ]. The former two proper -
ties arise due to the formation of dense oxide nanoparticles during alloy 
preparation. These precipitates are effective in pinning dislocations and 
grain boundaries, thereby increasing the thermal stability and strength 
of ODS steels [ 3 ]. The oxide nanoparticles in previously prepared 
commercial ODS steels were generally coarse (~ 20 nm) [ 4 , 5 ]. Since Y- 
containing composite oxide particles are brittle phases incompatible 
with matrix deformation, the Y
2
O
3 
content was limited to less than 0.35 
wt% to avoid a significant reduction in the formability of ODS steels [ 6 ]. 
In recent decades, breaking through the dilemma of the strength- 
ductility trade-off has been a significant objective for ODS steels.
As a critical element, Y is present in all oxide nanoparticles. Theo -
retically, the addition of more Y ₂ O ₃ can increase the volume fraction of 
oxide nanoparticles and enhance the dispersion strengthening effect, 
thus improving the mechanical properties of the alloy [ 7 ]. In addition, 
the oxide nanoparticles also trap sites for point defects produced during 
neutron irradiation, which avoids bubble formation by He accumulation 
in the vacancy clusters [ 8 , 9 ]. It is also worth noting that the oxide type in 
ODS alloys has an important effect on irradiation resistance. In order to 
further improve the mechanical properties and irradiation resistance of 
the alloys, modern ODS steels are added with reactive elements (Ti, Zr 
and Hf) to reduce oxide nanoparticle size ( < 10 nm) and increase their

[ 11 ]. The mechanism of interaction between oxide nanoparticles and 
dislocations (cutting or bypassing) is closely related to the particle size. 
Dislocation shearing of the oxide nanoparticles enables stress relaxation

introducing ductility through their sequential shearing during ongoing 
deformation. Meanwhile, the increase of Y ₂ O ₃ addition can also improve 
the strength of ODS alloys and achieve the simultaneous increase of 
ductility-strength.
In addition to introducing penetrable oxide nanoparticles, bimodal 
structures are another effective way to overcome the strength-ductility 
trade-off. The bimodal structure can release stress concentrations and 
promote strain dispersion, significantly improving ductility without a 
trade-off in strength [ 13 ]. Cao et al. [ 14 ] used internal oxidation and 
powder forging processes to prepare ODS steels, obtained an optimal 
microstructure consisting of coarse-grained “ core ” regions and inter -
connected fine-grained “ shell ” regions, and achieved a good combina -
tion of strength and ductility. However, the lower densities of powder 
forging alloys limit their use in ODS steels [ 15 ]. Currently, the main 
consolidation techniques for ODS steels are hot isostatic pressing (HIP), 
hot extrusion (HE), and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [ 16 ]. Among them, 
a specific bimodal grain distribution exists in hot extruded alloys. 
However, determining a positive design for the coarse grain fraction is 
still in the preliminary research stage [ 15 , 17 ]. Here, we propose a new 
bimodal grain size control method. Specifically, introducing oxide 
nanoparticles with and without grain boundary pinning effect simulta -
neously during thermo-mechanical processing of ODS alloys, realizing 
precise control of the bimodal structure.
To prove the above strategy and reveal the strengthening and 
toughening mechanism of ODS steels in-depth, we developed a dual- 
heterostructure ODS steel by compositional design. This steel is char -
acterized by the presence of both penetrable and impenetrable oxide 
nanoparticles in a matrix containing a bimodal grain structure. The ef -

4. Discussion
4.1. Grain structure and oxide nanoparticle evolution
As shown in 3.1, the formation of the bimodal grain structure of the 
alloy is closely related to the nano-oxide deformation mechanism. 
During thermomechanical processing, the coarser oxide nanoparticles 
have a stronger pinning effect, while the fine oxide nanoparticles can be 
plastically deformed via shear. Thus, large-sized (~20 nm) oxide 
nanoparticles can pin grain boundaries, while small-sized (~5 nm) oxide 
nanoparticles cannot pin grain boundaries effectively. It is also the main 
reason for obtaining bimodal grains by controlling the size of the oxide 
nanoparticles rather than by conventional heat treatment or thermal 
processing methods. It suggests that tuning the grain structure of ODS- 
FeCrAl by adjusting the oxide nanoparticle pinning ability is an effec -
tive and tailorable method.

oxide nanoparticles is smaller than the critical size, the strengthening 
mechanism changes from bypass to shear [ 10 ]. At the same time, this 
critical size increases with increasing temperature [ 41 ]. Thus, there is an 
unexpected decrease in the yield strength of the 0.25Y

O

temperatures compared to room temperature. Based on the room tem -
perature hardening model, the temperature dependence of the yield 
strength of ODS-FeCrAl is discussed through several assumptions. 
Although some of these are oversimplified, the uncertainties introduced 
by these assumptions are insufficient to alter the present description ’ s 
main conclusions.

Next, we will explain in more detail the origin of the extraordinary 
tensile ductility of 0.5Y
2
O
3 
alloys. Microstructural observations ( Fig. 3 ) 
show that the size of the ultrafine grains decreases, and the proportion 
increases with increasing Y
2
O
3 
content. In general, ultrafine grains are 
not favorable for achieving large elongations. Because dislocations are 
challenging to accumulate within these ultrafine grains, strain hard -
ening is often absent [ 51 ]. Developing microstructures with bimodal 
grain size distribution is a common strategy to improve the tensile 
ductility of ODS-FeCrAl alloys [ 52 ]. Since oxide nanoparticles distrib -
uted inside the coarse grains can generate, pin down, and thus accu -
mulate dislocations within the grains, this part of the nanoparticles 
enables the simultaneous increase of strength and plasticity of ODS- 
FeCrAl alloys. However, pinning grain boundaries by oxide nano -
particles distributed between medium-sized grains may lead to an 
advanced fracture mechanism transition to grain boundary debonding. 
Therefore, ODS-FeCrAl alloys require more coarse grain regions to 
achieve the best tensile ductility. Specifically, non-ODS alloys require 
only 25 % of the coarse grain area to achieve optimum tensile ductility 
[ 52 , 53 ]. In contrast, ODS-FeCrAl alloys require up to 50 % of the coarse 
grain area to achieve the same result.
The oxide nanoparticles at grain boundaries are observed to be the 
site of preference for crack initiation during tensile processes in ODS- 
FeCrAl alloys. The incompatibility of the oxide nanoparticles with the 
matrix deformation results in the formation of stress concentration at the 
oxide nanoparticles during plastic deformation. It may subsequently 
lead to interfacial debonding or particle cracking at the weaker grain 
boundaries, facilitating crack extension along the grain boundaries. The 
precipitation of large-sized Y
–
Al composite oxide particles is common 
in 0.75Y
2
O

and 1.0Y
2
O
3 
alloys. Given that these particles have lower

also more prone to fracture and thus exhibit reduced ductility. In 
0.5Y
2
O
3 
alloys, more stable Y
2
Ti
2
O
7 
and Y
2
Zr
2
O
7 
particles tend to form 
due to the lower Y
2
O
3 
content. It ensures that the oxide nanoparticles 
remain small after sintering and thermomechanical processing. It re -
duces the tendency of the 0.5Y
2
O
3 
alloy to intergranular fracture, 
thereby increasing the total elongation before fracture. These results 
emphasize again that grain boundaries and oxide nanoparticles should 
be carefully tuned to achieve excellent machinability in ODS steels.
