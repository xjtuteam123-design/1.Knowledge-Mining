The effect of heat treatment and niobium content on the room 
temperature tensile properties and microstructure of Ti3A1-Nb alloys 
A. K. Gogia, T. K. Nandy, K. Muraleedharan and D. Banerjee 
Defence Metallurgical Research Laboratory, Hyderabad-500258 (India) 
(Received February 25, 1992; in revised form June 17, 1992) 
Abstract 
The microstructure and room temperature tensile properties of two Ti3A1 base alloys, Ti-24at.%Al-1 lat.%Nb and 
Ti-24at.%Al-15at.%Nb, were evaluated as a function of cooling rate and aging temperature for fl solution treatment, and 
as a function of solution treatment temperature and aging condition for a 2 + fl heat treatments. While both alloys show 
similar trends of tensile behaviour as a function of heat treatment, the Ti-24AI-15Nb alloy in general possesses better 
strength and ductility than the Ti-24AI-11Nb alloy. 
1. Introduction 
The gross microstructural features of alloys based 
on Ti3A1, such as primary and secondary a2 morphol- 
ogy (based on Ti3A1 with P63/mrnc symmetry), distri- 
bution and size, are similar to those of their 
conventional disordered counterparts, the a/fl 
titanium alloys. Nevertheless, the fracture processes in 
the intermetallic based alloys are quite different, and 
surprisingly few studies have explored the effects of 
such variables on the room temperature tensile prop- 
erties of these alloys. The studies have either been of 
Ti-24AI-11Nb (24-11) [1-3] or Ti-25AI-10Nb- 
3V-1Mo (25-10-3-1) [4-6] alloys, and have not 
encompassed the entire range of microstructures for 
any given composition. In addition, the role of micro- 
structure is complicated by the presence of the O 
phase, based on Ti2AINb with Cmcm symmetry [7], 
and the ordering of the fl phase to the B2 structure 
(designated fl0) [8, 9]. In this work, earlier results on 
24-11 alloy [3] supplemented by additional informa- 
tion and new data on the composition Ti-24AI-15Nb 
(24-15) are presented in an attempt to redress this 
situation and, in addition, to understand the effect of 
niobium for similar microstructural conditions. 
2. Experimental details 
20 kg ingots of Ti-24AI-11Nb and Ti-24AI-15Nb 
were prepared by consumable electrode arc melting 
under vacuum in a water-cooled copper crucible. The 
alloy ingots were processed in a manner similar to that 
used for conventional titanium alloys. First they were 
forged in the fl region from 150 mm diameter ingots to 
70 mm square billets and then rolled in the two-phase 
a z + fl region down to 12 mm diameter rods at about 
1323 K. The rods were then mill annealed at 1333 K. 
The detailed chemistry of the alloys is given in Table 1. 
The alloys were subjected to various heat treat- 
ments, as listed in Table 2. The heat treatment was in 
two stages: higher temperature solution treatments and 
lower temperature aging treatments. Solution treat- 
ment above the fl transus with varying cooling rates 
resulted in a variety of widmanstatten microstructures. 
The second set of solution treatments utilized sub- 
transus temperatures and a water quench. This was 
carried out to obtain different amounts of equiaxed 
primary a2 phase. Subsequent to solution treatments, 
samples were subjected to aging treatments at lower 
temperature. The aging temperatures were chosen to 
be close to the anticipated range of application tem- 
peratures of these alloys and influenced the character 
of the "transformed fl0" constituent.

3. Results 
The results will be presented in three sections. The 
first deals with microstructure as a function of heat 
treatment, the second with tensile properties and the 
third with fractographic features associated with tensile 
failure. 
3.1. Microstructure 
3.1.1. fl heat treatments 
As cooled structures. The effect of cooling rate on 
microstructure is shown in Fig. 1 for 24-15 alloy, and 
the lath size (measured as the mean linear intercept of 
randomly oriented lines) as a function of cooling rate is 
given in Fig. 2. A given cooling rate results in a finer 
microstructure for the 24-15 alloy, as is expected from 
the well known effects of the stabilizing effect of higher 
fl phase content on the nucleation and growth kinetics 
of the ct 2 phase. It is also seen that no distinctive colony 
structure forms in 24-15 alloy, as for the slowest cool- 
ing rate in 24-11 alloy [3]. While a detailed charac-

note that the laths in the 24-15 alloy have the O phase 
structure, while those in the 24-11 alloy have the a 2 
phase structure.

3.3. Fractography 
3.3.1. /3 heat treated structures 
Fractographs of the tensile tested samples are shown 
in Figs. 11 and 12. Figure 11 demonstrates the simi- 
larity in fracture behaviour in relation to tensile duc- 
tility for the two alloys. The peak ductility condition is 
associated with a relatively smooth fracture surface (at 
low magnification) which shows no indication of the 
underlying prior fl grain size. The lower ductility con- 
ditions corresponding to cooling rates on either side of 
the peak ductility condition show a tendency towards 
faceted fracture behaviour. At higher magnifications 
(Fig. 12 for alloy 24-15), fracture is seen to initiate at 
prior/3 grain boundaries for rapidly cooled structures 
and travel across the grains leaving behind steps 
reminiscent of river markings in cleavage. For the peak 
ductility condition, cleavage of individual randomly 
oriented laths is seen together with ductile tearing. At 
slower cooling rates faceted fracture across colonies of 
parallel oriented laths is observed, although the facet 
size for 24-15 alloy is much smaller than that for 
24-11 alloy [3] because the colony sizes are finer. 
3.3.2. a 2 +/3 heat treated structures 
Fractographs of a 2 +/3 solution treated and aged 
samples are shown in Fig. 13. The fracture surface 
essentially consists of fine-scale cleavage associated 
with primary a 2 and secondary a2/O ductile tearing, 
and a fraction of dimpled failure which increases with 
increasing aging temperature or decreasing solution 
treatment temperature. 
4. Discussion 
4.1. /3 heat treatment 
4.1.1. YieM strength 
The yield strength of fl heat treated structures 
increases with decreasing cooling rate. The various 
parameters which influence the yield strength are (1) 
the volume fractions of a2/O and r0 phases, (2) the 
strength of the individual phases as influenced by 
changes in their composition with cooling rate, (3) 
grain size of the prior fl phase, (4) colony size, (5) lath 
size, and (6) dislocation substructure. No estimate has 
been made of the variation of the first two factors in 
this study. We conjecture that since the r0 volume 
fraction is extremely small (less than 5%) for all cooling 
rates, the strength of the two-phase mixture is essen- 
tially controlled by the a2/0 phase and its geometrical 
features. Also, as a consequence of the small/30 volume 
fraction, the composition of the a:/O phase is not 
expected to change significantly with cooling rate and 
will in fact approximate the alloy composition [11] 
although there may be composition gradients across 
the lath dimensions. The grain size of the prior/3 phase 
does not vary with cooling rate. 
Since the ae-fl0 interfaces of Burgers related a 2 
laths have been shown to be transparent to slip trans- 
mission [12], a colony of similarly oriented a2 laths is 
expected to behave as a single crystal [13]; the effective 
grain size of the /3 heat treated structure is then the 
colony size which decreases with increasing cooling 
rate to approach the dimension of a single a 2 lath as 
the colony structure changes to a randomly oriented 
basket-weave structure. Experimental measurement of 
the colony size with adequate statistics is difficult since 
transmission electron microscopy is required to define 
clearly the colony size. Figure 14 which is a plot of 
yield strength against the lath size for both alloys is not 
intended to demonstrate the validity of the Hall-Petch 
relationship but to indicate that at high cooling rates

4.1.3. Effects of niobium 
Figure 6 shows that the 24-11 alloy is stronger than 
the 24-15 alloy for similar microstructural scales. It 
appears, therefore, that the major constituent of 24-15 
alloy, the orthorhombic phase, is weaker in yield than 
the a 2 phase of the 24-11 alloy. The peak ductility of 
the 24-15 alloy is higher than that of the 24-11 alloy. 
A comparison of true stress-strain curves of the peak 
ductility conditions (remembering that these occur for 
the same lath dimensions) for the two alloys shows that 
fracture occurs at almost identical stress levels, and 
therefore the higher ductility of alloy 24-15 is derived 
from its lower yield stress. At slower cooling rates,

4.2.2. Ductility 
The principal factors influencing ductility are the 
size of the ct2/O particles, both of the primary a 2 par- 
ticles and the laths in aged fl0, the volume fraction of fl0 
phase and, in particular, its continuity around the a2/O 
phase. These criteria arise from various factors influ- 
encing the strain at which cleavage nucleates in the 
a2/O phase. It has been shown [14] that the fl0 phase 
delays the onset of cleavage by its ability to relieve 
stress concentration at a2 boundaries generated by 
anisotropic slip. A film of fl0 around all a2/O particles 
is considered essential for this mechanism to be effec- 
tive. Further, stress concentrations at such interfaces 
for a given strain are influenced by the size of the a2/O 
particles. Thus, the heat treatments which enhance 
ductility are those which promote the presence of 
retained fl0 phase in the aged structure as a continuous 
matrix forming a film around both the primary ct 2 and 
the precipitated lath a2/O phases. Of the three basic 
types of aged fl0 structure obtained in this study, shown
