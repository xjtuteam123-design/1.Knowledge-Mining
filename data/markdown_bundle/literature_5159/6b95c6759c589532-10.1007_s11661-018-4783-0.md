Effects of Cr Reduction on High-Temperature
Strength of High-Ni Austenitic Cast Steels Used
for High-Performance Turbo-chargers
JISUNG YOO, WON-MI CHOI, SEOK SU SOHN, BYEONG-JOO LEE, GI-YONG KIM,
YONG-JUN OH, and SUNGHAK LEE
Since greater high-temperature strength is required for maintaining high-performance
turbo-chargers at higher exhaust gas temperatures, e.g., 1323 K (1050 /C176C), high-Ni (20 wt
pct) austenitic steel (ASTM HK40 steel) is presented as an excellent turbo-charger candidate
material. To enhance the strength, three types of austenitic cast steel were fabricated in this
study by controlling the Cr content in HK40 steel, and high-temperature strength improvement
was achieved by detailed microstructural evolution including carbide formation and matrix
strengthening. Room temperature and high-temperature strengths were expected to be
proportional to the carbide volume fraction, but revealed an opposite trend because the steel
containing more Cr (having more carbides) revealed lower strength than the steel containing less
Cr (having fewer carbides). This result was associated mainly with the M 7C3 to M 23C6
decomposition occurring at high temperatures in the less-Cr-steel that beneﬁcially strengthened
the austenite matrix and reduced the hardness diﬀerence between the carbide and matrix,
consequently improving the high-temperature strength. In considering the alloying prices (14 pct
cost saving of alloying elements) as well as the high-temperature strength, the steel containing
less Cr is promising for new high-performance turbo-charger applications.

I. INTRODUCTION
AUSTENITIC cast steel have been applied to
heat-resistant turbo-chargers because their cast compo-
nents provide an excellent opportunity for high-perfor-
mance castings. [1–6] They demonstrate excellent
high-temperature strengths with a stable microstructure
and excellent thermal fatigue properties. These high-al-
loyed types of steel containing Ni, Cr, and W (or Nb) for
a stabilized matrix enhanced the corrosion resistance,
hardness, and carbide formation, respectively, to sustain
turbo-chargers at above high temperatures of exhaust
gases of 1223 K (950 /C176C).[7–21]
To use turbo-chargers at further higher exhaust
temperatures such as 1323 K (1050 /C176C), better high-tem-
perature strength is required for maintaining
turbo-charger structures. ASTM HK40 steel, which is
a high-Ni austenitic steel commercially used for heat-re-
sistant steam tubes or reformers in petrochemical
industrial areas, [22–27] has been presented as an excellent
turbo-charger candidate material. [26,28] This steel (com-
position; 0.4C-1.0Mn-1.2Si-25Cr-20Ni (wt pct)) con-
tains a large amount of expensive Ni (20 wt pct) to
obtain a high austenite stability even at 1323 K
(1050 /C176C), while strong carbide-forming elements (Nb
and W) are omitted to control the volume fraction of the
various carbides at a minimal level. [22–27] Recently, Jung
et al .[28] achieved excellent high-temperature strength
compared to that of ASTM HK40 steel by adding Mo,
while approximately 6 wt pct Ni was replaced by 6.9 wt
pct of another inexpensive austenite former, Mn. This
also led to a cost reduction of approximately 10 pct in
alloying elements.
In this study, three types of austenitic cast steel were
fabricated by controlling Cr content in HK40 steel (25
wt pct Cr), and high-temperature strength was improved
by optimizing the carbide formation and strengthening
the austenite matrix. For a detailed microstructural
evolution study, fractions of equilibrium phases existing
at high temperatures were estimated by thermodynamic
calculations, and were compared with the measured

carbide fractions diﬀer somewhat because the casting
state is not under an equilibrium state.

Since carbide hardness is much higher than the
austenite matrix hardness (1600 to 2400 VHN vs 200 to
220 VHN),[19,48–51] strength is expected to be proportion-
ally related to the carbide volume fraction at room and
high temperatures. However, they reveal an opposite
trend because the N14Mo1 specimen which contains
more carbide has a lower strength than the N14Mo1Cr-4
specimen containing less carbide (Table III). To explain
this, matrix hardness should be considered. The matrix
hardness results (Table III) indicate that the N14Mo1
specimen reveals lower matrix hardness than the
N14Mo1Cr-4 specimen at room and high temperatures.
This is associated mainly with the solid-solution harden-
ing eﬀects. The most signiﬁcant element aﬀecting
solid-solution hardening in austenite is C. C is contained
inside the austenite matrix as an austenite stabilizer, while
C works as a major constituent element of car-
bides.
[1,31,47] The C contents were measured using
wave-length dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) analysis;
the C contents in the N14Mo1 and N14Mo1Cr-4
specimens are 0.33 and 0.40 wt pct, respectively. This
indicates that more C atoms are dissolved inside the
austenite matrix of the N14Mo1Cr-4 specimen, thereby
leading to a higher matrix hardness in the N14Mo1Cr-4
specimen. Since austenite stability increases as the content
of Cr, a ferrite stabilizer, decreases,
[30,53–56] C atoms may
be suﬃciently dissolved in the austenite matrix. This also
conﬁrms the explanation of higher matrix hardness in the
N14Mo1Cr-4 specimen. Thus, the matrix hardness
increases with decreasing Cr content, contributing to

V. CONCLUSIONS
Three types of austenitic cast steel were designed by
adjusting the Cr content in ASTM HK40 steel (25 wt pct
of Cr). In addition, the high-temperature strength
improvement was explained by detailed microstructural
evolution including carbide formation and austenite
matrix strengthening.
1. Fractions of austenite, ferrite, and carbides were
thermodynamically calculated using a two-step
calculation method. The equilibrium phase dia-
grams of the N14Mo1 specimen contained approx-
imately 7.7 pct ferrite, whereas the actual specimen
microstructures contained no ferrite. The fractions
of total carbides calculated from the phase diagrams
(4.7 to 7.2 pct) corresponded to those measured
from the actual microstructures (2.1 to 6.2 pct),
although the calculated and measured fractions
slightly differed due to the non-equilibrium state of
the casting processes.
2. Strength measured at room and high temperatures
was expected to be proportional to the carbide
volume fraction, but revealed an opposite trend.
The N14Mo1 specimen containing more carbide
had a lower strength than the N14Mo1Cr-4 spec-
imen containing less carbide, as the N14Mo1
specimen revealed lower matrix hardness. The
results are associated mainly with the M
7C3 to
M23C6 decomposition occurring at high tempera-
tures in the N14Mo1Cr-4 specimen, which benefi-
cially strengthened the austenite matrix.
3. Hard carbides acted as initiation sites of voids or
cracks during deformation, and led to strength
deterioration. In the N14Mo1Cr-4 specimen of
which the carbide/matrix hardness difference was
small, the M
7C3 carbides initiated microvoids or
microcracks, but cracking processes were steady
and slow, thereby resulting in a higher strength than
that of the N14Mo1 specimen. The results indicated
that the matrix strengthening or reduction of the
carbide/matrix hardness difference was critical for
improving high-temperature strength.
