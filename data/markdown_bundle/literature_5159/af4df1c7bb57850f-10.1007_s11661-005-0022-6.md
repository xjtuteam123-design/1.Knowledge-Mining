Effect of Rare-Earth Element Additions on High-Temperature
Mechanical Properties of AZ91 Magnesium Alloy
F. KHOMAMIZADEH, B. NAMI, and S. KHOSHKHOOEI
The present article focuses on the high-temperature mechanical properties of the magnesium alloy
AZ91. The addition of rare-earth (RE) elements up to 2 wt pct improves both yield and tensile strengths
at 140 °C by replacing the Mg
17Al12 phase with RE-containing intermetallic compounds. This inter-
metallic phase is thermally and metallurgically stable and is expected to boost the grain-boundary
strengthening. It also increases the resistance of grain boundaries to flow at high temperatures. Fur-
ther increases of RE additions reduce strength and ductility due to growth of the Al
11RE3 brittle phase,
which has sharp edges. Still, at a 3 wt pct RE addition, the strength of the alloy at high temperatures
is more than that of AZ91.
I. INTRODUCTION
MAGNESIUM alloys have been identified as ideal
materials for lightweight structures in automotive and aero-
space industries. However, magnesium components are fac-
ing some challenges such as creep resistance and strength
at elevated temperatures. Another challenge for them is main-
taining adequate corrosion resistance.
[1]
Hexagonal close-packed magnesium creeps at even mod-
erately low temperatures, through a stress-recovery mecha-
nism. The creep mechanisms of pure magnesium at low
temperatures are basal slip within the grains and subgrain for-
mation (transient creep), while at the higher temperatures,
diffusion-dependent mechanisms of grain-boundary defor-
mation and sliding (steady-state creep) become predominant.
[2]
Commercial cast magnesium alloys fall into two classes.
The first group is based on the Mg-Al-Mn and Mg-Al-Zn
systems (AM and AZ alloys, respectively). These alloys have
been developed for room-temperature strength and/or duc-
tility but do not exhibit good creep resistance. They make
up the bulk of the magnesium alloys used in automotive
applications.
[3] The addition of aluminum and zinc gener-
ally leads to higher room-temperature strength.
The AZ91 alloy (Mg-9Al-0.8Zn-0.2Mn) is the most favored
magnesium alloy, being used in approximately 90 pct of all
magnesium cast products. It has excellent castability and
sound room-temperature mechanical properties; in its high-
purity form (AZ91E), the alloy shows good corrosion resis-
tance. However, the range of applications for this alloy is
limited by the low strength and poor creep resistance at tem-
peratures in excess of 150 °C, which makes it unsuitable for
many of the components in automobile engines.
[2,3,4]
The microstructure of the AZ91 alloy is characterized by
the Mg-Mg 17Al12 eutectic at the grain boundaries. The
Mg17Al12 intermetallic is incoherent with the magnesium
matrix, and it exists in a wide composition range of 48 to
52 wt pct Al. This intermetallic has a low melting point
(458 °C). Therefore, the compound has poor metallurgical
stability and may contribute to the poor creep resistance of
the alloy at high temperatures.
The discontinuous type of precipitation that occurs dur-
ing the aging of Mg-Al alloys effectively multiplies the grain-
boundary area available for easy deformation in
elevated-temperature creep. The relatively poor elevated-
temperature creep resistance of these alloys is attributed to
this factor.
[2,4] Dargusch et al. observed the grain-boundary
sliding in these alloys and suggested that discontinuous pre-
cipitation of Mg
17Al12 from a supersaturated matrix in die-
cast alloys helps grain-boundary migration and sliding, which
are instrumental in the creep deformation of Mg-Al.
[2,4–6]
So far, the high-temperature behaviors of magnesium
alloys have not been completely identified. The creep mech-
anisms in cast magnesium alloys become more complicated
by the behavior of second phases, especially at high solid-
ification rates (for example, in die casting) when alloying
elements supersaturate the matrix. As a result, precipitation
of metastable phases can be encountered even at room tem-
peratures.
[2,7] Nevertheless, this precipitation cannot be attrib-
uted to softening of the alloy at room temperature.
The second group of alloys has been developed for
improved elevated-temperature performance and is based
mostly on Mg-Al-RE and Mg-Al-Si systems (AE and AS
alloys, respectively).
[5] A silicon addition to AS alloys leads
to the formation of the Mg2Si precipitates, which, unlike
the 
/H9252(Mg17Al12) phase, show a high melting temperature
(1085 °C). Although these alloys exhibit slightly improved
creep resistance, they are quite difficult to die cast, and they
find limited usage in automotive transmission applications.
Volkswagen applied these alloys for automotive industries
in the 1970s.
[8]
A major development in creep-resistant magnesium alloys
has been the emergence of rare-earth (RE)–containing alloys
(AE41 and AE42). This group of Mg-Al-RE alloys contains
at least one and, in general, a mixture of RE elements, as a
precipitate. This alloy system exhibits a major improvement
in creep resistance by replacing the 
/H9252(Mg17Al12) phase with
the thermally stable Al-RE–containing intermetallic (Al11RE3
or Al4RE). These alloys did not find their place in industry
due to various reasons such as poor castability and low
strength.
[9]
Mg-Al-Sr alloys (such as AJ51, AJ52, and AJ62) are a new
development for increasing the creep resistance of magnesium

II. EXPERIMENTAL METHOD
Commercial-purity magnesium (99.95 pct) and aluminum
(99.99 pct) ingots were melted in a steel teapot ladle at 750 °C.
Manganese and zinc were added as manganese chloride and
an Al-75 wt pct Zn alloy, successively. The RE elements were
added by means of misch metal, with a rough nominal com-
position of 50 pct Cerium, 25 pct Lanthanium, and 18 pct
Neodymium. The surfaces of the charge and the melt were pro-
tected by Magrex 36 flux (Foseco Co.) during the entire melt-
ing process. Finally, the melt was stirred for 3 minutes (for
oxide reduction) and, after complete defluxing, it was poured
into a permanent mold, which was preheated to 300 °C.
Rounded tensile samples (ASTM E 8M) with different lev-
els of RE additions (0, 1, 2, and 3 wt pct) were prepared. The
mechanical properties of samples at room temperature and
140 °C were measured using an Instron 4400 machine (1 mm/
min crosshead speed). The hardness was measured using an
Instron Brinell hardness tester (5 mm ball–250 kg load).
Metallographic samples were prepared by mounting them
in cold-setting resin. They were etched in 2 pct Nital aque-
ous solutions for 30 seconds. The microhardness of differ-
ent phases was measured using a Matsuzawa MXT50 machine
(25 g load, using a Vickers indenter). The average of three
measurements was reported for each experiment. Microstruc-
tures and fracture surfaces were studied using an Olympus
PME3 optical microscope and a PHILIPS* XL 40 scanning

Compared with phases present in binary Mg-Al alloys,

[15]

IV. CONCLUSIONS
The addition of 1 to 2 pct RE elements to the AZ91 alloy
improved the high-temperature strength and ductility of the
AZ91 alloy. The RE element replaces Mg
17Al12 phases with
the thermally stable Al 4RE or Al 11RE3 compounds. These
intermetallics are thermally and metallurgically more sta-
ble and are expected to boost the effective grain-boundary
strength and the resistance to flow during creep loading. A
further increase of RE elements (up to 3 wt pct) reduces
the ductility due to Al
11RE3 brittle-phase growth.
