1. Introduction 
Nb – Si based alloys are mainly composed of metallic Nbss (niobium 
solid solution) and intermetallic silicides (Nb
5
Si
3 
and Nb
3
Si) [ 1 , 2 ]. The 
intermetallic silicides have the superior mechanical performance at the 
temperature above 1200 
◦
C, this guarantees that the Nb – Si based alloys 
can be promising applied at the operation temperature of 1200 – 1400 
◦
C 
(such as the hot sections of aircraft and spacecraft engines), which has 
exceeded the limit work temperature of the third-generation Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys currently in use. The metallic Nbss can offer 
the room temperature fracture toughness of alloys for achieving the aim 
of manufacture demands [ 2 , 3 ]. However, their room-temperature frac -
ture toughness is still far away from industrialization, meanwhile the 
poor high-temperature oxidation resistance also limits the application 
and further development of Nb – Si based alloys [ 3 , 4 ]. After three de -
cades of research and development, these drawbacks of Nb – Si based 
alloys have been ameliorated to some extent by alloying with elements 
such as Ti, Cr, Al, Hf, Mo, Zr, Ge, Sn, B, Ta, Ga, V, W, a multi-elemental 
Nb – Si based alloy system has also been developed [ 5 – 15 ]. 
The additions of Ti, Cr, Zr, Hf, B and Al in Nb – Si based alloys 
effectively improves the high-temperature oxidation resistance of the 
alloys. These element additions can improve the density of oxidation 
scales and retard the inward diffusion of oxygen via modification of 
oxidation products [ 5 , 6 , 10 , 11 , 16 ]. The additions of Ti, Zr, Hf and Ga in 
Nb – Si based alloys are generally accepted to enhance the 
room-temperature fracture toughness of the alloys due to refinement of 
microstructures or reduction of the Peierls-Nabarro (P – N) barrier energy 
of which phase or microstructure [ 11 , 13 , 17 , 18 ]. However, the additions 
of those elements more or less sacrifice the high-temperature mechani -
cal properties, although they contribute to the improvement of the 
oxidation resistance and room-temperature mechanical properties of the 
alloys [ 10 , 11 , 13 , 18 ]. Thus, one motivation of the present work is to 
enhance the high temperature mechanical performance without obvi -
ously decreasing other properties of the alloys via finding out an 
appropriate content of Mo addition, since molybdenum can increase the 
melting point of alloys and thus is beneficial for the high-temperature 
strength of alloys [ 9 , 19 ]. For instance, Kim et al. [ 20 – 23 ] reported 
that the addition of Mo in the Nb – Si binary alloys enhanced the offset

2. Experimental procedures 
Four multi-elemental Nb – Si based ultrahigh temperature alloys with 
compositions of Nb – 22Ti – 15Si – 5Cr – 3Al – 3V-2Hf-2Zr- x Mo ( x = 0, 2, 5 
and 10 at.%) were prepared by vacuum non-consumable arc melting 
under an argon atmosphere in a water cooled copper crucible (these 
alloys are respectively referred to as 0Mo, 2Mo, 5Mo and 10Mo alloys 
for convenience). In order to minimize the impact of uncontrollable 
factors and get homogeneous button ingots, the melting process for each 
button ingot must satisfy the following requirements: (i) The surface 
impurities and oxide films of raw materials were removed by acid/ 
alkaline cleaning; (ii) The same maximum melting current and the same 
cooling conditions were guaranteed for each melting cycle; (iii) All the 
four ingots were re-melted at least 3 times. 
Two cubic specimens for each alloy with size of 7 × 7 × 7 mm
3 
were 
prepared for isothermal oxidation at 1250 
◦
C. For obtaining the total 
mass change including the possibly spalled scales after oxidation, the 
specimens were placed in an alumina crucible and then they were set in 
open-ended tube electric furnace. The furnace was firstly heated up to 
450 
◦
C with a rate of 10 
◦
C/min, then was further heated up to 1250 
◦
C 
with a rate of 27 
◦
C/min. After holding for 1 or 20 h at 1250 
◦
C, the 
furnace was cooled down to room temperature with a rate of 5 
◦
C/min. 
The mass changes of oxidized specimens were weighed in an electronic 
balance with an accuracy of 0.001 g. 
Three single-edge notched bending specimens (SENBs) with di -
mensions of 2.5 × 5 × 30 mm
3 
were cut from each ingot by electro 
discharge machining (EDM) for room-temperature fracture toughness 
tests. The depth of notch in SENBs was 2.5 ± 0.1 mm. The measurement 
was conducted in Instron-3382 materials testing machine and the speed 
of cross-head down movement was 0.2 mm/min. The average K
Q 
values 
were calculated with the formula mentioned in Ref. [ 21 ]. The micro -
hardness (Hv) of microstructural constituents in the alloys were 
measured by HXP-1000TM hardness machine with an applied load of 
0.98 N, and the average value was employed at least 5 indentations. The 
evaluation of compressive behavior at 1250 
◦

ducted in a Gleeble 3500 testing machine. The strain rate is 5 × 10
 3

ment were cylinders with 8 mm in diameter and 12 mm in height. 
The constituent phases of alloys and the oxide products in scales 
were identified by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, Panalytical X ’ Pert 
Pro, Cu K α ), energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Inca X-sight) and 
electron back scattered diffraction (EBSD, Tescan Mira 3 XMU). The 
microstructures of specimens were observed by scanning electron mi -
croscopy (SEM, Tescan Vega 3 LMU) in a backscattered electron mode 
(BSE). The element distributions of oxidation scales were analyzed by an 
electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JXA-8100). Quantitative metal -
lographic analysis and average size of constituent phases in this work 
were performed using high resolution imaging analysis software of 
Image-Pro Plus version 6.0, and at least 5 BSE images were employed for 
each statistic.

The typical SEM micrographs of local fracture surfaces of the alloys

identified that four alloys are ruptured in a typical quasi-cleavage

exist some large flat and smooth planes (as shown by the ellipse in 
Fig. 5 a) which are considered to be the interface between Nbss and 
silicide and are possibly caused by the interphase separation. This is 
reconfirmed the weaker interfacial bond strength between Nbss and 
silicide phase in 0Mo alloy. While this phenomenon is barely observed in 
Mo-containing alloys, which thus suggests that the addition of Mo in the 
alloys improve the Nbss/silicide interface strength. It has been reported 
[ 39 – 41 ] that the alloying elements which substituted the Nb or Si atoms 
at the interfacial layer changed the interfacial bond strength. For 
instance, the addition of Ti in Nb – Si based alloys introduced the stronger 
ionic Ti – Si in interface structure and thus enhanced the interfacial bond 
strength between Nbss and silicide. While the addition of Al enriched at
