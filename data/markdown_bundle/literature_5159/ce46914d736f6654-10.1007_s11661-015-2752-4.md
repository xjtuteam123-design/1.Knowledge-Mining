A Review on Inertia and Linear Friction Welding
of Ni-Based Superalloys
AHMAD CHAMANFAR, MOHAMMAD JAHAZI, and JONATHAN CORMIER
Inertia and linear friction welding are being increasingly used for near-net-shape manufacturing
of high-value materials in aerospace and power generation gas turbines because of providing a
better quality joint and oﬀering many advantages over conventional fusion welding and
mechanical joining techniques. In this paper, the published works up-to-date on inertia and
linear friction welding of Ni-based superalloys are reviewed with the objective to make clariﬁ-
cations on discrepancies and uncertainties reported in literature regarding issues related to these
two friction welding processes as well as microstructure, texture, and mechanical properties of
the Ni-based superalloy weldments. Initially, the chemical composition and microstructure of
Ni-based superalloys that contribute to the quality of the joint are reviewed brieﬂy. Then,
problems related to fusion welding of these alloys are addressed with due consideration of
inertia and linear friction welding as alternative techniques. The fundamentals of inertia and
linear friction welding processes are analyzed next with emphasis on the bonding mechanisms
and evolution of temperature and strain rate across the weld interface. Microstructural features,
texture development, residual stresses, and mechanical properties of similar and dissimilar
polycrystalline and single crystal Ni-based superalloy weldments are discussed next. Then,
application of inertia and linear friction welding for joining Ni-based superalloys and related
advantages over fusion welding, mechanical joining, and machining are explained brieﬂy. Fi-
nally, present scientiﬁc and technological challenges facing inertia and linear friction welding of
Ni-based superalloys including those related to modeling of these processes are addressed.

I. INTRODUCTION
NI-BASED superalloys play an essential role in
aerospace and power generation industries. Application
of these alloys for various components in gas turbine
engines has provided higher operation temperatures and
improved eﬃciency. Such applications are due to their
high temperature strength, fatigue, and creep resistance
as well as satisfactory resistance to corrosion and
oxidation under harsh service conditions.
[1,2] These
alloys are mainly precipitation-strengthened, either by
c¢,N i
3(Al, Ti) or by a combination of c¢¢,N i 3Nb, and
c¢.[2,3] Also, alloying elements such as Co, Fe, Cr, Ti, Al,
Nb, Mo, W, and Ta supply extra strength by solid
solution mechanisms.
[4] Moreover, carbides precipitate
at grain boundaries and improve creep resistance
through inhibiting or retarding grain boundary sliding
at high temperatures. [5] The corrosion resistance is
mainly provided by Cr and Al through formation of
protective Cr
2O3 and Al 2O3 oxide ﬁlms. [6]
Eﬀective industrial application of Ni-based superal-
loys necessitates the use of welding in manufacturing of
engine components with complex geometries and repair
of parts damaged during service.
[7,8] Generally, the
manufacturing and repair processes are carried out
using fusion welding techniques such as gas-tungsten-arc
welding, electron beam welding, or laser welding.
[9]
While fusion welding of solid solution strengthened Ni-
based superalloys are commonly carried out, the weld-
ing of high strength, precipitation hardened Ni-based
superalloys has been a challenging issue over the years,
because these alloys are prone to solidiﬁcation cracking,
heat aﬀected zone (HAZ) cracking, strain age cracking,
segregation of alloying elements, and formation of
porosity in the weld zone.
[10–13] These problems limit
the manufacturing and repair weldability of Ni-based
superalloys.[13]
To address this issue, solid state joining techniques
such as inertia friction welding (IFW) and linear friction
welding (LFW) have been considered as alternative
joining techniques. In these techniques, since joining
occurs in the solid state, bulk melting of the weld parts
does not take place.
[14] Therefore, weld defects associ-
ated with melting and resolidiﬁcation such as solidiﬁca-
tion cracking, solute segregation, and porosity
formation are avoided.
[11,15,16]

At the present time, in the great majority of gas
turbines, each blade is mechanically inserted into the
disk slot (Figure 1). This fastening method requires
complex and costly machining operations and is at the
source of several mechanical damages. Speciﬁcally,
fretting fatigue damage initiates from the mechanical
joint of slotted blade-disk assembly and results in
premature failure.
[17] Also, using the ﬁr-tree arrange-
ment (Figure 1) in mechanical fastening method, the
weight of the whole blade-disk assembly is high leading
to high fuel consumption. Additionally, due to a very
tight ﬁt of blades to disk, the overhaul of these
components is diﬃcult, costly, and time-consuming.
As an alternative to mechanical joining, machining of
entire blisk (bladed integrated disk) from solid forged
billets was introduced in 1980.
[19] However, this tech-
nique is expensive when large size blisks are concerned
due to high machining costs and material waste.
Therefore, as an alternative joining technology to
mechanical joining and machining, LFW has been
introduced in 1990 and is being evaluated for repair of
damaged blades as well as for joining of blades to disks.
LFW of blades to disk enables removal of the ﬁr-tree in
conventional mechanical joining, which improves the
fatigue life and also results in signiﬁcant weight saving
(about 30 pct).
[17,20,21] Thereby, fuel consumption and
CO2 emission decrease considerably. [19,22] Furthermore,
as compared to machining of blisks from forged solid
billets, LFW of blades to disks provides substantial
savings in machining and material costs.
Similarly, for the same reasons, IFW has been used as
an alternative joining technology for mechanical joining
and machining of various Ni-based superalloy compo-
nents having symmetry about an axis of rotation such as
tubes, shafts, disks, and high pressure compressor
drums.
[23–25] IFW is the most common friction welding
method.[26,27] In this process, one of the joint parts is
attached to a ﬂywheel. The ﬂywheel is accelerated to a
programmed rotational speed and then disengaged while
the stationary part is forced into contact with the
rotating part under pressure (Figure 2). The kinetic
energy stored in the rotating ﬂywheel is converted into
frictional heat at the interface of the two parts resulting
in material softening, i.e., decrease in the ﬂow stress of
the material.
[27,28] Ultimately, the softened material
cannot resist the applied pressure and ﬂows plastically
to the extremities. [28] The applied pressure and torsional
forces eject the softened material at the interface as ﬂash
(Figure 2) and bring into contact fresh material at high
temperatures allowing for atomic diﬀusion across the
interface and the formation of a bond.
[29]
In LFW, heat is generated by the reciprocating
movement of one of the weld parts against the station-
ary part while they are subjected to an axial pressure
(Figure 3). Once enough softening has taken place at the
interface, the oscillatory part is aligned immediately
with the stationary part and the pressure is maintained
or increased to consolidate the two parts. Similar to
IFW, during the process, some of the plasticized
material is expelled to the sides as ﬂash.
The solid state nature of the joining process in IFW
and LFW makes them ideal for joining ‘‘diﬃcult to
weld’’ materials such as Ni-based superalloys. Since
there is no bulk melting, solidiﬁcation defects often
observed in fusion welding are avoided.
[30,31] Also,
liquation induced HAZ cracking can be reduced signif-
icantly or even suppressed with proper selection of
process parameters.
[32]
IFW and LFW are similar processes from the heat
generation aspect at interface by relative movement of
welding parts and ejection of plasticized material as ﬂash

existing theoretical challenges will be discussed. The
inﬂuence of process parameters on microstructure evolu-
tion, residual stress development, and mechanical prop-

IFW/LFW of single crystal Ni-based superalloys to
address associated challenges. The application and
advantages of IFW and LFW over conventional fusion
welding, mechanical joining, and machining techniques
are classiﬁed then. The last section of the paper will focus
on modeling of IFW/LFW processes and the complexities
associated with the development of reliable and robust
prediction tools for these processes.

IV. INERTIA AND LINEAR FRICTION WELDING
A. Historical Background
Friction welding is a solid state process developed for
joining materials together through generation of fric-
tional heat from the relative movement of one joint part
to another under axial pressure.
[36] Rotary friction
welding (RFW), also known as continuous-drive,
direct-drive, and conventional friction welding, is the
ﬁrst friction welding method specially developed for
welding of components having symmetry about an axis
of rotation such as tubes, shafts, disks, and drums.
[26,27]
In RFW, one of the weld parts is connected to a motor-
driven unit and the other is stationary. [102,103] While the
motor driven weld part rotates at a predetermined
constant speed, the stationary one is brought into
contact with it and a pressure is applied.
[102] Heat is
generated at the interface because of the friction, and
rotation continues for a predetermined time or axial
shortening (upset). Then the rotational driving force is
disengaged and a braking force is applied to stop the
rotating weld part.
[102] The pressure is maintained for a
speciﬁc time or increased (more common) after cessation
of rotation. [35,102,104] The applied pressure and rota-
tional movement expel the softened material as ﬂash and
a bond forms.
Historically, in 1956 Chudikov in Russia used RFW
for the ﬁrst time to generate scientiﬁcally high quality
welds between metal rods although the idea of using
heat generated at the interface by friction for joining
metals and plastics was older than 1956.
[103] Since then,
extensive research has been carried out mostly in Russia
by Vill and others to understand this process from
scientiﬁc aspects.
[103] The process was introduced to the
US in 1960. [103] Since then, focus has been on the
analysis of the process parameters and thermal as-
pects.
[105] In 1962 the Caterpillar Tractor Company
developed the so-called IFW, a modiﬁed version of
RFW in which a ﬂywheel is used to store the energy
required for welding.
[103] In IFW process, one of the
joint parts is attached to a ﬂywheel. The ﬂywheel is
accelerated to a programmed rotational speed and then
disengaged. The stationary part is forced into contact
with the rotating part under pressure. The kinetic energy
stored in the rotating ﬂywheel is converted into fric-
tional heat at the interface of the two parts leading to
material softening on both sides.
[27] The applied pressure
along with torsional forces eject the soften material as
ﬂash and a bond forms. [29] The transformation of the
mechanical energy to thermal energy is faster in IFW
than in RFW, therefore, the width of the thermome-
chanically aﬀected zone (TMAZ) is smaller in the joints
produced by IFW.
[28] Since the introduction of IFW in
1962 by Caterpillar Tractor Company, extensive research
and development have been performed to understand the
bonding mechanism, thermal behavior, microstructure
evolution, and optimize the process parameters for
integral welds for various materials including steel, Ti
alloys, and Ni-based superalloys.
[103,106–108]
The reciprocating motion for friction welding and
then LFW were, respectively, introduced in 1929 by
Richter in Germany and in 1959 by Vill in Russia. [17]
While development and employment of RFW was in its
peak, the possible application of LFW was explained as
‘‘very doubtful’’ by Vill due to diﬃculty in producing the
oscillatory movement.
[14,17] In 1969 the Caterpillar
Tractor Company used LFW to join diﬀerent steel
components.[17,109] This company patented the equip-
ment and the process in that year. [17] The world-wide
acceptance of RFW/IFW for joining circular metallic
assemblies with high quality weld and economic advan-
tages, created the incentive for applying the same

IFW consists of four distinct phases known as
contact, conditioning, burn-oﬀ, and forge as shown in
Figures 2 and 6. During the process, one of the weld
parts is attached to a ﬂywheel and the other is restrained
from rotation.
[27,112] The ﬂywheel is accelerated to a
programmed rotational speed and then disengaged. The
stored energy ( E) of the ﬂywheel in the SI unit is
formulated as E ¼

The stationary part is then moved into contact with
the rotating part under a pressure which leads to a fast
increase in the frictional torque and a slowing down in
the rotation speed due to dry friction between the two
faying surfaces (Figure 6).
[27,34,107] The ﬁrst phase of the
IFW process ends once the nominal pressure is
reached.
[112] The end of this phase is characterized by
an initial peak in torque, and in the contact phase there
is no axial shortening (Figure 6).[112] In the conditioning
stage, the kinetic energy stored in the rotating ﬂywheel is
converted into frictional heat at the interface of the two
parts.
[27,112] The material reaches high temperatures
within 2 to 10 seconds, depending on the material and
welding parameters.
[112] The decrease in the torque at
the initial stage of this phase results from material
softening.[34] Also, the almost horizontal torque curve
originates from a balance between frictional heating and
work hardening. [34] In the third phase (burn-oﬀ), con-
tinuous frictional heating results in the extension of the
plasticized region.
[34] Because of the applied pressure
and heat generation, material is expelled out as ﬂash and
axial shortening from both weld parts occurs. [112] In the
last stage of the process (forge phase), the speed of the
ﬂywheel reaches zero, the rotation stops and the
interface temperature starts to decrease.
[112] In this stage
a second sharp torque peak is produced which contrib-
utes to weld microstructure reﬁnement as well as to
further expulsion of material as ﬂash.
[34] Thereby, axial
shortening increases which leads to further cooling of
the interface.
[34] To have an integral joint, the axial
pressure is maintained or increased during the forging
phase, i.e., after all kinetic energy has been con-
verted.[34,107,112]
C. Linear Friction Welding Process
While the application of IFW is limited to axisym-
metric components, those with nonround or complex
geometries, such as blades to disks in gas turbines can be
welded together using LFW.
[36,110] The process is
presently well established for fabrication of Ti alloy
blisks in the compressor section and is being developed
for Ni-based superalloy components in particular for
manufacturing of blisks in the turbine section of the
aero and power generation gas turbines.
[14,20,110,113]
LFW relies on the development of frictional heat at
the interface by linear oscillation of one part relative to
another while subjected to a pressure. The generated
heat at the interface rapidly raises the temperature in a
narrow region of the interface between the two parts
being joined.
[114] Once the components to be joined have
been heated to a temperature that plasticizes the
material at the interface, the oscillating part is stopped
in an aligned position with the stationary part. Then, the
axial pressure is maintained or increased (more com-
mon) to consolidate the two parts together. During the
process, some of the plasticized material is expelled to
the sides as ﬂash.
The main components of the LFW machine are two
hydraulic actuators; the in-plane actuator that oscillates
one of the joint parts and the forge actuator that applies
a pressure on the stationary part.
[100] In addition, the
tooling allows positioning and clamping of the weld
parts during the process. [115] The software of the
machine records the position of the actuators (in-plane
and forge strokes) as well as the in-plane and forge loads
during the LFW process. This process consists of four
distinct phases known as contact, conditioning (or

and 7 and described below

of the fixtures and the weld parts.
[100] At the end of

ensure that contact is always made. The two weld
parts rest on asperities and no axial shortening and
variation in the temperature of the weld parts occur
at this point.

C. c¢ Evolution
Since c¢ is the main strengthening phase in Ni-based
superalloys, its evolution in these alloys during IFW and
LFW is well investigated.
[46,54,100] During IFW and
LFW of Ni-based superalloys the weld area is exposed
to temperatures close to 1573 K (1300 /C176 C) which is well
above the solvus of primary, secondary, and tertiary c¢
particles in polycrystalline alloys. [76,100] Accordingly,
dramatic changes in the distribution and size of c¢
precipitates occur across the weld interface. For IFWed
and LFWed Ni-based superalloys, generally two diﬀer-
ent trends of c¢ evolution have been reported. In alloys
with low c¢ volume fraction (£30 pct), such as Waspaloy,
the weld area is reported to be depleted from c¢
(Figure 16), leading to a precipitate-free region adjacent
to the interface. Consequently, a pronounced drop in
hardness in this region is observed.
[29,54,100,104,141] For
example, for LFWed Waspaloy rectangular blocks with
initial dimensions of 13 mm 9 11 mm 9 18 mm
(length 9 width 9 height) for each block, LFWed by
the length 9 width cross section, the soft region extends
from the weld interface to 1.5 mm (Figure 17) in the
height direction, where the microhardness is 65 pct of
the parent material value.
[100]
The dissolution of c¢ particles start with dissolution of
ultraﬁne tertiary c¢ particles since they have a chemical
composition signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from those of pri-
mary and secondary c¢.
[56] The dissolution progresses
with dissolution of secondary and primary c¢. Primary c¢
particles formed at higher temperature range, where
diﬀusion rate is higher, have more Al and Ti compared
to secondary c¢, formed at lower temperature range.
[56]
Similarly, tertiary c¢ particles have less Al and Ti
compared to secondary ones. For Astroloy, under
equilibrium conditions (heating rate £0.1 K/s (0.1 /C176 C/
s)[2]), the dissolution of c¢ particles start at 1073 K to
1123 K (800 /C176 C to 850 /C176 C) with dissolution of ultraﬁne
tertiary c¢ particles.[56] Also, under equilibrium condi-
tions, dissolution of secondary and primary c¢ in
Astroloy starts at 1173 K and 1359 K (900 /C176 C and
1086 /C176 C), respectively. [56] The solvus temperatures of
tertiary, secondary, and primary c¢ in Astroloy are,
respectively, 1173 K, 1361 K, and 1413 K (900 /C176 C,
1088 /C176 C, and 1140 /C176 C).[56] Therefore, for Astroloy, the
c¢ solvus is 1413 K (1140 /C176 C) (Table I), a temperature at
which all c¢ families are completely dissolved under
equilibrium conditions.
Dissolution of the strengthening precipitates in the
weld area during friction welding is a favorable incident
that allows easy material ﬂow, formation of ﬂash,
thereby, improving joint integrity.
[29,57] For alloys with
low c¢ volume fraction, although almost all of the c¢
particles dissolve, the low levels of Al and Ti do not
provide a suﬃcient driving force for c¢ reprecipitation at
the end of the welding process. Thus, a soft c¢-free region
develops.[29,100] Similarly, in IFWed Inconel-718 a

microstructure is not aﬀected thermally or mechanically.

E. Suppressed Second Phase Liquation and
Microcracking
One of the most important concerns during IFW/
LFW of Ni-based superalloys is the possible occurrence
of liquation of second phases. During IFW/LFW the
weld area is exposed to temperatures well above the
liquation temperature of the low melting point compo-
nents in the alloy. For example, during LFW of
Waspaloy the weld area experiences temperatures up
to 1553 K (1280 /C176 C) which is 35 K (35 /C176 C) above the
liquation temperature [1518 K (1245 /C176 C)] of the low
melting point components such as Zr sulfocarbides.
[32]
Therefore, segregation induced grain boundary liqua-
tion and thus, microcracking may occur. Also, because
of high heating rates during IFW and LFW, constitu-
tional liquation of c¢ and other second phases and
subsequent microcracking are possible, as was discussed
in Section III.
However, in most if not all of the published literature
on IFW/LFW of Ni-based superalloys liquation of
second phases and microcracking have not been
reported.
[29,32,53,76,142] The role of various thermome-
chanical factors in preventing liquation during IFW/
LFW is discussed in detail in Reference 32. These factors
can be outlined as: (i) ‘‘accelerated dissolution’’ of the

properties of the IFWed/LFWed Ni-based superalloys
are compromised due to almost complete dissolution of
c¢ in the weld area or its reprecipitation with a size and
distribution diﬀerent from those of the parent material.
Thus, it is essential to conduct a PWHT to restore c¢
precipitates with desired size, morphology, and distri-

present at the surface before welding and/or formed at

the probability of oxidation at extremities as less
material is exposed to atmosphere during oscillation. [35]

friction welding, attainment of integral welds free from
oxides is possible without using protective atmosphere.
Also, axial shortening is a convenient and precise metric
by which manufacturers can determine integrity of the
weld.

the extremities of the interface in the linear friction
welds whereas central region is well bonded and free
from oxides. This is due to exposure of extremities to
surrounding atmosphere during oscillation and elliptical
distribution of pressure during welding being lower at
extremities relative to the central region.
[35,100,104] Thus,
in designing test plans, it is important to extract tensile
testing samples from edge to center of the linear friction
weldments and investigate the integrity over the largest
cross-sectional area. For LFWed and PWHTed Waspa-

tensile properties from edge to edge of the weld-
ments.

to edge of the weldments, the yield strength and UTS

shortening above 2 mm. Also, almost uniform ductility
from edge to edge was observed for axial shortening
above 2 mm, as shown in Reference 104.

the developed ﬂash morphology and integrity.
[104] In

[104,117] In
this case, the real weld area is smaller than the original
cross-sectional area of the weld parts.
[117] In contrast,
for integral weldments which were LFWed with axial

the corners (Figure 8). Thus, the observation of a ‘‘well-
formed’’ or ‘‘continuous’’ ﬂash is a good indication of a
sound linear friction weld.
[104] A continuous ﬂash

welding, which is an essential condition to obtain a
sound joint.[104,117,, 153] In this case, the real weld area is
the same or larger than the original cross-sectional area
of the weld parts. [117] Thus, macroscopic examination of
the ﬂash is a simple, eﬀective, and rapid method for
evaluating the integrity of a linear friction weld.
[104]

VII. INERTIA/LINEAR FRICTION WELDING OF
SINGLE CRYSTAL NI-BASED SUPERALLOYS
The replacement of damaged/worn single crystal Ni-
based superalloy components used in gas turbines is
very costly and among the few repair possibilities fusion
welding is an economical alternative.
[9] However, fusion
welding of single crystal Ni-based superalloys is diﬃcult
because of extensive cracking in HAZ during cooling
upon completion of welding resulting from a high c¢
volume fraction ( ~70 pct) as discussed in Section III.
Also, fusion welding of single crystal Ni-based superal-
loys is associated with solidiﬁcation cracking in the
centerline of the fusion zone (Figure 25) and formation
of misoriented stray grains generally located in the
center of the weld which play a critical role in the
promotion of solidiﬁcation cracking.
[39,94] The forma-
tion of stray grains is due to constitutional supercooling
ahead of the solidiﬁcation front which can result in the
nucleation of new grains. [94] The degree of constitutional
supecooling increases as the ratio of thermal gradient
(G) to solidiﬁcation rate ( RS), G/RS, decreases. [94] This
value is the lowest in the center of the weld pool, where
the existence of stray grain is most possible.
[9,94] Solid-
iﬁcation cracks are frequently associated with these stray
grains as shown with arrows in Figure 25. Formation of
the low melting point liquid during solidiﬁcation and
complete wetting of such a small high angle grain
boundary area lead to cracking in the presence of
thermal and mechanical tensile stresses.
[94] Other factors
such as sudden changes in crystallographic orientation
close to the weld centerline, tendency for carbide
formation near the weld centerline, and higher hardness
of the weld compared to that of the parent material
contribute to weld centerline failure during low cycle
fatigue loading.
[156]
In this context, LFW could be an excellent alternative
in order to avoid solidiﬁcation cracking, formation of
stray grains, and HAZ cracking. However, many
scientiﬁc and technological challenges remain to be
addressed. During IFW/LFW of polycrystalline materi-
als it is easy to activate the main slip systems in both
weld parts and obtain suﬃcient plasticity to achieve an
integral joint.
[53] However, in the case of single crystals
only limited active slip systems exist. [53] For LFW of
single crystal to polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys,
Karadge et al .
[53] reported that there is a speciﬁc
relationship between weldability and orientation of the
single crystal. In other words, to have suﬃcient plasticity
near the weld interface, it is essential that the crystal-
lographic slip systems in the single crystal have a speciﬁc
orientation relative to the oscillation direction.
[53] The
welding is not successful if the single crystal is not
oriented for easy activation of primary slip systems.
[14,53]
Also, the occurrence of DRX during LFW of single
crystals (Figure 26) has been reported [40] which may not
be acceptable from operational perspective. More
research is needed to identify welding conditions under
which the formation of dynamically recrystallized grains
is minimized.
Formation of oxides at the weld interface of IFWed/
LFWed Ni-based superalloy single crystals is another
challenge. For LFWed single crystal CMSX-4
/C210 to
RR/C210 1000, presence of semi-continuous Hf-rich oxide
layer at the weld interface has been observed. [53]
Similarly, a continuous Hf-rich oxide ﬁlm has been
detected along a considerable part of the weld interface
in LFWed CMSX-486 /C210 Ni-based superalloy single
crystal.[40] Formation of oxides along the weld interface
is harmful to the integrity of the weldments. [104] Increas-
ing the forge pressure for expulsion of oxides could be a
solution[40]; however, more work is necessary to quantify
the inﬂuence of LFW/IFW process parameters on
microstructure evolution and integrity of single crystal
joints.

VIII. APPLICATION OF INERTIA
AND LINEAR FRICTION WELDING
IFW and LFW are key technologies for the aerospace
industry as they allow welding of ‘‘diﬃcult to weld’’
materials. As discussed above, these processes can be
used for repair of damaged parts and enable manufac-
turing of complicated components for modern gas
turbines.
[157] IFW has been used for joining various
Ni-based superalloy components having symmetry
about an axis of rotation.
[23–25,112] Speciﬁcally, IFW is
being used for joining small aeroengine components
(e.g., pistons, tubes, turbine wheels, and shafts) and
large aeroengine assemblies ( e.g., compressor- rotor,
disk-to-cone, drum-drive compressor, and disk-to-shaft
assemblies).[34] For instance, IFW of RR /C210 1000 to
Inconel-718 is planned for joining of turbine disks to
shafts in new generation jet engines. [108] Also, IFW is
ﬁnding new applications for joining of aircraft actuator
pistons, crankshafts, and other gas turbine parts.
[34]
LFW can be used for joining of disk to rim post. [23]
Most importantly, LFW is being used for joining of
Ni-based superalloy blades to disks in aerospace and
power generation gas turbines. This is because as
discussed in Sections IX–B and IX–C, LFW oﬀers
many advantages over conventional mechanical joining
of blades to disks and machining of blisks from solid
forged billets.
For enhanced performance, dissimilar welding of
diﬀerent grades of superalloys
[24,29] as well as diﬀerent
microstructures such as polycrystalline to single crys-
tal[53] is another capability of IFW and LFW processes.
With dissimilar welding, the properties of the weld parts
can be optimized. For instance, high tensile strength and
low cycle fatigue resistance are important for disk while
high cycle fatigue performance and high creep resistance
are required for blade.
[158] Using LFW, a ﬁne grain disk
can be joined to a coarse grain blade for optimized
properties.[17,119] Similarly, a single crystal blade can be
welded to a polycrystalline disk. [157] In general, dissim-
ilar welding allows tailoring the required mechanical
properties and high temperature capabilities while
providing signiﬁcant weight savings.
[29]
Also, using LFW the blade can be manufactured from
diﬀerent roots and airfoil parts reducing signiﬁcantly the
manufacturing costs speciﬁcally for directionally solid-
iﬁed and single crystal blades.
[20] In addition, LFW can
be used for repair of damaged blades; for instance
replacement of airfoil as an alternative for entire blade
replacement which leads to signiﬁcant savings in
costs.
[20]
IX. ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS OF
INERTIA AND LINEAR FRICTION WELDING
A. Advantages Over Fusion Welding
IFW and LFW oﬀer signiﬁcant advantages over
conventional fusion welding techniques from processing
and weld quality aspects. These advantages can be
summarized as follows:
1. Because of the low energy input, IFW and LFW
take place in the solid state, i.e., the temperature of
the interface remains well below the melting point
of the alloy, thus, bulk melting of the welding parts
does not occur.
[14] Therefore, weld defects associ-
ated with melting and resolidification such as solidi-
fication cracking (shrinkage cracking), precipitation
of detrimental phases ( e.g., low melting point c/
Laves eutectic in Inconel-718
[15]), voids, pores, pin-
holes, microinclusions, slag inclusions, cast struc-
ture, severe solute segregation (leading to formation
of low melting point compounds), and excessive dis-
tortion are avoided.
[11,15,16,28] Thus, c¢ strengthened
Ni-based superalloys that have significant amounts
of Al and Ti ( >6 wt pct), which are often consid-
ered to be unweldable by fusion welding, can be
joined and a weld with comparable mechanical
properties to the parent material can be
attained.
[27,29,97,104,159–161]
2. Ni-based superalloys are also susceptible to HAZ
cracking induced by liquation of the second phases
as noted in Section III. On the other hand, as
mentioned in Section V–E, using IFW and LFW
for joining of Ni-based superalloys, the liquation
of the second phases and consequent microcrack-
ing are suppressed.
3. Due to the solid state nature of the process, for
IFW and LFW ﬁller metal addition, ﬂux, protec-
tive shielding gas, or vacuum are not necessary to
protect the molten metal from oxidation and con-
tamination, thus, friction welding can be per-
formed in air.
[30,110,162] In contrast, in fusion
welding, shielding of the weldments by inert gas is
essential to avoid absorption/reaction of O, N,
and H with molten weld metal.
[111,163]
4. Although no special surface preparation before
welding is necessary, IFW and LFW generate high
integrity welds, since they are self-cleaning pro-
cesses.
[157] In IFW and LFW, oxides, voids, impu-
rities, and other contaminations are broken down
and expelled out with the plasticized material from
the weld area into ﬂash, while clean and pure sur-
faces are pushed into close contact.
[14,17,162] This
allows metal-to-metal contact between parts and
formation of a joint. [14] Flash is subsequently re-
moved by machining. [40] Furthermore, because of
the high applied pressure, inertia and linear fric-
tion weldments are often free from volumetric
ﬂaws.
[17]
5. Fusion welding of dissimilar materials is often
associated with problems such as formation of
brittle intermetallic phases and segregation of
alloying elements which deteriorate the mechanical
properties of the joint.
[31] Such problems are elimi-
nated or minimized using IFW and LFW as no
melting occurs and segregation of alloying ele-
ments is limited due to lower peak temperatures
associated with these processes.
[23,24,29,53,125]
6. IFW and LFW are particularly appropriate for
joining of dissimilar materials with diﬀerent melt-
ing temperatures and thermal expansions because
with proper selection of process parameters, the

exposure time at high temperatures can be con-
trolled.[164]
7. Since the TMAZ size in IFW/LFW is small due to
very localized band in which heat is generated and
the very short heating and joining time ( e.g., 5 sec-
onds for integral linear friction welds in Waspaloy
rectangular blocks with initial dimensions of

[37]), welding induced distortion is minimum
as compared to fusion welding. [17,27,100,157,165–168]
Thus, IFW and LFW are effective for near-net-
shape and additive manufacturing. [34]
8. A forged rather than a cast microstructure is gen-
erated by IFW and LFW. [115] Grain reﬁnement in
the weld area due to severe plastic deformation at
high temperatures and, consequently, occurrence
of DRX, improves the mechanical properties
(strength and toughness) of the joint compared to
the parent material.
[14,169,170] In contrast, during
fusion welding grain growth occurs in the HAZ
due to dissolution of the c¢ particles leading to
lower strength, toughness, and ductility in the
weldments.
[25,171,172] In general friction welds have
a superior combination of strength and toughness
compared with fusion welds. [110]
9. Control and monitoring of the process parameters
via automated system enhance uniformity and
reproducibility of IFW and LFW processes com-
pared with fusion welding. [110] Also, IFW and
LFW generally tolerate wide changes in the weld-
ing process parameters without compromising the
joint quality, therefore reliability is high.
[104,110,139]
Aeroengine manufactures have not observed even
a single failure in tens of thousands of produced
inertia/linear friction welds. [35]
10. Control and optimization of the IFW and LFW
processes are also easy and convenient since the
number of parameters to be controlled is signiﬁ-
cantly lower than that of fusion welding. [34,104]
11. IFW and LFW are clean processes from environ-
mental aspect since no fume, harmful gas, or
smoke is generated during the process. [34,173]
B. LFW Advantages Over Mechanical Joining
Over mechanical joining of blades to disk, LFW oﬀers
many advantages. As shown in Figure 27, LFW of
blades to disk enables removal of the ﬁr-tree in
conventional mechanical joining, which would improve
the service life (in particular, the fatigue life, since
fretting damage originates from slotted blade/disk
assembly), eliminate leakage ﬂow, improve fuel con-
sumption, increase engine eﬃciency, and lead to con-
siderable weight saving (20 to 60 pct, typically about
30 pct) in the blisk, shaft, and other related
parts.
[17,20,21,53] Thereby, fuel consumption and ex-
haust-gas volume, in particular CO 2 emission, decrease
considerably.[19,22]
C. Advantages Over Machining
Compared to machining of blisk from forged solid
billets, LFW of blades to disks provides signiﬁcant
savings in machining and material costs particularly
when large diameter blisks are concerned. Furthermore,
LFW enables selection of dissimilar materials for blade
and disk to optimize the properties for both, whereas in
machining of blisk from forged billets both are made of
the same material, being mostly optimized for service
requirements of disk.
[158,173] Additionally, production of
large size blisks is possible by LFW without the need for
large forged pancakes.
[173] Also, angular and location
tolerances of blades in blisks manufactured by LFW are
very accurate and distortion of the welded parts is
low.[173]
D. Limitations of IFW and LFW
IFW needs a signiﬁcant capital investment for the
machinery and tooling. [34] IFW can be used only for
components with circular cross-sections. [174] Also, dur-
ing IFW of rods and shafts depending on the heat
conductivity of the materials being welded, heat gener-
ation at the weld interface may not be uniform and
varies with distance from the center of the surfaces to be
welded.
[174,175] This may lead to development of non-
uniform TMAZ and weaker integrity at the center. [107]
However, with controlling the IFW process parameters,
e.g., increasing the welding time, this nonuniformity in
temperature can be minimized or even eliminated. In
addition, it is expected that during IFW of tubes this
temperature diﬀerence is negligible. In IFW/LFW the
material must be plastically deformable at the welding
thermomechanical conditions.
[28] Therefore, it might not
be easy to use IFW/LFW for joining cast Ni-based
superalloys, e.g., Inconel-713. Also, the size and geom-
etry of the weld parts should be such that they can be
clamped, rotated, or oscillated.
[28]
Although LFW has been available since 1990, this
process has been mostly used in the manufacturing of
blisks in aerospace and power generation gas turbines
partly due to the high cost of the LFW machine.
[26] As
discussed in Sections IX–B and IX–C, LFW oﬀers
economic beneﬁts over conventional mechanical joining
of blades to disks and machining of blisks from solid

X. MODELING OF IFW AND LFW
Currently, optimization of the IFW and LFW process
parameters is mostly empirical. For more eﬃcient
application at the industrial scale, it is essential to
develop simulation models to predict temperature var-
iation, stress distribution, and distortions after welding
of large size components with complex geometries. By
developing eﬀective and robust models, the number of
experimental trials for process optimization is reduced
and signiﬁcant time and cost saving is provided.
IFW and LFW are complex thermomechanical pro-
cesses since steep temperature gradients and large
amounts of plastic deformation at very high strain rates
are induced in a narrow zone.
[52] Speciﬁcally, since many
aspects such as thermal proﬁle and strain rate at the
interface cannot be directly measured, due to severe local
plastic deformation, modeling is a very useful tool in
understanding the behavior of the material during weld-
ing.
[19] For modeling IFW and LFW reliably, it is essential
to know the following features [14,19,116,122,174,175,177,178]:
(a) Variation of the material properties such as ﬂow
stress and microstructure with temperature from
room temperature to 50 K (50 /C176 C) below the melt-
ing point and strain rates over a wide range (0.001
to 1600 s
/C0 1).
(b) Variation of thermal conductivity, speciﬁc heat,
thermal expansion, and elastic modulus with tem-
perature over a wide range (from ambient to
50 K (50 /C176 C) below melting point).
(c) Variation of friction coeﬃcient with temperature,
pressure, sliding speed, and material ﬂow.
(d) Thermal boundary conditions including heat con-
duction from contact area to the bulk of the weld
parts, heat loss to environment through convection
and radiation, and heat loss due to ﬂash forma-
tion.
(e) Heat generation by plastic work adiabatic heating.
Considering all of these necessary features, develop-
ment of a reliable process model for IFW/LFW is
challenging. Specially, only limited experimental equip-
ments are available to apply the very high strain rates
(up to 1600 s
/C0 1) experienced by interfacial material
during IFW/LFW and generate ﬂow stress and micro-
structure data. [19,37,131,132] In this case, extrapolation of
data obtained by compression, Gleeble, or torsion
testing is used for higher strain rates. [19,37,174]
The process models for LFW of Ni-based superalloys
are not available in the open literature while several IFW
process models are presented in literature for various
alloys. However, most of the IFW models do not
consider the rotational motion of the process. IFW of
Astroloy was modeled by Soucail et al. using a 2D
coupled thermal and mechanical ﬁnite element mod-
el.
[56,57] Hot torsion tests in the 1023 to 1473 K (750 to
1200 /C176 C) temperature range, 0.5 to 15 strain range, and
0.1 to 30 s /C0 1 strain rate range were carried out to develop
ﬂow stress constitutive equations and implement them
into the model. [57] The modeled peak temperature at the
interface during IFW, i.e., ~1493 K ( ~1220 /C176 C) is rela-
tively close to the one determined by infrared pyrometer,
1553 K (1280 /C176 C).[57] However, the model was validated
using a non-accurate temperature measurement method
for the interface, as explained in Section IV–F. Also, the
maximum strain rate in the weld area during IFW of
Astroloy is predicted to be about 0.15 s
/C0 1 which is
considerably smaller than the one (1 to 10 s /C0 1) estimated
from comparison of the microstructure of the welds with
those obtained from samples subjected to high strain rate
hot torsion testing. [57] Nonetheless, factors playing role
in this discrepancy have not been discussed in the above
study. One contributing factor can be neglecting the
rotational motion of the IFW process. Also, as discussed
in Section IV–G, extrapolation of thermomechanical
behavior from torsion testing to IFW can add to
uncertainties due to a considerable diﬀerence between
maximum achievable strain and strain rate in torsion
testing and those experienced by the material during
IFW.
Using DEFORM 2D, Wang et al. developed a
coupled thermal and mechanical ﬁnite element model
for IFW of RR
/C210 1000 tubes. [122] In this model, the
rotational velocity of ﬂywheel during experimental IFW
was recorded and used to calculate the rate of kinetic
energy loss and heat input. In addition, axial shortening
was recorded during the IFW experiments and imple-
mented into the model as a boundary condition to
determine the forging rate. Similar to other models, in
this work the rotational movement of the weld part
during IFW was not considered. Heat generation from
friction and torsional plastic deformation and heat loss
to environment, radiation, and conduction between and
within the weld parts were taken into account. Complex
contact between two deforming weld parts was simpli-
ﬁed by considering a single deforming weld part against
a ﬂat rigid plane. From this model, thermal history,
deformation of welding parts, and residual stresses were
predicted and compared with experimental measure-
ments. Temperature predictions were validated by
comparing the evolution of c¢ in the weldments with
those obtained from rapidly heated samples. A 70 K to
100 K (70 /C176 C to 100 /C176 C) temperature discrepancy was
observed between predictions and those obtained from
microstructure studies. The factors contributing to this
discrepancy have not been addressed in the study. Also,

the inﬂuence of the applied compressive strain during
IFW on c¢ evolution, as described in Section V–C, has
not been considered in the experimental thermal simu-
lations. However, the heating rate and peak temperature
predictions were found smaller than the reported data in
the literature. Also, the model generally overestimates
the residual stresses. For instance, the predicted hoop
stress (1900 MPa) near the inner diameter was found
considerably larger than the one (1500 MPa) measured
by neutron diﬀraction. These overestimations are partly
due to lack of creep strain rate data at temperatures
above 1023 K (750 /C176 C) and partly because of neglecting
DRX in the weld area.
Grant et al.
[52] proposed a model using Deform 2D to
compare the inﬂuence of the IFW parameters on the
development of the residual stresses, peak temperature,
and strain rates in TMAZ of RR /C210 1000 inertia friction
welds. Although the Grant et al. study[52] is based on the
Wang et al .[122] model, in the former: (i) better material
data base was included, (ii) the algorithm for smoothing
the forging curve and heating model was improved
substantially, and (iii) for estimation of residual stresses
an elastoplastic analysis was used instead of a purely
elastic one. Such improvements led to better predictions
for temperature and distribution of residual stresses.
In the Grant et al .
[52] study, the torque, angular
rotational speed, and axial shortening data measured
during IFW were implemented into the model to
determine the rate of heat input and forging rate as
boundary conditions. Also, a coupled thermomechani-
cal formulation was used to capture the interaction
between heating and material ﬂow to predict tempera-
ture proﬁle and axial shortening. To develop the ﬂow
stress relationships with temperature and strain rate of
deformation and implement them into the model,
compression testing in the range of 973 K to
1423 K (700 /C176 C to 1150 /C176 C) and strain rates from 0.01
to 20 s
/C0 1 were performed. The residual stress predictions
were validated against measurements using neutron
diﬀraction. Also, accuracy of temperature predictions
were evaluated by examination of c¢ distribution across
weld interface compared with those determined during
experimental thermal simulations. The model predicts
that with increasing pressure during welding, the width
of the TMAZ decreases whereas peak temperature and
strain rate increase. Also, the peak residual stresses in
the hoop direction close to the weld interface are almost
independent of the weld pressure though for smaller
welding pressure the high tensile hoop stress region is
larger, which is consistent with the larger width of the
TMAZ. The agreement between residual stress proﬁle
prediction and measurement in the hoop and radial
directions is very good. However, the predicted peak
residual stresses in the axial direction ( /C0 470 to
470 MPa) are signiﬁcantly smaller than the measured
ones ( /C0 740 to 900 MPa). This diﬀerence has been
related to neglecting the eﬀect of the clamping forces on
the residual axial stresses. It is determined that clamping
forces aﬀect the residual axial stresses while they have
little inﬂuence on the residual stresses in the hoop and
radial directions. Also, the model underestimates the
peak temperature across the weld by about
20 K (20 /C176 C). However, it must be noted that neglecting
the inﬂuence of the applied strain during IFW or LFW
on the dissolution kinetics of c¢ particles
[66] during
experimental thermal simulations aﬀects the accuracy of
the temperature estimations. This aspect has not been
reported in the author analysis and may be the source of
observed discrepancies.
Also, Grant et al.
[52] model predicts that the peak strain
rates are in the 1.4 to 4.5 s /C0 1 range with increasing the
weld pressure from 1 to 1.87 P. Such strain rates are not
comparable to values ( >1000 s /C0 1) reported by Midling
et al.[132] for RFW of Al-Mg-Si alloy. The diﬀerence may
be due to ignoring the rotational deformation in the Grant
et al .[52] model and thus neglecting the high rotational
strain rates at the interface.
The discrepancies between the predictions of diﬀerent
IFW process models as well as those between IFW
model predictions and experimental measurements indi-
cate that developing a reliable model for IFW and LFW
is diﬃcult and there is a clear need for extensive future
research. Such models need a very good deﬁnition of the
friction coeﬃcient and material behavior over a wide
temperature and strain rate ranges.
[14] Additionally, the
heating rate in the weld area is very high, leading to
extreme temperature gradients and, thus, development
of nonequilibrium conditions that are very hard to
model.
[14] The accurate modeling of material behavior at
the interface between deforming weld parts is also
complicated and diﬃcult since at the interface there is
severe plastic deformation.
[122] The model complexities
are even more important when it comes to LFW where
the asymmetric nature of the process in terms of heat
generation and plastic deformation bring major model-
ing challenges. Moreover, compared to IFW, much less
experimental data are available for LFW rendering the
validation process more diﬃcult.
XI. CONCLUSIONS
A literature survey on the published work regarding
IFW and LFW of Ni-based superalloys was carried out.
From this survey following conclusions can be made:
1. IFW and LFW are being used increasingly to gen-
erate high performance integral welds for various
Ni-based superalloy components in aerospace and
power generation gas turbines. The application of
these technologies for near-net-shape manufactur-
ing oﬀers many advantages over conventional fu-
sion welding, mechanical joining, and machining
from solid part techniques.
2. Amount of axial shortening can be considered as a
convenient and precise metric for evaluation of the
weld integrity. For obtaining a sound weld,
increasing the axial shortening beyond a critical
value is essential for expulsion of oxides and impu-
rities from the weld interface into the ﬂash. Also,
formation of continuous ﬂash is a good indication
of an integral weld.
3. In IFW and LFW joining occurs by mechanical
intermixing of the interfacial materials due to
1666—VOLUME 46A, APRIL 2015 METALLURGICAL AND MATERIALS TRANSACTIONS A

## Page 29

severe plastic deformation at high temperatures as
well as by diﬀusion of alloying elements across the
interface, electron sharing, and bond formation be-
tween atoms.
4. Exposure of the interface material to very high
temperatures at very high heating rates during
IFW and LFW along with applying severe plastic
deformation and rapid cooling upon the comple-
tion of welding induce signiﬁcant changes in the
microstructure and mechanical properties across
the weld. These mainly include substantial changes
in the distribution, size, and volume fraction of c¢
precipitates as well as variation in grain size.
5. For alloys with low c¢ volume fraction ( £30 pct),
almost complete dissolution of c¢ occurs in the
vicinity of the weld interface during IFW and
LFW and c¢ reprecipitation does not occur dur-
ing cooling. Therefore, a soft-region ( e.g., with a
hardness ~65 pct of the parent material value for
LFWed Waspaloy) extending from the weld
interface to 1.5 to 2 mm from it develops.
Therefore, it is needed to conduct a proper
PWHT for recovering the c¢ particles and restor-
ing the hardness.
6. In contrast, for alloys having higher c¢ volume
fraction ( >40 pct), c¢ particles dissolve during
welding but reprecipitation of very fine c¢ particles
occurs near the weld interface during cooling cycle
of the welding due to high levels of c¢ forming ele-
ments. The c¢ reprecipitation results in relatively
higher hardness values in TMAZ.
7. The spherical morphology, unimodal size distribu-
tion, high density, small size, and small interparti-
cle spacing are the characteristics of the
reprecipitated c¢. Thus, it is essential to perform a
proper PWHT to generate the desired distribution,
morphology, size, and volume fraction for c¢ parti-
cles.
8. During IFW and LFW, substantial grain reﬁne-
ment due to DRX occurs in the weld area and
TMAZ. This is speciﬁcally damaging for creep
resistance of single crystal Ni-based superalloys
since the single-crystalline nature of the alloy is
lost. Formation of a continuous Hf-rich oxide ﬁlm
at the weld interface of single crystal Ni-based su-
peralloys, which is harmful for the weld integrity,
is another challenge for friction welding of single
crystals.
9. Large tensile residual stresses develop in the IF-
Wed and LFWed Ni-based superalloys. This
necessitates conducting an appropriate PWHT to
relieve or reduce the residual stresses.
10. Process models are being developed to determine
the thermal and deformation conditions imposed
on the material during IFW and LFW. However,
development of a reliable model for IFW or LFW
is challenging because of the need for well deﬁni-
tion of the material properties over a wide temper-
ature and strain rate ranges, nonequilibrium
thermal conditions, and severe plastic deformation
at the weld interface.
