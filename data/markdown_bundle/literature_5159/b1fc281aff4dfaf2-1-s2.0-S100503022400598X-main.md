Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) exhibit outstanding strength at room temperature, but their high- 
temperature applications are hindered by severe strain-softening. Here, we report slip-band-driven dy- 
namic recrystallization to enhance the high-temperature strain hardening of HfNbTaTiZr RHEA. By intro- 
ducing partial lattice defects through hot forging, we increase the nucleation sites for dynamic recrys- 
tallization during subsequent thermomechanical deformation, thus suppressing the strain-softening be- 
havior. We reveal that the high-temperature deformation is governed by the formation of heterogeneous 
bimodal grains along slip bands, which effectively constrain dislocation motion and improve strength, 
while microbands prevent premature failure. The fracture mode also changes from ductile to mixed to 
cleavage-dominated with increasing temperature. Our results demonstrate a simple and effective method 
for overcoming high-temperature strain-softening for BCC high entropy alloys.

1. Introduction 
The aerospace sector’s relentless pursuit of enhanced fuel eﬃ- 
ciency and environmental compliance demands materials with ex- 
ceptional high-temperature strength. Traditional superalloys, like 
Ni-based superalloys, have struggled to meet the demanding 
strength criteria when exposed to the extreme temperatures as- 
sociated with their melting points [ 1 ]. This necessitates novel 
alloy design for next-gen high-temperature or refractory mate- 
rials. High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are catalyzing this shift. Par- 
ticularly, refractory HEAs (RHEAs) with high-melting-point com- 
ponents promise structural stability and heat resistance in ex- 
treme temperature environments [ 2-7 ]. Even at 1600 °C, NbMoTaW 
RHEAs can maintain a high compressive strength of up to 600 MPa, 
a temperature at which traditional steels and Ni-based superalloys 
have already melted or failed [ 8 ]. The exceptional performance is 
attributed to solid-solution strengthening and the controlled dif- 
fusion of refractory elements within the alloy [ 9-11 ]. Despite ex- 
hibiting remarkable strength at elevated temperatures, the bottle- 
neck for utilizing them as high-temperature structural materials is 
persistent softening after yielding serving under monotonic tensile

gineering applications of RHEAs, as it increases the risk of material 
failure suddenly without any early warning, primarily attributable 
to localized stress concentration, especially for fatigue loading

solve post-yield softening through precipitation strengthening in 
compression tests of RHEAs [ 17 , 18 ], the incoherent precipitates at 
grain boundaries induce strain localization causing the intergranu- 
lar fracture under tensile loading [ 19 ]. The twinning-induced plas- 
ticity (TWIP) and transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) effect is 
regarded as a prospective toughening strategy [ 20-23 ]. However, 
the occurrence of TWIP and TRIP is typically associated with alloys 
possessing low stacking fault energy (SFE). The temperature depen- 
dence of SFE dictates that as temperature increases, the SFE also 
increases [ 24 ]. Thus, although some RHEAs may exhibit suﬃciently 
low SFE to undergo the TRIP effect at room temperature, their 
SFE becomes too high to satisfy the conditions for the TRIP effect 
as the temperature rises. Moreover, phase transformations in BCC 
RHEAs often nucleate at grain boundaries [ 23 , 25 ], a characteristic 
that is detrimental to the TWIP effect. Consequently, exploiting the 
TWIP or TRIP effect as a toughening strategy at high temperatures 
remains virtually unattainable in BCC RHEAs. In addition, during 
high-temperature deformation of RHEAs, recrystallized grains typ- 
ically emerge at grain boundaries, leading to accelerated softening

TEM techniques are conducted. Furthermore, we elucidate the nu- 
cleation and strengthening mechanisms associated with the recrys- 
tallization process, which are attributed to the presence of intra- 
granular slip bands and the formation of multi-layered recrystal- 
lized grains at the grain boundaries. 
2. Materials and experimental procedure 
The present RHEA specimens were meticulously prepared 
through a sequence of controlled steps as indicated in Fig. 1 . Ini- 
tially, ingots weighing approximately 40 g each were meticulously 
crafted by subjecting a blend of exceptionally pure metals (with 
a minimum purity of 99.95 %) to arc-melting, maintaining spe- 
ciﬁc molar ratios. To ensure optimal chemical uniformity, these 
initial ingots underwent no less than four additional remelting 
cycles within an environment of high-purity argon, employing a 
Ti-gettering technique. Subsequently, a batch of tens ingots was 
subjected to cold crucible induction levitation melting within an 
argon-ﬁlled atmosphere, yielding a larger primary billet measuring 
around 40 mm in diameter and approximately 31 mm in thickness. 
To enhance the initial grain structure, a high-temperature forging 
technique was employed, involving multiple orientations applied to 
the primary billet. The process commenced with heating the pri- 
mary billet to 10 0 0 °C, followed by an isothermal multi-directional 
forging. The subsequent step involved air cooling, ultimately result- 
ing in the attainment of a ﬁnal thickness measuring approximately 
15 mm. This thickness was achieved through a reduction rate of 
approximately 52 %. 
High-temperature tensile evaluations were executed using an 
MTS-653 testing apparatus, maintaining a strain rate of 1 × 10−3 
s−1 . Dog-bone-shaped specimens with pin-loaded ends were 
meticulously fashioned by electrical discharge wire-cutting for ten- 
sion assessments in adherence to ASTM E8M-09. These specimens 
exhibited dimensions of 14 mm in gage length, 3.5 mm in width, 
and 1.5 mm in thickness. The high-temperature tensile specimens 
underwent a controlled heating regimen, ascending to tempera- 
tures of 40 0, 60 0, 80 0, and 10 0 0 °C, succeeded by a 10-min hold 
at the designated temperature. For precise temperature monitoring 
within a range of ±3 K, a thermocouple was securely positioned in 
the sample holder. The elastic portion of the tensile stress-strain 
curve was accurately rectiﬁed through the utilization of a 12 mm 
gage length extensometer. Notably, each testing condition was sub- 
jected to three replicate tensile samples. 
Phase analysis of mechanically polished specimens was per- 
formed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Dmax-2550 X-ray 
diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) with Cu-K α radiation. Evaluation 
of the microstructure and deformation structure for both me- 
chanically and electrochemically polished samples was carried 
out through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) utilizing an 
SU1510 (Hitachi, Japan). Additionally, electron backscatter diffrac- 
tion (EBSD) analysis was conducted using an Apollo300 scanning 
electron microscope (CamScan, UK) equipped with an HKL Tech- 
nology EBSD system. The microstructure of the samples was ex- 
amined by a JEM-2100F (JEOL, Japan) transmission electron micro- 
scope (TEM) operating at 200 kV. The TEM specimens were pre- 
pared by grinding them to about 50 μm thickness, followed by 
twin-jet electro-polishing in a solution containing ∼6 vol% per- 
chloric acid, ∼34 % n–butyl alcohol, and ∼60 vol% methyl alco- 
hol at 243 K and 20 V. The specimens were then further thinned

3. Results 
3.1. Initial microstructures 
The XRD patterns acquired for HfNbTaTiZr RHEAs, prepared 
through both levitation melting and forging, are depicted in 
Fig. 2 (a). It is noteworthy that both patterns exclusively exhibit 
peaks characteristic of a body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal struc- 
ture. This identiﬁcation is supported by the TEM results ( Fig. 3 ). 
While the process of hot forging does not induce phase trans- 
formation, it triggers tiny alterations in the crystal structure. 
Compared to the levitation melting samples, hot forging samples 
demonstrate an increase in the intensity of the (200) peak. This 
phenomenon is attributed to the absorption of the original smaller 
grains and the preferential growth of the (100) texture during the 
high-temperature forging process, as supported by further EBSD 
images ( Fig. 2 (d, e)). 
Fig. 2 (b, c) and Fig. 2 (d, e) show the typical microstructure of 
as-levitation melting and as-forging specimens, respectively. Both 
the two specimens exhibit equiaxial-grained microstructures. Post- 
forging, the grains exhibit a propensity to transform into oblate 
and elongated forms. Simultaneously, the grain dimensions un- 
dergo an incremental enlargement, transitioning from the initial 
average grain size of ∼189.6 μm to the present average grain size 
of ∼230.9 μm, as described in the insets of Fig. 2 (b, c). These 
morphologies are clariﬁed by the EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure results 
presented in Fig. 2 (d, e), which also further conﬁrms the single- 
phase BCC structure. However, observable crystal orientation vari- 
ations within the grain interiors post hot forging highlight signiﬁ- 
cant intragranular deformation. Unidentiﬁed white regions on cer- 
tain grain boundaries point to areas of signiﬁcant strain localiza- 
tion. Additionally, the detection of insuﬃcient dynamic recrystal- 
lization at these grain boundaries is notable. Speciﬁcally, areas of 
high strain at the triple junctions of grain boundaries are indica- 
tive of the onset of ﬁne recrystallized grains, a phenomenon cor- 
roborated and illustrated by arrows in the EBSD maps (Fig. S1 in 
Supplementary materials). 
Representative TEM results of two samples are presented in 
Fig. 3 . The bright-ﬁeld TEM image and corresponding selected area 
electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of as-levitation melting RHEA, 
as depicted in Fig. 3 (a), demonstrate that the crystal band axis of 
the alloy conforms to the [011] direction of the BCC phase, and the 
(01 ¯1 ), (21 ¯1 ), and (200) crystal planes are also labeled. The HRTEM 
image in Fig. 3 (b) and the corresponding applied fast Fourier trans- 
form (FFT) presented in Fig. 3 (b2 ) conﬁrm that the as-levitation 
melting RHEA is a single BCC phase. Similarly, TEM images of the 
post-forging specimens in Fig. 3 (c) reveal the presence of residual 
dislocation lines within insuﬃcient recrystallized microstructures. 
The corresponding SAED patterns and HRTEM image coupled with 
subsequent FFT analyses in Fig. 3 (d) once again substantiate the 
absence of phase transformation during the hot forging process. 
EPMA test is employed for detecting element segregation at grain 
boundaries, and it reveals that the distribution of Hf, Nb, Ta, Ti, 
and Zr elements along the grain boundaries is uniform in both the 
as-levitation melting RHEAs and as-forging RHEAs samples, elim- 
inating the possibility of the precipitates formation at the grain 
boundaries, as shown in Fig. 4 (a, b).

4.1. Slip bands induced dynamic recrystallization 
EBSD analyses for the before and after fractured structures 
of the F400 sample were conducted to determine the underly- 
ing deformation mechanism at 400 °C, as shown in Fig. 8 . The 
high strain on grain boundaries before deformation, as shown in 
Fig. 8 (a3 ), was identiﬁed due to the incomplete recrystallization 
during the forging process. Approximately 5.9 vol% of the deformed 
microstructure at grain boundaries as shown in Fig. 8 (a4 ), arises 
from high-strain localization. Comparatively, when examining the 
deformed microstructure after high-temperature tensile deforma- 
tion, a signiﬁcant presence of SBs inside the grain can be observed, 
as indicated by arrows 1–5 in Fig. 8 (b1 –b5 ). The misorientation 
evolution curves in the inset of Fig. 8 (b2 ) discriminate against the 
possibility of twins in the deformed microstructure indicated by 
the arrows. The crystal defects introduced during hot forging re- 
main within the grain interior and undergo deformation preferen- 
tially with increasing strain and elevated temperature. Therefore, 
SBs initiate from the lattice defects within the grain interiors, grad- 
ually extending to the grain boundaries and terminating. The high 
strain localization near the SBs indicates intense deformation as in- 
dicated in Fig. 8 (b3 ). This deformation pattern has not been identi- 
ﬁed in previous studies [ 3 , 14 , 16 , 19 , 23 ]. Furthermore, the increased 
strain localization at the grain boundaries ( Fig. 8 (a3 )) compared to 
the unstretched deformation structure ( Fig. 8 (b3 )) suggests the in- 
volvement of grain boundaries in the deformation mechanism. 
The grain orientation spread (GOS) map shows recrystallized 
structures indicated by the blue particles dispersed inside grains 
and grain boundaries in Fig. 8 (b4 ). Grain nucleation initiated 
through the protrusion of grain boundaries is referred to as dis- 
continuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) [ 40 ]. Concurrently, 
the dislocation network generated during hot deformation driv- 
ing grain nucleation inside grains was identiﬁed as continuous dy-

formation of the F400 specimen may be attributed to the hetero- 
geneous bimodal grain structure induced by the slip bands. 
In addition, the intergranular recrystallized grains due to DDRX, 
beneﬁtting from direct access to grain boundaries, promote their 
rapid growth through favorable diffusion pathways, driven primar- 
ily by signiﬁcant strain-induced grain boundary migration (SIBM) 
[ 43 ]. The dynamic heterogeneous structure, comprising both DDRX 
and CDRX grains, impels the sustained strain-hardening behavior, 
which is discussed in detail as follows. The dislocations accumu- 
late at the interfaces between SBs and the matrix when they en- 
counter CDRX grains with large effective radii. This accumulation 
results in a reduction in the mean free path of dislocations, leading 
to signiﬁcant strain hardening, in addition to the Orowan bowing 
mechanism. For the grain subjected to a relatively high strain, as 
indicated by arrows 2 and 3 in Fig. 8 (b), planar dislocation arrays 
on several independent slip planes form dislocation network sub- 
structures, further reducing the mean free path. Therefore, this elu- 
cidated deformation mechanism plays a crucial role in sustaining a 
high strain-hardening rate and delaying necking at high levels of 
strain and stress. 
Furthermore, the detailed TEM analysis of the deformation 
structure of the F400 specimen has uncovered the microscale 
mechanism responsible for signiﬁcant strain hardening involv- 
ing dislocation movement around SBs, as shown in Fig. 10 . The 
bright-ﬁeld TEM image illustrated in Fig. 10 (a) demonstrates that 
the main deformation characteristics are deformation-induced mi- 
crobands. The microbands act as an important deformation mode 
to provide an additional work-hardening source to accommodate 
the imposed strain, leading to exceptional plastic stability at the 
high-strength level. The HRTEM image shown in Fig. 10 (b) exhibits 
the dislocation pile-up at the interface between the microband and 
matrix. Due to the presence of LAGBs between microbands and 
matrix, microbands facilitate the control of deformation stability, 
commonly referred to as the microband-induced plasticity effect, 
without being susceptible to rapid fracture [ 44 ]. 
The schematic diagram schematically illustrates the deforma- 
tion mechanism of the F400 specimen, as shown in Fig. 8 (c). Dur- 
ing the high-temperature tensile process, dynamic recrystallization 
occurs at both the original grain boundaries and inside the grains, 
resulting in the formation of a dynamic heterogeneous grain struc- 
ture (DHGS). This DHGS signiﬁcantly diminishes the mean free 
path of dislocations, consequently causing dislocation accumula- 
tion at the new grain boundaries, which promotes strain harden- 
ing, ultimately leading to an increasing strain hardening rate with 
increasing strain. 
4.2. Recrystallization at grain boundaries 
The present RHEAs deformation model gradually concentrates 
at the grain boundaries as the deformation temperature increases, 
as shown in Fig. 11 , which displays the fractured deformation 
structures of the F600 and F800 specimens. Fig. 11 (a) reveals a sig- 
niﬁcant reduction in the number of intragranular SBs for the F600 
specimen, with only a minimal amount indicated by the white ar- 
rows. As shown in Fig. 11 (a2 ), strain primarily accumulates around 
the grain boundaries, facilitated by high temperature during the 
deformation process, promoting the formation of dynamically re- 
crystallized grains at the grain boundaries. The black arrows 1–5 
in Fig. 11 (a) indicate the high strain localization and recrystallized 
grains at the grain boundaries, where the volume fraction of re- 
crystallized grains increased from 2 % in the F400 specimen to 
2.2 % with a growth rate of 10 %. Meanwhile, the average grain area 
increased to 131.0 ± 115.9 μm2 , with a growth rate of 24 %. With 
further temperature increases, the intragranular SB structure disap- 
peared for the F800 specimen, accompanied by partial intracrys- 
talline plastic deformation (white arrows in Fig. 11 (b3 )) and se- 
vere grain boundary deformation (black arrows in Fig. 11 (b3 )). Se- 
vere strain localization promotes the generation of DDRX at grain 
boundaries, leading to an increase in the volume fraction of re- 
crystallized grains to 3.4 %, with an average grain area growing by 
150 % to 264.5 ± 201.6 μm2 than the F400 specimen. 
Traditionally, the dynamic recrystallization at grain boundaries 
often accompanies strain-softening behavior, occurring when the 
temperature is suﬃciently high (usually ≥ 0.5 Tm ), where grain 
boundary sliding is driven, indicating dominant deformation at the 
grain boundaries [ 6 , 45 ]. The strain-softening behavior observed in 
the F10 0 0 specimen indicates a potential grain boundary sliding 
mechanism, although this aspect has not been extensively inves- 
tigated as it falls outside the primary scope of the current study. 
However, the EBSD results ( Fig. 11 (a3 , b3 )) conﬁrm that even at 
800 °C, intracrystalline plastic deformation still plays a signiﬁcant 
role, as evidenced by the high strain localization indicated by the 
white arrows. This is the main reason why F800 samples main- 
tain signiﬁcant strain hardening despite dynamic recrystallization 
at grain boundaries. It is worth noting that the DDRX partially alle- 
viates high strain localization at grain boundaries rather than elim- 
inates it. Therefore, the increasing plasticity deformation leads to 
crack initiation in these regions and eventual intergranular frac- 
ture, as illustrated in the fractography in Fig. 7 (d2 ) of the F800 
specimen. The observations in Fig. 11 (a, b) lead to the deforma- 
tion mechanism associated with the heterogeneous necklace mi- 
crostructures, different from the F400 sample, as drawn schemat- 
ically in Fig. 11 (c). Dynamic recrystallization occurs along grain 
boundaries, especially at triple junctions. Due to the concentration 
of strain primarily at grain boundaries and the absence of recrys- 
tallization induced by intragranular SBs, there is an increased risk 
of transgranular fracture for F60 0 and F80 0 samples.

5. Conclusions 
In this work, we investigated the room and elevated tempera- 
tures monotonic tensile behavior of the forged HfNbTaTiZr RHEAs, 
and extensive XRD, SEM, EBSD, and TEM investigations were car- 
ried out to unravel failure origins. The key ﬁndings were summa- 
rized as follows. After hot forging, the current RHEAs maintain a 
single BCC structure without transformation, with residual strain 
only at the grain boundaries and little within the grains. The room- 
temperature tensile strength of the forged samples increases by 
15 % from the YS of 875 ± 11 MPa to 1004 ± 40 MPa, yet still ex- 
hibits strain-softening behavior. The high-temperature deformation 
is dominated by slip-band-driven dynamic recrystallization, which 
enhances the strain-hardening behavior by forming heterogeneous 
bimodal grains that effectively constrain dislocation motion and 
improve strength, while microbands prevent premature failure. The 
fracture mode varied from ductile to mixed to cleavage-dominated 
with increasing temperature. This work demonstrated slip-band- 
driven dynamic recrystallization for overcoming high-temperature 
strain-softening for BCC high entropy alloys.
