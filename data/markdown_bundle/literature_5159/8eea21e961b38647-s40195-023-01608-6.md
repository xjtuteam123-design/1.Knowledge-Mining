Abstract
The new Ni–Co-based superalloy featuring a "fusion structure" was produced utilizing electron beam smelting layered 
solidification technology (EBSL). Experimental examination of hot compression deformation with varied settings for EBSL 
and conventional duplex process melting Ni–Co superalloys was performed. As per the study, EBSL-Ni–Co superalloys 
exhibited enhanced recrystallization susceptibility during hot deformation. Furthermore, elevating deformation temperature, 
lowering strain rate, and augmenting strain collectively contribute to enlarging the volume fraction of dynamically recrys-
tallized grains. Aberrant growth of grains occurred when the deformation temperature equaled γ′ sub-solvus temperature 
and the strain rate was slower. Moreover, exceeding the γ′ solvus temperature during deformation significantly increases the 
particle size of dynamic recrystallization (DRX) grains. The γ′ phase can effectively modulate the DRX grain size through 
the pegging effect. Additionally, it was revealed that the presence of the fusion structure aids in the generation of continuous 
dynamic recrystallization, discontinuous dynamic recrystallization, and twinning-induced dynamic recrystallization while 
the alloy undergoes hot deformation. This mechanism promotes DRX granule formation and permits complete recrystal-
lization. Ultimately, the fusion structure was identified as playing a catalytic role in the dynamic recrystallization process 
of the new Ni–Co superalloy.

1 Introduction
Deformed superalloys are the preferred materials for tur -
bine disks in advanced aero-engines due to their favorable 
performance-to-cost ratio and exceptional high-temperature 
capabilities. A new Ni–Co-based superalloy with a high-
temperature capacity has been created by incorporating 
 Co3Ti into the U720Li turbine disk alloy [1 ]. Nevertheless, 
the enhanced strength of the alloy will subsequently pose 
greater challenges to the alloy forging process [2 ]. Incor-
porating Co3Ti with a γ/γ′ structure amplifies the extent of 
microsegregation within the alloy, subsequently heighten-
ing the demands for homogenization of the alloy. An urgent 
necessity is to manage the microscopic segregation during 
the production of this novel Ni–Co-based superalloy and to 
optimize its malleability.
The electron beam smelting layered solidification tech-
nology (EBSL) capitalizes on electron beam refining's 
high vacuum and rapid cooling rate, in conjunction with 
the "small to large" principle in additive manufacturing, to 
mitigate the extent of segregation in large alloy ingots [3 ]. 
After a complete homogenization treatment, the research 
revealed that the grain size of the alloy had not increased 
substantially, and the fusion structure was also preserved [4].

These microstructures have the potential to provide a novel 
approach to addressing the aforementioned problems. Previ-
ous investigations into electron beam melting primarily cen-
tered on the purification and homogenization of superalloys 
[3, 5]. On the basis of previous research, the fusion structure 
can substantially improve the thermoplastic properties and 
recrystallization behavior of an alloy, but its effect on the 
recrystallization mechanism remains unclear.
Over the past few decades, extensive research has been 
undertaken to explore the hot deformation behavior and 
recrystallization mechanisms of superalloys. The hot defor-
mation behavior of U720Li superalloy with coarse, fine, and 
mixed grain revealed that reduction in grain size accelerates 
the dynamic recrystallization (DRX) kinetics and the coarse 
grain structure is susceptible to continuous dynamic recrys-
tallization (CDRX) [6 ]. When a superalloy is subjected to 
hot compression deformation, the γ′ phase and carbides 
facilitate the formation of high-density dislocation substruc-
tures and inhibit the growth of DRX grains [7]. Furthermore, 
the frequency of DRX occurrence rises with the augmenta-
tion in size and number of MC carbides [8]. Examining the 
hot deformation behavior of powdered Ni-based superalloys 
with different initial structures, the researchers discovered 
that coarse and large grain samples have poor hot workabil-
ity [9]. Research on the nucleation of DRX during hot defor-
mation of columnar grain Ni-based superalloys unveiled that 
dynamic recrystallization primarily initiates as discontinu-
ous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) and the volume frac-
tion of DRX grains generated through CDRX experiences 
gradual growth with increasing deformation [10]. Never -
theless, most investigations into hot deformation behavior 
have primarily focused on a singular structure comprising 
either fine or coarse grains. EBSL-Ni–Co superalloy has 
incorporated a fusion structure into its ingots, which retain 
their distinctive coarse–fine-coarse particulate arrangement 
even after homogenization [3, 11]. Hence, there is a need to 
explore the hot deformation behavior and the corresponding 
deformation mechanism of the new Ni–Co-based superalloy 
featuring a "fusion structure".
In this study, the newly developed Ni–Co-based superal-
loys, produced via EBSL and conventional duplex process 
melting (DM), underwent hot compression tests employing 
varied deformation strains. Additionally, hot compression 
experiments were conducted on the EBSL alloy at γ′ sub-/
super-solvus temperature using different strain rates. Com-
prehensive microscopic characterization of the hot-com-
pressed samples will unveil the influences of microstruc-
ture, γ′ phase, and strain rates on DRX behavior. Additional 
attention was directed toward the impact of the fusion 
structure on dynamic recrystallization, and an analysis of 
the recrystallization mechanism of the EBSL alloy was con-
ducted. This investigation has the potential to establish a 
theoretical basis for designing and optimizing the integrated 
control of microstructure and hot deformation processing in 
wrought superalloys.

2.2 Hot Compression Tests and Microstructure 
Observation
Hot compression experiments were conducted using the 
Gleeble 1500 thermomechanical simulator system. The φ 8 
mm × 12 mm compression specimens were extracted from 
homogenized EBSL-Ni–Co and DM-Ni–Co superalloys, 
depicted in Fig. 2a. Figure 2b illustrates the hot compression 
experimental procedure, parameters, and sample morphol-
ogy prior to and after hot compression. The hot compres-
sion experiment procedure involves heating up to the desired 
temperature at a rate of 10 °C/s, holding the temperature for 
600 s to ensure uniformity, and then initiating the experi-
ment following the designated parameters. Upon completion 
of compression, the sample should be quenched in water. 
The hot compression experiments involve deformation tem-
peratures of 1120 °C and 1160 °C, strain rates of 0.001  s−1, 
0.01  s−1, 0.1  s−1, and 1  s−1, as well as strains of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 
and 0.7. Two tantalum sheets are placed between the sample 
and the compression mold during the hot compression trials 
to lessen friction and preserve consistent deformation. Fig-
ure 2c illustrates the sampling and characterization orienta-
tions for electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) tests. EBSD specimen 
preparation steps: First, the heated compression deformation 
specimen is wire-cut into a 3-mm-thick sheet sample along 
the axis. The sheet sample was then mechanically polished 
and finally, 3.5 h of vibrational polishing with a 50 nm sili-
con dioxide polishing solution. TEM specimen preparation 
consists of initial wire-cutting of the heated compression 
deformation specimen into a 0.8 mm thick sheet along the 
axis. The material is subsequently mechanically ground to 
a thickness of 50 μm. Finally, the samples undergo elec-
trolytic double-spray thinning at − 20 °C with 20 V. The 
microstructure of the central region of the deformed sample 
is assessed using a JSM-IT800 scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) equipped with an Oxford symmetry EBSD detec-
tor. The parameters for EBSD characterization tests in this 
study were as follows: an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a 
working distance ranging from 15 to 20 mm, and a sample 
tilt angle of 70°. Microstructure observations were carried 
out using a TEM equipped with a 200 kV JEM-F200. Dis-
tinguishing criteria based on the misorientation angle of 
adjacent grains allowed differentiation between low-angle 
grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2°–10°), medium-angle grain 
boundaries (MAGBs, 10–15°), and high-angle grain bounda-
ries (HAGBs, > 15°).

3.2 True Stress–Strain Curves
Figure 4 illustrates the true stress-true strain curves of the 
new Ni–Co superalloy obtained from hot compression 
experiments conducted under different deformation param-
eters and microstructures. The true stress–true strain curve 
of the new Ni–Co superalloy under hot compression, as 
shown in Fig. 4, exhibits three distinct stages: strain harden-
ing, rheological softening, and steady-state rheology. During 
the initial deformation stage, dislocation density increases 
rapidly and accumulates as strain increases [13, 14]. During 
the strain hardening stage, the flow stress increases sharply, 
revealing the effect of work hardening. Subsequently, as 
strain increases, the dynamic softening mechanism becomes 
prominent, and the deformation specimen transitions into the 
rheological softening stage. The dynamic softening mecha-
nism encompasses dynamic recovery (DRV) and DRX [15]. 
Since the range of DRX in the deformed specimen gradually 
increases with increasing strain [15]. The delayed decrease in 
flow stress can be attributed to the dynamic softening effect 
rather than the work hardening effect. As strain increases to 
a point where work hardening and dynamic softening reach

4.3.1 DDRX Mechanism
Figure  10 illustrates the DDRX mechanism involved in 
the dynamic recrystallization of the EBSL-Ni–Co superal-

the misorientation angle from the point of origin experiences 
an initial rise followed by a decline. This behavior signifies 
the bulging of the original grain boundary. As depicted in 
Fig. 10d, the grain boundaries of the deformed alloy exhibit 
significant bow convexity, which would provide a site for the 
DRX grains to nucleate via the DDRX mechanism. As illus-
trated in Fig. 10e, the DDRX mechanism's dislocation evolu-
tion transpires within the deformed grains. The process starts 
with free dislocations getting tangled together. After that, 
they get rearranged into dislocation walls when a certain 
density is reached. In the end, sub-grains are formed. Previ-
ous researches have revealed that the emergence of DRX in 
deformed alloys relies on the disparity in dislocation den-
sity across the alloy [34– 36]. Consequently, the difference 
in dislocation density caused by deformation is proportional 
to the size difference between adjacent granules in an alloy.

the DDRX mechanism for the EBSL-Ni–Co superalloy. As

4.3.5 Comprehensive Analysis
In summary, the dynamic recrystallization mechanism 
occurs during the hot deformation of the EBSL-Ni–Co 
superalloy, as illustrated in Fig.  14. The dynamic recrys-
tallization mechanism in the EBSL-Ni–Co superalloy 
is primarily driven by DDRX, CDRX, TDRX, and PSN 
mechanisms. The extent of deformation differs between 
coarse and fine grains due to the presence of the fusion struc-
ture during hot deformation. The EBSL-Ni–Co superalloy 
achieves the critical dislocation density required for dynamic 
recrystallization more readily through the introduction of a 
fusion structure. The fusion structure in the EBSL-Ni–Co 
superalloy enhances the occurrence of dynamic recrystal-
lization. And the DRX grains become markedly finer when 
the deformation temperature is within the γ′ sub-solvus 
range (1120 °C).
