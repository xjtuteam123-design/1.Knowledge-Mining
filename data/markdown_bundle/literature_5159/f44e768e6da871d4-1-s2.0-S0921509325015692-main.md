1. Introduction
Engineering structures and components are often subjected to severe 
loading conditions, necessitating the development of novel materials 
with exceptional mechanical properties. Among the latest and most 
promising material systems for advanced applications are High Entropy 
Alloys (HEAs) and Medium Entropy Alloys (MEAs). These alloys are 
characterized by their equiatomic or near-equiatomic compositions, 
which diverge from traditional alloying principles that typically rely on 
a single principal element [ 1 ]. Consequently, the term Multi-Principal 
Element Alloys (MPEAs) has gained traction to describe this innova -
tive class of materials [ 2 ]. MPEAs exhibit remarkable mechanical 
properties across a broad range of temperatures, including cryogenic 
conditions. Notably, several MPEAs have demonstrated simultaneous 
enhancements in both strength and ductility at cryogenic temperatures; 
a phenomenon attributed to the activation of multiple deformation 
mechanisms in addition to crystallographic slip. These mechanisms 
include twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) and transformation-induced 
plasticity (TRIP), both of which contribute to the alloys ’ superior 
performance [ 3 ].
The dominant deformation mechanisms in HEAs include slip, twin -
ning, and phase transformation [ 4 ]. Slip is the most common deforma -
tion mechanism observed in HEAs such as AlCoCrFeNi, where it was 
found that slip was the dominant deformation mechanism at room 
temperature. At the atomic scale, slip involves the motion of dislocations 
along specific crystallographic planes and directions, enabling plastic 
deformation through the collective shearing of atomic layers [ 5 ]. 
Twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) can also occur in HEAs and involves 
the formation of deformation twins, which can improve the ductility and 
strength of the material. It has been reported that the Cantor alloy, 
FeMnCrCoNi, considered one of the first and most well-known HEAs, 
and played a key role in establishing the concept of HEAs, experiences 
twinning as the primary deformation mechanism at cryogenic temper -
atures (e.g., 77 K) and at high strain levels [ 6 , 7 ]. TWIP behavior has also 
been observed in CrCoNi-based MEAs and other FCC-structured HEAs at 
low temperatures and under high strain rate conditions, reinforcing its 
role in strain rate-dependent plasticity [ 8 – 10 ].

Strain rate sensitivity plays a pivotal role in the activation of TRIP 
and TWIP mechanisms. At high strain rates, adiabatic heating and 
reduced time for diffusion-assisted nucleation may suppress phase 
transformations; whereas, moderate rates may enhance twinning or 
phase transitions due to local stress concentration [ 13 , 14 ]. The defor -
mation behavior through TWIP and TRIP shows a more pronounced 
strain rate dependence compared to slip-dominated responses and is 
highly dependent on crystal orientation [ 15 ]. However, studying these 
effects in polycrystalline materials can be challenging due to the 
simultaneous presence of multiple crystal orientations and grain 
boundary effects. Single crystals serve as a model system for evaluating 
the fundamental mechanical response of MPEAs by eliminating grain 
boundary effects and providing consistent orientation control [ 16 ]. 
Recent studies have shown that single-crystal HEAs, such as CrFeCoNi 
and CoCrFeMnNi, display distinct dislocation activities and strain rate 
dependencies compared to their polycrystalline counterparts [ 17 – 19 ]. 
Despite the growing interest in MPEAs, research on the strain rate 
sensitivity of single crystalline MPEAs remains limited, particularly 
under dynamic loading conditions.
VFeCoCrNi is a multi-principal element alloy that has been recently 
studied for its unique mechanical properties [ 13 , 20 – 24 ]. In terms of its 
mechanical properties, single crystal VFeCoCrNi HEA loaded along 
[111] direction has been found to exhibit a combined TWIP and TRIP 
effect with a unique FCC to BCC martensitic transformation at cryogenic 
temperatures, leading to increased strength. In contrast, deformation at 
room temperature was characterized by initial slip, followed by a 
delayed activation of the TWIP effect at higher strain levels [ 20 ]. The 
alloy has shown promising results in terms of its strength, ductility, and 
fracture toughness levels. Recent studies have investigated the room and

(LN: 77 K) across four crystallographic orientations: [110], [123], 
[001], and [111]. The focus was on stress – strain response, slip system 
activation, and microstructural evolution using Digital Image Correla -
tion (DIC) and Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD). The results 
revealed strong orientation dependence under all loading conditions. At 
RT in quasi-static loading, [111] exhibited the highest yield stress (197 
MPa), followed by [110] (117 MPa), [123] (104 MPa), and [001] (102 
MPa). Dynamic RT loading increased yield stresses significantly, with 
[111] reaching 310 ± 15 MPa (57.4 % increase), [110] at 167 ± 22 MPa 
(42.7 % increase), [123] at 148 ± 8 MPa (42.3 % increase), and [001] at 
180 ± 1 MPa (77 % increase). Under cryogenic dynamic conditions, 
further increases were observed: [111] reached 450 ± 21 MPa (45.2 % 
increase over RT dynamic), [110] at 295 ± 5 MPa (76.7 % increase), 
[123] at 298 ± 7 MPa (101.3 % increase), and [001] at 271 ± 4 MPa 
(50.6 % increase).
Deformation in all cases was governed by slip-dominated mecha -
nisms, with the specific slip systems activated varying with orientation 
and strain rate. For [110] and [123], the (111)[10 1] system was 
consistently active, driven by high Schmid factors. The [001] orientation 
exhibited multiple activated slip systems of near-equal Schmid factors, 
contributing to more complex deformation. In [111], multiple systems 
were active early in deformation, but two became dominant under 
quasi-static loading, showing strain localization; dynamic loading 
retained multiple active systems throughout. EBSD confirmed that 
plastic deformation led to lattice reorientation and shear-band-like 
features, without evidence of twinning. No large-scale twinning 
(TWIP) or transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) was observed in any 
orientation, even at high strain rates and cryogenic temperatures. In the 
dynamic results, the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) for slip ranged 
from ~64.7 to 87.1 MPa at RT to ~116 – 136.9 MPa at 77 K, reflecting 
increased slip resistance with temperature reduction but remaining 
below the threshold for twinning.
