ABSTRACT
The present work investigates the tensile deformation behavior and fracture mechanism of Mo-14 %Re alloy 
(MR14) at both room and high temperatures. The results show that the tensile strength of MR14 alloy is 613 MPa 
at room temperature and 260 MPa at 1000 
◦
C. Moreover, the MR14 exhibits remarkable ductility, with elon -
gation surpassing 60 % both at ambient temperature and elevated temperatures. As the temperature rises, dy -
namic recrystallization and the rotation of subgrains lead to the evolution of texture during fracture, which has 
not been reported hitherto. A high proportion of low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) facilitates the dislocation 
transfer, thereby preventing the nucleation of cracks caused by stress concentration. The strong texture, abun -
dant substructure, movable edge dislocation and mixed dislocation on the bundle structures are the main 
toughening factors. Additionally, during the high-temperature tensile deformation process, the MR14 alloy 
maintains a single-phase structure without precipitation, demonstrating excellent thermal stability. These 
research findings hold profound significance for broadening the application scope of the Mo
–
Re alloy.
1. Introduction
Compared to pure molybdenum, Mo
–
Re alloys display superior 
high-temperature strength, excellent room-temperature workability, 
remarkable creep resistance, and impressive radiation resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. 
The reaction of Re with oxygen impurities forms a composite oxide, 
thereby reducing the precipitation of carbides and oxides while 
enhancing the binding force at grain boundaries [ 4 , 5 ]. The “ Re effect ” 
significantly overcomes the brittleness of pure molybdenum, elevates 
the recrystallisation temperature of the Mo
–
Re alloy, and decreases the 
ductile-brittle transition temperature, making the Mo
–
Re alloys a 
pivotal high-temperature structural material [ 6 – 8 ]. In particular, the 
Mo-14 wt% Re alloy (MR14) has the ability to resist corrosion from 
nuclear coolant and is utilized as the cladding of segmented compact 
reactors. Moreover, the operating temperature of this material can 
exceed 1100 K, rendering it a crucial component in the design of space 
heat pipe reactor cores [ 9 ].
Mannheim et al. [ 10 ] investigated the influence of Re content on the 
microstructure of Mo
–
Re alloys and pointed out that σ and χ phase 
(brittle phases) were formed when Re content exceeds 40 wt%. At a Re 
content of 14 %, the alloy exhibits optimal ductility and demonstrates 
excellent processing performance and creep resistance [ 11 ]. Nemoto 
and Fabritsiev et al. researched the mechanical properties of Mo
–
Re 
alloys with various Re contents under diverse irradiation conditions 
[ 12 , 13 ]. The results demonstrated that Mo
–
Re alloys retain high 
ductility even when subjected to high-temperature irradiation. Yu et al. 
[ 14 ] reported that the tensile strength of recrystallized Mo-27 at. % Re 
alloy was approximately 400 MPa at 500 
◦
C with an elongation of 
approximately 30 %. However, the ductility significantly decreased at 
800 
◦
C, which might be related to the recrystallisation behavior during 
the deformation process. Due to the driving of deformation energy 
storage, the formation and migration of high-angle grain boundaries 
promoted the formation of recrystallized grains, which affected the 
strain hardening behavior of the material. Recently, Wu and Xu et al. 
reported the hot compression behavior of Mo
–
Re alloy, established a 
hot processing map, and proposed that a large number of stored dislo -
cations were the main reason for its high plasticity [ 2 , 15 ]. These studies 
provide information on the deformation behavior of Mo
–
Re alloys, 
which is important for determining optimal industrial processing 
conditions.

reactors and are safeguarded by patented technology, coupled with a

2. Experimental
The MR14 alloy was prepared via powder metallurgy. Mo powder 
(Jinduicheng Molybdenum Co. Ltd., 3 – 5 μ m, 99.9 %) and Re powder 
(China Rhenium Co., Ltd., 5 – 10 μ m, 99.99 %) were completely mixed, 
and the nominal component was Mo-14 wt% Re. The mixed powder was 
cold isostatically pressed at 200 MPa to form a billet that was subse -
quently sintered at 2200 
◦
C for two hours in a hydrogen atmosphere. The 
sintered billet was then rotary forged at 1300 
◦
C with a deformation 
extent up to 80 % and the resulting product was a MR14 alloy bar.
Tensile tests were carried out on the Shanghai Hualong universal 
high temperature testing machine, keeping a strain rate at 10
 3
. The 
specimen dimensions were in accordance with the reference [ 18 ] and 
met national standard GB/T 228 requirements. High-temperature ten -
sile tests were performed by heating the samples in a furnace chamber to 
600, 800, and 1000 
◦
C, respectively. The samples were held for 10 min 
to ensure uniform heating, while maintaining the vacuum below 10
 3 
Pa. To ensure the accuracy of the data, three samples were measured for 
each temperature.
The phases were confirmed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D/MAX- 
2500). A scanning electron microscope (SEM, Regulus 8230) equipped 
with an electron back-scattered diffraction detector (EBSD, Oxford, 
Symmetry S2) was employed to analyze fracture morphology of the 
tensile fracture and to ascertain the orientation and texture information 
of the fracture region. Electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JXA-8230) 
was utilized to characterize the elemental distribution. For transmission 
analysis (TEM, Talos F200 X), thin foils were mechanically polished to 
100 μ m and further thinned using an ion beam thinner (Gatan 695).

3.2. Room and high temperature tensile properties
From the tensile engineering stress-strain curve in Fig. 3 (a), it can be 
seen that the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) at room temperature is 613 
MPa with a total elongation over 60 %. The UTS decreases to 260 MPa at 
1000 
◦
C, which is still at the level of TZM alloy [ 21 ]. When it exceeds the 
yield point, the strength still increases, indicating that the work hard -
ening occurs. The strain hardening curve ( Fig. 3 (b)) indicates that the 
tensile curve at room temperature shows a high strain hardening rate. 
However, the strain hardening rate decreases as the temperature in -
creases. Even at 1000 
◦
C, the material still retains a certain degree of 
hardening capacity, although it has a shorter hardening phase. Notably, 
the tensile ductility at elevated temperatures is maintained at a level 
comparable to that observed at room temperature rather than dimin -
ishing as the temperature increases.
When compared with the properties of other molybdenum alloys 
reported in the literature ( Fig. 3 (c-d)) [ 16,17,21 – 28 ], it is evident that 
the second phase strengthening of rare earth oxides and carbides and the 
refinement of grain size to the nanoscale can enhance strength at the cost 
of ductility. The MR14 alloy possesses exceptionally high tensile 
ductility, indicating its superior workability compared to other 
molybdenum alloys. This characteristic is particularly advantageous for 
manufacturing components with complex geometries.

The grain orientation spread (GOS) is the mean of the orientation 
deviation of all points in a grain from the average orientation of the 
grain and utilized for the assessment of the extent of grain distortion. A 
higher value indicates a greater degree of grain deformation. In general, 
GOS ≤ 2 indicates the grain is recrystallized, a value of 2 to 7 suggests 
recovery grain, and a value of more than 7 indicates deformed grain 
[ 30 ]. In this instance, we have set the threshold at 2, with the color 
component of the GOS distribution map representing recrystallized 
grains, as illustrated in Fig. 6 (a-d).
There are basically no recrystallized grains at RT, but the proportion 
of recrystallized grains gradually increases as the temperature rises. At 
1000 
◦
C, the proportion of recrystallized grains is close to 10 % in Fig. 6
(e). However, this temperature is lower than the static annealing 
recrystallization temperature (approximately 1350 
◦
C) [ 17 , 31 ]. There -
fore, dynamic recrystallization (DRX) occurs below the temperature of 
static recrystallization under the combined action of energy storage 
generated by tensile strain and high temperature. The DRX process in -
volves the interaction, reaction and rearrangement of high-density dis -
locations at LAGBs, which subsequently migrate and evolve into HAGBs 
gradually. Grains formed by DRX are equiaxed with serrated GBs, and 
the proportion of LAGB decreases slightly ( Fig. 6 (e)). It should be noted 
that the grains formed by DRX are still deformed at the same time, so the 
rotation of the grains will occur. This continuous deformation and 
rotation are responsible for the evolution of texture during high- 
temperature tensile stretching. Concurrently, the rotation of subgrains 
helps to relieve local stress and inhibit crack expansion. This can be 
confirmed by the disappearance of long and deep cracks in the section 
under high temperature tension in Fig. 4 (a
2

2
). For BCC metal, the 
(110) plane is close-packed plane, and the < 111 > direction is close- 
packed direction. In the close-packed plane, the Burgers vector as -
sumes its smallest value, as does the Peierls barrier [ 29 ]. Therefore, the 
rotation of grain to this orientation is conducive to further slip, which is 
why the new {110}[111] texture is formed and high ductility is guar -
anteed. As temperature increases, the thermal motion of atoms in -
tensifies, while the bonding force between atoms weakens, resulting in a 
decrease in tensile strength.

3.4. Strengthening and toughening mechanism
The MR14 alloy has no second-phase strengthening, thus the main 
strengthening mechanisms are solid-solution strengthening and dislo -
cation strengthening. The main strengthening contributions can be 
summarized by the following formula: Δ σ = Δ σ
solid solution
+ Δ σ
dislocation
. 
For solid-solution strengthening, defects such as jogs and dislocation 
loops caused by the interaction between Re atoms and dislocations 
enhance resistance to dislocation movement, resulting in strain hard -
ening. The strain hardening effect is particularly pronounced at RT. 
Elevated temperature promote a transition from plane slip to cross slip 
in the dislocation movement, thereby reducing the strain hardening 
effect ( Fig. 3 (b).
For dislocation strengthening, a large number of dislocation cells 
formed by the intersection of dislocation movement play an important 
role in strengthening MR14 alloy. During the tensile deformation pro -
cess at RT, the formed dislocation grid will produce dynamic grain 
refinement, reduce the mean free path of dislocation movement, and 
produce dynamic Hall-Petch strengthening [ 31 ]. Besides, Chen et al. 
also reported that the < 110 > //RD texture can improve dislocation 
activity and dislocation storage capacity, which is conducive to the

The most outstanding highlight of MR14 alloy lies in its exceptional 
ductility observed in both room temperature and high temperature 
tensile tests. The section shrinkage of MR14 exceeds 70 % in both RT and 
high temperature tensile tests, indicating its excellent plastic deforma -
tion capability prior to failure. And the primary reasons can be sum -
marized as follows: On the one hand, the layered bundle structure plays 
a role in blunting cracks. Rupp et al. reported that when cracks initiate, 
they only extend through shear fracture of ligaments, rather than rapid 
through-fracture similar to that of equiaxed fully recrystallized grains 
[ 34 ]. On the other hand, the bundled structure has a higher density of 
substructures composed of LAGBs. A large number of simulation works 
have demonstrated that LAGBs effectively reduce the requirement for 
independent slip systems and facilitate strain transfer without causing 
fracture [ 35 ]. Compared with HAGB, LAGB possess a more coherent 
lattice structure, which promotes dislocation transfer, alleviates strain 
incompatibility, and enhances ductility.
Besides, the type and mobility of dislocations also play an important 
role. For BCC metals, screw dislocation has an inherently compact and 
non-planar core structure with a high Peierls energy barrier, making 
them the dominant dislocation configuration controlling slip [ 29 ]. 
Screw dislocation exhibits relatively low mobility and require the 
assistance of high temperature to move. However, edge dislocation re -
quires less energy to move and are more active at RT. Ren et al. found 
that pure edge-type dislocations in tungsten alloys move seven orders of 
magnitude faster than pure screw dislocations [ 19 ]. Numerous litera -
tures have reported that the solid solution of Re atoms alters the sym -
metrical structure of the dislocation core, making the dislocation change 
from planar slip to non-planar slip, thereby reducing the energy barrier 
and facilitating dislocation movement [ 36 – 39 ]. Here, in order to 
determine the dislocation type, this work uses two-beam conditions to 
obtain bright field images of the dislocation morphology at the subgrain 
with different diffraction g and the Burgers vector b of the dislocation 
can be determined according to the dislocation extinction condition g •
b = 0 [ 40 – 42 ]. Fig. 9 shows the bright field images (BF) of the disloca -
tion under three g diffraction under [ 111 ] zone axis. According to the 
dislocation extinction conditions in Table 1 , the b of the dislocation is 
determined in different colors and marked with short arrows of corre -
sponding colors. It is worth noting that the dislocation line with b =
1 / 2 [ 111 ] is always invisible under the [ 111 ] zone axis.
The type of dislocation is determined by the orientation of the 
dislocation line with respect to b. The Burgers vector of a screw dislo -
cation aligns parallel to the dislocation line, while the Burgers vector of 
an edge dislocation is perpendicular to the direction of the dislocation 
line. When the angle between the dislocation line and the Burgers vector 
ranges from 0
◦
to 90
◦
, it represents a mixed dislocation [ 26 ]. Mixed 
dislocations or edge dislocations are marked with a dotted line. It can be 
observed that the majority of curved and untangled thin dislocation 
lines are edge dislocations or mixed dislocations. Hence, it can be 
concluded that the main dislocation configuration is edge dislocations 
and mixed dislocations, rather than screw dislocations. These mobile 
dislocations demonstrate excellent adaptability to plastic deformation, 
effectively shielding cracks and playing a pivotal role in enhancing 
ductility.
