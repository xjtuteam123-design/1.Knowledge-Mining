ABSTRACT 
The L1
2
-strengthened high-entropy alloys (HEAs) exhibit excellent mechanical properties. The commercial 
CoNiCr-based MP159 superalloy with high configuration entropy up to that of HEAs is revisited. And the 
coherent L1
2 
nano precipitates with different sizes and volume fractions are introduced into MP159 alloys by 
tuning the aging time. Especially at 298 K, the alloys after aging for 10 h can achieve a good combination of 
1360 MPa high tensile strength and 41% tensile strain. In contrast, the tensile strength is as high as 1710 MPa, 
and the tensile strain remains at 45% at cryogenic temperatures, much better than most of HEAs with similar 
composition. Importantly, the optimal size of the precipitates is calculated to be 41 nm. The present work 
provides a basic guidance for developing nano-precipitated CoNiCr-based alloys with high mechanical 
performances. 
1. Introduction

to their high strength and toughness at a large temperature range. The 
face-centered-cubic (fcc) structure HEAs possess outstanding plasticity 
at ambient and cryogenic temperatures [ 1 – 4 ]. However, the strengths 
are not enough to support the engineering applications in extreme 
environment. Recently, the introduction of L1

the fcc matrix has been proved to be one of the most effective ways of 
strengthening [ 5 ]. This strategy has been widely used to achieve the

3. Results and discussion 
The microhardness variation of MP159 alloys aged at 650 
◦
C with 
different aging time is displayed in Fig. 1 (a). The initial microhardness 
increases sharply, which is generally accompanied by the formation of 
precipitates and the increase in the volume fraction. It is noted that the 
alloys aged for 1 h are sufficient to produce saturated precipitates [ 17 ]. 
With increasing the aging time, the size of precipitates gradually in -
crease, but the volume fraction basically remains constant [ 12 ]. The 
microhardness reaches the peak value after aging for 10 h, and then 
decreases slightly. In other words, CRRA10 alloys have reached the 
"peak aging state". Fig. 1 (b) and (c) present typical engineering 
stress-strain curves of the current alloys at 298 K and 77 K for different 
aging time, respectively. The corresponding mechanical behaviors are 
essentially consistent with the variation trends of the microhardness. At 
298 K, the yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 
CRRA0 alloys are 470 MPa and 860 MPa, respectively, and the elon -
gation at break (EL) is 66%. After cold rolling and aging, the YS and UTS 
of CRRA10 alloys are increased by 106% and 58% to 970 MPa and 1360 
MPa, respectively. The tensile strain is decreased marginally, yet re -
mains at 41%. Interestingly, the strength of the alloys is significantly 
increased with a slight improvement in plasticity at 77 K. In particular, 
the CRRA10 alloys exhibit most excellent strength-plasticity synergy at 
77 K, in which the YS and UTS are about 1.2 GPa and 1.7 GPa, respec -
tively, and EL is about 45%. Unlike the low-temperature ductile-brittle 
transition in most traditional alloys, the current alloys achieve a great 
breakthrough in strength-ductility trade-off. Therefore, in order to 
reveal the potential deformation mechanisms of the 
precipitation-hardened alloys, the microstructures of CRRA10 alloys are 
mainly analyzed in detail.

The homogeneous MP159 alloys have a single-phase fcc structure as 
reported previously [ 14 ]. After aging, L1
2 
nanoparticles emerge in the 
matrix, producing a precipitation strengthening. In essence, the rein -
forcement by precipitation is obtained through particle dispersion that 
generates dislocation movement barriers, and particle dispersion is 
achieved through a secondary-phase precipitation process. When an 
individual precipitated particle interacts with a dislocation, there is a 
balance of between the two forces: F = 2 T sin θ [ 21 ]. T is the line tension 
of mobile dislocations, and F is the resistance of precipitated particles to 
dislocations. When the sizes of the precipitated particles are large and 
incoherent with the matrix, the dislocations will bypass the particles, 
that is Orowan mechanism. Particles will be sheared by dislocations 
when the precipitated particles are small and coherent with the matrix 
[ 21 ]. Considering the small size of the precipitated L1
2 
nanoparticles in 
the alloys and its coherent interface relationship with the matrix, the 
shear mechanism dominates the plastic deformation mechanism in the 
current alloys. 
During plastic deformation, dislocations must travel in pairs when 
the ordered precipitated particles are sheared [ 21 ]. The leading dislo -
cations shear the particles to generate anti-phase boundaries (APBs), 
and the trailing dislocations restore the order of the particles [ 23 ]. There 
are two mechanisms of the dislocation pair shearing particles, which 
depend on the size of particles and the equilibrium spacing of paired 
dislocations. For small particles, the dislocation pairs may not be in an 
individual particle, leading to weak pair coupling ( Fig. 4 (a)). The stress 
required for shear-ordered precipitations is proportional to the nano -
particle size, as indicated by the solid line in Fig. 4 (c). When the 
nanoparticles are larger, the trailing dislocations enter the nanoparticles 
before the leading dislocations leave the particles. The dislocation pairs 
are located in the same nanoparticles, which produces a strong coupling 
effect ( Fig. 4 (b)) [ 24 , 25 ]. As the nanoparticles grow, the shear stress 
decreases, as shown by the dotted line in Fig. 4 (c). Therefore, an optimal 
particle size, corresponding to the maximum shear stress of dislocations 
on the nanoparticles, necessarily exists throughout the aging process, 
within the particle size of the weakly and strongly pair coupled regions, 
and its quantitative calculation is significant for the effect of precipita -
tion on the strength improvement of the alloys.

To investigate the deformation mechanism of CRRA10 alloys at 
different temperatures, the TEM analysis is performed on the specimens 
after tensile fracture. As presented in Fig. 5 (a), there are many dislo -
cations after deformation, and they are predominantly slip at ambient 
temperature, with grain boundaries, annealed twin boundaries and 
precipitated particles acting as strong barriers to dislocation movement 
[ 29 ]. The local disruption of the ordered structure of nanoprecipitates 
will promote the propagation of dislocations on the same slip plane in a 
manner similar to the slip plane softening phenomenon [ 30 , 31 ]. 
Therefore, when the interaction between dislocations and precipitates is 
strongly coupled, it is more favorable for the accumulation of disloca -
tions, and the spacing between pairs of dislocations decreases as the 
strong coupling effect increases. In other words, the mean free path 
( d
mean
) of dislocations becomes smaller. The storage rate of dislocations 
upon straining is described as d ρ / d γ = 1/ bd
mean
, here d ρ is the disloca -
tion density per shear strain ( d γ ), and b is the Berger vector [ 23 ]. Thus, 
the dislocation density is higher in CRRA10 alloys compared to samples 
with shorter aging time. In addition, Peng et al. [ 32 ] demonstrated 
through dislocation activity that larger nanoscale precipitates require 
smaller strains to trigger dislocation nucleation upon loading. Hence, 
the larger the precipitates activate more dislocations under the same 
strain conditions, which in turn leads to increased dislocation accumu -
lation. Nevertheless, when the precipitate size is larger than the critical 
value, the dislocations accumulate severely causing local stress con -
centration and loss of alloy plasticity and strength leading to premature 
fracture. It is clear that the strong coupling effect of dislocations and the 
increase of dislocation generation collectively lead to a significant in -
crease in dislocation accumulation in CRRA10 alloys, which reasonably 
justifies high mechanical performances in CRRA10 alloys. Meanwhile, 
the accumulation of the dislocation density is more favorable to the 
formation of high-density dislocation walls (HDDWs), as shown in Fig. 5 
(b), which has a more pronounced hindering effect on the dislocation 
slip [ 33 ].

Besides, the twinning and dislocation slip are generally two 
competing mechanisms during plastic deformation in metallic materials, 
with the dominant deformation mechanism being determined by the 
inherent properties of the material (e.g., SFE and grain orientation) and 
the loading conditions (e.g., the temperature and strain rate). Lower SFE 
or conditions of high-speed deformation (e.g., low-temperature or high 
strain rate) can promote the formation of twins [ 40 ]. Striated structures 
can be found in Fig. 6 (c), and the corresponding HRTEM images indicate 
that these striated structures are identified as deformation twins (DTs). 
The presence of deformation twins are not found at ambient tempera -
ture. On one hand, the SFE is higher at room temperature. On the other 
hand, the low strength does not reach the critical twinning stress at 
ambient temperature. By introducing additional interfaces, twins 
effectively reduce the mean free path of dislocations and thus the 
strength increases at 77 K. Furthermore, deformation twins provide 
additional channels for dislocation slip while hindering dislocation 
movement. Consequently, CRRA10 alloys have UTS up to 1.7 GPa at 77 
K and tensile ductility is maintained at 45% through the combined effect 
of microbands and deformation twins, achieving a good combination of 
strength and ductility. 
4. Conclusions 
In this study, the evolution of coherent precipitates at different aging 
time in MP159 alloys was investigated. Additionally, the mechanism of 
the strengthening at 298 K and 77 K was explored. The conclusions are 
summarized as follow. 
(1) By calculating the OVEC of MP159 alloys, it was found that the 
value was within the formation range of L1
2 
phase. L1
2 
pre -
cipitates was observed in the microstructures of CRRA10 alloys. 
(2) At 298 K, the changes of strength and hardness of MP159 alloys 
with aging time are consistent. The peak value is reached after 
aging for 10h. Compared with the recrystallized CRRA0 alloys, 
the YS and UTS of CRRA10 alloys increased by 106% and 58%, 
respectively, to 970 MPa and 1360 MPa, and the tensile strain 
remained 41%. At 77 K, YS and UTS of CRRA10 alloys reached 
1.2 GPa and 1.7 GPa respectively, and the tensile strain increased 
slightly to 45% instead of loss, achieving a good combination of 
strength and plasticity. 
(3) During aging of MP159 alloys, the interaction between disloca -
tions and precipitates changes from weak coupling to strong 
coupling with the increase of the precipitate size. The transition 
point corresponds to the optimal size of the precipitation, which 
is quantitatively calculated to be 41 nm. 
(4) The deformation mechanism is dominated by dislocation plane 
slip, and a high-density dislocation wall is formed at 298 K. At 77 
K, the microbands and deformation twins dominate plastic 
deformation. The synergistic effect has achieved remarkable 
strengthening and toughening.
