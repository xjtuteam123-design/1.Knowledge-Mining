based on the Al-Ni binary system

ABSTRACT 
To find high -temperature high -entropy alloys, a series of quaternary 
non-equimolar AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x (x in atomic ratio, 0 ≤ x ≤ 35) alloys was designed , 
based on the Al-Ni binary system and prepared by arc -melting. The crystal structure, 
microstructure, and compressive properties of these alloys at both room and high 
temperatures were characterized . This Al xCo15Cr15Ni70-x system showed a lattice 
structure transition from face-centered cubic (FCC) to body-centered cubic (BCC), as 
the Al content was increased. In the Al content range of 12.5 at.% to 19.3 at.%, the 
L12 structured γ′ phase formed and strengthened of the alloys greatly at both room and 
elevated temperatures. The alloy with an Al content of 19.3 at.% has lamellar eutectic 
structure composed of γ′ and B2 phases. Due to the high γ′ phase fraction of 70 vol.%, 
this alloy has a compressive yield strength of 924 MPa and a fracture strain of 29% at 
room temperature as well as good yield strength at high temperatures. Due to its high 
Al content, this alloy has a density of 7.25 g/cm 3, 20% lower than that of the Inconel 
718 alloy. At 1, 123 K, its specific yield strength is about twice that of the Inconel 
718.

1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are a rapidly -evolving class of metallic alloys with 
multiple principal elements [1-6]. The high entropy effect of HEAs [2, 4, 7] proposes 
that the higher mixing entropy in near -equimolar alloys with five or more elements 
may stabilize solid solution phases. Thus, much of effort had been made to search for 
single solid solution HEAs with equimolar compositions [8, 9]. In recent years, some 
studies have shown that the maximization of mixing entropy is not necessary to 
determine the formation of solid solution s [10-13]. Then, the strict restrictions on the 
compositions of HEAs have been relaxed [12, 14, 15] , and some quaternary 
non-equimolar element alloys have been defined as HEAs [12, 14, 16].

## Page 4

Recently, with the deepening of fundamental understanding of HEAs, the cost 
has been taken into account in HEA designing [12, 16-18]. Designing non-equimolar 
HEAs is an appropriate way to effectively reduce the cost. On the one hand, it may 
make sense to design HEAs basing on conventional low cost Fe -based, Al-based or 
Ni-based alloys. Namely, to reduce the cost, Fe, Al, or Ni could be selected as the 
principal elements. On the other hand, lowering the content of expensive elements is 
also an important option to reduce the cost [12, 16]. 
In the widely -studied equimolar or near equimolar HEAs [1, 14, 15, 19] , each 
element is regarded as equivalent. The consequent unpredictability of the 
microstructure and property is usually explained by the “cocktail effect” [2, 20]. It has 
been reported that in the Fe 80-xMnxCo10Cr10 HEAs [12, 16] , all the microstructure, 
phase composition and properties are very similar to that of advanced high manganese 
steels [21-23]. In such kind of HEAs, usually two or three elements are principal and 
concentrated. Thus, the microstructure and properties of these HEAs are li kely to be 
somewhat more affected by the principal elements. Consequently, the unpredictability 
of the microstructure and properties is lowered in these HEAs. It is possible to 
roughly predict the microstructure and properties of these HEAs according to th e 
principal elements. In a similar way, we determined a strategy to purposefully design 
non-equimolar HEAs with aimed properties by selecting proper principal elements. In 
another word, alloying proper elements to the known binary or ternary systems may 
be an efficient way to design non -equimolar HEAs, whose properties might be 
roughly predicted according to the basic systems.

## Page 5

In the present work, with the purpose of finding high temperature HEAs, the 
abovementioned high-entropy-alloying strategy basing on the Al -Ni binary system is 
implemented, and a series of non -equimolar quaternary HEAs w ere designed. 
Considering the fairly -good high -temperature strength of NiAl and Ni 3Al 
intermetallic compounds, it is expected that this non -equimolar HEAs base d on the 
Ni-Al system [24] may also have good high temperature performance. It has been 
pointed out that the composition of commercial Ni-based superalloys and 
3d-transition metal (3d-TM) HEAs substantially overlap [20]. However, it also means 
that the sys tematic research of 3d -TM HEAs may promote the development of 
Ni-based superalloys via totally different approaches. 
In comparison with the studied Al -containing 3d-TM HEAs [2, 5, 25 -29], only 
Co and Cr are selected as alloying elements and the content of them is set as 15 at.% 
in order to lower the cost and avoid the formation of σ phase [12]. The HEA system to 
be investigated is designed as AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x (x in atomic ratio, 0 ≤ x ≤ 35). A 
systematic study on the influence of Al content on crystal structure, phase evolution, 
morphology and room and high -temperature compressive properties of the 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x alloys were conducted. 
2. Experimental 
Ingots with a nominal composition of AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x (x = 0, 5, 12.5, 17.5, 19.3, 
27.5, and 35) were alloyed by arc-melting a mixture of metals (purity > 99.5 wt .%) in 
a Ti -gettered high -purity Argon atmosphere on a water -cooled copper hearth. The 
ingots were flipped and remelted at least six times to ensure the chemical

## Page 6

homogeneity. The homogenization annealing at 1, 273 K for 12 hours in vacuum was 
conducted to the ingots except for a eutectic alloy that contains 19.3% Al. The 
crystalline structure identification of the Al xCo15Cr15Ni70-x HEAs was conducted via 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation. The morphology, phase constitution 
and chemical composition of the samples were characterized by transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) with a JEM-2010 and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a 
Hitachi S3400, which was equipp ed with an energy-dispersive-spectrometry (EDS) 
detector. Ingot samples were sectioned, ground, polished and finally chemically 
etched for SEM observation. SEM samples were etched in solutions mixed by 10 ml 
water, 10 ml HCl, and 4 g CuSO 4. TEM samples were first mechanically ground to 30 
μm thick, and then were thinned using a Gatan Precision Ion Polishing System . 
V olume fractions of the phases were analyzed by the Photoshop software. Cylindrical 
compressive samples with a length of 10 mm, a diameter of 5 mm for room 
temperature test and a diameter of 8 mm for elevated temperature tests were prepared 
via electric discharging machining. The room temperature compressive tests were 
performed on an Instron 3382 universal electronic testing machine with a strain rate 
of 4 × 10-4 s-1. The elevated temperature compressive tests were conducted on a 
Gleeble 3500 system with a strain rate of 4 × 10-3 s-1. To determine the specific yield 
strength of the alloys, density of selected samples was measured by the Archimedes 
method. 
3. Results 
3.1 Crystal structure evolution

## Page 7

The XRD patterns of the Al xCo15Cr15Ni70-x (denoted as Al x hereafter) alloys are 
presented in Fig. 1. A lattice structure transition from FCC to BCC can be observed as 
Al content is increased. In the Al 0 and Al 5 alloys, only a disordered FCC phase is 
observed. As shown in the inset of Fig. 1, at the 2 θ angle of ~ 36°, a minor (110) FCC 
peak appears in the Al 12.5–Al19.3 alloys, indicating the presence of an ordered FCC 
phase (γ′ phase). With the increase of Al content, the BCC lattice structure begins to 
appear in the Al 17.5 alloy, along with a minor (100) BCC peak at ~ 31 ° (see the inset) 
from ordered BCC (B2) phase. As the Al content is increased, in the Al 17.5–Al27.5 
alloys, the relative intensity of the BCC lattice increases, while that of the FCC lattice 
decreases significantly. Finally, in the Al35 alloy, the FCC lattice disappears, and only 
the BCC (B2) lattice is identified.

3.3 Mechanical properties 
Compressive engineering stress -strain curves tested at 293 –1273 K for the 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x alloys are depicted in Fig. 3, respectively. The yield strength of the

Alx alloys as a function of Al concentration and temperature is exhibited in Fig. 4. At 
room temperature (Fig. 3a) , the Al 0–Al12.5 alloys are of very good ductility (not 
fractured in compression). While with the increase of the Al content, the Al 17.5–Al35 
alloys are fractured and the fracture strain decreases gradually. On the other hand, the 
yield strength ( σ0.2) basically increases with the increase of Al content, except for a 
descending in the Al27.5 alloy. Comprehensively, at room temperature, the Al12.5–Al19.3 
alloys with γ′ phase (see Figs.1 and 2) exhibit both good yield strength and ductility. 
Specifically, the yield strength and fracture strain of the Al 19.3 alloy are 924 MPa and 
29%, respectively. 
At 973, 1, 123 and 1, 273 K (Figs. 3b –d), all the alloys exhibit good ductility 
(not fractured at the strain exceeding 60%). With the increase of the Al content, the 
evolution of yield strength is similar to that at room temperature. The Al 0, Al5, Al27.5, 
and Al35 alloys show continuous strengthening after yield. While the Al 12.5 exhibits a 
tendency of strain softening after yi elding owing to the severe intergranular cracking, 
which also contributes to the softening of Al19.3 at 1, 023 and 1, 273 K.

4. Discussion 
4.1 Formation of the γ′ phase in the AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x system 
The γ′ phase is found in the Al 12.5, Al 17.5, and Al 19.3 alloys by transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) and shown in Fig 5 . In the Al 12.5 alloy (Figs. 5a –c), a 
coarse (0.3 –0.5 μm) and a medium -sized (50–100 nm) population of the γ′ 
precipitates are observed at first. The selected area electron diffraction pattern (SAED) 
in Fig. 5c confirms their ordered L1 2 lattice structure. In the precipitate -free zone 
(PFZ) between the coarse γ′ precipitates, fine nano -precipitates (see the inset in Fig. 
5a) with a size of 5–10 nm are also observed at a highe r magnification. It has been 
reported that the coarser precipitates form at higher and the finer ones form at lower 
temperatures, and based on the formation sequence, the three sized precipitates are 
also named as primary, secondary, and tertiary γ′ precipitates, respectively [30]. The 
SAED pattern from the PFZ (Fig. 5b) is actually an overlap of the patterns from the γ

## Page 13

matrix and the tertiary γ′ precipitates. The superlattice spots from the γ′ phase are 
circled in green. The coincidence of the two sets of patterns indicates the tertiary 
precipitates not only share the orientation relationship, but also the same lattice 
constant with the matrix. The SAED patterns corresponding to the three sized 
precipitates reconfirm indicate that they share the same orientation relationship, {100}
∥{100} and <011>∥<011>. 
The same t rimodal size distribution of γ′ precipitates is also observed in the 
dendritic area in the Al 17.5 alloy (see Fig. 5d), where the primary precipitates grow 
into a rectangular shape and have a maximum size of 0.5 by 1.5 μm. The imperfect 
round spots in the SAED pattern (Fig. 5e) from the PFZ imply the imperfect overlap 
of the patterns from the tertiary γ′ precipitates and the γ matrix, which also indicates 
the imperfect coincidence of their orientation relationship. According to the 
Photoshop analyses, the volume fraction of the primary and secondary γ′ precipitates 
is 47% in the Al12.5 alloy, and 63% in the dendritic region in the Al 17.5 alloy. Since the 
volume fraction of the B2 phase is 16%, in the Al 17.5 alloy, the volume fraction of the 
primary and secondary γ′ precipitates is determined to be 53%, still above that of the 
Al12.5 alloy. 
Figure 5g shows the typical lamellar microstructure of the as cast Al 19.3 eutectic 
alloy. The SAED patterns in Figs. 5(h and i) confirm that the dark lamella is the B2 
phase, while the light lamella is the L12 structured γ′ phase. Both the phase constituent 
and the lamellar space are similar to the previously reported AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 eutectic 
HEA [31]. The analyses of the SEM image reveal that the volume fraction of the γ′

## Page 14

phase is 70%, close to that in the AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 eutectic HEA [32]. However, the Al 
content of this alloy (19.3%) is higher than that of the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA (16.4%).

The lattice transition from FCC to BCC with the increase of the Al content has 
been reported in many Al -containing 3d-TM HEA systems [2, 25, 26]. In the present 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x system, the presence of the L1 2 structured γ′ phase in a large Al 
content range is observed. Fig. 7 shows the phase evolution of the Al xCo15Cr15Ni70-x 
and AlxCoCrFeNi [25] systems at room temperature and the Al -Ni system [24] at 1, 
300 K, as a function of the Al content. The phase formation and evolution of the 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x system is intermediate between the Al-Ni and the AlxCoCrFeNi [25] 
system. Different from the Al -Ni system, no single γ′ phase zone is found in the 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x system. In the present system, the γ′ phase is observed in the Al 
content range of 12.5% to 19.3%.

AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x, and AlxCoCrFeNi [25] systems. 
4.2 Mechanical properties 
As shown in Fig. 4, the yield strength of the Al x alloys varies greatly with the Al 
content. This could be attributed to the phase constitution and morphology evolution 
with the increase of the Al content, which results in the variation of the dominant 
strengthening mechanism. In the Al 0 and Al 5 alloys, the solid solution strengthening 
dominates, although the strengthening effect is weak. In the Al 12.5–Al19.3 alloys, the 
yield strength improves greatly at both the room and elevated temperatures with the

## Page 16

formation of the γ′ phase. As shown in Fig. 7a, in the Al 12.5–Al19.3 alloys, the yield 
strength exhibits a linear relationship with the volume fraction of the γ′ phase. At 
293–1123 K, the slopes of the fitted straight lines are almost the same, indicating that 
the strengthening effect of the γ′ phase does not decrease even at 1, 123 K. While at 1, 
273 K, the slop of the straight line is nearly zero, which means the effect of the 
volume fraction of γ′ phase on the yield strength is sharply reduced at this temperature. 
However, the formation of B2 phase and its lamellar structure also contribute to the 
higher strength of the Al17.5 and Al19.3 alloys, at the price of ductility. In the Al27.5 alloy, 
the γ′ phase disappears and the NiAl -rich B2 phase dominates. As a result, a 
substantial drop in the yield strength is caused, especially at the elevated temperatures. 
The Al 35 alloy consists entirely of NiAl -rich B2 phases, leading to its highest yield 
strength and the worst ductility at room temperature. Like many intermetallic 
compounds, the brittleness at room temperature may limit the application of the Al 35 
alloy, although it has reasonably good yield strength at room and elevated 
temperatures and fairly low density for its high Al concentration.

the SYS of the Al 19.3 alloy is higher than that of Inconel 718, especially at 1, 123 K,

high-temperature applications in the temperature range of 1, 100–1, 300 K. 
5. Conclusions 
With the purpose of finding high -temperature HEAs, a series of non -equimolar 
AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x (0 ≤ x ≤ 35) alloys was designed and studied, based on the Al -Ni 
binary system. Systematically, t he crystal structure, microstructure , and compressive 
properties at room and elevated temperatures of the AlxCo15Cr15Ni70-x HEAs were 
investigated. Alloying effect of Al on the phase formation and mechanical properties 
were discussed. Based on above results and analyses, the following conclusions were 
made: 
1. With the increase of Al content, the lattice structure evolves from FCC (x  12.5) 
to FCC plus BCC (17.5 ≤ x ≤ 27.5), finally to BCC（x = 35. When 12.5 ≤ x ≤ 17.5, 
the L12 structured γ′ precipitates form in the γ matrix. In the Al 19.3 alloy, lamellar 
eutectic structure consisting of γ′ phase and B2 phase forms. 
2. At 293 –1, 123 K, the Al 12.5–Al19.3 alloys have very good yield strength, which 
drops rapidly at temperatures above 1123 K. In particular, the eutectic Al 19.3 alloy 
has a yield strength of 924 MPa and fracture strain of 28% at room temperature as 
well yield strengths of 789, 546 and 129 MPa at 973, 1,123 and 1, 273 K, 
respectively. In all the Alx alloys, the Al35 alloys possess the highest yield strength 
of 1, 055 and 164 MPa at 293 and 1, 273 K, respectively, as well the worst 
compressive fracture strain of less than 2%. at 293 K.

## Page 19

3. In the Al 12.5–Al19.3 alloys, the volume fraction of γ′ phase increases with the 
increase of Al content. And a positive linear relationship between the yield 
strength and the volume fraction of γ′ phase is observed at 293 –1, 123 K. At 1, 
273 K, the positive linear relationship is lowered to nearly zero, indicating the 
effect of the volume fraction of γ′ phase on the yield strength is drastically 
reduced at this temperature. 
4. The density of the Al19.3 eutectic alloy is determined to be 7.25 g/cm3. At 293, 973 
and 1, 123 K, its specific yield strengths are 127.4, 108.8, and 75.4 MPa*cm 3/g, 
respectively, showing great potential in high -temperature applications in the 
temperature range of 1, 100–1, 300 K.
