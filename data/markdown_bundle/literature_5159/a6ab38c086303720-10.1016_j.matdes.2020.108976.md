Abstract 
The present work contributes to a better understanding of the basic assumptions and princi-
ples behind the design of Ni-based single crystal superalloys (SXs). For this purpose, we 
cast and heat treat four Ni-based single crystal superalloys (SXs) and compare their creep 
performances: ERBO/1 (with Re) and three ERBO/15 variants (no Re but increa sed levels of 
Ti, Mo and W). We show that Re can be replaced by other elements without losing creep 
strength. To come to this conclusion one has to consider both, alloy composition and micr o-
structure. We analyze the mechanical, microstructural and chemical results (creep rates, γʼ -
volume fractions, average γʼ -particle sizes, average γ -channel widths and the chemistry of 
γ- and γʼ-phases) obtained for ERBO/15 and its two leaner variants (less Mo: ERBO/15 -Mo 
and less W: ERBO/15 -W). ERBO/15 -Mo and ERBO/15 -W sh ow higher creep rates than 
ERBO/15, because they exhibit lower Mo and W concentrations in the γ-channels. This r e-
sults in higher diffusion rates , accelerated rafting and faster disloc ation climb at γ/γʼ -
interfaces. 
Keywords: Ni-based single crystal superalloys (SX); Heat treatment; Effects of Re, Ti, Mo 
and W; Microstructure; Local alloy composition; Creep rate. 
 
Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys (SXs) are used for first stage blades in gas turbines for 
aero engines and power plants [1 -5]. They have to withstand mechanical loads at temper a-
tures exceeding 1273 K, where creep [6-14] is one key factor limiting their exploitable service 
lives. The development of SX alloys has been described in the literature [e.g. 3, 4, 15-18]. 
The early history of alloy development has been described by C.T. Sims [15], later develo p-
ments by R.C. Reed [3] and T.M. Pollock and S. Tin [4]. Three facts are today well esta b-
lished: (1) Alloy elements like Mo, Re and W improve the creep resistance [1 9-25]. (2) The 
creep strength of SX relies on a sub -µm scale two phase microstructure, which consists of 
cuboidal γ’-particles (typical cube edge length : < 0.5 µm, typical volume fraction: 70 %, crys-
tal structure: L12), which are separated by thin γ -channels (typical γ-channel width: < 50 nm, 
typical volume fraction: 30 %, crystal structure: fcc) [26, 27]. (3) SX alloy elements have a 
tendency to partition either to the γ’ - or to the γ -phase. Re, which partitions to the γ -phase 
[28-31], has a beneficial effect on creep resistance at high temperatures (> 1273 K) and low 
stresses (< 200 MPa). This is related to its slow diffusion in the γ -phase [32-37]. This implies 
that its presence in the γ -phase slows down dislocation climb (for qualitative explanations 
see [24, 38]). Re is an expensive strategic alloy element and therefore there is interest in

replacing Re by other elements. The background of the present work was a computer assist-
ed attempt to develop new SXs by combining CALPHAD methods with multi criteria optimiza-
tion methods [3 9-41]. The experimental program started with ERBO/1, a CMSX -4 type of 
alloy [42], as a reference material. This alloy wa s cast and heat -treated by Doncasters and 
its chemical composition and microstructure have been documented elsewhere [30]. 
One objective of the present work is to demonstrate that alloy concepts based on comput a-
tional materials design [3 9-41] can yield Re -free SXs with creep properties, which match or 
even outperform those of ERBO/1. This is demonstrated for the computationally designed 
alloy ERBO/15 [40, 41], where the elimination of Re is balanced by a number of measures, 
including a strong increase of Mo and Ti levels, a moderate increase of W, a decrease of Al - 
and Co-contents and a complete elimination of Ta and Hf. In the present work, we assess 
the creep properties of as-cast and heat-treated ERBO/15 at 1323 K and 160 MPa and co m-
pare them to ERBO/1. A second objective of the present work is to find out how much ind i-
vidual alloy elements contribute to creep strength above 1273 K. For this purpose, we co m-
pare ERBO/15 with two leaner alloys with reduced Mo and W contents balanced by an i n-
crease of Ni concentration (alloys: ERBO/15-Mo and ERBO/15-W). All three ERBO/15 var i-
ants were subjected to the same heat treatment. We show that computer-based methods 
can help to identify new compositions with excellent creep strength . Our study demonstrates 
how important individual alloy elements are for a good creep strength and that one always 
has to take chemical and microstructural param eters into account when assessing creep 
performance.

4. Discussion 
On comparing the creep properties of different superalloy single crystals: The present work 
has shown that it is by no means simple to compare different SXs with the objective to identi-
fy the role of individual alloy elements in establishing creep strength. In the present work, we 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 20

Journal Pre-proof
19 
 
compare a commercial CMSX -4 alloy (ERBO/1) with three experimental alloys (ERBO /15, 
ERBO/15-Mo, and ERBO/15-W). The CMSX -4 material was received in a fully heat-treated 
condition, the heat treatment steps are listed in Table 2. It was not straightforward to use this 
heat treatment for ERBO/15 and its derivatives , because intercipient melting occurred. A 
modified heat treatment was used for ERBO/15 and its derivatives, Table 2 . As a cons e-
quence, the two alloy systems considered in the present work not only differ in average alloy 
composition but also in terms of γʼ -volume fraction, γʼ-particle size (respectively after rafting: 
γʼ-raft thickness) and γ -channel width. In order to be able to compare the influence of diffe r-
ent alloy elements, an attempt must be made to compare SX materials with the same micr o-
structural parameters. This is not easy and further work is required which allows to separate 
the effects of alloy chemistry and microstructure. In the present work, we focus on the high 
temperature (> 1273 K) low stress (< 200 MPa) creep regime, where the d -shell elements 
strongly affect creep strength, due to their low atomic mobility [3]. 
Comparison of as -cast and fully heat treated material states: As-cast and fully heat treated 
material states are compared. The as cast material states of both alloy systems show strong 
chemical and microstructural differences between prior dendritic and interdendritic regions, 
Figures 6 and 9. The chemical heterogeneities are associated with the cast mi crostructure 
(partitioning of alloy elements to dendritic and interdend ritic regions on the sub-mm scale), 
Figures 6a and b and Table 4 . At 1323 K and 160 MPa (high temperature low stress creep 
regime), the as-cast material states show significantly less creep strength than the fully 
heat-treated materials, where alloy elements are homogeneously distributed throughout sig-
nificantly finer microstructures, Figures 6c and d and Table 5 . The differences in creep ru p-
ture times are less pronounced for ERBO /1 (post cast heat treatment results in an increase 
of rupture time from 130 h to 191 h) than for the t hree ERBO/15 variants (ERBO/15: 27 h / 
307 h, ERBO/15-Mo: 26 h / 111 h and ERBO/15-W: 21 h / 101 h). 
Experimental support of computational alloy design: The present experimental study shows 
that computational alloy design [39-41] can indeed suggest alloy compositions, which allow 
to replace the expensive strategic alloy element Re without jeopardizing creep strength. The 
results of the present work show th at under creep conditions of 1323 K and 160 MPa, 
ERBO/15 (without Re) can match and even outperform ERBO/1 (with Re) , Figure 13 . The 
elements W and Mo, which partition to the γ -channels (Table 6 and Figure 8) play a key role 
in this respec t. ERBO/15-Mo (Mo reduced from 2.6 at. -% to 1.3 at. -%, balanced by an i n-
crease of Ni) and ERBO/15 -W (W reduced from 2.5 at. -% to 1.3 at. -% balanced by an i n-
crease of Ni) are much less creep resistant than ERBO/15 (2.6 at. -% Mo and 2.5 at. -% W). 
From the fact that the micro structural parameters of ERBO/15 and its two variants are very 
similar (Table 7 and Figures 10 and 11) one can conclude that the lower creep resistance of 
ERBO/15-W and ERBO/15/-Mo is governed by alloy chemistry. 
Effect of full heat treatments: Subjecting the as -cast material states to the heat treatments, 
given in Table 2, results in significantly finer γ/γʼ -microstructures in all four materials states , 
Figures 10 and 11 and Table 7 . The refinement is less pronounced for ERBO /1 than for the 
three ERBO /15 variants, Figure 10. Under creep conditions of 1323 K and 160 MPa, 
ERBO/15 shows lower minimum creep rates and higher rupture times than ERBO 1 (ERBO/1 
- minimum creep rate: 4.3 10-8 s-1, rupture time: 191 h); ERBO/15 - minimum creep rate: 7.0 
 10-9 s-1, rupture time: 307 h). The creep performance of ERBO/1 can probably be improved 
by refining its γ/γʼ -microstructure. ERBO /15 significantly outperforms its two derivatives 
ERBO/15-Mo and ERBO/15-W in terms of creep performance. From the fact that the γ/γʼ-
microstructures of these three alloys are similar (Figures 10 and 11, Table 7) we can co n-
clude that the alloy elements W and Mo, which partition to the γ-channels (Figure 8 and T a-
ble 6), are responsible for this finding. W and Mo act in the γ-channels when improving creep 
strength. We suggest that they slow down dislocation climb processes in the dislocation ne t-

## Page 21

Journal Pre-proof
20 
 
works, which enwrap all γʼ-particles [53-55] by exerting negative Peach Koehler climb forces 
on the network dislocation segments, as suggested by Wollgramm et al. [24]. 
High temperature plasticity: All four materials in both microstructural states (as-cast and fully 
heat treated) show pronounced primary creep ranges, which reach gl obal creep rate minima 
at accumulated creep strains below 2 %, Figures 12 and 13 . After the full heat treatments, 
the three ERBO/15 variants show early local creep rate minima followed by intermediate lo-
cal maxima. The effect is small but significant. The local maxima are highlighted by small 
arrows in the magnified creep rate plot shown in Figure 13d. No double minimum behavior is 
observed for ERBO/1 in the present work (black curve in Figure 13d) , even though double 
minima have been desccribed in the high temperature and low stress creep regime for Ni - 
[56] and Co -based [57] SXs (without microstructural explanation). For the low temperature 
and high stress creep regime, these were explained on the basis of specific dislocation rea c-
tions, which are required to nucleate planar faults which shear the γ’-phase [58, 59]. It is well 
known that the nature of the elementary dislocation processes which govern creep differ b e-
tween the low temperature high stress and the high temperature and low stress creep re-
gimes. Therefore, the double minimum explanation of Wu et al. [5 8, 59] cannot rationalize 
the double minim a observed for ERBO/15 and its two derivatives in the present work . It 
seems reasonable to assume that during high temperature low stress creep of ERBO/15 and 
its two derivatives, a stabilization of the particle population during creep rationalizes this fin d-
ing (additional in-situ tempering effect). The effect merits further analysis. 
When comparing ERBO/1-C with ERBO/15-C in Figure 13, one finds that ERBO/15-C shows 
lower minimum creep rates and higher rupture times, even though its γʼ -volume fraction is 
lower (Figure 10, Tables 7 and 8). This may be related to the significantly coarser γ/γʼ -
microstructure of ERBO/1-C, especially to a larger γʼ -particle size and to a larger γ -channel 
width (ERBO/1-C: w = 56 nm, ERBO/15-C: w = 36 nm). This is in line with a scenario where 
it is either more difficult to squeeze dislocations into narrower γ -channels and/or where a 
lower density of γ/γʼ-interfaces requires less rate controlling cutting processes of dislocations 
from the γ-channels into the γʼ-phase. While this seems reasonable, it represents an interpre-
tation rather than a proof, because, after all, the two alloys also differ in the chemical comp o-
sition of phases. Further work is required to compare the two alloys after heat treatments, 
which produce similar microstructures in terms of γʼ-sizes and γ-channel width. 
Directional coarsening of the γʼ-phase: The alloys investigated in the present work are ne ga-
tive misfit alloys (coherency between the lattices of the γ’ - and γ-phase; smaller lattice co n-
stant of the γ’-phase than in the γ-phase), which are known to show directional coarsening of 
the γ’-phase during [100] -tensile creep testing [3]. For the alloys investigated in the present 
work, this is shown in Figure 14. Significant coarsening processes of the microstructure a c-
company the apparent change of morphology , from cuboidal particles to rafts perpendicular 
to the direction of the applie d tensile stress. Thus it was found that the widths of vertical γ-
channels and that the thickness of γ’ -rafts increase significantly (quantitative met allographic 
evaluation in Table 8). The metallographic data also allow to conclude that, as expected (see 
compilation of SX -data in [60]), the volume fractions of the γ’ -phase decrease in the high 
temperature and low stress creep regime. The metallographic images shown in Figure 14 
and the derived data compiled in Tables 8 and 9 must be interpreted with care . After all, the 
four SX had different creep exposure times, Figures 12 and 13. However, it is safe to co n-
clude that the coarsening processes are less pronounced for ERBO/1 and ERBO/15 than in 
ERBO/15-Mo and ERBO/15-W, even though the creep exposure t imes of ERBO/15-Mo and 
ERBO/15-W are much shorter. This suggests that the slowly diffusing elements (Re in 
ERBO/1 and higher levels of Mo and W in ERBO/15) account not only for lower creep rates 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 22

Journal Pre-proof
21 
 
but also slow down γ-channel widening. The data shown in Table 8 als o show that the thick-
ening of the γ’ -rafts is significantly more pronounced in ERBO/15 -Mo and ERBO/15-W than 
in ERBO/15. This suggest that a reduction of atomic mobility by adding d-shell elements (Re, 
W and Mo) impedes both, dislocation plasticity and mic rostructural coarsening processes. 
This is in line with a view where rafting and high temperature plasticity are coupled : accumu-
lating creep strains are associated with higher densities of γ -channel dislocations, which can 
promote rafting [61-66]. 
Resumée: The results obtained in the present work allow to draw two conclusions. First, a 
comparison between ERBO/1 -C and ERBO/15 -C shows that Re can be replaced by higher 
amounts of Mo and W without jeopardizing creep strength. It has recently been suggested 
that Re-segregation to network dislocations in the γ-channels close to the γ/γ’-interfaces con-
tributes to creep strength by hindering knitting reactions between free channel dislocations 
and interfacial dislocation networks, slowing down dislocation climb and decelerating pipe 
diffusion, resulting in slower rafting [ 67,68]. It seems reasonable to assume that Mo and W 
act in a similar way and further work is required to clarify this point. It has been discussed in 
the literature that the higher Ti -levels assist in this respect [ 69], by promoting the transfer 
from Mo and W from the γ’ - to the γ-phase. Second, when comparing ERBO/15 -C to its two 
leaner variants, one finds that, even when the concentration of only one of the two d -shell 
elements, Mo or W, is reduced (Mo: 2.6 → 1.3 at.-%, W: 2.5 → 1.3 at.-%), the creep rate 
significantly increases, even though the γʼ -volume fraction increases. Our experimental r e-
sults show that these reductions most strongly affect the γ -channel chemistry without affect-
ing their widths. This is in line with a scenario, where Mo and W slow down γ -channel dislo-
cation climb at γ/γʼ-interfaces as suggested by Wollgramm et al. [21] (Figure 16d of their p a-
per). Ritter et al. [ 70] have shown that an increase of Mo - or W-levels results in an i ncrease 
of the γʼ-solvus temperature in their alloy system. Further research is necessary to prove this 
statement for the ERBO/15 variants. 
 
5. Summary and Conclusions 
In the present work, we compare the local alloy chemistry, the microstructures and the creep 
properties of four Ni -base single crystal superalloys, a CMSX -4 variant with Re (referred to 
as ERBO/1) and three Re-free alloys referred to as ERBO/15, ERBO/15-Mo (less Mo than in 
ERBO/15) and ERBO/15-W (less W than in ERBO/15). From the results obt ained in the pre-
sent work the following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) The creep behavior of an alloy depends on its chemical composition and on its micr o-
structure. A full SX heat treatment establishes a material state, which is microstructurally and 
chemically more homogeneous and has a finer microstructure than the as -cast material 
state. All heat treated material states show better creep properties than the as -cast material 
states. 
(2) The results obtained in the present work show that one can combine CALPHAD methods 
with multi criteria optimization methods to design Re -free alloys, which do not suffer from 
reduced creep strength as compared to alloys with Re. This is demonstrated experimentally 
by comparing an alloy of type ERBO/1 (CMSX -4 type, with Re) with the computationally d e-
signed alloy ERBO/15 (no Re; increased Ti, Mo and W levels).

(3) In the as -cast condition, Re -free ERBO/15 and its two leaner derivatives ERBO/15 -Mo 
and ERBO/15-W exhibit similar creep strengths, which do not reach the creep st rength of 
ERBO/1 (creep conditions: 1323 K, 160 MPa, [001] tensile loading direction). However, after 
appropriate heat treatments, the creep properties of ERBO/15 can match the creep strength 
of ERBO/1. It must be kept in mind, however, that the SX ERBO/1 and ERBO/15, which were 
compared in the present work, not only differ in alloy chemistry but also in microstructure. 
(4) Reducing Mo and W levels in ERBO/15 results in a higher volume fraction of the γ’ -
phase, similar γ -channel widths and bigger γ’ -particles. These reductions lead to higher 
creep rates. Changes in Mo and W levels have similar effects on creep properties. The TEM 
results of the present work suggest, that the effects are most probably related to a decrease 
of the concentration of these two e lements in the γ -channels, where higher atomic motilities 
result in higher creep rates and faster microstructural evolution. 
(5) Further work is required to develop a systematic procedure, which allows to determine 
heat treatment parameters which establish similar microstructures in different alloys.

Highlights 
 A computer aided newly developed Ni-based superalloy ERBO/15 without Re outperforms the 
creep performance of ERBO/1 (CMSX-4 type) with Re. 
 Adjustment of overall alloy composition and microstructure are equally important for alloy de-
sign. 
 The influence of an individual alloy element on creep can only be analyzed by changing the con-
centration of this element and the amount of the base element. 
 Reduction of the nominal concentration of Mo or W in ERBO/15 results in significantly higher 
creep rates. 
 These higher creep rates can be explained by lower concentrations of Mo or W in the γ-channels.
