ABSTRACT
In this study, commercially pure titanium (CP-Ti) parts were successfully fabricated by selective laser melting
(SLM) using cost-eﬀ ective hydride-dehydride (HDH) Ti powders for the ﬁrst time modi ﬁed by jet milling. Jet
milling eﬀ ectively improves the particle-shape sphericity, suppresses the impurity pick-up, and produces loca-
lized plastic deformation. The ﬂowability of the jet-milled powders is tremendously improved to 29.7 s/50 g that
satisﬁes the SLM processing well, while the oxygen content only increases by 0.02 wt.% (the raw oxygen level:
0.15 wt.%). The oxide layer in the powder surface is determined with the thickness of ∼8 nm and TiO being the
predominant phase before and after jet milling. The SLM-made (SLMed) CP-Ti achieves dominant martensitic α’
phase with the fracture tensile strength up to 731.5 ± 5.7 MPa and elongation of 20.5 ± 1.1%, comparable with
those using expensive atomized powders. Contrary to the conventional metallurgical mechanism for Ti which
suﬀers the cost-performance dilemma, this work presents SLMed CP-Ti with excellent synergy of strength and
ductility while using the cost-a ﬀordable HDH Ti powders.
1. Introduction
Titanium (Ti) and its alloys have attracted ever-increasing attention
in many industries such as aerospace, chemical engineering and med-
ical areas, for their low density, high speci ﬁc strength, high bio-
compatibility and excellent corrosion resistance [ 1,2]. Due to the strong
work-hardening, reactive nature and poor machinability, the manu-
facturing of Ti parts by conventional means usually incurs high costs
[3], limiting their range of applicability. Fortunately, additive manu-
facturing (AM) has demonstrated a promising pathway to produce Ti
parts of complex geometry without extensive machining, which sig-
niﬁcantly lowers the processing cost [ 4–6].
Recently, a large number of studies have been focused on the
commercially pure Ti (CP-Ti) made by selective laser melting (SLM, one
of the most investigated AM approaches) in terms of densi ﬁcation be-
havior [ 7], phase evolution [ 7–11], thermal behavior [ 10,12], proces-
sing optimization [ 7,13,14], and mechanical properties [ 11,15–17].
The achieved superior properties in these aforementioned studies show
attractive perspectives to the biomedical and engineering industries.
Despite this, one bottleneck issue limiting the extensive deployment of
SLM Ti is the high cost of atomized spherical Ti powder feedstock
(∼350 USD/kg [ 18]) that provides su ﬃcient ﬂowability for AM pro-
cessing [ 19,20]. This has been a long-standing problem in the powder-
bed AM technologies including SLM [ 18,20] – the high price of feed-
stock is obviously not a ﬀordable for the scale-up of this technology. In
such a case, as pointed out by Froes and Ma [ 21], an alternative means
to produce low-cost Ti powder feedstock suitable for AM is in strong
demand from an economical viewpoint. As a result, the trial use of
aﬀordable hydride-dehydride (HDH) Ti powders (price: ∼40 USD/kg,
and oxygen level: > 0.25 wt.% [ 18]) for SLM is gradually increasing,
intending to replace expensive atomized powders. However, due to the
poor ﬂowability arising from the irregular shape of HDH Ti powders,
either mechanical ball milling [ 22,23]
or granulation [ 24,25] has been
performed to modify its morphology to achieve suitable ﬂowability and

2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Jet milling and selective laser melting
Irregular-shaped HDH CP-Ti powders, purchased from Tech-long
(Tianjin) Metal Material Co., Ltd. P.R. China with a price of 40 USD/kg,
were used as the raw feedstock for modi ﬁcation. Its mean particle size
(D50) is 23.5 μm( Fig. 1(c)), while the chemical compositions are listed
in Table 1, which shows its original oxygen level of 0.15 wt.%. The
chemical compositions all meet the standard of ASTM B265-15 Grade 2
for CP-Ti. The ﬂowability of the raw powders could not be measured
due to its extremely poor morphology. Jet milling was conducted for 10
min to modify the HDH Ti powders with high-pressure nitrogen gas (4
N purity with an oxygen level below 3 ppm) at a pressure level of 0.72
MPa and a rotating speed of 4000 rpm at room temperature. The de-
tailed information on the equipment and processing is described in Ref.
[28].
The ﬂowability of the HDH Ti powders after jet milling was sig-
niﬁcantly improved, suitable for SLM. The jet-milled powders with a
D50 of 40.1 μm as shown in Fig. 1 (c) were used as raw feedstock in an
SLM 125HL machine (SLM Solutions GmbH). The SLM machine is
equipped with a 400 W Yb:YAG ﬁber laser with a spot size of 80 μm and
a wavelength of 1.06 μm. High-purity (5 N purity with an oxygen level
below 1.5 ppm) argon gas ﬂow was used during the entire SLM process
to minimize oxidation. An optimal processing window was chosen to
fabricate fully densiﬁ ed
 samples based on the previous study [ 23]. The
SLMed parts were manufactured with a powder layer thickness of 30

2.2. Powder characteristics
Analysis of interstitial element (O, N, H) content of powders and
SLMed samples was performed using an inert gas fusion analytical in-
strument (Leco-Tch600), while C content was measured with a carbon
and sulfur analyzer (EMIA-820V, HORIBA). Additionally, Fe content
was determined using an inductively coupled plasma emission spec-
trometry (ICP-AES, Agilent ICPOES730). Powder ﬂowability was mea-
sured as per the ASTM B213-13 standard with a Hall ﬂowmeter.
Particle size distribution (PSD) of powders was determined using a laser
diﬀraction analyzer (BE-9300S, Bettersize Instruments Ltd.). Laser ab-
sorptivity of powders was tested using di ﬀuse re ﬂectance spectroscopy
(DRS, Varian Cary5000). Powders were placed in a quartz cuvette,
sealed and mounted on a Te ﬂon sample holder. Then, the DRS mea-
surement was carried out in the wavelength range from 700 to 1500 nm
by employing a UV –visible-NIR Cary5000 spectrograph. Surface che-
mical and phase composition of powders were measured by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, SPECS Surface Nano Analysis GmbH)
with the survey spectra recording at 50 eV pass-energy and 1 eV step
size.
2.3. Microstructural characterization
Density of the SLMed samples was measured using the Archimedes
method as speci ﬁed in the ASTM B962 standard. Phase constituents
were determined by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD, Siemens D500) at room
temperature. Microstructure and morphology of samples were observed
by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss LEO-1450) and trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI G2) combined with the selected
area electron di ﬀraction (SAED) analysis. An FEI Titan Themis 300
high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), equipped with a probe aberration cor-
rector and a highly e ﬃcient energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) system, was
further used for atomic and nanoscale characterization in the scanning
mode operating at 300 kV. Chemical composition of powders was in-
vestigated at a sub-nanometer scale by atom probe tomography (APT,
Cameca Instruments LEAP ™ 3000X HR). APT measurements were per-
formed in a laser mode at 70 K, with 50 nJ laser energy, a repetition
rate of 200 kHz, and a target detection rate of 0.5%. Three-dimensional
atomic map reconstruction, visualization, and data analysis were car-
ried out using the software package IVAS
Ⓡ. HRTEM foils and needle-
shaped APT samples were prepared using focused ion beam (FIB, FEI
Helios NanoLab 600i). The raw and jet-milled powders with a similar
particle size of 40 μm were sectioned, mounted to a micromanipulator
by deposition of platinum/chromium (Pt or Cr), and further thinned for
characterization.
2.4. Tension test
The SLMed and heat-treated CP-Ti sheets perpendicular to the
building direction were cut into dog-bone shaped tensile bars (with a
gauge length of 15.0 mm, a center width of 2.0 mm and a thickness of
1.5 mm) using an electric arc wire cutter. Tensile properties of the CP-Ti
bars were measured on an Instron 5982 machine using an extensometer
with a cross-head speed of 1 mm/min at room temperature. Five sam-
ples were used to ensure repeatability in each experiment, and average
mechanical properties were reported.
3. Results
3.1. Powder characteristics, microstructure and surface chemistry
Fig. 1 compares the particle morphologies between the raw and jet-
milled HDH Ti powders. Sharp edges can be observed in the raw
powders with irregular shapes, Fig. 1(a). In contrast, after jet milling
most of sharp edges are ground, as indicated in Fig. 1(b). Two apparent
eﬀects produced by jet milling can be observed when comparing
Fig. 1(a) and (b). First, the number of ﬁne powders below ca.1 0 μmi s
reduced after jet milling i.e., particle classi ﬁcation. This is con ﬁrmed by
the PSD analysis in Fig. 1(c). In fact, the ﬁne powders were classi ﬁed
and collected to the secondary hopper during jet milling. Moreover, the
D50 value increases from original 23.5 –40.1 μm after jet milling.
Second and more importantly, the modi ﬁed particle shape reveals a
signiﬁcantly enhanced sphericity and a greater smoothness. Similar
results were also reported in Refs. [ 28,29] in other metallic powders.
Both aspects bene ﬁt the powder ﬂowability [ 30]. Finally, as compared
to the raw powders (originally unprintable), the ﬂowability after jet
milling is intensively enhanced to 29.7 s/50 g that is comparable with
that of atomized Ti-6Al-4V powders which is approximately 30 s/50 g
as reported in Ref. [ 19]. Hence, the improved particle ﬂowability in this
case ﬁts the SLM processing well [ 5,19]. As displayed in Table 1, the
impurity contents of the jet-milled powders barely change because of
the inert atmosphere involved and short period undertaken during the
modiﬁcation process, except a slight increment in O (by 0.02 wt.%) and
Fe (by 0.024 wt.%). The laser absorptivity is an important parameter
for
SLM, since it may a ﬀect the heat transfer and consequent micro-
structure. We measured the laser absorptivity of both jet-milled and raw
powders whose PSD are similar. As shown in Fig. 1(d), the laser ab-
sorptivity is 62.4% for the raw powders, while it rises to 70.7% after jet
milling. The laser wavelength used in the absorptivity measurements
was 1046 nm, an identical value in the SLM process in this study. The
absorptivity result of the jet-milled powders is close to that of atomized
Ti–6Al–4V powders in a recent report [ 31]. It is believed that surface
modiﬁcation after jet milling improves the laser re ﬂection behavior and
minimizes the loss of laser energy, which promotes the spectral ab-
sorptivity. The enhanced laser absorption is considered bene ﬁcial to the
SLM processability and thus product quality e.g., dimensional accuracy
[32].
Fig. 2 (a) presents the XRD results of the CP-Ti powders and SLMed
parts. No noticeable di ﬀerence in the di ﬀraction peaks is observed in
the CP-Ti powders before and after jet milling. STEM images of the
powder surface are shown in Fig. 2(b) and (c). As shown in the inset of
Fig. 2(b), the SAED analysis indicates a well-deﬁ ned h.c.p. α grain ex-

These are typical features of plastic deformed α-Ti as reported in Refs.
[29,33,34]. In our case, this is caused by collision and friction occurring
on the powder surface during jet milling, leading to plastic deforma-
tion. The TEM image in Fig. 2(d) further con ﬁrms the existence of high-
density dislocations. It documents a detailed HRTEM ﬁgure near the
powder surface, showing grain boundaries (GBs) and dislocations
owing to the plastic deformation. The HRTEM image in Fig. 2(e) pre-

Fig. 3(a) and (b) show the STEM-HAADF images with corresponding
elemental mapping analysis (O and Ti) of the interfaces between the
oxide surface and Ti matrix. The following points are noted by com-
paring Fig. 3(a) and (b). First, the oxide layer thickness in the powder
surface seems to remain unchanged after jet milling. Compared with the
2.9 nm oxide thickness for atomized Ti powders [ 36], the original
thickness of oxide layer on HDH powder is ca. 8 nm ( Fig. 3(a)), which is
the typical oxide layer naturally formed on the Ti substrate at room
temperature. The jet-milled powder has a similar-thickness surface
oxide layer, Fig. 3(b). This is due to the nitrogen atmosphere which
minimizes the oxidation during jet milling. The similar surface oxide
layer is also re ﬂected in the similar level of oxygen content, as pre-
sented in Table 1. It also suggests that the oxygen primarily stays near
the powder surface and only a small amount is distributed uniformly in
the Ti matrix. Hence, the total oxygen content in the Ti powders con-
sists of surface oxide and oxygen atoms in solid solution, which is
consistent with the ﬁndings in Refs. [ 24,30,37]. The oxide layers in the
raw and jet-milled powders are further determined by XPS in Fig. 3(c).
Based on the peaks of Ti
4+,T i +3,T i +2 and Ti° atoms, the oxide status
in the powder surface implies the co-existence of TiO 2,T i 2O3, TiO and
metallic Ti, respectively. These oxides are the commonly found and
thermodynamically stable Ti oxide phases formed on the Ti surface by
oxidation [ 35,36,38–40].
XPS has been extensively performed to investigate the oxide state in
the powder surface of Ti and its alloys [ 31,32,41,42]. Although the Ti
oxide constituents can be acquired by XPS, it is di ﬃcult to know how it
is distributed at a micrometer scale and which oxide phase dominates.
Therefore, three-dimensional APT analysis was conducted to study the
elemental (O and Ti) atomic-scaled distribution for the raw and jet-
milled powders as displayed in Fig. 4. Similar with Fig. 3, Fig. 4 (a) and
(b) con ﬁrm the existence of an oxide-matrix interface in each type of
powder. In both cases, the oxygen atoms are enriched in the surface
area while some are distributed in the Ti matrix. The Ti atoms gradually
increase from the interface area to the interior matrix (note that the Cr
atoms come from the coating on the powder surface during sample
preparation by FIB). The oxide layer thickness is ∼10 nm for each
powder sample, consistent with the elemental mapping results in Fig. 3.
Again, these observations con ﬁrm that the modi ﬁcation by jet milling
does not signi ﬁcantly raise the oxidization level in the powder surface.
To further inspect the oxygen state within the interface/layer, the in-
plane composition of oxide for each powder sample was computed in
IVAS using the decomposition of mass spectrum analysis, Fig. 4(c). It is
surprising to see that the atomic ratio of Ti to O is almost equal to 1:1
from Fig. 4(c), which strongly suggests that the predominant oxide
phase in the powder surface is TiO in both raw and jet-milled Ti
powders. The calculated oxygen content in the oxide layer is ∼23.1 wt.
% based on the APT data of the jet-milled Ti powder. XPS results in
Fig. 3 (c1) and (c2) also suggest the presence of other oxides such as
TiO
2 and Ti 2O3. It is well accepted that the oxidation of Ti powders is
mainly dependent on the temperature, time, oxygen pressure and
physical properties of the oxide [ 35,39]. Therefore, our study provides
a ﬁrst-hand evidence that the predominant phase in the commercial
HDH Ti powders is TiO with the co-existence of other oxides. This is an
important ﬁnding for the passivation, handling and storage of Ti
powders from a technical and industrial point of view.
3.2. Characterization of SLMed products and tensile properties
Because of the signi ﬁcantly improved ﬂowability, the jet-milled
powders are able to spread well onto the substrate during SLM as shown
in Fig. 5(a). Subsequently, tensile bars and university logos were suc-
cessfully fabricated by SLM using the jet-milled Ti powders as shown in
Fig. 5(b). Unlike the powders, the XRD results in Fig. 2(a) indicate that
the SLMed CP-Ti parts most likely contain the martensitic α’ phase. The
α’ phase is attributed to the rapid cooling occurring during SLM that
causes the nonequilibrium phase transition and lattice distortion
[7,43]. The ultra ﬁne martensitic α’ phase leads to the shift of the XRD
peaks to a lower angle as compared with the α phase (Fig. 2(a)). The α’
existence features in the SEM images in Fig. 5(c) and (d). This ﬁnding
has also been reported in other studies [ 7,11,16,17,22,23,26]. The
average values of width and length of the martensitic α’ laths are 1.6
and 15.8 μm respectively, when the laser scanning speed was 800 mm/s
in this study. By comparison, the SLMed CP-Ti samples from atomized
powders exhibit the martensitic α’ laths with larger width and length.
For instance, Ref. [ 13] reported that the width and length of martensitic
α′ laths were 2.1 and 15.3 μm, respectively, if atomized powders were
used in SLM. Similar ﬁndings were also reported in a recent study [ 22].
In addition, no columnar morphology is observed in Fig. 5(c)
and (d),
which reveals the resultant weak texture. Further, the SEM images
demonstrate almost full densi ﬁcation in the SLMed parts, reaching a
relative density of 99.4 ± 0.3%. The oxygen increment is negligible in
the SLMed part, as compared to the jet-milled powders ( Table 1).
The representative true tensile stress-strain curve and typical frac-
ture surface of the SLMed CP-Ti are shown in Fig. 5(e). The resultant
fracture tensile strength and elongation of the SLMed samples are
731.5 ± 5.7 MPa and 20.3 ± 1.1%, respectively, with the 0.2% o ﬀset
yield strength of 629.4 MPa. It seems that the SLMed CP-Ti shows a
steady work hardening capacity at high-stress levels, leading to a uni-
form tensile elongation well beyond that of conventional CP-Ti.

4. Discussion
Driven by the high-speed gas ﬂow during jet milling, collision and
friction amongst HDH Ti powders introduce severe plastic deformation
particularly in the powder surface, which is manifested by the presence
of various crystal defects e.g., dislocations and increased grain bound-
aries as shown in Fig. 2(c) and (d). Moreover, the dislocations give rise
to the lattice strain as presented in Fig. 2(e), which should increase with
milling time owing to the increased dislocation density [ 34,53]. Si-
multaneously, shear bands (indicated by arrows in Fig. 2 (c)) can be
formed in the α-Ti due to the severe plastic deformation [ 53,54].
Therefore, the crystal within the heavily strained regions gradually
disintegrates into subgrains or grains that are separated by grain
boundaries ( Fig. 2 (a) cf. Fig. 2(b)). This is a ubiquitous phenomenon
occurring during milling for metallic powders [ 53]. In this case, jet
milling associated with plastic deformation and microdefects generated
in the Ti powder surface contributes to three major outcomes that de-
termine the ﬁnal printing performance. First, the sharp edges on the
powder surface undergo severe plastic deformation and are
subsequently ground down. Consequently, it yields a much high powder
sphericity and a good surface smoothness, and thus remarkably en-
hancing its ﬂowability. Good ﬂowability is a critical requirement for the
SLM processing, as evidenced in Fig. 5(a). Second, the associated de-
fects at an atomic level improve the interaction with laser, rendering
the jet-milled powders more laser-absorptive ( Fig. 1(d)). An increased
laser absorption could bene ﬁt the dimensional control and lower the
processing power [ 55–57]. Third, jet milling is conducted under a ni-
trogen atmosphere and lasts for very short period, which minimizes the
oxygen contamination, as con ﬁrmed by the HRTEM and APT results in
Figs. 3 and 4. Therefore, the impurity level of the jet-milled HDH Ti
powders all meets Grade 2 CP-Ti, much better than the modi ﬁed HDH
Ti powders reported in other studies [ 22,23].
Impurity pick-up is al-
ways a big concern in metallic powders by milling particularly for Ti
and its alloys. In view of the above, we believe jet milling o ﬀers a
promising route to produce high-quality powders for use in AM and
metal injection molding.
The high cooling rate of 10
4∼106 K/s [ 4,5] during SLM favors the
transformation of β → α′ for CP-Ti, a typical nonequilibrium phase
transformation. In addition to cooling rate, the non-equilibrium phase
transformation also depends on the impurity level and processing
parameters used in SLM [ 7,58]. The α′ martensite formation has been
previously identi ﬁed in CP-Ti made by either SLM [ 7,13,17,22,23]o r
electron beam melting [ 59]. The XRD peak broadening, as shown in
Fig. 2 (a), suggests the grain re ﬁnement after SLM. Interestingly, we do
not observe strong texture from the microscopic results in Fig. 5(c) and
(d). This is because the crystallographic orientation relationship asso-
ciated with the β → α′ transformation complies with the Burgers re-
lationship {
1 10}//(0001)α′, ⟨111⟩β//⟨11 2 0⟩α′ [3,59]. Thus, the
twelve α’ variants can be formed from one β grain during solidi ﬁcation,
leading to the random distribution of grains.

5. Conclusions
In summary, we fabricated CP-Ti by SLM using low-cost HDH
powders modi ﬁed by jet milling. The powder characteristics after jet
milling have been examined in terms of ﬂowability, oxide status and
microstructure. The microstructure and mechanical properties of the
SLMed CP-Ti were investigated. The following conclusions can be
drawn.
(1) The use of jet milling leads to plastic deformation in the powder
surface, which remarkably promotes the ﬂowability to 29.7 s/50 g,
introduces microdefects, and enhances the laser absorption by ca.
13.3%.
(2) Impurity pick-up during jet milling is minimal. Predominant TiO
with TiO 2 and Ti 2O3 coexist in the commercial HDH CP-Ti powder
surface.
(3) The resultant superior tensile properties of the SLMed CP-Ti using
the modi ﬁed HDH powders satis ﬁes the ASTM Grade 2 standard,
demonstrating comparable performance as compared to the coun-
terparts made from the expensive atomized powders.
