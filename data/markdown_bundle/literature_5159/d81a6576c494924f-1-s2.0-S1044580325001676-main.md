ABSTRACT
Microstructure informatics, an emerging field, combines traditional quantitative metallography with computer 
vision, algorithmic geometry and data science. It uses automated procedures to retrieve statistically relevant 
information from micrographs. Its power is demonstrated in a case study which focusses on competitive dendrite 
growth during directional solidification of single crystal Ni-base superalloys (SXs) in 3D. We show how micro -
structure informatics allows to follow the evolution of all dendrites in a cylindric SX bar (diameter: 12 mm, 
analyzed length: 76 mm), evaluating serial cross sections taken in 1 mm distances. The method presented in this 
work relies on three basic components: (1) A deep learning object detection network for detecting dendrite core 
positions. (2) A 3D image reconstruction routine for tracing dendrite paths and (3) a relational geometric 
ontological (RGO) database, documenting all relevant relationships between individual dendrites. The method 
allows to characterize crystal mosaicity, individual dendrite growth directions, interactions between dendrites 
and dendrite deformation. The performance of different deep learning classification architectures (AlexNet, 
GoogleNet and MobileNetV2) in combination with a YOLOv2 subdetection network is investigated. The network 
hyper parameters were optimized to achieve detection rates > 99 %. A resulting ontological database of 16,631 
individual dendrites provides a foundation for further automatic quantitative microstructural characterization.
1. Introduction
Cast Nickel-base single crystal superalloys (SXs) are used to make 
blades which operate in the hot parts of gas turbines, where they have to 
withstand high mechanical loads at temperatures not too far from their 
melting point ( ≈ 1000 
◦
C) [ 2 – 4 ]. SXs directionally solidify in a Bridgman 
process [ 5 – 7 ] with carefully adjusted process parameters to control 
crystal orientation [ 5 ] and to avoid solidification defects such as freckles 
[ 8 – 10 ], slivers [ 11 , 12 ] and elongated stray grains [ 13 , 14 ]. These defects 
were studied extensively. However, another microstructural element of 
SXs, referred to as crystal mosaicity, has only recently received adequate 
attention [ 15 – 17 ]. Crystal mosaicity refers to small orientation differ -
ences which exist within a real single crystal [ 5 ]. In SXs mosaicity is 
related to the growth and deformation of dendrites during solidification 
[ 15 – 19 ]. These slightly misoriented regions are separated by low angle 
grain boundaries (LAGBs) which are associated with dislocation net -
works [ 17 , 20 ].
It is well known that local alloy chemistry (preferential partitioning 
of elements to the γ - and γ’ -phase and to defects) and all elements of the 
microstructure (widths of γ -channels, size and volume fraction of 
γ’ -particles, dislocation densities, planar fault densities) strongly affect 
the high temperature strength of Ni-base SX [ 2 – 4 ]. Crystal mosaicity in a 
technological SX is another defect which has been studied. Its evolution 
is directly linked to the physical nature of dendrite formation and 
growth. It has been reported that crystal mosaicity is associated with 
dendrite interactions during directional solidification [ 21 – 24 ] with 
dendrite/mold wall-interactions [ 25 , 26 ], and with the interaction of 
dendrites with the surrounding liquid interdendritic phase [ 27 – 29 ]. 
Crystal mosaicity and its relation to directional dendritic solidification 
has been studied combining optical microscopy and/or scanning elec -
tron microscopy with X-ray [ 30 – 41 ], synchrotron [ 42 – 44 ] and electron 
beam diffraction studies [ 45 , 46 ]. These studies have provided crystal -
lographic information and have helped to identify elementary dendrite 
growth processes.

What is presently missing is information on how crystal mosaicity is 
related to collective phenomena which govern the 3D nature of dendrite 
growth in large material volumes. In order to obtain such information, 
three experimental steps were taken working with a CMSX-4 type of 
alloy. First, a seeded Bridgman technique was developed and docu -
mented which allowed to produce precisely oriented SXs [ 15 ]. Second, a 
series of cross sections in 1 mm distances were taken from a 12 mm 
diameter bar and quantitatively analyzed [ 16 ], to manually identify 
local orientation differences and to analyze elementary dendrite pro -
cesses [ 17 ]. Third, a high-resolution electron back scatter diffraction 
method was developed, referred to as the rotation vector baseline EBSD 
method (RVB-EBSD method), which allowed to resolve orientation dif -
ferences significantly smaller than 0.1
◦
. The manual evaluation in [ 16 ] 
considered a few thousands of dendrites and provided reliable reference 
data. However, the manual method [ 16 ] is tedious and extremely time 
consuming and therefore does not allow to assess larger SX volumes. 
Presently no information exists which provides statistically relevant 
information on the complete dendrite population in a directionally so -
lidified SX-component.
The objective of the present work is to show how modern micro -
structure data science [ 47 – 53 ] can help to overcome this bottleneck. We 
present an automatic machine learning based characterization proced -
ure, which relies on a deep-learning (DL) object detector, is based on a 
3D image registration routine and uses a newly developed database 
architecture which reflects relational geometric ontology. The focus of 
the present work is on the development of the microstructure informatic 
method which allows to fully account for the evolution of the dendrite 
population and crystal mosaicity in large material volumes. But it also 
provides a few examples which demonstrate how the method allows to 
gain new insight. It provides answers to open questions like why large 
dendrite families with similar orientations develop at mold walls, how 
the direction of the orientation of one single misaligned dendrite sur -
vives multiple extinction events, or how the average number of den -
drites in a dendrite family can increase and how the number of 
extinction events evolves during solidification. A subset of the data 
obtained with the present method (full dataset: 16,631 dendrites; 
13,809 branching events; 12,532 extinction events; 130,394 analyzed 
neighborhoods; overall: 156,735 relations) is available for further 
analysis [ 1 ].

A CNN-based object detector learns to identify objects by progres -
sively extracting hierarchical features, localizing objects, and classifying 
them using deep feature representations. The training process consists of 
several key phases, including dataset preparation, model initialization, 
feature extraction through convolutional layers, and classification with 
localization refinement. The core of the object detector consists of 
multiple convolutional layers, which progressively extract spatially 
hierarchical features from the input image. Initial layers capture low- 
level edge and texture information, while deeper layers learn complex 
patterns, such as object shapes and semantic attributes. In single-stage 
detectors (e.g. YOLO [ 76 , 59 ]), object classification and bounding box 
regression occur directly from feature maps without explicit region 
proposals. These models predict bounding box offsets and class proba -
bilities at multiple spatial locations using a dense grid or predefined 
anchor boxes. The learning process is guided by a multi-component loss 
function that jointly optimizes object classification and localization. 
Gradient-based optimization algorithms, such as Stochastic Gradient 
Descent (SGD) [ 81 ], update model parameters iteratively using back -
propagation. For a more detailed explanation of the working principles 
of CNN-based object detection architectures, we refer to the technical 
literature (e.g. [ 58,59,70 – 81 ]).
In the present work we combine the YOLOv2 subdetection network 
with these three backbone networks and with multiple feature extrac -
tion layers (FELs). One of these combinations consisting of a YOLOv2/ 
MobileNetV2-combination is shown in Fig. 5 . When considering all 
layers (including trainable and non-trainable layers) the classification 
network MobileNetV2 consists of 154 layers, subdivided into 7 blocks 
(MATLAB implementation). The image data is fed into the classification 
network where one specific layer acts as FEL. This layer feeds infor -
mation into the sub-detection network, which is trained to localize 
dendrite core positions. The initial layers of a CNN are sensitive to 
simple lower-level features such as edges, corners and rudimentary color 
patterns. Deeper layers represent more complex geometric features. The 
depth of the FEL has a strong influence on the overall performance of 
dendrite detection. Therefore, the performance of multiple FEL was 
systematically explored for each network combination. The blue arrows 
pointing up in Fig. 5 represent FEL which were considered in the present 
work.
Several FELs were chosen for each network, the first convolutional

2.5. Relational geometric ontology (RGO) - database
After detection and three-dimensional registration of dendrite core 
positions, the results are transferred into a database. This database 
represents a digital replica of the dendritic microstructure. Its structure 
reflects a relational geometric ontology (RGO). RGO-data storage re -
quires the detection of all microstructural entities. Geometric repre -
sentations are used to create a database entry for each individual object- 
entity. A database entry corresponds to one object-entity, which 
geometrically represents one specific microstructural feature. Each 
object-entity must be assigned to a microstructural class. The classes can 
be “ grain ” , “ dendrite ” , “ precipitate ” , “ pore ” , “ crack ” , “ dislocation ” , etc. 
Each object-entity is composed of a specific set of attributes of three 
types: (1) The origin: This attribute indicates the identity of the inves -
tigated material, its geometry, the location where microscopy was per -
formed and on which specific micrograph this particular object-entity 
exists. (2) The geometry: This attribute is associated with the geometric 
representation of the object-entity describing its shape and position in 
the micrograph. The geometric representation may be a polygon on a 2D 
cross-section. In 3D space, wireframe models or triangulated surfaces 
can be used. In both 2D and 3D cases, other forms of representations can 
be considered. (3) Class-specific attributes: Class-specific attributes 
represent additional relevant geometric, chemical and physical infor -
mation. For example, the object-entity grain, can be associated with 
additional attributes like grain size, crystal structure and crystal orien -
tation. It is also possible to add information on mechanical properties 
such as elastic properties, hardness, yield stress etc.
There is a second type of entities, which we refer to as relation- 
entities. Relation-entities provide information about the relationships 
between multiple object-entities. Each relation-entity points to two or 
more object-entities, which are involved in a specific relationship. An 
easy example of a relationship could be the neighborhood of two adja -
cent object-entities. Relation entities also contain class-specific attri -
butes. For example, for two adjacent grains, a class-specific attribute can 
be the separating grain boundary represented by line segments together 
with information about the misorientation. Fig. 9 illustrates how the 
RGO method is used to describe polycrystalline grain microstructure. 
The microscopic image in the center of the figure was captured using a 
backscattered electron detector (BSE) in a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM).
In Fig. 9 , two classes are considered, “ grain ” and “ pore ” . Two object- 
entities of the class “ grain ” (G1 and G2) and one object-entity of the class 
“ pore ” (P1) are highlighted. The first attribute highlighted in all three 
object-entities is the origin of the micrograph pointing to the image 
database [ 85 ]. The second attribute specifies the geometry of each 
object-entity. Here, this corresponds to polygons, which geometrically 
represent the morphology of the object-entities. Class-specific details 
represent the third type of attributes that are assigned to each object- 
entity. For the class “ grain ” , class-specific attributes are, for example, 
grain size, equivalent diameter, shape factor, aspect ratio, etc. Relation- 
entities can belong to the class “ neighborhood ” . For instance, the 
relation-entity N1 expresses that the object-entities G1 and G2 are 
neighbors. A class-specific attribute of a neighborhood of grains could be 
a grain boundary associated with a misorientation. An example of a RGO 
database for this grain constellation is available in the scientific re -
pository Zenodo [ 1 ]. This type of RGO concept can be fully generalized 
and applied to all types of micrographs, as schematically illustrated in 
Fig. 10 .
Creating a robust RGO-database and thus creating a microstructural 
digital replica requires automated analysis of micrographs using com -
puter vision (CV) techniques. First, microstructural features are detected 
with either conventional CV methods (binarization [ 86 – 88 ], edge 
detection [ 89 – 91 ], filling algorithms [ 92 ] or advanced techniques based 
on CNNs (object detection [ 93 ], semantic segmentation [ 94 ], instance 
segmentation [ 71 ], etc.). Then, a geometric representation is generated 
for each detected feature.
This information is used to initialize object-entities which geomet -
rically represent individual microstructural features, Fig. 11 . Object- 
entities inherit all information associated with the raw micrograph 
data, such as manufacturing parameters or, in case of ex-service mate -
rials, operating conditions. Spatial relationships between multiple 
object-entities can be identified by using geometric algorithms (Delau -
nay triangulation [ 95 , 96 ], graph theory [ 97 ], Voronoi tessellation 
[ 98 , 99 ], etc.) or by machine learning approaches, e.g. [ 100 , 101 ]. This 
information is stored in relation-entities, which represent the geometric 
relationships between multiple objects, Fig. 11 . In this way, a machine- 
readable digital replica of the investigated sample is created which can 
be used for further automatic microstructure characterization. This type 
of database can be queried to find and analyze the geometry of special 
constellations or to visualize different attributes of microstructural en -
tities. Statistics of subsets can be used for either correlation with

3.1. Network evaluation
A systematic evaluation of network performance is required to 
identify which object detector is best suited for assessing a dendritic 
microstructure. This assessment can be based on the frequencies of right 
positive and false positive detections (abbreviations: RPs and FPs). Care 
must be taken: If one dendrite is detected multiple times, it is only 
counted once (as one RP). Each detection made by an object detector is 
associated with a confidence score value CS, which ranges between 
0 (complete uncertainty) and 1 (high certainty). A low CS ensures that 
most dendrite cores are identified and that the number of RPs is high. It 
will, however, also result in an increase of FPs. A high CS, on the other 
hand, has the opposite effect: not all dendrites will be detected (lower 
RPs) while the number of FPs decreases. The influence of CS also needs 
to be explored. For each test image, both the RPs and FPs were 
normalized by the number of dendrites per image D. With these defi -
nitions one can define the performance of a detector using a detection 
loss parameter DL (CS): 
DL ( CS ) = 1 RP ( CS ) / D + FP ( CS ) / D (1) 
For best detection results DL(CS) must be minimized. Optimum 
detection corresponds to DL(CS) → 0. Fig. 12 shows how the confidence 
score influences results obtained with the network M13 using an anchor 
box size of 5 × 5 px. Figs. 12 a, b and c show that good detection is 
reached for CS = 0.6 (all dendrites are recognized – Fig. 12 a, no false 
detections are made – Fig. 12 b, the detection loss is close to 0 – Fig. 12 c). 
Fig. 12 d shows how the results improve as the size of the anchor box 
decreases.

3.2. Characterization of dendrite growth and microstructure evolution
The best performing detector (M30) was chosen to detect dendrite 
cores in samples between height positions of 12 mm (start of newly 
grown crystal) and 88 mm, Fig. 14 . The objective was to reconstruct the 
evolution of dendritic microstructures and to assess changes in the de -
gree of mosaicity. All relations between dendrites, including neighbor -
hoods and branching/extinction events, were automatically assigned 
using the method described above.
In order to fully appreciate the data presented in Fig. 14 , we recall 
fundamental aspects of dendritic solidification processes: First, each 
dendrite grows into its own 〈 001 〉 direction [ 102 ]. Therefore, de -
viations in growth directions of specific dendrites or of groups of specific 
dendrites can be used as an indicator for crystal mosaicity [ 15 , 16 ]. 
Second, there are thermodynamic and kinetic factors which govern the 
dynamics of dendritic solidification [ 5 – 7 , 103 – 105 ]. In general, dendrite 
growth results in dendrite spacings which fall into a specific range 
[ 106 ]. When two dendrites grow apart and the local spacing between 
them exceeds a threshold value, the available interdendritic space is 
filled with a new primary dendrite [ 106 ]. These new dendrites result 
from branching events [ 107 , 108 ], where a pre-existing primary dendrite 
forms a secondary dendrite arm which grows in lateral direction. A 
tertiary dendrite arm emanates from this secondary dendrite in the same 
direction as the primary dendrite. When it reaches a critical size, it 
evolves into an apparent new primary dendrite growing in the solidifi -
cation direction. Third, dendrite growth is competitive [ 108 ], when 
dendrite growth directions are not parallel [ 15 , 16 ]. Converging and 
diverging growth results in the extinction of individual dendrites, which 
are overgrown. Further details on these solidification phenomena can be 
found in literature [ 108 – 111 ].

4. Discussion
4.1. Dendrite Core detection
Many aspects of dendrite growth can in principle be manually 
analyzed retrieving tomographic information from micrographs taken 
from sequential cross sections of a single crystal bar using conventional 
quantitative image analysis procedures [ 15 , 16 ]. While this works well 
for not too large data sets, it becomes tedious when several thousands of 
dendrites need to be analyzed and details regarding the evolution of 
dendrite relations and their interactions are of interest. This short -
coming was recognized, and it was proposed to use cross-correlation 
algorithms for dendrite detection, which rely on one reference 
dendrite to locate all others [ 112 ]. This helped to shorten detection 
times while maintaining a high detection rate. However, the results 
strongly depended on the features of the chosen reference dendrite and 
generalization was difficult. Recently [ 113 , 114 ], artificial intelligence 
approaches were used to detect dendrite cores from SEM-images. These 
seminal papers inspired the present work. R-CNN object detection net -
works were trained to detect dendrite cores.
Here we provide additional information on how the underlying 
backbone networks, the feature extraction layer (FEL), the sizes of an -
chor boxes and the selected confidence score (CS) value influence the 
results, Figs. 12 and 13 . Anchor box sizes which fully cover the core of 
the dendrite without intersecting surrounding anchor boxes or con -
taining too much interdendritic region yield the best results ( Table 3 , 
anchor box sizes: between 25 × 25 px and 50 × 50 px). However, as 
shown in Fig. 13 a, even anchor boxes as small as 5 × 5 px allow to 
achieve a low detection loss, however only in a narrow CS range. When 
deeper layers are used for the FEL, DL minima shift to lower CS values, 
Fig. 12 . Detectors then exhibit increasing uncertainties regarding the 
presence/absence of dendrite cores. Earlier layers of a CNN focus on 
simple features such as edges and differences in color. The more complex 
features of deeper network layers cannot be fully explored, when a small 
5 × 5 px anchor box does not provide enough information for learning 
how to recognize a dendrite.
The regions around the DL minima (close to zero detection loss) in 
the DS(CS) curves are also important. Figs. 12 shows that optimum 
detection requires that the right positive detection rate is high (e.g. 
Fig. 12 a: CS < 0.6), while there are no more false positives (e.g. Fig. 12 b: 
CS > 0.5). The left part of the DL-curve in Fig. 12 c is dominated by the 
exponential decrease of the FP-rate, before its right part is fully governed 
by RP. Comparing the DL-curves for box sizes 5 × 5 px and 40 × 40 px in 
Figs. 13 a and b, one can see that for M30 and M56, the CS-regions were 
the DL-curves show the lowest values are wider for a box size of 40 × 40 
px than for 5 × 5 px. Object detectors trained with 40 × 40 px anchor 
boxes perform better in differentiating between false and right positives. 
Detectors trained with middle size anchor boxes show a higher robust -
ness in dendrite core detection, especially when dealing with micro -
scopic images with varying etching quality.
The SEM images used in for previous AI-work [ 113 , 114 ] were more 
consistent in terms of color distribution, contrast, and brightness. Thus, 
high quality SEM images ( μ m scale) are easier to work with. SEM im -
aging, however, is limited to smaller sample regions and suitable 
metallographic cross sections are more difficult to prepare. Therefore, 
for the present dendrite study, optical microscopy (mm to cm scale) is 
the method of choice. The associated difficulties related to etching 
variations can be compensated by employing a suitable computer vision 
architecture. Even in unfavorable etching conditions, detection rates are 
high ( > 97 % of right positives; < 2 % of false positives were detected; 
M30, Table 3 ).
4.2. Relational geometric ontology (RGO)
One central element of the proposed procedure is the design and 
implementation of a database which fully reflects relational geometric 
ontology (RGO). In the present context, ontology refers to structured 
information related to the evaluation of dendrites, covering individual 
morphological details and collective growth phenomena. For this pur -
pose, microstructural information must be converted into machine 
readable digital twins by applying computer vision (CV) techniques. In 
the present methodology, the CV approach relies on neural networks 
which detect the positions of dendrite cores. Two different approaches 
for the generation of digital representations have been proposed: 
Descriptor representations and spatial representations. Descriptor rep -
resentations generally transform experimental or simulated micro -
structures into one or multiple numeric parameters, which are 
translation-invariant and stationary. These can represent classical 
microstructural elements, such as grain size distributions or particle 
volume fractions [ 115 ]. Recently, more advanced multi-dimensional 
descriptors were developed, which can be based on lineal-path func -
tions [ 116 ], spatial n-point correlations [ 117 ], cluster correlation 
functions [ 118 ] or fractal boundary dimension [ 119 ]. The possibility to 
automatically generate descriptors using CNNs has also been considered 
[ 120 ]. Automatically generated descriptors are difficult to physically 
interpret and are highly dependent on network architecture and training 
data. Descriptor based digital representations allow to generate artificial 
microstructures and to correlate microstructural parameters with pro -
cess conditions and material properties.
For the analysis of microstructural processes, spatial representations 
in 3D are required. No generally accepted standard software for pre -
senting the large variety of different types of microstructural data (e.g. 
EBSD, EDX, APT, etc.) exists. Providers of commercial microstructure 
software tend to keep customers within their own software bubbles. The 
developers of Dream3D [ 121 ] have proposed to unify microstructural 
information by transforming the raw microscopic data into a generalized 
concept of a geometric mesh [ 121 ]. Each data point on the discrete mesh 
is associated with exactly one unique microstructural feature. The 
microstructural features are then stored together with the transformed 
raw microscope mesh data and metadata about filter procedures within 
a hierarchical data structure (HDF5) [ 122 ].
The RGO developed in the present study is based on spatial repre -
sentations and differs from other methodologies. First, we detach the 
ontological microstructural information from the imaging process. Mi -
croscope data remain unmodified as ground truth data and can be 
organized by any file system (from any operating system) and stored in 
online repositories, such as Zenodo [ 1 ]. This eliminates the necessity to 
rely on specific software packages to browse through the raw data and to 
display individual datasets. Second, we detach microstructural features 
from the geometric mesh associated with the microscope system, by 
converting them into object-entities. The object-entities are represented 
by geometric figures using absolute coordinates, which are not corre -
lated to the raw data ’ s pixel grid. This allows to represent microstruc -
tures in a flat hierarchy. Object- and relation-entities are the only types 
of entities considered in the present RGO approach. They are grouped 
together at the same level of hierarchy. Object-entities geometrically 
represent microstructural features. They contain metadata about the 
sample as attributes. Relation-entities provide information about spatial

relationships between multiple object entities. Our RGO represents a 
dictionary, listing all entities (and their relationships) on a micrograph 
and indicating where the raw microscope data is stored. RGO data can 
be exported in a variety of ways for accessible exchange. In the micro -
structure informatics community, different data structures are used to 
store microstructural information. The HDF5 format is widely used since 
it can represent complex data structures in a hierarchical manner in 
combination with metadata [ 122 ]. It was also suggested to use an open- 
source CAD format [ 123 ], which allows to investigate microstructures 
geometrically, by using established CAD software. In our approach we 
have chosen the JSON file format to store RGO data, a format which is 
established in multiple fields of science, including biology and genetics

4.3. Dendrite growth and crystal Mosaicity
Wang et al. [ 125 ] explored the evolution of dendrites in shrouds of 
single crystal blades. The present work represents a new attempt to 
analyze the evolution of the dendritic microstructure in large sample 
volumes (here: close to 8500 mm
3
). In reconstructing the solidification 
history of one cylindrical bar, one can account for each individual 
dendrite in the test volume. The original objective of the present work 
was to explore how the evolution of the dendrite population leads to 
crystal mosaicity. Mosaicity is partly inherited from the seed crystal 
[ 54 ], but it then evolves further during solidification [ 15 , 16 ].
Although the present study primarily focuses on dendrite in -
teractions during solidification, it is essential to consider how variations 
in seed quality and orientation influence the resulting microstructure. 
Small deviations of the seed crystal from the ideal [001] target orien -
tation can introduce an angular offset in dendrite growth directions. 
During solidification, already existing misorientations within the seed 
may be amplified by competitive dendrite growth, branching events, 
and local deformation phenomena, leading to an increased spread in 
dendrite orientations and, consequently, greater mosaicity. Further -
more, the presence of pre-existing LAGBs and dislocations in the seed 
crystal can act as preferential sites for heterogeneous nucleation at the 
seed/melt interface. This can result in variations in primary dendrite 
spacing, which in turn affects the competitive growth dynamics of the 
developing microstructure. If the seed crystal contains regions with 
different stored elastic energy due to residual dislocations, local strain 
fields may further contribute to variations in dendrite bending and 
mosaicity development.
Dendrite bending, as demonstrated in Fig. 15 b, is caused by thermal 
and solidification stresses [ 15 , 16 , 18 , 36 , 45 , 46 ] and contributes to the 
formation of crystal mosaicity. In-situ synchrotron studies have shown 
how the solid/liquid-interface propagates during solidification 
[ 9 , 11 , 25 ]. Aveson et al. [ 25 ] could document the deformation of den -
drites during an in-situ synchrotron experiment. In-situ experiments 
provide impressive insights into elementary processes. But they are 
restricted to small sample volumes, which allow to only study a limited 
number of dendrites. Moreover, thermomechanical interactions be -
tween dendrites and the crucible wall can play a significant role. X-ray 
studies allowed to monitor dendrite growth and deformation processes 
in parts of single crystal turbine blades [ 37 , 41 ]. But X-ray studies, which 
are not combined with serial sectioning, only provide 2D information. 
The need to analyze the unconstrained growth of a large number of 
dendrites to capture collective phenomena was pointed out by Hallen -
sleben et al. [ 15 ]. They performed quantitative image analysis on 2D 
longitudinal cross sections and could identify local dendrite growth 
events. However, the longitudinal cross sections were never exactly 
parallel to individual dendrites and therefore specific dendrites could 
never been traced back over sufficiently long distances to appreciate 
their full history. Scholz et al. showed in their semi-manual study [ 16 ], 
how this can be achieved in a 3D tomographic approach, where many 
dendrites are continuously tracked, and collective growth phenomena 
can be revealed. The present work builds on the approach of Scholz et al. 
[ 16 ] but uses microstructure informatics which allows to characterize a 
much larger number of dendrites and thus allows to reveal collective 
solidification phenomena. The procedure introduced in the present 
study not only allows to reconstruct the growth paths of all dendrites in a 
solidifying bar, but moreover provides new microstructural information. 
It shows how specific microstructural features evolve on a centimeter 
and decimeter length scale, which is relevant for cast components, 
Fig. 14 . As outlined in the result section, the method reveals that all 
dendrites deviate by a few degrees from the ideal targeted orientation 
([001] ǁ z
s
, Fig. 1 b). These small deviations are caused by the mosaicity 
of the seed crystal, and by a slight misorientation of the seed crystal. 
These deviations are small as compared to the angular deviations of up 
to 15
◦

Figs. 14 b to d. Our results show that the average misorientation of 
dendrites remains nearly constant during solidification, while the 
orientation spread increases, Fig. 14 c and d.
Fig. 15 a and b reveal that many dendrites deviate from perfectly 
straight growth paths. Fig. 15 b clearly shows that several blue dendrites 
continuously change their growth trajectories. It is well known that the 
growth directions of dendrites are governed by their crystallography 
[ 126 , 127 ]. The [001] direction represents the natural solidification di -
rection for face centered cubic crystals like the γ -phase in Ni-base SX. 
Thus, in an ideal SX, all dendrites in ideally solidifying crystal would be 
perfectly straight and parallel to each other. Figs. 14 a, 15 a and b show 
that this is not the case. This represents microstructural evidence for the 
deformation of dendrites during solidification. Most likely these defor -
mation processes are caused by thermal or solidification stresses as 
discussed in [ 19 , 128 – 130 ]. These findings merit further analysis. Thus, 
there is a need to proof whether certain arrangements of dendrites (e.g. 
related to the numbers and distances of nearest neighbor dendrites) can 
also promote deformation events. This type of statistical analysis re -
quires further analysis and is beyond the scope of the present work. 
Another striking observation is that the frequency of branching events 
increases during solidification, growth competition becomes more 
intense, Fig. 15 d. The propagation of non-parallel dendrites continu -
ously results in convergent and divergent growth scenarios [ 131 – 133 ]. 
Branching occurs when the increasing distance between non-parallel 
nearest neighbor dendrites exceeds a certain threshold value 
[ 106 , 132 , 134 ], such that elongated secondary dendrites can fill the 
resulting space and emit tertiary dendrites, which grow parallel to the 
primary dendrites.
While the present study focuses on the CMSX-4 alloy, the method -
ology developed can be readily applied to other Ni-base single crystal 
superalloys. The solidification dynamics observed in CMSX-4, such as 
dendrite competition, branching, and extinction events, can be expected 
to be similar in other Ni-base single crystal superalloys. Future work is 
required to understand the influence of alloy chemistry and solidifica -
tion conditions on the features of dendrite configurations. The deep 
learning object detection framework can be easily re-trained on new 
datasets corresponding to different alloys, if the morphology of den -
drites differs too much from those existing in the current training data. 
To further validate the generalizability of this approach, we are planning 
future publications that involve testing the methodology on a broader 
range of single crystals with varying processing conditions. This will 
allow for a more comprehensive assessment of dendrite growth mech -
anisms and the evolution of crystal mosaicity across different alloy 
systems. Such an extension could also provide valuable insight into how 
alloy-specific solidification kinetics influence dendritic interactions and 
microstructural heterogeneity in technologically relevant cast 
components.

high temperature strength. In the as-cast SX-material states, the parti -
tioning tendencies of alloy elements lead to significant chemical dif -
ferences between dendritic and interdendritic regions of the 
microstructure. For example, Re and W prefer dendritic regions while Al 
and Ti partition to interdendritic regions [ 54 , 137 ]. These chemical 
heterogeneities associated with dendritic solidification never fully 
vanish but are significantly reduced during the post cast multiple step 
homogenization/precipitation heat treatment [ 54 , 137 ]. What remains, 
however, are the local and global crystallographic orientations which 
result from the dendritic solidification. Global crystallographic de -
viations from the [001] crystallographic growth directions must be kept 
at a minimum. There are cases where deviations by 15
◦
are not 
acceptable [ 138 ]. From the fact that crystal mosaicity is associated with 
an orientation spread one must conclude that technical single crystals, 
even when working with seed crystals, are never precisely oriented in 
one [001] direction. Instead, they exhibit an orientation spread of a few 
degrees around an average direction, which is close to [001]. These 
small deviations do not result in significant changes in resolved shear 
stresses in glide systems, so that they most probably do not have a strong 
effect on glide and climb processes. However, these small deviations are 
associated with the presence of subgrain boundaries, which separate 
dendrites or groups of a few dendrites. These subgrain boundaries may 
well affect high temperature creep. They can act as sources of disloca -
tions in the early stages of creep [ 20 , 139 ]. High temperature solid so -
lution strengtheners like Re can segregate to low angle grain boundaries 
and change local properties [ 140 – 143 ]. Representing internal in -
terfaces, low angle grain boundaries can act as nucleation sites for un -
desired intermetallic phases [ 144 , 145 ]. Subgrain boundaries form 
during the dendritic solidification and remain in the microstructure after 
a multiple step heat treatment of a Ni-base SX. They effect the high 
temperature strength of superalloys, which has long been ignored. 
Further work is required to fully describe how subgrain boundaries form 
during dendritic solidification and how they affect creep properties. 
Recently, electron beam manufacturing has been used to produce Ni- 
base single crystals with very small dendrite spacings and an associ -
ated high density of low angle grain boundaries [ 146 , 147 ]. Here, due 
their much higher area density, the role of subgrain boundaries is likely 
to be more important than in directionally solidified Ni-base superal -
loys. Further quantitative metallographic work is required to explore 
this aspect.
5. Summary
The present work documents the development of a microstructure 
informatics procedure and demonstrates its potential for analyzing 
crystal mosaicity caused by competitive dendrite growth during the 
directional solidification of a Ni-based single crystal superalloy. Optical 
wide field micrographs from 1 mm spaced cross sections were taken 
from a cylindrical bar with 12 mm diameter over a length of 76 mm. The 
method presented in this work relies on three basic components: (1) A 
deep learning object detection network for detecting dendrite core po -
sitions. (2) A 3D image reconstruction routine for tracing dendrite paths. 
(3) And an ontological database of relational geometric data, doc -
umenting relationships between individual dendrites. The results ob -
tained in the present work can be summarized as follows:
(1) The present method accounts for the 3D morphological aspects of 
dendritic solidification in a technologically relevant SX volume. It not 
only allows to follow the paths of all dendrites (16,631 objects) in the 
sample volume but moreover provides new quantitative insight into 
competitive growth with a focus on branching and extinction events and 
neighborhoods (156,735 relations).
(2) The method allows to identify dendrite families and to rationalize 
the evolution of crystal mosaicity during directional solidification. The 
data shows that due to bending events during solidification, dendrites 
can exhibit curved growth paths. This deteriorates single crystallinity by 
promoting mosaicity without affecting the average crystal orientation.
(3) Using dendritic solidification of SX as a case study, it was 
demonstrated that an optimized computer vision methodology, based on 
deep learning neural network object detection, achieves superior 
detection rates and better statistics than can ever be obtained using 
conventional quantitative metallography.
(4) In the present work multiple deep learning architectures were 
trained using manually annotated reference images. Detection perfor -
mances were evaluated based on manually curated ground truth data. 
The best performing network consisted of the first 30 layers of a Mobi -
leNetV2 architecture combined with a YOLOv2 sub-detection network. 
Optimum results were obtained when combining this architecture with 
an anchor box size of 35 × 35 px using a confidence score (CS) of 0.4.
(5) A central element of the proposed procedure is a microstructure 
specific relational geometric ontology database (RGO). It contains in -
formation on 16,631 individual dendrites and provides a foundation for 
further quantitative evaluation. It represents a digital replica of all 
relevant morphological features of the 3D dendritic microstructure. As 
one example, the present work shows how the database can be accessed 
to evaluate the orientation spread of growth directions, to detect mis -
aligned dendrites and to evaluate the evolution of dendrite families 
resulting from repeated branching events. The proposed methodology 
and especially the developed database scheme can be fully generalized. 
It can act as a foundation for digital infrastructures for microstructure 
characterization in the future.
