ABSTRACT 
Coupling a multi-scale thermo-kinetic and thermo-mechanical model with critical experiments, the present study 
investigates the role of process parameters on the resulting densification, cracking behavior, and microstructural 
evolution in laser-based powder bed fusion (LPBF) of pure tungsten. Thermal management strategies comprising 
shorter scan length (1 mm) and/or substrate preheating (873 K) were explored to achieve higher densities and 
mitigate cracks. A finite element (FE) based thermo-kinetic and thermo-mechanical computational model was 
developed to simulate the process spanning from the melt pool scale to the component level. FE simulations 
indicated pronounced heat accumulation with reduced scanning lengths. Such increased heat accumulation 
resulted in melt pools with larger dimensions (width and depth) and more favorable rheological characteristics 
resulting in printed tungsten parts with higher densities. Using an input laser fluence (ILF) of 28.6 J/mm
2 
and a 
shorter scan length of 1 mm, a relative density of maximum 99.2% was achieved, the highest reported value using 
LPBF in case of pure tungsten . FE simulations indicated nearly 5-fold reduction in thermal gradients and 2-fold 
reduction in cooling rates associated with the processing conditions corresponding to low degree of cracking 
(ILFs:28.6 J/mm
2 
and ILF 15.9 J/mm
2 
augmented with substrate preheating at 873 K) compared to the pro -
cessing conditions of high degree of cracking (ILF: 15.9 J/mm
2
). Further, high ILF samples were characterized by 
significant extent of in-grain misorientations and low angle boundaries indicating that process induced in-situ 
restoration mechanisms are operative, thereby potentially aiding in reduction of cracking. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten is a unique metal with an outstanding combination of 
properties such as high melting point (3695 K), superior strength (yield 
strength > 1 GPa at room temperature [1] ), high thermal conductivity 
(~174 Wm

(4.3 × 10

These properties make it a material of choice for some of the critical 
applications, such as radiation collimation [ 5 , 6 ], fusion reactor 
plasma-facing cladding [7 – 9] and hypersonic vehicles [10] . However, 
conventional bulk processing of tungsten is complicated, as it falls under 
the category of difficult-to-process refractory material due to its high 
melting point, low ductility at room temperature, and distinctly high 
ductile-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) onset in the range of 473 K 
to 673 K [11] . As a result, powder metallurgical processing has been 
adopted as the most viable commercial option for manufacturing 
tungsten-based components [12] . However, the emergence of 
high-energy beam (laser or electron) fusion-based additive 
manufacturing (AM) may provide potential solutions to the aforemen -
tioned challenges in processing pure tungsten. The layer-by-layer 
melting and consolidation of powder feedstock into a fully dense part 
have already been successfully achieved for a host of other refractory 
materials (with high DBTTs) such as Mo [13] , Ta [14] , Cr [15] , and Nb 
[16] . In fusion-based AM, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) as an alter -
native processing route for fabrication of tungsten has been extensively 
explored in the recent past [ 4 , 17 – 19 ]. However, considering the high 
thermal conductivity, high melting temperature, and high DBTT of 
tungsten material, LPBF of such material primarily poses challenges in 
achieving higher densification and suppression of cracking (solid-state).

In the context of densification of tungsten in the LPBF process, crit -
ical process parameters such as laser power (P), hatch spacing (h), 
scanning speed (v), and layer thickness ( l

melting mode and post melting consolidation mechanisms [ 20 , 21 ]. 
Furthermore, parameters such as powder bed packing density ( φ ), laser 
absorptivity ( α ), and laser beam diameter (d) are additional factors that 
critically influence densification of the AM printed component in LPBF 
[20] . Most of the open literature reports on LPBF of tungsten focused on 
parametric optimization to achieve fully dense printed parts. The re -
ported relative density (with process parameter variation) ranged from 
80% to 98% [ 4 , 6 , 22 , 23 ]. The experimental observations attributed 
lack-of-fusion type pore defects as the primary bottleneck for achieving 
higher level of densification. As tungsten undergoes melting during 
interaction with the laser beam, its high dynamic viscosity and thermal 
conductivity detrimentally affect the melt pool convection, resulting in 
poor wetting of the melt pool with the seed plate/preceding layer and 
concurrently limits its spreading [24] . Therefore, fabrication of tungsten 
parts with 99% or higher density is challenging using LPBF [19] . 
Based on the available open literature, the complete elimination of 
cracking is the most challenging facet in LPBF of tungsten [19] . The 
DBTT onset range (~473 – 673 K) of tungsten ensures that any LPBF 
component eventually attains the concerned level of temperatures dur -

consolidated material experiences appreciable level of residual stress

that higher level of residual stress and strain rate were experienced when 
the material cooled down in the DBTT regime after solidification [25] . 
The modeling efforts related to LPBF of tungsten have been confined to 
single track and melt pool scale simulations without consideration to 
component scale thermo-kinetic evolution often experienced in actual 
component level AM fabrication. 
Based on this background, the current work aims to develop an un -
derstanding of the thermo-kinetic and thermo-mechanical aspects 
associated with the LPBF of tungsten, aided by a unique custom- 
designed multi-physics FEM-based thermo-mechanical model appli -
cable across multiple length scales, from the melt pool to the component 
scale. In this context, numerical results from the melt pool scale thermal 
model are used to calculate pertinent non-dimensional numbers to 
delineate different densification mechanisms with respect to different 
input energies. In addition, the evolution of thermal gradients, cooling 
rates, equivalent stresses, and strain rates in the DBTT range (post so -
lidification) were examined at the melt pool as well as component length 
scales. The numerical results shed some light on manipulation of laser 
scanning length within a layer scan pattern for better control of above- 
mentioned thermo-mechanical parameters. Thus, the current work 
employed stripe scanning strategy with stripe length considerably less 
than the length and width of the printed layer. The primary motive of 
employing such a scanning pattern is to achieve sustained temperature 
elevation during layer-by-layer printing. The physics-informed experi -
mental investigations demonstrated that the change in stripe length 
(scan length within the stripe scan pattern) led to improvement in 
densification, resulting in a highest ever achieved relative density of 
99.2%, as well as reduction in crack density in the absence of substrate 
preheating. For the purpose of further understanding of available crack 
mitigation strategies, a set of experiments were conducted with pre -
heating the substrate to the highest available temperature of 873K, 
within the limits of capabilities of the AM machine. Lastly, multi-scale 
microstructural characterization of the fabricated samples with respect 
to input energy and substrate preheat conditions provided insights into 
possible in-situ recovery/recrystallization during LPBF of tungsten.

2.2. Numerical method 
The present study employed a multi-physics multi-scale finite 
element (FE) framework to predict the thermo-kinetic and thermo- 
mechanical effects induced during LPBF of tungsten at the melt-pool 
and the component length scales. As reported in the previous litera -
ture [ 20 , 21 ], laser-material interaction during LPBF is a complex phe -
nomenon which involves multiple reflections of laser beam, melt pool 
convection, and vaporization. Inclusion of these discrete physical events 
in the numerical model is computationally expensive, moreover, scaling 
of such a numerical model from melt pool up to the component length 
scale, if not impossible, is extremely complex and difficult. Therefore, a 
computationally tractable approach which tackles the laser-material 
interaction as a purely conduction-based problem, was adopted. At the 
melt pool length scale, a volumetric heat source-based pure conduction 
thermal model was coupled with a quasi-static mechanical model which 
comprises a bilinear hardening function along with consideration of 
thermal expansion and contraction effects. In order to predict accurate 
thermal representation of the LPBF process, careful calibration of the 
volumetric heat source-based pure conduction thermal model is 
required. Thus, single track/ single layer experiments were conducted 
with same processing parameters as discussed in the sections above, and 
the thermal model was calibrated with respect to the experimentally 
generated melt pool geometries. The calibrated thermal model coupled 
with quasi-static elastic-plastic formulation was used to predict 
thermo-kinetics (thermal cycling, thermal gradient, heat accumulation) 
and thermo-mechanical (residual stress, strain rate) effects corre -
sponding to a single melting event (consolidation of single track) and 
multiple melting events (consolidation of multiple tracks in the single 
layer). The above approach also helped in delineating effects of different 
stripe length ( S
L
) on temperature, densification, and state-of-stress 
during layer-by-layer printing. 
The scaling of the melt pool scale model up to the component level 
was achieved using the heat source scaling method prescribed in the 
literature [ 41 , 42 ]. The calibrated thermal model was used to extract 
information such as melt pool depth, layer exposure time, and thermal 
history. After that, an analytically equivalent volumetric heat source is 
formulated and supplied to the entire layer geometry to emulate heat 
accumulation during layer-by-layer printing which in current study is 
expressed as the evolution of a range of temperature over corresponding 
duration (time). Although, such an approach provided an approximate 
solution of the layer heating phenomenon, the equivalent heat source for 
an entire layer alleviates the requirement of fine mesh size along the 
laser scanning path. Thus, residual stress evolution due to thermal 
cycling effect of the entire LPBF component can be calculated with 
reasonable computational time and resources. The mathematical 
formulation for the thermal-elastic-plastic problem is described in the 
following subsections.

2.2.5. Solution procedure 
The mathematical formulations described in the sections above were 
solved using a commercial FEM code COMSOL Multiphysics ® , the 
governing equations ( Eqs. (2) , 15 – 8 ) were solved using the already 
existing FEM framework prescribed for thermal and thermal elastic- 
plastic problems. The thermal model and the quasi-static mechanical 
model were solved using a bi-directional coupling with the help of a 
segregated solver. In order to emulate layer-by-layer printing, a quiet 
activation/deactivation strategy was utilized [ 20 , 52 ]. In case of melt 
pool length scale thermo-mechanical model, the activation/deactivation 
status of mesh elements depended on the transient laser beam position 
and the nodal temperature. Whereas, in case of component scale model, 
each layer/meta-layer was activated at the start of the layer heating 
simulation time step. The computational domain consisted of a 100 mm 
× 100 mm × 10 mm substrate. The printed computational domain size 
was 10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm. In case of melt pool length scale model, 
each printed layer size was as per the actual layer thickness (i.e., 50 µ m). 
On the other hand, for the component scale model, the agglomeration 
size factor was taken as ζ = 10. The meshing of the computational domain 
was performed using quadrilateral mapped elements. Separate mesh 
convergence analysis was employed for both melt pool scale as well as 
components scale models. As per the mesh convergence analysis, in case 
of melt pool length scale model, the minimum mesh element size was 
kept at 10 µ m in the powder bed domain, and 150 µ m in the substrate 
domain. On the other hand, a uniform mesh size of 100 µ m was assigned 
to both substrate and powder bed domain for the components scale 
model. The computation was performed on an Intel(R) Xeon (R) (Gold 
6252CPU @2.10 GHz-190 GB) processor. The solution procedure in the 
form of flow diagram is expressed in Fig. 4 . Note that, the indigenous 
delineation of the entire problem in terms of melt-pool length scale and 
component scale model provided unique capabilities in obtaining 
thermo-kinetic and thermo-mechanical evolution experienced by the 
material during layer-by-layer fabrication. For instance, the component 
scale model helps in evaluating the overall increase in temperature of 
the component with sequential laser processing (considering the cooling 
period due to powder spreading after each layer). In addition, the 
temperature evolved within the component during laser heating period 
and after appropriate cooling can be utilized as model input to melt pool 
length scale model. Such an approach helps in resolving the effects of 
thermal-management and pre-heating on the thermo-mechanical evo -
lution, which in turn provides reasonable understanding of densification 
and cracking behavior of tungsten. Furthermore, the entire model 
extensively described in this section was based on transient thermoki -
netic and thermomechanical phenomena at multiple scales evolving 
during multi-track multi-layer laser based additive manufacturing of 
tungsten. Such comprehensive and integrated multiscale-multiphysics 
computational approach to explaining physical and microstructural 
evolutions in multi-track multi-layer laser based additive manufacturing 
of tungsten holds tremendous potential and establishes a platform for 
AM processing of difficult to fabricate refractory materials.

In summary, the present approach of integration of experiments and 
numerical simulations reveal the possibility of interesting in-situ resto -
ration mechanisms, promoted by process induced deformations, thermal 
cycles, and thermal stresses unique to the LPBF process. These mecha -
nisms in turn led to substantial microstructural and morphological 
modifications such as substructures, in-grain mis-orientations, disloca -
tions patterning and sub-grain boundaries, all of which mitigate 
cracking tendency. The inherent rapid thermal cycling to higher tem -
peratures, heat accumulation due to multi-track and multi-layer nature 
of the AM processing, was accentuated by the application of shorter scan 
lengths which further promoted these micro-mechanisms and resulted in 
a comparatively lower cracking tendency. The current study highlights 
the promising prospect of reducing the cracking and improving the build 
density via the application of high ILF ( > 20 J/mm
2
) combined with a 
shorter stripe length (1 mm). Moreover, with the help of multiphysics 
numerical modeling, the role of thermo-mechanical parameters such as 
heat accumulation, thermal gradient, cooling rate, state of stress and 
strain rates on densification and cracking mechanism was revealed to 
provide future pathways towards successful and reproducible LPBF 
processing of tungsten without or with minimal physical defects. Lastly, 
crack mitigation strategies such as substrate preheating and their 
consequential impact on thermal gradients and the state-of stress pro -
vides significant insight into crack density reduction.
