abstract
A series of TiNb(Sn) alloys were synthesized by copper mold suction casting and subjected to different
heat treatments (furnace cooling or water quenching). The microstructure, thermal and mechanical prop-
erties of the as-cast and heat treated samples were investigated. For the Ti–8.34 at.% Nb alloy, the as-cast
and water quenched samples possess martensitic
a00 phase at room temperature and compression tests of
these samples show occurrence of shape memory effect. For b phase Ti–25.57 at.% Nb alloys, stress-
induced martensitic transformation was found during compression in the as-cast and water quenched
samples. For the ternary Ti–25.05 at.%Nb–2.04 at.%Sn alloy, conventional linear elastic behavior was
observed. It is shown that the addition of Sn increases the stability of the b phase. The Young’s moduli
of these alloys were also measured by ultrasonic measurements. Water-quenched Ti–25.57 at.%Nb alloy
was found to exhibit the lowest Young’s modulus value. Sn addition has small impact on the Young’s
moduli of the TiNb alloys.

1. Introduction
Titanium based alloys have been widely used as biomaterials
for body implants [1] such as hip joints, dental implants and med-
ical devices due to their excellent corrosion resistance, high yield
strength and good ductility. However, most commercially used
Ti-based implant alloys exhibit much higher Young’s modulus (E)
when compared to human bones (around 30 GPa or less) and the
resulting stress shielding effect enhances bone resorption [2].S o
it is of great importance to develop Ti-based alloys with lower
Young’s modulus.
There exist four non-equilibrium phases in the Ti alloy at room
or body temperature. These are body-centered b phase which has a
Young’s modulus well below that of the equilibrium hexagonal a
phase, hexagonal a0 martensite, orthorhombic a00 martensite and
hexagonal x phase. Among these phases, a0 and a00 can be obtained
through quenching from above the equilibrium a/b transition tem-
perature depending on compositions and quenching rates, while, x
particles can be formed either by quenching, referred to as ather-
mal
x, or by aging, referred as isothermal x [3]. Young’s modulus
(E) of x phase is highest among the above four non-equilibrium
phases, but the value of E for single phase a00 and b has not been
determined with certainty and different researchers have pre-
sented contradictory results [4–6]. Therefore, from the view point
of lowing Young’s modulus, x phase should deﬁnitely be avoided,
and it can be retarded by addition of Al, Sn, O [7]. Moreover, meta-
stable b phase can be retained with increasing contents of b stabi-
lizer elements such as Mo, V, Ta and Nb. With the addition of such
elements, many b and near b alloys have been synthesized, such as
Ti–Nb–Ta–Zr [8], Ti–Nb–Si [9], Ti–V–Sn [10].
As far as biocompatibility is concerned, most of the widely used
Ti-based implant alloys contain toxic elements. For example, V has
been classiﬁed in the toxic group and the biocompatibility of Al is
also not good due to tissue response of its capsule [11], while toxic
Ni can be released as metal ions and results in hypersensitivity
[12]. Thus, implant alloys free of toxic elements are needed and
for this reason, TiNb has drawn great interest due to its good bio-
compatibility and excellent corrosion resistance.
Copper mold injection casting can provide high cooling rates
[13,14]. Using this method, chill-zone alloys with ultraﬁne grain
microstructure and bulk metallic glasses can form with suitable
compositions [15–17]. Both types of alloys exhibit good mechani-
cal properties, such as high yield strength, high fatigue resistance,
and high hardness. Until now, the bulk TiNb based alloys are syn-
thesized by slow cooling procedures, i.e. arc-melting of raw mate-
rials followed by high temperature homogeneity. From a scientiﬁc
and technological view point, it is of interest to study the effect of
fast cooling process of copper mold casting on the properties of
TiNb based alloys.
The shape memory effect is observed in TiNb alloys with lower
Nb content (up to /C2410 at.%) and superelasticity in the Ti alloys with
intermediate Nb content ( /C2410–30 at.%). In order to exploit the
possibility of practical application in medical implants, the

2. Experimental procedure
Ti–Nb binary alloys with 8.34 at.%Nb and 25.57 at.%Nb and Ti–Nb–Sn ternary
alloy Ti–25.05 at.%Nb–2.04 at.%Sn were synthesized in the form of large brilliant
button (3–5 g) by arc-melting under Ar atmosphere from high purity Ti (99.9%),
Nb (99.9%) and Sn (99.8%). The ingots were re-melted 6 times to ensure composi-
tional homogeneity. The arc-melted ingots were then injected into copper molds
by suction casting. The as-cast samples were subsequently wrapped in Ti foil,
encapsulated in sealed quartz tube under Ar, and annealed at 1000 /C176C for 5 h. An-
nealed samples were either slowly furnace cooled to room temperature or fast
quenched in ice water. Compression tests of 4 mm diameter and 8 mm length spec-
imens of alloys in three different states (as-cast, furnace cooling and water quench-
ing) were conducted at a strain rate of 2.08 /C210
/C04/s. Extensometers were used to
correct the strain recorded from cross head displacements. Ultrasonic measure-
ments were conducted to obtain the Young’s Moduli of the investigated alloys on
ultra sonic plus echo measurement equipment. The samples are cylinders with
8 mm in diameter and 4–5 mm in height. Microstructure was investigated by con-
ventional X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu K

and X-ray diffraction in transmission with monochromatized high energy beam at
the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) ID15 beamline. A photon energy
of 100 keV, corresponding to an X-ray wavelength of 0.012454 nm was used. The
direction of transmission beam was perpendicular to the axis of cylindrical com-
pression sample. The patterns have been converted from wave-vector Q (nm

3. Results and discussion
The XRD patterns of the ingots and casting samples in different
diameters (4 mm and 8 mm) of all three compositions are pre-
sented in Figs. 1 and 2 . It is shown in Fig. 1 a that the ingot of Ti–
8.34Nb exhibit a-phase reﬂections, while spectra for the two cast
samples possess single a00 Bragg peaks. From this point of view,
suction casting with higher cooling rate generates martensitic a00 ,
while slow cooling facilitates formation of a phase. The conven-
tional XRD patterns of ingot and as-cast samples of Ti–8.34Nb
are further zoomed in between 30 /C176and 45 /C176in Fig. 1 b. It can be
more clearly seen that the two as-cast samples possess same Bragg
peaks, indicated as martensitic a00 phase, which are different from
those of ingot sample. A small contribution to the diffracted inten-
sity between the (0 0 2) and the (1 1 1) peaks (2 h /C2539/C176) is most
likely due to residual b. As shown in Fig. 2a and b, the compositions
with higher Nb content, Ti–25.57Nb and Ti–25.05Nb–2.04Sn, are
constituted of the b-phase as their main structure feature regard-
less of processing history (ingots and castings in both 4 mm and
8 mm diameter). This results from higher concentration of Nb as
b stabilizer element.
The DSC curve of the as-cast sample of Ti–8.34Nb is shown in
Fig. 3. It is shown that there exists a transformation during heating
and following cooling. This transformation corresponds to a00 /b
transition and the characteristic temperatures are as follows: mar-
tensite start temperature (Ms) 710 /C176C, martensite ﬁnish tempera-
ture (Mf) 570 /C176C, austenite start temperature (As) 645 /C176C and
austenite ﬁnish temperature (Af) 830 /C176C. The Ms is slightly higher
than that estimated according to Moffat’s Ti–Nb metastable phase
diagram [18]. There also exists a peak after a00 /b transition during
heating, and it disappears during the subsequent reheating. The
reason to this phenomenon is still under investigation. Enthalpy
of the transformation during heating and cooling is 2362 J/mol
and 1225 J/mol, respectively. These values are between those of
transformation in pure Ti (4200 J/mol, [19]) and in Ti–26Nb binary
alloy (340 J/mol, [20]). This is in agreement with widening of b re-
gion with increasing Nb content in Ti–Nb binary phase diagram [3].
Since Ms of Ti–25.57Nb is around 0 /C176C [20], the main phase in the
as-cast sample should be single b phase. No signal of transforma-
tion upon heating and cooling of single b phase is seen (not shown
here) in DSC curves.
The compressive behaviors of all the samples in three different
states (as-cast, heat treatment followed either by furnace cooling
or water quenching) were investigated.
It is shown in Fig. 4 a that the Ti–8.34Nb samples of all three
states exhibit non-linear behavior during loading–unloading
cycles. Moreover, the residual strain was recovered to some extent
when the sample was heated above the Ms temperature. The shape
memory effect was observed for Ti–8.34Nb samples and it results
from the reorientation of existed martensites. However, due to
the slower cooling rate, the non-linear elastic behavior is much less
obvious in furnace cooled sample than in samples which were sub-
jected to higher cooling rate. This results from precipitation of
equilibrium
a rather than transformation of martensitic a00 in the
furnace cooled sample, and precipitation of equilibrium a leads
to the diminishing of the shape memory effect. This is conﬁrmed
by results of synchrotron experiment, seen in Fig. 4 b. The furnace
cooled sample contains mainly equilibrium a with small quantity

Fig. 5a shows that the non-linear elastic behavior is much more
marked for the Ti–25.57Nb alloy compared to the Ti–8.34Nb alloy.
This superelasticity originates from the transformation of stress-
induced martensite. The Ms of Ti–25.57Nb is around 0 /C176C [20],
and previous XRD patterns show that b phase is the only phase
in the samples at ambient temperature. However, with the aid of
stress during loading, this high temperature b phase can transform
into martensitic a00 phase; while during unloading, the martensitic
a00 phase becomes unstable and mostly transforms back to the b
phase. The existing constituting phases in the samples after
compression were revealed by diffraction in transmission. The
occurrence of retained martensitic a00 phase is visible in Fig. 5 c.
Fig. 5 b shows the cyclic loading–unloading stress–strain curve of
water-quenched sample. It indicates that with increasing strain,
the superelastic behavior becomes incomplete. This is due to the
onset of permanent plastic deformation of TiNb alloy. In this
Ti–25.57Nb alloy, there exist two competitive deformation mecha-
nisms, stress-induced martensitic transformation and permanent
plastic deformation. The deformation with lower critical stress
commences ﬁrst and diminishes the extent of the other deforma-
tion. However, the low critical stress of plastic deformation of TiNb
alloys impedes the occurrence of superelasticity. Therefore any
methods that increase the critical stress for permanent plastic
deformation will enhance the stability of superelasticity. It has
been established that introduction of high densities of dislocations
by cold rolling or high densities of dispersed particles by heat
treatment can indeed improve the stability of superelasticity
[20–22].

4. Conclusions
The microstructure, thermal stability and mechanical properties
of three TiNb(Sn) alloys were investigated. It was found that
Ti–8.34Nb alloys possess martensitic a00 phase in the as-cast and
water quenched state and equilibrium a phase in the furnace
cooled state. DSC results show an a00 /b transition during heating
of the as-cast sample and the characteristic temperatures of this
martensitic transformation were evaluated. Ti–25.57Nb and Ti–
25.05Nb–2.04Sn alloys, however, possess single b phase due to
the higher content of b stabilizer element.
The mechanical properties of Ti–8.34Nb, Ti–25.57Nb and Ti–
25.05Nb–2.04Sn alloys in different cooling state (as-cast, heat
treatment followed by either furnace cooling or water quenching)
were studied by cyclic loading–unloading compression tests. It was
shown that for Ti–8.34Nb alloys, the as-cast and water quenched
samples exhibit shape memory effect. This is due to the reorienta-
tion of existing martensites. For Ti–25.57Nb alloys, stress-induced
martensitic transformation was observed during compression. The
transmission X-ray diffraction results show initial b phase trans-
forms into martensitic a00 phase with deformation. Moreover, with
increasing compressive strain, superelasticity becomes incomplete.
Finally, the ternary Ti–25.05Nb–2.04Sn alloy only shows normal
linear elastic behavior prior to plastic deformation. The addition
of Sn suppresses the transformation of b to a00 , which in turn
increases the stability of the b phase. The Young’s moduli of alloys
after different thermal treatments were determined by ultrasonic
measurements. The results showed that the equilibrium a phase
exhibits the highest Young’s modulus and Sn addition has little
inﬂuence on the Young’s moduli of the TiNb alloys. It was also
found that water quenched samples of all Nb contents possess low-
er Young’s moduli.
