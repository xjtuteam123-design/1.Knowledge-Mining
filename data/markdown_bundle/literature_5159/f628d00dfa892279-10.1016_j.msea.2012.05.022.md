1. Introduction
A large effort has been spent over the past decade or so on the
development of Fe–Al alloys for applications as structural mate-
rials at high temperature and typically in aggressive oxidising or
reducing atmospheres [1–3]. Much of this work has been devoted
to improve the creep behaviour, in view of the relatively poor
creep resistance of binary Fe–Al alloys, and has typically involved
the introduction of large volume fractions of second phases, often
brittle intermetallic phases [4–15]. Very often such alloying addi-
tions have left the material in a state of near-zero ductility,
and often with high brittle–ductile transition temperatures, even
approaching the relevant creep temperatures [7–10,12,13,16–22].
Alternative attempts to improve creep strength by incorporating
ﬁne precipitates have often been only partially successful due to
the small volume fraction of reinforcing phase or its instability
against re-dissolution or coarsening at typical creep temperatures
[4,17,20,21,23–29]. Other attempts have made use of mechanical
alloying methods to give excellent creep strength, but suffer from
the very high cost of processing [30–33]. Yet another approach to
improve strength while maintaining useful ductility has recently
been explored involving the use of high-strain thermo-mechanical
forming processes to break down the cast-in continuous network
of brittle phases and reﬁne the second phase particle size [34–36].

Such recent work [36] has developed a composite-like mixture
of equiaxed Laves phase particles distributed relatively homoge-
neously throughout a Fe–Al matrix, and it is the evaluation of the
mechanical behaviour of such a material that is presented here. It
may be noted that hot working techniques have been used many
times previously (e.g. Refs. [37–40]) to reﬁne the coarse as-cast
microstructure of iron aluminides and thereby improve strength
and ductility, but such studies have not speciﬁcally examined
microstructure and high temperature strength of alloys with com-
position modiﬁed in order to achieve creep resistance at very high
temperatures.
2.
 Experimental
The material used and processing methods have been dis-
cussed elsewhere [36], and only a brief summary is given here. The
alloy composition was a nominal Fe–15%Al–5%Nb (atomic percent)
which was induction melted and then cast in a Cu mould to produce
a 25 mm thick ingot. Bars were hot forged at 1150 ◦C to a total true
strain of 6. At the end of forging, the bars were reheated to 1150 ◦C
for 10 min to remove any remaining dislocations, and slowly cooled
in a sand bath. They were subsequently re-annealed at 1000 ◦C for
1 h, furnace cooled for 1 h each at 900 ◦C and 800 ◦C, then furnace
cooled for 24 h each at 700 ◦C, 600 ◦C, 500 ◦C, and 400 ◦C, followed
by an air cool. The objective of such slow cooling was to precipitate
out essentially all the Nb solute in the form of coarse Laves phase
particles and leave a structurally stable microstructure where no
further precipitation is expected during mechanical testing in the

moderately coarse precipitates at temperatures above 400–500◦C

Mechanical testing was carried out by tensile testing at room
temperature, compression testing at temperatures from room tem-
perature to 700 ◦C, and by creep testing in tension at 700 ◦C. A few
hardness tests were also carried out, at room temperature, by Vick-
ers microhardness indentation using a load of 200 g. Samples were
machined from the forged bars and polished using 1000 grade SiC
abrasive. Samples for tensile testing (and creep) had a cylindri-
cal gauge of 3 mm diameter and 20 mm length, while compression
samples were 3–4 mm diameter and 6–7 mm in length. The strain
rate for tensile testing and much of the compression testing was
nominally 3 × 10−4 s–1. Additional compression tests were carried
out using different strain rates, typically 10 and 100 times slower
than given above, with two types of tests carried out: in some
the strain rate was jumped down by the factor of 10 after a given
amount of plastic strain, the sample allowed to stabilise at the new
strain rate and then the strain rate jumped a factor of 10 either up or
down; in the second type of test the selected slower strain rate was
maintained throughout the entire test. In tests conducted at high
temperature, the samples were inserted into the preheated fur-
nace, and after imposing the required amount of strain were rapidly
removed, in order to retain the deformation substructure corre-
sponding to the given test conditions, by opening the split furnace.
Creep testing was carried out, also in tension, using a creep machine
equipped with an Andrade lever arm to ensure a constant stress
during creep. Samples were installed in the cold furnace, which was
heated to the test temperature in about 1/2 h and the sample left to
soak for a further 1/2 h before loading. Creep strain and strain rate
were monitored continuously by a data acquisition system, with
some tests taken to failure while others were interrupted. For such
tests the split furnace was also removed and the sample quickly
extracted and cooled to retain the deformation microstructure.
Microstructures of as-forged and as-deformed samples were
examined, generally on transverse sections of the test samples, by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and by transmission electron
microscopy (TEM). Samples were electropolished using a 20% per-
chloric acid in methanol mixture at −30 ◦C and about 30 V. SEM
images were generally obtained in Back-Scattered Electron con-
trast (BSE) in order to show chemical and orientation differences
between matrix and particle phases as well as between grains and
subgrains. SEM was carried out using a JEOL 6500F and a Hitachi
S-4800 instrument, and TEM using a JEOL 2010 instrument, with
chemical analysis by Energy Dispersion Spectroscopy carried out
in the SEM using a windowless detector.
3. Results
3.1. Initial microstructures
The microstructures of cast and of forged materials have been
shown before [36], but are shown again in Fig. 1, emphasizing
the important aspects of this material. As-cast material has a
coarse grain size, about 250 /H9262m, with dendritic segregation lead-
ing to Fe(Al) dendrites, a layer of Laves phase on the dendrites
and a Laves-Fe(Al) eutectic mixture within the inter-dendritic
regions, see Fig. 1a. Following forging, Fig. 1b, the microstructure is
relatively
 homogeneous with a nearly uniform distribution of Laves
phase particles of average size 1 /H9262m distributed throughout a
matrix of grain size about 10 /H9262m. There is 19% volume fraction of
Laves phase present. About two-thirds of the material contains a
denser dispersion of particles and about one third contains a lower
fraction of Laves phase and a wider spacing of the particles. The
slow cooling after forging has led to some ﬁne precipitates within
the Fe(Al) grains and a discontinuous ﬁlm of Laves phase along
the grain boundaries, see Fig. 1c. The majority of the precipitates
observed in as-forged material, as well as the thin grain bound-
ary ﬁlm, were identiﬁed by TEM studies as Laves phase of C14
crystal structure. Identiﬁcation of the ﬁnest precipitates was dif-
ﬁcult, and some of these may perhaps be the metastable Heusler
phase observed in other studies [14,22,41–43]. Neither the ﬁne
precipitates nor the discontinuous ﬁlm were evident after giving
the preliminary 1000–400◦C soaking treatment. It is important to
note, thus, that the annealing and slow cooling procedure followed
after forging was successful in removing any ﬁne precipitates which
would modify the strength of material when tested at low or high
temperatures.
Analysis of phase compositions by EDS showed that the matrix
contained about 16% Al (atomic % is quoted throughout) and only
very small amounts of Nb (about 1%), while the Laves phase con-
tained about 12% Al and 26% Nb. Such compositions are consistent
with analyses of the Fe–Al–Nb system [44] for a C14 Fe2Nb Laves
phase and disordered Fe(Al) phase mixture. The C14 crystal struc-
ture of the Laves phase was conﬁrmed by electron diffraction in the
TEM, as well as the disordered nature of the Fe(Al) matrix. It may be
noted that the choice of a relatively low Al content for the present
material was made in an attempt to improve ductility, although
this may lead to some loss of oxidation resistance [2]. The absence
of order in the iron–aluminium matrix is not expected to signiﬁ-
cantly affect mechanical behaviour, especially since many typical
iron aluminides are essentially disordered (or only weakly ordered)
at the typically high creep temperatures [45].
3.2. Mechanical behaviour of as-cast and forged materials
The as-cast material was evaluated at room temperature by
hardness, compression and tensile testing. The hardness was
2.64 GPa, compression testing showed a yield stress of 630 MPa
and tensile testing showed brittle behaviour with fracture near
180 MPa. In marked contrast, the forged material showed good ten-
sile ductility at room temperature, see Fig. 2. The yield stress was
somewhat lower (545 MPa) than measured on as-cast material, but
the forged material showed good work hardening up to a tensile
stress of 782 MPa with tensile elongation of over 6%. This ductil-
ity is in very good agreement with expectations based on the yield
stress and grain size of the material [18–20].
Flow stresses measured up to high temperatures in compres-
sion tests are shown in Fig. 3, where data from a range of Fe–Al
base alloys with similar compositions are shown for comparison.
The Fe–15Al–5Nb alloy is seen to soften gradually with increase
in temperature, especially at temperatures above 500 ◦C. The gen-
eral range of stresses, as well as the gradual loss of strength above
500 ◦C, is very similar to the observations here on binary alloys of
only slightly higher Al contents as well as data on Fe–Al–Ta alloys of
similar Al content with small Ta additions [9]. The two binary alloys
(containing 18 and 20% Al) were cast under similar conditions to the
present Fe–15Al–5Nb alloy, annealed for 1 h at 1000 ◦C, and ﬁnished
by furnace cooling (unpublished work). The Fe–Al–Ta alloys were
also given a similar prior anneal at 1000 ◦C, as for the present work,
in order to stabilise the microstructure [9]. For all these materials
the ﬂow stress falls rapidly above 500 ◦C to reach relatively low
values by 700 ◦C. The low temperature strength of binary alloys
is known to depend on the Al content [46], consistent with the

interface has relatively low energy, and the Laves phase is close
to wetting the grain boundary. It is emphasized that deformation

The deformation substructure produced by room temperature
tensile testing is shown in more detail in Fig. 7. The montage of TEM
photographs of Fig. 7a shows a matrix cell/subgrain microstructure
of size about 2 /H9262m with a high density of dislocations inside these
cells/subgrains. Many of the cells/subgrains have Laves particles
at their corners, see Fig. 7a. The Laves phase particles themselves
(conﬁrmed to have the C14 structure by Selected Area Diffraction
(SAD) analysis) are about 1 /H9262m in size and show bend contours due
to slight buckling in the thin foil sample. They do not, however,
contain dislocations indicating that they have not deformed during
straining of the sample. Detailed examination of the dislocations
in Fe(Al) matrix conﬁrms that several slip systems have operated,
leading to single dislocations of Burgers vector 1/2 ⟨1 1 1⟩. Fine pin-
ning points are seen on the dislocations, occasionally leading to
short dipoles or loops trailing behind, see Fig. 7b, but it is not clear
whether these are due to intersection jogs or to ﬁne (nanosized)
particles which are themselves not visible.
Deformation at 600 ◦C, Fig. 8, leads to the recovery of matrix
dislocations into a subgrain structure, consistent with the SEM
observations reported in Fig. 6b. Dislocations within the Fe(Al)
matrix intersect to form angular segments that knit together to
form sub-boundaries, Fig. 8b. The subgrain size seen here is of the
order of 0.5 /H9262m in size, Fig. 8a. This ﬁgure also shows the cusped
Laves particle-matrix grain boundary morphology, the absence
of any strain and dislocations inside the Laves particles, and the
absence of any precipitation within the Fe(Al) matrix.

4.3. Analysis of creep strengthening in forged Fe–15Al–5Nb
composite
Analysis of the creep behaviour at 700 ◦C (Fig. 5) shows a power
law dependence of creep rate on applied stress with an exponent
of 5.3. Noting that this exponent is slightly above the expected
value (4–5) [26,27], it can perhaps be argued that there is a small
threshold stress (about 15 MPa) created by the coarse Laves phase
particles. However, in view of the speciﬁc microstructure of the
forged material, namely grains of size about 10 /H9262m and Laves
phase particles of size about 1 /H9262m (Figs. 1, 6c–d and 12), with the
Laves phase particles arranged preferentially at the matrix grain
triple points, it seems best to consider the material as a com-
posite with the Laves phase particles generating a reﬁned matrix
microstructure and grain/subgrain boundaries interconnecting the
particles. This structure changes little over the range of deforma-
tion conditions studied, due partly to the coarse, stabilized particle
distribution produced by the preliminary heat treatment.
Treating the material as a composite, the concepts of load shar-
ing and of partial unloading of the elastically soft matrix by the
elastically hard, undeforming Laves phase particles may be used.

5.
 Summary
Forging to high strains at high temperatures has successfully
converted a solidiﬁcation segregation network of Laves phase into
a relatively uniform dispersion of coarse Laves particles arranged
at the triple points of a ﬁne-grained Fe(Al) matrix.
Heat treatments involving slow cooling from a temperature of
1000–400◦C has stabilised the coarse particle/ﬁne matrix grain
structure against further precipitation or coarsening at interme-
diate test temperatures (700 ◦C).
The dispersion strengthened Fe(Al) alloy shows good room tem-
perature strength with tensile ductility in excess of 6%.
The forged material shows good creep resistance at a tempera-
ture of 700 ◦C, comparable with many other cast (and brittle) iron
aluminides containing a signiﬁcant volume fraction of second phase
particles and with Laves phase strengthened ferritic steels.
The good creep strength is explained by concepts of load sharing
and internal barrier stresses generated by the hard Laves phase
particles in the soft matrix.
