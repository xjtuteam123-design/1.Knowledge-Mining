Abstract 
The dependence of the absorbed energy of ultrafine-grained (UFG) Ti-6Al-4V alloy on the test 
temperature is studied . Tensile test s and Charpy test s were carried out at room temperature, 
cryogenic temperature, and temperatures elevated up to 500 С. A UFG state was produced by 
equal-channel angular pressing. For the sake of comparison , coarse-grained (CG) alloy was 
subjected to heat treatment to produce a duplex structure. The UFG alloy demonstrates enhanced 
strength and reduced du ctility-related properties at room temperature . It is shown that at 
operating temperatures of 200 and 300 С, the best balance between high strength and absorbed 
energy is achieved in the UFG alloy . The UFG alloy demonstrates higher strength compared to 
the CG alloy at the liquid nitrogen temperature, but their absorbed energy is practically the same. 
The failure mechanisms of CG and UFG alloy and promising engineering applications of UFG 
alloy are discussed. 
 
Key words : Titanium alloy ; Severe plastic deformation; Ultrafine-grained structure; Tensile 
properties, Absorbed energy; Fracture characteristics. 
 
 
1 Introduction 
Structural components are subjected to different loads during exploitation , such as alternating 
cyclic loads, one -time loads, impact loads, and others, which can lead to early initiation of 
cracking and subsequent failure. Therefore, in order to ensure the structural strength of an item,

## Page 3

one should combine not only the required st rength and ductility of material but also high 
absorbed energy, which controls the resistance to catastrophic (brittle) failure. Material with high 
ductility does not always have high absorbed energy and fracture toughness. For example, the 
fracture toughness К1с of ductile Al alloys has low values (~35 MPa m-2) compared to less 
ductile Ti alloys (~ 60 MPam-2, on average, for Ti-6Al-4V), and steels have the highest fracture 
toughness (~115 MPam-2) [1,2]. 
As is known, a decrease in the exploitation temperature usually results in a reduction of 
the absorbed energy [3]. Thus, there is a temperature range of ductile–brittle transition for 
structural steels within which, at a certain temperature (often at low and below -zero 
temperatures), the absorbed energy drops dramatically [3,4]. It should be noted that such a range 
is less defined for Ti alloys than for steels . This is apparently one of the reasons for the 
application of widely used Ti-6Al-4V alloy in a wide temperature range from –196 to 450 С [1]. 
It is known that the mechanical properties of two-phase Ti alloys, including absorbed energy, are 
strongly depend ent on the microstructure type that forms as a result of heat and thermo -
mechanical treatments [1,2]. For example, the fracture toughness of coarse -grained Ti alloys 
with a plate-like structure is higher than that of alloys with globular or bimodal structures. In [5], 
it is shown for the example of Ti17 that the crack path is more crooked in the plate-like structure 
due to a drastic change of the crack direction on the flat boun daries of plates, which requires 
more energy. In this case, the lower ductility of plate -like structures is balanced out by a higher 
fracture toughness compared to globular and bimodal structures [5]. On the other hand, in [4] the 
absorbed energy of low-carbon dual -phase steels is improved as a result of grain refinement 
down to 1 µm, which is connected with augmentation of the steel’s ability to undergo strain 
hardening. 
Thus, the problem of achieving a balance between high strength and good absorbed 
energy in metals and alloys is still urgent in modern materials science . One of the promising 
approaches to solving the problem is the formation of ultrafine -grained (UFG) structures in

## Page 4

metals and alloys by severe plastic deformation (SPD) techniques [6–8]. For instance , i t is 
established that the formation of a UFG structure in Ti and Ti -6Al-4V alloy lead s to high 
strength in the whole range of operating temperatures , including cryogenic ones, improved 
fatigue properties , and even a superplastic forming capability [ 9–15]. At the same time , the 
recent studies of the absorbed energy of different nanostructured metals and alloys processed by 
SPD techniques demonstrate contradictory results. Thus, in low- and medium-carbon steels with 
grain size reduction , one can observe a decrease in the ductile –brittle transition range towards 
lower temperatures [16]. It is assumed that such a shift in UFG steels occurs due to a 
considerable increase of the stress of brittle failure compared to the yield stress . In [17], an 
unusually high absorbed energy is observed in nanostructured Ti compared to its coarse-grained 
(CG) counterpart (0.56 and 0.32 MJ/m2, respectively) at negative temperatures. The authors [17] 
explain this phenomenon by the fact that nanostructured Ti with higher strength and enhanced 
ductility should have higher energy of failure propagation and as a consequence higher impact 
strength. Alongside this, in most studied nanostructured metals, the impact strength Kcv at room 
temperature is usually lower than that in CG counterparts [18–20]. Such regularity occurs for Ti-
6Al-4V processed by different SPD techniques [12, 21]. The problem of enhancing brittle failure 
resistance in UFG Ti-6Al-4V alloy in a wide range of temperatures including cryogenic ones is 
of special interest, as this would create conditions for its use under extreme conditions. 
In this work, equal channel angular pressing (ECAP) is used to form the UFG structure in 
the studied Ti-6Al-4V alloy, which enables the production of bulk billets to study the mechanical 
properties in standard samples. The aim of this investigation was to study the absorbed energy in 
a wide range of operating temperatures of the UFG Ti-6Al-4V alloy from –196 to 500 C and to 
identify the conditions for promising applications of it. 
2 Experimental materials and procedures 
The experiments were conducted with Ti-6Al-4V alloy produced in the form of hot -rolled rods 
with diameters of 20 mm and the following chemical composition (in wt%): 6.2% Al, 4.3% V,

## Page 5

0.02% Zr, 0.039% Si, 0.16% Fe, 0.06% C, 0.168% O, 0.015% N, 0.003% H, and Ti-base. In 
order to produce a duplex structure in the Ti-6Al-4V rod, the rods were subjected to standard 
heat treatment (HT) of quenching at 960 °С ( 20 С lower than -transus) for 1 h and then 
tempering at 675 °С for 4 h. This state is hereafter referred to as “ CG”. The ECAP was 
conducted at a temperature of 650 °C for six passes with a die-set having a channel angle of 120 
and using the route ВC, where the sample was rotated by 90 between passes. The scheme of the 
ECAP technique is shown in figure 1. 
The microstructures of the samples were analysed by scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) and by transmission electron microscop y (TEM). The samples for SEM were 
mechanically polished to abrasive paper P1000 , using LabaPol Struers mashine. Then these 
samples were chemically etched in acid solution HF -HNO3-H2O in the ratio of 1:1:1 within 10-
15 seconds. SEM-images were made using JEOL JSM 6390 microscope at accelerating voltage 
of 20 kV . The samples for TEM foils were cut out using electrical discharge machining, then 
mechanically thinned to a thickness of 100 m, and then electro -polished using the TenuPol -5 
facility with a solution of 5% perchloric acid, 35% butanol, and 60% methanol at a polishing 
temperature within the range from –20 to –35 °C. The microstructures were examined using a 
JEOL JEM 2100 microscope operating with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. 
The experimental X -ray data were obtained with the help of a DRON-4-07 X-ray 
diffractometer with the goniometer focused as per the Bragg-Brentano technique. The Ti-6Al-4V 
samples were radiated with a Cu Кα beam (35 kV, 30 mA) and a flat graphite monochromator 
<0002> on the reflected beam. The wave lengths λКα1 = 1.54060 Å and λКα2 = 1,54439 Å were 
used for calculations. The general view of the diffraction patterns was taken with a scanning step 
of 0.05 and an exposure time of 5 seconds at each point . The p recision exposure o f the 
highlighted X -ray peaks was performed with a step of 0.02 and a time period equal to 10 
seconds. The quantitative evaluation of the dislocation density was carried out using the software 
package PDWin (“Burevestnik”, Saint-Petersburg).

Cylindrical specimens with diameters of 3 mm and gauge lengths of 15 mm were used for 
the tensile tests (Fig. 2a). The tensile tests were performed at an initial strain rate of 1.0 × 10 -3 s-1 
in liquid nitrogen using an Instron testing machine operating at a constant rate of cross -head 
displacement. A relatively uniform elongation of the samples was obtained from the engineering 
stress-strain curves as the value corresponding to the maximum forcing. The tests were carried 
out with a traverse speed of 1 mm/min on the Instron test machine at temperatures of –196, 25, 
300, and 500 C. V-shaped notched specimens were cut out from the longitudinal sections of the 
rods, as shown in Fig. 2b. Samples with cross-section dimensions of 10 × 10 mm (Fig. 2c) were 
cut out from the rods. The samples were tested using a JB-W300 Charpy impact tester in the 
temperature range from –196 to 500 С in accordance with ASTM E23-16, “Standard Test 
Methods for Notched Bar Impact Testing of Metallic Materials ”. At least three samples were 
used per experimental point. The surface f racture features we re examined after testing using a 
JEOL JSM 6390 scanning electron microscope (SEM). 
3 Results 
3.1. Microstructure 
Figure 3 displays the microstructure of the alloy after HT . It is a mixed globular -platelet 
structure consisting of grains of primary αp-phase with an average size of ~8 µm, a volume 
fraction of ~30% , and areas ( α + β) with thin plate -like structures, in which the average plate 
thickness is 1.5 µm. 
Figure 4 displays the UFG structures of Ti -6Al-4V alloy processed by ECAP . SEM-
images of microstructure in transverse and longitudinal section of the billet are shown in Fig. 4a 
and 4b, respectively. The primary αp-phase grains are poorly deformed. The average size of 
ultrafine grains/subgrains in the (α + β ) regions is ~ 350 nm , and their shapes are essentially 
equiaxed in both the longitudinal and transverse directions of the billet (Fig. 4c). 
The UFG structure formed in the alloy via E CAP processing is characterized by high elastic 
distortions of the lattice and an increase of the dislocation density by almost an order (0.8 and

subjected to SPD [8] 
 
3.2. Mechanical properties of Ti-6Al-4V alloy 
Table 1 lists the mechanical properties of the CG and UFG alloy s at different temperatures. 
Figure 5 shows typical tensile curves of the Ti -6Al-4V alloy samples in the CG and UFG states 
at room temperature and –196, 300, and 500 C. 
 The shape of the curves at 20 and –196C characterizes a similar mechanical behaviour of 
the alloy in the CG and UFG states at both temperatures: the ultimate tensile strength increases 
due to grain refinement ( Fig. 5). The formation of the UFG structure in the alloy allows the 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) to increase from 950 to 1180 MPa at room temperature and from 
1580 to 1780 MPa at cryogenic temperature (Table 1). The significant increase in the UTS and 
YS at cryogenic temperature in both alloy states appears to be due to the suppression of 
thermally activated processes of dislocation motion, which require higher stresses for dislocation 
glide [12]. 
 Alongside this, it is observed that the uniform elongation δu in UFG samples decreases, as 
testified by a change in the shape of the curves of the samples compared to the CG alloy (Fig. 5 
and Table 1). Such behaviour often characterizes SPD -processed metals and alloys and is 
associated with a decrease in the strain-hardening capacity of nanostructured materials [20, 22]. 
After the testing at 300 and 500 С in both states, the ultimate tensile strength decreases, 
but the value of YS in the UFG state remains considerably higher than that in the CG state (1055 
and 690 MPa at 300 С and 645 and 570 MPa at 500 С, respectively) (Table 1). The total 
relative elongation, for example, after testing at 500 С is practically the same (30% on average), 
but the uniform elongation of th e UFG alloy is almost twice as low (2.5 and 5.0%, respectively) 
(Table 1). Softening of the CG and UFG alloys at 300 and 500 С is quite predictable, as at these

temperatures the recovery processes take place, accompanied by a reduction in the dislocation 
density, and at 500 С the signs of recrystallization and grain growth are observed [23]. 
3.3 Absorbed energy 
Figure 6 displays the dependence of the Кcv value on the testing temperature for the CG and 
UFG Ti-6Al-4V samples. 
One can see in Fig. 6 that with increases in the testing temperature, the impact strength of 
the alloy in the CG and UFG states grows smoothly. Up to 200 С, the Kcv values for the two 
states of the alloy are close. The largest difference in the absorbed energy is observed at 500 С 
(2.9 and 2.0 MJ/m2 for the CG and UFG alloys, respectively ). It should be noted that at 
cryogenic temperature the UFG alloy , which has a higher yield stress of YS = 1720 MPa 
compared to the CG alloy (1480 MPa), demonstrates the same impact strength (0.2 MJ/m2). 
This is an unusual fact as most alloys exhibit a reduction in the impact strength when the yield 
stress increases [18–20]. 
3.4 Fracture characteristics 
(a) At room temperature 
Figure 7 shows the fracture surfaces of sample s after testing at room temperature . The macro-
images of fractures in the CG and UFG samples are characterized by a flat area and areas of 
shear lip typical of Ti alloys (Fig. 7a,d). The fracture surfaces in the CG and UFG samples have 
the same character , and the area of shear lip in the two samples is practically the same and is 
about 35–40% (Fig. 7b,e). Macro-delamination of the material is observed i n the fracture of the 
CG sample in the zone of fail ure initiation (Fig. 7e), which characterizes its higher impact 
strength compared to the UFG alloy. This is testified by the Kcv values, which are 0.45 and 0.27 
MJ/m2 for the CG and UFG alloys, respectively. The microstructure of fractures in the f lat area 
has a ductile-dimple character, and the sizes of dimples in the CG sample (9 µm on average) are 
almost three times larger than those in the UFG sample (3 µm on average).

images of fractures of the CG and UFG samples also have flat areas and areas of shear lip. In the 
fracture of the UFG sample the area of the she ar lip is about 23%, and in the fracture of the CG

(c) At elevated temperatures. 
Figures 9 and 10 display the macro- and microstructures of fractures of samples with the CG and 
UFG structures after testing at 300 and 500 С. The CG sample does not fail completely during 
testing at 300 С (Fig. 9a). In both CG and UFG samples , the macro-failure in the zone of crack 
initiation is characterized by macro-delamination and ductile dimples ( Fig. 9 b–f) and the area 
occupied by shear lip is about 70% in both cases. Thus, during testing at 300 С, the CG and 
UFG alloys have an identical macrostructure of fractures and very close values of Kcv (2.2 and 
2.0 MJ/m2, respectively). Slight differences are observed only in the morphology of fractures at 
the micro-level, when the average size s are about 8.5 and 10 µm for the UFG and CG alloys, 
respectively. 
The samples of the CG and UFG alloys tested at 500 С are characterized by a more 
ductile failure compared to the samples tested at 300 С. In both cases , the samples d o not fail 
completely and the shear lip size i s about 80% of the fracture surface (Fig. 10a, d). The fracture 
consists of large macro-delamination and dimple relief (Fig. 10b, c). The difference in the Kcv 
values for the CG and UFG alloy s is higher than that at 300 С (2.9 and 2.0 MJ/m2 for the CG

and UFG alloys, respectively) but is not critical. The microstructure of fractures is characterized 
by increases of the dimple size up to 13 and 16 µm for the UFG and CG alloys, respectively. 
The dependence of the testing temperature on the dimple size in the relief of the fracture 
surface of samples is shown in Fig. 11. 
It is seen that as the testing temperature increas es, the dimple size grows in both CG and 
UFG samples. As is known , during failure of two -phase Ti alloys , pores are generated in the 
boundaries and sub -boundaries of α-phase and in the interphase α/β boundaries [24]. The high 
length of boundaries in the UFG alloy leads to the generation of a larger number of pores during 
material failure as compared to the CG material, which evidently explains the considerable 
difference in the dimple size in the CG and UFG alloys, especially at cryogenic temperatures. As 
the test temperature increases, this difference decreases, which could be connected with recovery 
processes and annihilation of disl ocations and sub -boundaries [17]. This is evidenced by the 
reduction in dislocation density and increase of the coherent-scattering region (CSD) size in the 
samples after testing at elevated temperatures (Table 2). After testing at 500 C, the dimple size 
in the CG and UFG fractures is not uniform (Fig. 10c, f), which could be connected with the start 
of recrystallization processes in the microstructure. 
Table 2 lists the results of X -ray studies, which show that after ECAP processing of the 
alloy, the fraction of the β-phase decreases from 13 to 9%, which is conditioned by partial 
dissolution and decomposition of metastable β-phase under the conditions of severe plastic 
deformation. Such regularity was repor ted in our early works [25,26]. In this case , the testing 
temperature does not affect the fraction of β-phase in both states of the alloy. 
4 Discussion 
4.1 Mechanical behaviour during tension 
The results of this work show that the formation of the UFG structure in Ti-6Al-4V leads to 
enhancement of UTS and YS in a wide range of temperatures from –196 to 500 С. This 
confirms the previously published data for this alloy [9–12]. Considering the dependence of the

## Page 11

ultimate tensile strength and yield stress of the CG and UFG alloy s on the testing temperature, 
one should note th at the greatest difference in the yield stress (over 200 MPa) of the UFG alloy 
compared to the CG one i s observed at both room and cryogenic temperatures (Table 1) . The 
ductile characteristics of the UFG alloy decrease, which characterizes most metals and alloys 
subjected to SPD [6,20,22]. The total a nd uniform elongations decrease (Fig. 5), which is 
conditioned by hindered generation and accumulation of dislocations in ultrafine grains and early 
strain localization [22]. The increase in the testing temperature to 300 and 500 C in the CG and 
UFG alloy s results in softening and enhancement of ductility due to recovery processes ; 
however, the values of ultimate tensile strength and yield stress of the UFG alloy remain higher 
than those of the CG one (1110 and 770 MPa at 300С). It should be noted that the operating 
temperatures of Ti-6Al-4V usually do not exceed 350 С [26,27], so the application of this alloy 
in the UFG state is quite suitable for manufacturing of gas turbine engine ( GTE) parts, for 
example compressor blades. 
 
4.2 Absorbed energy at room and elevated temperatures 
In this work , a tendency to ward absorbed energy reduction in the UFG Ti-6Al-4V at room 
temperature is observed, which is typical of various metals and alloys subjected to SPD [20]. 
For example, the Kcv value of the UFG alloy is about 0.26 MJ/m2 compared to 0.43 
MJ/m2 in the CG alloy, which on the whole does not contradict the previously published data in 
[13,21], in which a grain size reduction to 0.5 µm in the Ti-6Al-4V alloy occurs with a Ксv of 
about 0.18 MJ/m2. The difference in the Kcv values of the UFG alloy in this wo rk and in [13] 
can probably be connected with the route and regimes of SPD processing and, therefore, with 
different microstructure parameters . In this study , as a result of heat treatment a nd ECAP , a 
duplex UFG structure i s formed in the alloy, which consists of poorly deformed grains of the 
primary α-phase and two-phase α + β regions (Fig. 4). The morphology of this structure is close 
to that of bimodal struc tures in one -phase alloys [22]. As is known, the formation of UFG

## Page 12

structures of a bimodal type in metals contributes to the enhancement of ductility-related 
properties at room temperature, including the absorbed energy. A similar effect was reported for 
Ti-6Al-4V alloy in [13]. 
Alongside that, the decrease in the absorbed energy of UFG metals compared to their CG 
counterparts could be explained not only by the reduction of ductile characteristics. If the total 
fracture energy is divided in to initiation and crack propagation energy, then it is evident that 
their contributions to the total energy will differ between the CG and UFG materials [29]. In the 
UFG alloy , the crack initiation energy will be higher than that in the CG material due to its 
significant hardening. When the grain size decreases to an ultrafine one, the crack propagation 
takes place mainly along grain/subgrain boundaries, and the rate of its propagation increases 
during both static and cyclic loading [29]. It becomes evident that the main contribution to the 
absorbed energy of the UFG alloy is made by the crack initiation energy as opposed to the CG 
alloy, in which the energy spent for crack propagation will be higher . This conclusion wa s 
confirmed experimentally for VT6 alloy (the Russian counterpart of Ti-6Al-4V alloy) in [30]. 
It is interesting to note that the difference in Kcv at 500 C is the highest (2.9 and 2.0 
MJ/m2 for CG and UFG alloys respectively ) as compared to the absorbed energy at room 
temperature, although the samples ha ve very similar strength and ductility values (Table 1). 
During testing at 300 С, when a higher strength of the UFG alloy is retained, the difference in 
the Kcv values for the CG and UFG alloys is low (Fig. 6). Probably, this could be explained first 
of all by the morphology of structural components of the alloy, which could impact the crack 
propagation path and therefore the value of the energy to failure . In the duplex structure of the 
CG alloy, more energy is required for pore generation on the flat boundary of an α-phase plate as 
compared with a crooked boundary of equiaxed grains in the UFG alloy [5]. Therefore, under 
almost equal crack initiation work, the work spent on fracture propagation in the CG alloy will 
be higher. 
4.3 Absorbed energy at cryogenic temperature

## Page 13

Another situation is observed in the mechanical behaviour of the UFG alloy at –196 С. The 
UTS of the UFG alloy is considerably higher than that in the CG alloy (1780 and 1540 MPa, 
respectively). The relative elongation in the UFG material is lower at room temperature than that 
in the CG alloy (8 and 11%, respectively). Therefore, one may expect the absorbed energy of the 
UFG alloy to be lower than that in the CG material. However, the testing results in this work 
show an irregular result, when the Кcv value of the CG and UFG alloys at cryogenic temperature 
is at almost the same level (about 0.2 MJ/m2) ( Fig. 6). The absorbed energy reduction in the 
alloy with CG and UFG structures at –196 С compared to room temperature is conditioned by 
the reduction in the fracture energy due to the hindered motion of dislocations at cryogenic 
temperatures. One should note that in the CG alloy, the Kcv is approximately halved (from 0.45 
to 0.2 MJ/m2), and in the UFG alloy it decreases less significantly from 0.27 to 0.20 MJ/m2. Such 
behaviour of the alloy at liquid nitrogen temperature c ould be explained by considering its 
mechanical behaviour, when in the CG sample the uniform elongation value decreases from 2.8 
to 2.5% compared to room temperature and that in the UFG alloy grows from 1.1 to 1.5% (Table 
1). This could indicate that s train hardening of the UFG alloy increases at liquid nitrogen 
temperature as compared to room temperature, which contributes favourably to the absorbed 
energy. The d ecrease in the uniform elongation at high strength of the CG alloy considerably 
decreases the fracture energy at cryogenic temperatures. 
An unusual effect is observed in nanostructured Ti in [17] where, at –196 С, nano-Ti has 
an absorbed energy 1.5 times higher than that of CG Ti. The authors connect this phenomenon 
with the fact that nanoTi at cryogenic temperature has both high strength and high ductility. Both 
high strength and high ductility require a lot of energy to join pores during material “failure” , 
and the number of dimples in the fracture of nanoTi produced at –196 С is higher than that in 
the fracture at roo m temperature [17]. Thus, t he higher strength, higher ductility , and larger 
amount of dimple ligaments are primary factors that contribute to the enhanced absorbed energy 
of nanostructured Ti with decreasing temperature. It should be noted that a similar situation i s

## Page 14

observed in this work, where the number of dimples in the fracture relief of the UFG alloy at –
196 С is higher than that in the fracture after testing at room temperature (Figs. 7f and 8f). 
However, an effect of Kcv enhancement at –196 С similar to that in nanostructured Ti is not 
observed in the studied alloy . Apparently, the mechanisms of this phenomenon require the 
application of more careful study to different metal materials. 
Summing up the results of the investigation performed, it should be noted that at an 
operating temperature of this alloy not higher than 3 00 С, a good balance between strength, 
ductility, and absorbed energy is achieved, which is promising for the manufacture of parts such 
as GTE blades. The unique correlation between strength and absorbed energy of UFG Ti-6Al-4V 
alloy at cryogenic temperature creates the conditions for its engineering applications in extreme 
conditions of exploitation. 
5 Conclusions 
The results of mechanical behaviour studies of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy in the CG and UFG states in 
the temperature range from 196 to 500 С show the following: 
1. The formation of UFG structure in Ti-6Al-4V alloy by ECAP results in enhanced v alues 
of the ultimate tensile strength in the studied range of temperatures . The UFG alloy 
demonstrates the highest difference in the strength from the CG alloy (over 200 MPa) at 
room and cryogenic temperatures . When the test temperature increases up to 500 С, the 
difference in strength from the CG alloy is less than 100 MPa. Such softening of the UFG 
alloy is conditioned by more active recovery processes in the material subjected to SPD. 
2. The absorbed energy of the UFG alloy at room temperature is lower than that in the CG 
alloy (0.27 and 0.43 MJ/m2), which c ould be conditioned by the reduction in the 
ductility-related properties (relative and uniform elongation of samples ) and decrease of 
the crack propagation energy. 
3. The absorbed energy Kcv grows when the testing temperature increases to 500 С in the 
CG and UFG states . The difference in the absorbed energy of the CG and UFG alloy s is

## Page 15

small at 200 and 300 С, the Kcv values at 300 С were 2.2 and 1.9 MJ/m2, respectively, 
and the UFG alloy has higher strength (770 and 1110 MPa). The greatest difference in 
the Kcv is obtained at 500 С (2.0 and 2.9 MJ/m2 for the UFG and CG alloys, 
correspondingly). 
4. The absorbed energy of the UFG and CG alloys at 196 С is at approximately the same 
level (0.2 MJ/m2 ) when the strength of the UFG alloy is considerably higher (UTS 1780 
and 1580 MPa, respectively) at values of relative elongation of 8 and 11%.
