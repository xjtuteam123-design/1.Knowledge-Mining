Abstract
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) stand as promising candidates for the development of a new generation of high-
temperature materials due to their exceptional mechanical properties. However, the inherent low ductility and high density of 
RHEAs have hindered their widespread adoption in industrial applications. In this study, an Nb38Ti35Al15V6Cr4(TaHfMoW)2 
RHEA with a BCC/B2 dual-phase structure is successfully developed. The RHEA exhibits excellent specific yield strength 
of 162.4 MPa·g−1·cm3 and 84.3 MPa·g−1·cm3 at room temperature (RT) and 800 °C, respectively. It was found that the pin-
ning effect of the B2 phase induces a gradual transition in dislocation slip mode from planar slip to cross slip, homogenizing 
the plastic flow and resulting in excellent compression plasticity (ε > 50%). Additionally, the B2 phase endows the alloy 
with excellent yield strength at high temperatures by precipitation strengthening. Moreover, the dominated a/2 < 111 > type 
dislocation contributes to the alloy's superior plasticity at high temperatures.
Keywords Refractory high entropy alloy · B2 phase · Microstructure · Mechanical property · Deformation mechanism
1 Introduction
The rapid development of the aerospace industry has led to 
an increasing demand for high-temperature materials, which 
can no longer be met by traditional nickel-based alloys [1 ]. 
The emergence of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) has enabled 
the design of new high-temperature structural materials. 
HEAs differ from conventional alloy design principles, as 
they comprise five or more principal elements [2, 3]. HEAs 
are noted for their unique composition, microstructure, and 
mechanical properties, capturing considerable interest in 
the materials science community [4 –6]. Refractory high-
entropy alloys (RHEAs) represent a specialized category of 
HEAs, composed of refractory metals with melting points 
exceeding 1600 °C, such as Nb, Mo, Ta, W, Zr, Ti, V, and 
Cr [7–9]. This compositional characteristic endows RHEAs 
with heightened melting points and exceptional high-tem-
perature strength, rendering them suitable for applications 
demanding extreme thermal resistance.
The initial RHEAs, including NbMoTaW and VNbMo-
TaW, were developed by Senkov et al. [10]. These alloys 
exhibit exceptional high-temperature strength, maintain-
ing a yield strength (YS) exceeding 400 MPa at 1600 °C, 
markedly surpassing traditional nickel-based superalloys 
like Inconel 718 and Haynes 230 [11]. However, the room 
temperature (RT) compression elongation of NbMoTaW 
and VNbMoTaW remains relatively low, which is 1.5% 
and 0.5%, respectively. In addition, the alloys have high

low density and a tensile elongation of more than 10% at 
RT. Consequently, the RHEA with BCC/B2 dual-phase 
structure is a promising solution to the challenges of high 
density and RT brittleness simultaneously. However, the 
high-temperature softening resistance of the BCC/B2 
dual-phase structure still needs to be further improvement.
The objective of this study is to develop a BCC/B2 
dual-phase RHEA with low density, acceptable plastic-
ity, and superior high-temperature strength. Refractory 
elements Nb and Ti were selected for their low density 
and exceptional intrinsic ductility [19– 21]. The addition 
of the low-density Al element can introduce the ordered 
B2 phase to the BCC matrix [22, 23]. Cr and V are incor -
porated to enhance solid solution strengthening, while 
trace elements such as Ta, Mo, W, and Hf are introduced 
to further augment solid solution strengthening effect 
and elevate the melting point of the alloy. Ultimately, a 
 Nb38Ti35Al15V6Cr4(TaHfMoW)2 (at.%) RHEA is success-
fully developed, which exhibits excellent specific yield 
strength (SYS) and plasticity. This study provides a com-
prehensive analysis of the microstructural and mechani-
cal properties of this alloy, delving into the alterations 
in microstructure and dislocation characteristics after 
deformation.
2 Materials and Methods
The Nb38Ti35Al15V6Cr4(TaHfMoW)2 RHEA was synthesized 
using a vacuum melting furnace under an argon atmosphere, 
with the purity of all metal elements greater than 99.9 wt%. 
Given the notable differences in the melting points of Nb, Hf, 
Mo, Ta, W, Ti, Al, V, and Cr, the intermediate alloys Nb-Hf-
Mo-Ta-W and Ti-Al-V-Cr were initially prepared separately. 
Subsequently, these intermediate alloys were amalgamated to 
form the final button ingots. The resultant ingots were approxi-
mately 40 mm in diameter and 10 mm in thickness. The actual 
alloy composition is given in Table 1. The measured density 
of the alloy was determined by Archimedes principle to be 
6.3 g·cm−3.
Alloy specimens measuring Φ3 mm × 6  mm and Φ6 
mm × 9 mm were produced from as-cast ingots. Compression 
tests at RT were carried out on cylindrical specimens of Φ3 
mm × 6 mm on an Instron 5582 testing machine at a strain rate 
of 1 × 10–3  s−1. Each test was repeated three times to ensure 
the accuracy of the experimental results. High temperature 
compression tests were performed using a GLEEBLE 3800 
thermomechanical simulation system on specimens of Φ6 
mm × 9 mm. These tests were performed at temperatures of 
800 °C, 900 °C, 1000 °C, 1100 °C, and 1200 °C at a strain 
rate of 1 × 10–3  s−1 and each temperature condition was also 
repeated three times. All specimens were heated to the test 
temperature at a rate of 10 °C/s and held isothermally for 300 s 
to ensure uniform temperature distribution. Each high tem-
perature compression test was performed until a 50% reduction 
in height was achieved.
Phase composition was determined by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD; Smart Lab 9 kW) using Cu- Kα radiation with a 2θ 
range of 20°–100° at a scanning rate of 10°/min. The micro-
structural evolution before and after deformation was charac-
terized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; XL30FEG, 
Holland) equipped with electron backscatter diffraction 
(EBSD) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Samples 
for SEM and EBSD analyses were first mechanically polished 
down to 2000-grit SiC papers and then electrochemically pol-
ished using a mixed solution of 6 vol.% perchloric acid, 35 
vol.% n-butanol, and 59 vol.% methanol for 60 s at 25 V. The 
average grain sizes and the geometrically necessary disloca-
tion density (ρGND) were calculated using Aztec Crystal soft-
ware. The microstructure and deformation mechanisms were 
further analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; 
Talos F200X). The samples were first ground mechanically

3 Results
3.1 Microstructure of the as‑Cast REHA
XRD analysis of the as-cast Nb38Ti35Al15V6Cr4(TaHfMoW)2 
RHEA is shown in Fig. 1(a), revealing a single BCC struc-
ture with a lattice parameter of 3.2 Å. Figure  1(b and c) 
presents the microstructures of the as-cast RHEA at low 
and high magnification, respectively, revealing typi-
cal dendritic morphologies characteristic. No secondary 
phases are detected. Figure  1(d) shows the back scattered 
electron (BSE) image and EDS maps, where the brighter 
areas are identified as dendritic regions and the darker areas 
are identified as inter-dendritic regions. The high melting 
point elements such as Nb, Mo, Ta, and W are predomi-
nantly localized in the dendritic regions, while lower melt-
ing point elements such as Ti, Al, V, and Cr are enriched 
in the inter-dendritic regions, with Hf being relatively uni-
formly distributed. The uneven distribution of these ele-
ments primarily relates to their differing melting points. 
During solidification, high melting point elements solidify 
first, while lower melting point elements are expelled to the 
inter-dendritic regions [24].
EBSD and TEM techniques are used to further charac-
terize the microstructures of the as-cast RHEA. Figure  2(a) 
shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) of the as-cast RHEA, 
revealing a random grain orientation without significant tex-
ture. The inset in Fig. 2(a) shows the grain size distribution 
of the alloy, and the area fraction (AF) as a function of grain 
size shows an approximately normal distribution pattern, 
with an average grain size of 329 μm ± 48 μm for the as-cast 
RHEA. Figure  2(b) displays the phase map, indicating the 
BCC structure within the as-cast RHEA, consistent with the 
findings of XRD and SEM results. Additionally, as shown in 
Fig. 2(c and d), the TEM dark-field (DF) image and selected-
area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern confirm the pres-
ence of the nanoscale B2 phase. Notably, the ellipsoidal B2 
nanoparticles are clearly observed within the BCC matrix.

Figure 4 displays the microstructure of the as-cast RHEA 
after deformation at RT. The SEM image in Fig. 4(a) shows 
the microstructure of the as-cast RHEA sample after 20%

GBs serve as significant barriers to dislocation movement. 
In Fig. 4(b), a magnified view of Fig. 4(a) shows the network 
of slip bands (indicated by the green and blue lines) inter -
woven inside the grains, as well as a few instances of cross 
slip indicated by red dashed lines. Figure 4(c) shows the IPF 
map of the as-cast RHEA after 20% compression, showing 
slip bands in directions similar to those observed in the SEM 
image. Figure  4(d) shows the Kernel average misorienta-
tion (KAM) map of the same sample, revealing a higher 
stress concentration along the slip bands compared to other 
regions, indicating significant local strain in these areas.
A detailed microstructural analysis of the as-cast RHEA 
samples compressed by 50% compression is conducted using 
two-beam bright field (BF) TEM. Figure 5 shows the micro-
structural images of the as-cast RHEA samples after 50% 
compression under different g vectors. Significant disloca-

an uneven stress distribution within the alloy. In particular, 
Fig. 5(b) reveals the presence of high-density dislocation 
walls (HDDWs) consisting of densely packed dislocations, 
which may play a crucial role in the strengthening mecha-

4 Discussion
4.1 Relationship Between RT Mechanical Properties 
and Microstructure
Usually, high strength is associated with low ductility for 
metallic materials [43– 45]. Notably, the newly developed 
RHEA in this study not only has high strength but also 
retains more than 50% of its compressive plasticity. This 
remarkable property closely relates to the microstructure 
of the alloy. Detailed analysis of Figs.  4 and 5 reveals 
that the plastic deformation of the alloy predominantly 
occurs through dislocation slip, with no evidence of twin -
ning during the deformation process. This behavior can 
primarily be ascribed to the high stacking fault energy 
(SFE) of the BCC matrix, which typically promotes dislo-
cation slip and inhibits twinning formation [46]. The stag-
gered network of dislocation slip bands visible in Fig.  4 
suggests that the alloy predominantly undergoes planar 
slip in the initial stages of deformation. In addition, the 
formation of multidirectional slip bands can more effec-
tively dissipate localized stresses and reduce damage 
caused by stress concentrations [47]. This characteristic 
is likely a pivotal factor contributing to the exceptional 
compressive plasticity of the alloy. Furthermore, Fig.  4(b) 
demonstrates the phenomenon of dislocation cross-slip. 
The initiation of cross-slip allows dislocations to trav -
erse multiple slip systems, thereby providing an addi -
tional degree of freedom for dislocation motion, which is 
crucial for averting premature fracture. Typically, BCC-
based alloys are characterized by planar slip dominance

This observation aligns with the studies of Lei et al. [51],

evidence of the interaction between the B2 nanoparticles 
and dislocations was observed in Ti1.6ZrNbAl x RHEAs

in inducing dislocation cross-slip, thereby improving the 
plasticity of the alloy.
In Fig.  5, a considerable number of dislocation pile-
ups and HDDWs are observed. On the one hand, the 
stress concentration around the dislocation pile-ups and 
HDDWs can trigger the formation of new dislocations, 
subsequently increasing the dislocation density within the 
matrix and thereby enhancing the alloy’s strength [ 52]. 
On the other hand, the dense arrangement of dislocations 
within the HDDWs increases dislocation interaction, and 
this interlocking effect of dislocations hinders the move-
ment of dislocations in the lattice [53]. As a result, the 
presence of this dislocation structure during alloy defor -
mation significantly boosts material deformation resist -
ance, thereby greatly enhancing the alloy’s strength.
4.2 Mechanism of High Temperatures Deformation 
of the as‑Cast RHEA
The as-cast RHEA exhibits an excellent combination of 
density, strength, and plasticity at 800 °C. However, as the 
deformation temperature increases, the strength of the alloy 
significantly decreases, necessitating an investigation into 
the deformation mechanisms at higher temperatures. In this 
study, we systematically analyzed the evolution of disloca-
tions in the as-cast RHEA at 800 °C and 1200 °C. TEM 
observations revealed numerous slightly curved dislocations, 
primarily of mixed type, with only a few short and straight 
pure screw or edge dislocations. Figure 8 shows the disloca-
tion structure of the as-cast RHEA in different g -directions 
after 50% compression at 800 °C. Dislocations indicated 
by red arrows are visible under the g = [110] and g = [101] 
conditions (Fig.  8(a and b)), but are invisible under g = [1] 
condition (Fig.  8(c)). Conversely, dislocations indicated 
by blue arrows are visible under the g = [110] and g = [1] 
conditions (Fig.  8(a and c)), but not under the g = [101] 
condition (Fig.  8(b)). Based on the invisibility criterion 
g·b = 0, the dislocations marked by red arrows have Burgers 
vectors of b = a/2[111], and those marked by blue arrows 
have b = a/2[11−1]. Both possess Burgers vectors parallel 
to < 111 > . In BCC metals, the < 111 > directions are the 
most densely packed, allowing dislocations to slip rela-
tively easily. Dislocations of the a/2 < 111 > type are active 
on {110}, {112}, and even {123} slip planes [54], enhanc-
ing the pathways for plastic deformation. This is believed 
to be the primary reason for the alloy's excellent plasticity 
at 800 °C.

It is noteworthy that pinning of dislocations by the B2 
phase, indicated by the white arrow in Fig.  8, leads to the 
dislocation twist and formation of ring-like structures near 
the pinning points. This interaction between the B2 phase 
and dislocations is crucial for maintaining the alloy’s high 
compressive strength at 800 °C. As the compression temper-
ature increases, there is no significant change in the nature of 
the dislocation structure as shown in Fig.  9. This indicates 
that plastic deformation at 1200 °C continues to be domi-
nated by dislocations with Burgers vectors of b = a/2[111] 
and b = a/2[11−1]. However, unlike at 800 °C, the disloca-
tions at 1200 °C become much more elongated and straight, 
with the pinning points associated with the B2 phase almost 
entirely absent. This change is mainly due to the fact that, 
at higher temperatures, the dislocations acquire sufficient 
energy to overcome the pinning points.
In addition, the dislocation density in the as-cast RHEA 
after deformation at 1200 °C shows an apparent decrease 
compared to deformation at 800 °C. The ρGND, obtained by 
local misorientation (LM) analysis as shown in Fig.  10(a), 
indicates a decrease from 2.4 ± 0.5 × 1014  m−2 at 800 °C to 
0.8 ± 0.2 × 1014  m−2 at 1200 °C. The significant reduction 
in dislocation density is mainly attributed to the effects of 
DRX. During DRX, the formation and growth of recrystal-
lized grains result in the original dislocations being 'swal-
lowed' by new GBs, thereby eliminating much of the initial 
dislocation structure. In addition, as the temperature con-
tinues to rise, the mobility of the dislocations increases, and 
the interactions between dislocations (e.g. entanglement, 
interlacing, etc.) decrease. Consequently, the strength of the 
alloy diminishes when it deforms at temperatures exceeding 
900 °C.

5 Conclusions
In this study, we developed a novel 
 Nb38Ti35Al15V6Cr4(TaHfMoW) 2 RHEA characterized by 
a BCC/B2 dual-phase structure. We conducted a compre-
hensive analysis of its microstructure, mechanical prop-
erties, and deformation behavior in the as-cast state. The 
main results are outlined as follows:
1. The as-cast RHEA consists of a BCC matrix and 
a nanoscale B2 phase, exhibiting typical dendritic 
structures and an excellent SYS and plasticity syn-
ergy. The as-cast RHEA exhibits excellent SYS 
(σ0.2/ρ = 162.4 MPa·g−1·cm3) and compressive plastic-
ity (ε > 50%) at RT, while still retaining appreciable SYS 
(σ0.2/ρ = 84.3 MPa·g−1·cm3) and compressive plasticity 
(ε > 50%) at 800 °C.
2. The high strength of the alloy at RT is predominantly 
attributed to dislocation pile-up and HDDWs. The pin-
ning effect of the B2 phase induces a gradual transi-
tion in the slip mode from planar to cross slip, thereby 
homogenizing the plastic flow. This results in the alloy 
exhibiting excellent plasticity at RT.
3. The excellent high-temperature strength of the alloy at 
800 °C can be attributed to the pinning effect of the 
B2 phase dislocations. As the deformation temperature 
increases, DRX occurs, resulting in the annihilation of 
a significant number of dislocations and consequently 
a notable decline in the alloy’s strength. The primary
