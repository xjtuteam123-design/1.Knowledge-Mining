Abstract: 
A high -strength Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr alloy developed b y a novel extrusion method called 
differential-thermal extrusion (DTE) was detailed characterized, to reveal the effects of the 
DTE on microstructure and mechanical properties. Different from traditional iso -thermal 
extrusion (ITE) with the same billet and mold temperature, the billet temperature for the 
DTE is remarkably higher than the mold temperature. The finer grains and less long 
period stacking ordered (LPSO) structure in the DTE sample result ed from its higher 
cooling speed after deformation. Meanwh ile, the stronger precipitation strengthening 
effect was caused by the higher billet preheat temperature which reserved the 
precipitation ability before extrusion. As a result, the finer grains and stronger precipitation 
strengthening led to the strong ten sile yield strength (380 MPa) and ultimate tensile 
strength (461 MPa) of the aged DTE sample, while those of the aged ITE sample are only 
338 MPa and 420 MPa, respectively. The higher strengths indicate the DTE is a better 
extrusion process for producing high-strength Mg alloys than the traditional ITE.

1. Introduction 
Magnesium (Mg) alloys are very promising lightweighting structural materials in 
automobile and aircraft industries [1, 2] . However, the ir inherent low mechanical 
properties are one of the greatest bottlenecks to their widespread applications [3-5]. 
Gadolinium (Gd) addition is effective in introducing dense prismatic precipitates due 
to the sharp decline of equilibrium solid solubility of Gd in Mg with decrea sing 
temperature [6-8]. In detail, the equilibrium solid solubility decreases from 23.5 wt.% 
at 548°C to 15.7 wt.% at 450°C, 12.0 wt.% at 400°C and only 8.7 wt.% at 350°C 
[7-9]. Meanwhile, the nanoscale prismatic precipitates are regarded to be the most 
effective strengthening precipitates in Mg alloy [6, 7] . Therefore, t he strong 
precipitation strengthening make Mg-Gd based alloys to be ones of the promising 
candidates of high -strength Mg alloys [1, 7, 8] . U ntil now, a series of the Mg-Gd 
based alloys have been investigated for the development of high-strength Mg alloys [7, 
10-15]. In addition, microalloying such as addition of Zn to Mg -Gd based alloys can 
further enhance the age hardening response due to the introduced basal precipitates [2, 
7, 15, 16]. 
For engineering application purpose s, warm extrusion forming is a n easy and 
effective method to produce Mg components, which can significantly improve the 
mechanical properties than casting Mg alloys by refining grains and reducing casting 
defects [17-20]. For example, a Mg-10Gd-5.66Y-1.62Zn-0.65Zr (wt.%) alloy with 
~0.9 μm fine recrystallized grain size has been warm extruded and then aged to obtain 
the outstanding yield strength (YS) of 473 MPa and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 
542 MPa [19]. Generally, the i so-thermal extrusion (I TE) with the same billet and 
mold temperature [11, 18, 19, 21-25] is widely chosen, probably because it is easy to 
control and evaluate the extrusion temperature . Most of t he reported preheated 
temperatures for both the billets and molds range from about 350°C to 450°C, while 
the billets are usually homogenized at above 500°C [11, 18, 19, 21-24]. As put before, 
the equilibrium solid solubility of Gd in Mg is very sensitive to the temperature 
changes [6-8]. Hence, the relatively low preheated temperature certainly leads to a 
large amount of precipitation and then weakens the precipitation strengthening in the

ageing treatment after the extrusion. Increasing the preheated temperature can reduce 
the precipitation during the strengthening. However, if the extrusion temperature is 
too high , the grain growth during the extrusion process will be quite aggressive, 
which leads to coarse grains and poor grain boundary strengthening [25]. Besides, 
surface cracks easily form during high temperature ITE, because the Mg alloys’ low 
stress at high temperature is poorly resistant to the surface frictio n [26, 27]. Hence, 
the ITE with both low and high temperatures ha s non-ignorable disadvantages for 
improving the mechanical performance. It is necessary to overcome th ese 
disadvantages of the traditional ITE in order to well utilize the extrusion technology. 
In view of this aim, the restriction caused by the same or close temperature of the 
billet and mold should be lifted from the extrusion process, namely, the 
differential-thermal extrusion (DTE) is required . On one hand, if the billet is 
preheated at a relatively high temperature close to the homogenized temperature, the 
precipitation during the preheat ing can be maximally restrict. On the other hand, the 
mold can be preheated at a much lower temperature to control the actual extrusion 
temperature and avoid grains coarsening. This paper studied the effects of the DTE on 
extruded microstructure and mechanical properties compared with the ITE. 
2. Experimental procedure 
A Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) alloy was prepared from pure Mg (99.9%), pure 
Zn (99.9%), Mg -87 wt.% Gd and Mg -30 wt.% Zr master alloys in an electric 
resistance furnace protected by a mixed atmosphere of CO 2 and SF 6 with the 
volumetric ratio of 99:1. The actual composition is Mg -14.98Gd-0.97Zn-0.39Zr 
(wt.%) which is measured by an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission 
spectroscopy analyzer (ICP -AES, Perkin -Elmer, Plasma 400). The melt was poured 
into a heated steel mold with an inner diameter of 65 mm and an inner height of 100 
mm, and then naturally air cooled. After that, the as -cast billets were homogenized at 
500 °C for 2 h plus 520 °C for 12 h and then air cooled. These homogenized billets 
were then machined into cylinders with 60 mm in diameter and 40 mm in height for 
the following extrusions.

3. Results and Discussion 
3.1 Simulation results of temperature distributions during ITE and DTE 
Fig. 1 shows the FEM simulation results of temperature distributions during 
three different steps of ITE and D TE, where the tracking of temperature at the front 
end of the extruded billets (Tf) is more necessary as the end can represent the 
deformation zone. According to Fig. 1b and e , deformation during the ITE and DTE 
has finished by the time when the Tf reaches the peak. According to the colours of the 
molds in Fig. 1 , the mold temperatures change quite negligibly compared with the 
billet temperature changes in the ITE and DTE. Therefore, the mold temperatures can 
be considered to remain constant during the extrusions . Furthermore, the billet 
temperature changes differently in the ITE and DTE, because of the influences of the 
plastic deformation generating heat and heat transfer from the billets to the molds [29]. 
During the ITE, the heat transfer is relatively weak as the billet and mold 
temperatures are close especially at the initial stage. T he Tf keeps raising with the 
increasing deformation degree (Fig. 1a ~ b) because more heat is generated by larger 
scale of plastic deformation [29]. During the DTE, the heat greatly transfers from the 
mold to the billet due to the higher billet temperature and lower mold temperature, 
which performs oppositely with the heat generated by the deformation. As a result, the 
Tf firstly decreases when the deformation degree is relatively small and then increases 
with the increasing deformation degree (Fig. 1d ~ e). The simulations predict the peak 
values of the Tf to be nearly the same, namely, 469°C and 471°C, during the ITE and 
DTE, respectively. Meanwhile, the Tf values reach their peaks at quite close time 
(5.42 s for the ITE versus 5.44 s for the DTE). Hence, the ITE and DTE samples can 
be regarded to be produced with the same extrusion temperature . Then, when the 
deformations finish, the Tf values start to fall but with different falling speed s in the 
ITE (Fig. 1b ~ c) and DTE (Fig. 1e ~ f). As predicted in Fig. 1c and f , the Tf falls to

430 °C in 5.2 s during the ITE, while it falls to 382 °C in only 4.2 s during the DTE,

3.2 As-extruded microstructures of the ITE and DTE samples 
Fig. 2 shows the BSE -SEM micrographs of the as-extruded ITE and DTE 
samples. Most of the grains are equiaxed dynamic recrystallized (DRXed) grains , 
which indicates both the samples are nearly fully recrystallized . Meanwhile, the 
grains of the as -extruded DTE sample are obviously finer than those of the ITE 
sample. As the grain growth of the DRXed grains is close associated with the 
temperature [36-38], the finer grains of the DTE sample was caused by the higher 
cooling speed as inferred in Section 3.1. The actual grain sizes will be calculated 
according to the EBSD micrographs. 
Obvious blocky and rod -like particles can be observed in both the ITE and DTE 
samples. Considering the extrusion process rarely changes the phase composition of 
the samples for its short duration, the particles are suggested to from before the 
extrusion processes. Fig. 3 shows the TEM results of the particles. The SAED pattern 
in Fig. 3a [34] shows the blocky particles have a face-center-cubic (fcc) structure with 
a lattice constant (a) of 0.72 nm, which indicates the particles to be (Mg, Zn) 3Gd 
phase [39-41]. The EDS result of the particles in Fig. 3c show accordant result s. 
Referring to the rod -like particles, the EDS result in Fig 3d indicate the partic les are 
Zn-rich and Zr -rich p hase. Furthermore, a lattice constant (a) of 0.77 nm can be 
calculated based on the SAED pattern in Fig. 2b, which matches the lattice structure 
of the Zn2Zr3 particle (tetragonal structure with a = 0.76 nm and c = 0.70 nm [42, 43]). 
Hence, residual secondary (Mg, Zn) 3Gd phase [34] and rod-like Zn 2Zr3 compounds 
[44, 45] existed in both the ITE and DTE samples.

formation and amount of the LPSO structur e by changing the billet and mold

3.3 Age hardening response of the samples before and after ITE and DTE 
Fig. 7 shows the age hardening curves of the samples under the homogenized 
(520 °C for 12 h ) [35], preheated, ITE and DTE conditions. The aim of testing the 
preheating samples is t o know what the alloy hardness experienced before the 
extrusions, by preheating the homogenized alloy at ~430 °C for 1 h for the ITE, or at 
~490 °C for 1 h for the DTE, respectively. The corresponding key fea tures of the 
curves are summarized in Table 1. It can be seen that, a fter being aged at 200 °C for 
64 h, the hardness increased from 83 HV (the homogenized alloy) to 127 HV with an 
increment of 44 HV [35]. The hardness increments decreased to 35 HV and 41 HV , 
respectively, after the preheated treatments for the ITE and DTE. Hence, precipitation 
has occurred during the preheat treatments. However, both the peak hardness (126 HV) 
and hardness increment ( 41 HV) of the sample preheated at 4 90 °C are higher than 
those (119 HV and 35 HV , respectively) of the sample preheated at low 430 °C, which 
means the higher preheating temperature decreased precipitation during the preheat 
treatment and reserved stronger precipitation ability. 
When it comes to the as -extruded samples, both the ITE and DTE processes 
enhanced the hardness of the pre-heated samples because of the remarkabl e grain 
refinement effect. The hardness values of the ITE and DTE samples increased from 
84 HV and 87 HV to 93 HV and 100 HV , respectively. Namely, the hardness 
increments caused by the grain refinement are 9 HV in the ITE and 13 HV in the DTE, 
respectively. The finer grains of the DTE sample resulted in the higher hardness 
increment than the ITE sample. Meanwhile, the maximum hardness increments of the 
ITE and DTE samples are 30 HV and 34 HV , respectively, lower than the

precipitation occurred during the ITE and DTE. To be noticed, the gap between the

3.4 Room temperature tensile properties of the ITE and DTE samples 
Fig. 8a shows the representive engineering stress -strain curves of the ITE and 
DTE samples under the as -extruded and aged conditions . The room temperature 
tensile properties of the samples are illustrated in Fig. 8b. The YS, UTS and EL of the 
as-extruded DTE sample are 297 MPa, 368 MPa and 18.9%, respectively , which are 
267 MPa, 341 MPa and 8.8%, respectively, of the as -extruded ITE sample. After the 
ageing treatment, the strengths of both the ITE and DTE samples were improved with 
sacrifices in the duclities. In detail, the aged DTE sample exhibited the YS of 380 
MPa, UTS of 461 MPa and EL of 2.7%, while the aged ITE sample exhibited the YS 
of 338 MPa, UTS of 461 MPa and EL of 2.6%. Accroding to the above comparions , 
the DTE samples have both stronger strength s and better duclit ies than the ITE 
samples under both the as-extruded and aged conditions. 
The difference of the curves of the as-extruded and aged samples need to be 
detailed analyzed to reveal the effects of grain size and precipitates, respectively. The 
gaps in the YS and UTS of the as -extruded ITE and DTE samples are 30 MPa and 27 
MPa, respectively. As both the textures and Schmid factors of the ITE and DTE 
samples are quite similar, the difference in the as-extruded strengths should be mainly 
caused by the grain size and LPSO structure. Considering the high strengths of the 
as-extruded sample, the fi ner grains in the DTE sample (grain size of 2.5 μm 
compared with 3.1 μm in the ITE sample) overweighted the more LPSO structure in 
the ITE sample . Meanwhile, as seen in the inset in Fig. 8a, a yield plateau can be 
clearly found in the curve of the as -extruded ITE sample, while the curve of the 
as-extruded DTE sample only has an inconspicuous yield jog. According to our 
previous work [34], the yield plateau is caused by the Zn-Zr particles in Mg alloys 
with very fine grains. The ITE sample does not have such an obvious plateau because

of the coarser grains. The Zn2Zr3 particles pinned the dislocations during the tensile 
tests and temporarily increased the strength (namely, the YS) of the DTE sample. 
However, when the stress further increased and the dislocations escaped from the pins, 
the Zn2Zr3 particles could not keep increasing the strength [37]. That is why the 
strength gap of the as -extruded stress -strain curves is only about 10 MPa after the 
yield points (Fig. 8a), much smaller than the difference in the YS (30 MPa). The 
Zn2Zr3 particles enhanced the advantage of the finer grains in the DTE sample. 
When the samples are aged , the difference in the YS between the ITE and DTE 
samples also increased to 42 MPa . Meanwhile, the gap (about 38 MPa ) between the 
curves after the yield points is close to the difference in the YS, since neither the 
curves has a yield plateau. Namely, the ageing treatment enhanced the advantage in 
the strength of the DTE sample compared with the ITE sample, no matter in the 
aspect of the YS or the strength after the yield points. The broadened gap in strength 
is due to the stronger precipitation strengthening of the DTE sample, which is 
accordant with the results of the ageing hardening curves shown in Fig. 7 and Table 1. 
According to the microstructure comparison and tensile properties evolution , the 
DTE process can firstly produce a stronger as -extruded bar than the traditional ITE 
process, because of the finer grains result ing from the higher cooling speed in the 
DTE. Then the ageing treatment further develops the advantages of the DTE in 
strength, which is mainly due to the stronger precipitation strengthening ability 
reserved by the high billet preheat temperature. 
4. Conclusion 
The Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) alloy was warm extruded with a novel method 
called the differential -thermal extrusion ( DTE). The billet temperature was 
considerably higher than the mold temperature for the DTE, while the billet and mold 
temperatures for the traditional iso-thermal extrusion (ITE) were nearly the same. The 
finite element method (FEM) simulation was applied to estimate the temperature 
evolutions during the extrusions. The DTE samples were characterized and compared 
with the ITE samples to reveal the effects of the DTE on the microstructures and 
mechanical properties. Following conclusions were obtained:

## Page 17

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
(1) The lower mold temperature controlled the actual extrusion temperature and 
accelerated the cooling speed after deformation during the DTE process. As a result, 
finer DRXed grains and less LPSO structure which are sensitive to the cooling speed 
formed in the DTE sample than those in the ITE sample. 
(2) Compared with the ITE, the higher billet preheat temperature before the DTE 
greatly suppressed precipitation during the preheat treatment and reserved the 
precipitation ability, which enhanced the precipitation strengthening in the ageing 
treatment after the DTE. 
(3) The aged DTE sample exhibited the YS and UTS of 380 MPa and 461 MPa , 
respectively, which are ~40 MPa higher than those (338 MPa and 420 MPa ) of the 
ITE sample. Hence, the novel DTE is more suitable for producing high -strength Mg 
components than the traditional ITE.
