Submitted: 29 January 2022 / Revised: 14 April 2022 / Accepted: 26 April 2022 / Published online: 20 June 2022
Ti6Al4V is one of the most useful alloys because of its high strength, low weight, high oxidation resistance
and low CTE. However, it is weak in shear strength which has affected its performance. This research was
aimed at developing high-entropy alloy of Ti 20-Al16-V16-Fe16-Ni16-Cr16 at near equi-molar level via spark
plasma sintering technique which would possess better mechanical and oxidation characteristics than Ti64.
The powders were blended, sintered at varying temperatures from 700 to 1100 /C176C and characterized.
Results showed that HEA sintered at 1000 /C176C possessed the best mechanical, oxidative and microstructural
properties, while that sintered at 700 /C176C had the least mechanical and thermal properties. The developed
HEA had elastic modulus improvement of about 336% over Ti6Al4V and about 277% over Ti6Al4V-0.55B
alloys. It had creep resistance of 2.47% at loading of 300 mN and 4.38% at loading of 500 mN, densiﬁcation
of 98.86%, porosity of 1.14% and a parabolic oxidation proﬁle. It was concluded that the developed alloy
has superior mechanical and oxidation properties than Ti64 and so can perform better in applications
where high strength at high temperature is required.
Keywords creep, elastic modulus, high-entropy alloy,
nanohardness, oxidation, stiffness
1. Introduction
Conventional superalloys of Ni, Ti, Co and Al suffer loss of
strength when tempered at 350-550 /C176C. This implies that their
performance in jet engines, rockets, gas turbines, boilers and
other high-temperature cum high-strength applications is chal-
lenged by oxidation and creep. It is because of this that high-
entropy alloy (HEA) was developed by Y eh and his colleagues
in 2004. HEA is an alloy comprising ﬁve (5) or more elements
combined in equi- or near-equi-molar conﬁguration. Studies
show that high number of elements that form HEA favors the
formation of solid solution more than intermetallic compound.
The exceptional characteristics of HEA are induced by high-
entropy effect, lattice distortion effect, sluggish diffusion effect
and cocktail effect. The high-entropy effect promotes the
formation of solid solution more than intermetallic compounds;
lattice distortion effect promotes distortion and strain in the
lattice conﬁguration thereby improving solid solution strength-
ening and forestalling dislocation; sluggish diffusion lowers
grain growth and phase segregation, improves thermal stability
and creep resistance, while cock tail effect provokes the
reaction of the constituent elements in order to promote high-
temperature performance of the alloy (Ref 1-5). All these
effects synergize and give HEA its excellent properties and
profound recognition/ interest by the research community.
Numerous methods have been employed by researchers to
fabricate HEA such as severe decomposition (Ref 6), ultrason-
ication (Ref 7), carbothermal shock synthesis (Ref 8), fast-
moving bed pyrolysis (Ref 9), but it was discovered that these
methods were either not economically viable or low in
productivity which necessitated further research in the area in
order to improve the production efﬁciency and industrial
scalability. The choice of spark plasma sintering (SPS) in the
development of HEA was motivated by the advantages of the
technique. Fu et al., (Ref 10) developed Co
0.5FeNiCrTi0.5 HEA
via mechanical alloying (MA) and SPS and obtained high
improvement in the microhardness and microstructure. Yeh
et al., (Ref 11) observed that NiCoCrCuFe HEA developed via
SPS had better mechanical and microstructural properties than
its counterpart prepared via stir casting. Fu et al., (Ref 12)
further observed that HEAs developed via SPS showed lower
porosity than those produced with hot pressed (HP) technique
since the application of pulsed DC during SPS reduces
activation energy of defect diffusion and migration, thus
vaporizing impurities and eliminating voids. So, SPS is one
of the best tools in developing HEA as it produces microstruc-
ture devoid of micropores with low grain growth and inter-

2. Experimental Procedure
2.1 Materials
The powders used in this experiment included Ti with purity
of 99.9%, particle size of 45-55 lm and density of 4.54 g/cm 3;
Al that has purity of 99.9%, particle size of 45 lm and density
of 2.7 g/cm 3; V of 99.9% purity, 45-50 lm particle size and
density of 6.1 g/cm 3; Fe of 99.9% purity, 45 lm particle size
and density of 7.87 g/cm 3; Ni of 99.9% purity, particle size of
45-65 lm and density of 8.9 g/cm 3; and Cr of 99.9% purity,
particle size of 40-65 lm and density of 7.19 g/cm 3. They were
supplied by TLS Technik, Germany. The powders were blended
with tubular mixer after measuring out appropriate weight of
corresponding powders that would generate Ti
20-Al16-V16-
Fe16-Ni16-Cr16 equiatomic HEA. The mixing was conducted
for 10 h to ensure homogenous dispersion of particles at a
rotating speed of 69 revolutions per minute. Steel balls of 8-mm
diameter were incorporated into the powder being blended to
enhance homogenous dispersion at ball-to-powder ratio of 4:5
(Ref 20). The mixed powders were measured into a graphite
die, closed with graphite punches and transferred into the
sintering chamber of the SPS machine.
2.2 Method
After blending the as-measured powders, appropriate weight
to produce pellet of 40-mm diameter and 5-mm thickness was
measured into a graphite die and transferred to sintering
chamber of SPS machine (KCE-FCT-HHPD 25, Germany).
Then, initialization of the machine was conducted as follows:
vacuum pressure = 70.5 Pascal, relative pressure = /C0 6910
4

samples were fabricated with the same parameters, changing
only the sintering temperature one after the other as follows:
800, 900, 1000 and 1100 /C176C. At the completion of each
sintering, the pellet was sandblasted to remove contamination

Figure 8 shows proﬁles of applied load versus penetration
depth. Penetration proﬁle recorded in the samples varied as
their properties varied. The loading, holding time and unload-
ing regimes depict the strength of the material as well as the
fracture mode that will be exhibited by the material when used.
It can be seen that sample sintered at 1000 /C176C returned almost
undeformed when the load was removed showing that it had the
best fracture toughness. The trend decreased as the sintering
temperature decreased. The sample with the highest deforma-
tion was that sintered at 700 /C176C because the sample had the
least dislocation resistance. It is the dislocation interlock that
limited the deformation of the nano-indenter in sample sintered
at 1000 /C176C (Ref 47), while high dislocation slip ﬁeld
perpetrated high deformation as the indenter was put on the
sample sintered at 700 /C176C. Just as expected, the depth proﬁle
generated by 500 mN was higher than that impacted by 300 mN
as higher load generates higher impacts.
Figure 9 shows penetration depth against penetration time
for the two loading applied. The penetration depth of the
sample sintered at 1000 /C176C was least followed by that sintered
at 1100 /C176C. The sample with the highest penetration depth was
that sintered at 700 /C176C because it had the least resistance to
dislocation, while that with the most strengthened microstruc-
ture resisted penetration most.
Figure 10 shows displacement proﬁle used in ascertain the
deformation mechanism exhibited by the developed HEA. It
was reported that deformation of material during nano-inden-
tation resulted to pile-up of material which is material out ﬂow
or sink-in of material. Plastic deformation can occur in either
ways, while elastic deformation exhibits sink-in only (Ref 48,
49). During the loading regime in nano-indentation, elastic and
plastic deformation occur; but during unloading regime, only
elastic deformation occurs (Ref 50). So, a plot of ratio of ﬁnal
penetration depth (Hf) and maximum penetration depth (Hm),
i.e., Hf/Hm (Fig. 10a) was used to identify its elasticity
behavior. Also in Fig. 10(b), plot of ratio of elastic modulus ( E)
and nanohardness (Hv), i.e., E/Hv was done. If a large value
occurs, it is an indication that heavy pile-up of the material took
place (Ref 51). It can be seen that the highest value of Hf/Hm
was 0.54 at 500 mN loading and 0.32 at 300 mN loading in
sample (700 /C176C). This means that there was pile-up of material
at the contact area of the indenter, however, insigniﬁcant. For
sample with the highest strength and elastic modulus (sample
1000 /C176C), a ratio of 0.19 and 0.08 at 500 mN and 300 mN
loading, respectively, was obtained. This shows that the pile-up
was very little. Therefore, pile-up mechanism is inversely
proportional to the material /C213s strength and modulus. The pile-up
was the highest in sample 700 /C176C and least in sample 1000 /C176C
according to the following trends: pile-up (1 >2>3>5>4);
strength (1 <2<3<5<4); low value of pile-up infers high
strength. Moreover, the ratio of modulus to hardness (Fig. 10b)
showed a similar trend with Hf/Hm. This corroborates the
earlier results that sample sintered at 1000 /C176C possessed
superior strength and modulus, while that sintered at 700 /C176C
possessed the poorest strength and modulus. More so, the
density of materials has close relationship with penetration
dynamics of materials. Density determines how atoms are
closely packed in a crystal lattice of materials. It will be recalled
that relative density of material determines its mechanical
characteristics, including resistance to indentation. In Fig. 9,i t
will be discovered that even though the same load was applied
on different samples, the penetration depth was not the same.
Samples with high relative density such as those sintered at
1000 /C176C and 1100 /C176C had lower penetration depth, while those
with low relative density had higher penetration depth. This is

varies inversely as the density.
3.4 Thermal Properties
Resistance to high-temperature oxidation is one of the
attributes of HEA as a result of ‘‘cock tail’’ effect (Ref 4, 52).
To demonstrate this property, TGA test was conducted on the
developed samples. It was observed that throughout the heating
regime (25-1000 /C176C), the increase in weight (oxidation) was
very minimal. Sample 700 /C176C increased by 0.6%, sample 800
/C176C increased by 1.2%, sample 900 /C176C increased by 0.48%,
sample 1000 /C176C increased by 0.23% and sample 1100 /C176C
increased by 0.48%. The thermal stability of all the samples up
to temperature of 1000 /C176C was high. However, sample 1000 /C176C
recorded the lowest oxidation and hence, highest thermal
stability. The resistance to oxidation of this sample is attributed
to the fact that it possessed the most reﬁned grains in the
microstructure devoid of pores and impurities which helped in
the formation of thick protective layer that resisted oxidation as
reported in Ref 23. It will be recalled that the kinetics of
oxidation which describes the oxidation time relationship have
it that oxidation of materials can occur in any of the three curve
forms: logarithmic, parabolic, or linear with time (Ref 53), in
which no engineer will work with material that oxidizes
linearly with time. For the other forms, the initial oxidation will
be swift but as time goes on, oxide layer formed will prevent
further oxidation. So, looking at Fig. 11(b), it will be observed
that the oxidation proﬁle followed parabolic curve which
indicates that the material will perform well in high-temperature
application. Comparing this proﬁle with the oxidation proﬁle of
Ti64 in Guleryuz et al. (Ref 54) and Brice et al. (Ref 55) where
oxidation above 650 and 750 /C176C, respectively, exhibited linear
proﬁles; it will be agreed that this HEA possesses better
oxidation resistance. This is because its oxidation subsides after
sometime when oxide scales may have enveloped the material.
Quantitatively, it will be observed that sample sintered at 1000
/C176C suffered lowest oxidation, while sample sintered at 800 /C176C
suffered the highest oxidation as shown in Fig. 11(a). As the
temperature increased, the weight gain increased. This trend
can be explained by the thermodynamic stability and metallur-
gical bonding of the samples. From the microstructure (Fig. 3),
it will be noticed that sample sintered at 700 and 800 /C176C had
more micropores which may be as a result of entrapped air and
impurities, and it will be recalled that impurities stimulate
oxidation reaction. So, these samples sintered at lower temper-
ature had lower thermodynamic stability than those sintered at

4. Conclusion
The development of Ti 20-Al16-V16-Fe16-Ni16-Cr16 HEA via
SPS was successfully achieved and the following conclusion
can be drawn.
i. The optimum SPS temperature was 1000 /C176C which pro-
duced a sample with fully homogenized solid solution
HEA with reﬁned grains replete of FCC and BCC phases.
ii. The elastic modulus and nanohardness of the sample sin-
tered at 1000 /C176C were the highest, while that sintered at
700 /C176C were the least. The improvement in the strength
and modulus was attributed to the full evolution of solid
solution at that temperature with the subsequent solid
solution strengthening cum lattice distortion mechanism.
An increase in the temperature to 1100 /C176C saw segrega-
tion of phases/elements, and re-evolution of cracks which
diminished the mechanical and thermal properties.
iii. The pile-up behavior of the samples was inversely pro-
portional to the mechanical strength and modulus. The
creep resistance increased when the sintering tempera-
ture increased up to 1000 /C176C and started decreasing,
while the stiffness increased as the sintering temperature
increased as well.
iv. Oxidation resistance of the developed HEA was high
exhibiting a parabolic oxidation proﬁle and low weight
gain of 0.23% in sample sintered at 1000 /C176C. The ther-
mal stability was attributed to the high grain reﬁnement
which induced formation of oxidation protective ﬁlm.
v. The mechanical and thermal properties of the developed
HEA thus tested showed better characteristics than Ti64.
