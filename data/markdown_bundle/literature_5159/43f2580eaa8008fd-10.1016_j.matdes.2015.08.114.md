Tensile properties of three Ni-based disk superalloys with 5 wt.%, 15 wt.% and 23 wt.% Co contents were investi-
gated from room temperature (RT) to 725 °C with a constant strain rate of 3 × 10−4 s−1. It is found that addition of
Co enhances the yield strength and the strain hardening capacity of the alloy in the studied temperature regime. It
is due to the following two reasons: i.e. the interactions between a large volume fraction of ﬁne secondary and
tertiaryγ′ precipitates with the dislocations in slip bands at lower temperatures and the formation of deformation
microtwins at higher temperatures. However, the formation of deformation microtwins in the high Co-containing
content alloy sharply decreases the ductility at higher temperatures.
© 2015 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys have been widely used in aircraft engines and
land-based gas turbines owing to their excellent high temperature
mechanical properties including high yield strength and good creep
resistance [1]. It is well known that Ni-based superalloys could retain
a high temperature performance because the strengthened γ′ (Ni3Al)
precipitates which are coherent, ordered L1 2 crystal structure embed-
ded in the disordered solid-solution face centered cubic (fcc) γ matrix.
Among all the types of Ni-based superalloys, disk superalloys have been
developed for applications in the temperature regime from 650 °C to
750 °C. Hence, much attention has been paid on the deformation
mechanisms of disk superalloys at the service temperatures. Alloying
elements play a great role in the mechanical properties of disk superal-
loys. Among all the elements in disk superalloys, Co acting as a critically
strategic element has been studied previously. Previous studies [2,3]
suggested that Co content had no obvious effect on the yield strength
of disk superalloys at both ambient and intermediate temperatures.
However, Yuan et al. [4] concluded that Co addition in TMW-4 M3
could improve the yield strength at intermediate temperatures com-
pared with U720Li. Another viewpoint proposed in their work is that
the enhanced yield strength by adding Co in TMW-4 M3 was
attributed to stacking faults shearing and deformation microtwinning.
However, it is worth noting that other chemical compositions such as
Cr and Ti contents were simultaneously modi ﬁed in TWM-4 M3 and
U720Li alloys in their study. More importantly, the total volume fraction
of γ′ precipitates in the two alloys was found to be different. Therefore,
the effects of Co addition stated in their research on the yield strength
might be accompanied with the roles of other elements and the volume
fraction of γ′ precipitates. In addition to the strength, a good ductility at
high temperature will be also expected. Recently, the work on the effect
of Co on the ductility of Ni-based disk superalloys is rarely reported. A
minimum ductility has been observed in the intermediate temperature
regime for many superalloys [5–9]. This behavior is generally called
intermediate temperature brittleness (ITB). Unfortunately, the causes
of the ITB behavior in Ni-based superalloys are still unclear.
In this study, Co was systematically substituted by Ni for investigating
its effects on tensile deformation behaviors including the ductility at
different temperatures in the disk superalloys. In the meanwhile, the
so-called ITB behavior will be well discussed in the text.

4. Discussion
4.1. Effects of SFE on deformation mechanisms
In the present paper the sequence of deformed microstructures
with the rising temperature for the alloys with different SFE could
be exhibited as follows: APBs formed in γ′ precipitates by coupled
α/2 b110 N dislocation pairs shearing at RT and 400 °C, and isolated
faulting or microtwinning took place due to partial dislocations
cutting γ′ precipitates at 650 °C and 725 °C. Obviously, SFE has little
effect on the deformation microstructures at lower temperatures.
However, the effect of SFE on the deformation mechanisms becomes
signiﬁcant at higher temperatures.
It has been reported[20] that lowering SFE could promote the disso-
ciation of a/2 b110N dislocations in the matrix during deformation at
intermediate temperatures. For the Alloy1 with a relatively high SFE,
the α/2 b110N dislocation is difﬁcult to dissociate in the matrix at 650
°Cand 725 °C. They would accumulate and tangle at the γ/primary γ′
interfaces during tensile deformation. The concentrated stress at the
γ/primary γ′ interfaces increases with the increment of plastic strain,
resulting in the dissociation of α/2 b110 N dislocations with aiding of
coherency stress at the interfaces. Caron et al. [21] believed that the
dissociation process complied with the following reaction:

4.2. Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength and strain hardening
It is well known that the strength of γ′ precipitates increases with
the rising temperature [36]. Hence, the decrease of yield strength of
the γ matrix with the rising temperature can be compensated by the
increment in strength of γ′ precipitates. As a result, the yield strength
for three alloys slightly decreases in the testing temperature regime,
as shown in Fig. 3 (a). Meanwhile, the ultimate tensile strength and
strain hardening exponent for three alloys only slightly changed by
temperature below 650 °C, as shown in Figs. 3(b) and 4(d).
When the temperature reaches to 650 °C, the ultimate strength as
well as the strain hardening exponent for three alloys dramatically
decrease. As known, the sessile dislocation locks are created as a result
of the dislocation intersections at lower temperatures. The mobile dislo-
cations could be impeded by the locks or interact with other moving
dislocations, resulting in a higher strength and a larger strain hardening
exponent. However, the ease of cross slip at elevated temperatures
would activate the dislocation segments and destroy the dislocation
locks. At the same time the dynamic recovery of the dislocations is dom-
inated at higher temperatures, leading to the formation of low energy
dislocation substructures [37]. Besides, the softening of γ′ precipitates
due to the repeated cutting by stacking faults or microtwins at higher
temperatures also reduces the strength and strain hardening ability of
the alloys.
It is manifested that Co addition could enhance the strength and
strain hardening at each temperature in Figs. 3 (a)–(b) and 4(d). At
lower temperatures, the large primary γ′ precipitates are not effective
on the strengthening of the alloys because the spacing between the
primary γ′ precipitates is very wide [38]. On the contrary, a larger
amount of ﬁne secondary and tertiary γ′ precipitates would play a
more signiﬁcant role in the strength and strain hardening of the alloy
due
to their interactions with the dislocations in the slip bands. Thus
Co addition raises the volume fraction of ﬁne secondary and tertiary γ′
precipitates so as to increase the strength and strain hardening ability
of the alloys at lower temperatures. At higher temperatures, the lower
SFE by adding Co in the alloy would promote the formation of deforma-
tion microtwins during tensile deformation, which could block the
mobile dislocations and increase the resistance of cross-slip [39].A sa
result, the strength and strain hardening are enhanced by the interactions
between microtwins and mobile dislocations at higher temperatures.
4.3. ITB
It is well known that grain boundaries could provide substantial
strength for materials through impeding mobile dislocations [40].I n
the present work, the dislocations in the slip bands interact with the
matrix andγ′ precipitates for the alloys at lower temperatures. It reveals
that the dislocation bands could not slip across the grain boundaries at
lower temperatures for two alloys in Fig. 7 (a)–(b) and (e) –(f). Then
the dislocations would accumulate at the grain boundaries, creating dis-
location pipe-ups. For Alloy1, the glide of grain boundaries could be
prevented by the intense γ′ precipitates along on them at RT, which
promotes strain localization and consequently cause the intergranular
fracture, as shown in Fig. 8(a). However, very few γ′ phase precipitates
at the grain boundaries in Alloy3, which is available for the grain bound-
aries glide and the stress concentration release. Thus the decohesion of

The ITB behavior is prominent at higher temperatures for Alloy3.
Fiore [5] concluded that the strain localization caused by deformation
mechanisms could lead to the ductility minima of alloys at intermediate
temperatures. According to the theory, some researchers suggested [9,
41] that the strain localization due to the stacking faults shearing in γ′
precipitates contributed to the ITB behavior of the alloys. However,
the isolated faulting mechanism does not reduce the ductility of
Alloy1 at higher temperatures. For Alloy3 it can be realized that the
ITB behavior is accompanied with the microtwinning mechanism. As
we know, the microtwins can enhance the strength because numerous
formed twin boundaries could pin mobile dislocations. On the other
hand, microtwins can improve the resistance of cross slip and reduce
the dislocation mobility in the matrix. Moreover, the extension of
microtwins can be stopped by the grain boundaries, where a large
concentrated stress is produced. Afterwards, the cracks would initiate
and propagate along the grain boundaries, as show in Fig. 8 (d). Kim
et al. [8] presented that the minimum ductility of the superalloys at
intermediate temperatures occurred when the microtwinning mecha-
nism predominated during tensile deformation. Furthermore, many
other investigators also stated [42,43] that the microtwinning played
an important role in brittle fracture of the materials. In conclusion, Co
addition tends to decrease the SFE, which facilitates the formation of
numerous deformation microtwins as well as the ITB behavior during
tensile deformation for the alloy at higher temperatures.

behaviors of three Ni-based disk superalloys have been investigated. It
was found that Co addition tends to decrease the volume fraction of
primary γ′ precipitates and increase the volume fraction of secondary
and tertiary γ′ precipitates. Co addition also reduces SFE of the alloy,
