ABSTRACT 
Room temperature tensile and compressive properties of refractory complex, concentrated alloys (RCCAs) have 
been analyzed to explore correlations capable of anticipating ductility in this growing class of structural mate -
rials. The Pugh-Pettifor and Rice-Thomson ductility criteria are generally satisfied by the current dataset, but the 
dataset does not cover a sufficiently wide range to provide a critical evaluation. A previously suggested ductility 
criterion based on the average valence electron concentration ( VEC ) of an alloy, i.e. abrupt ductile to brittle 
transition at VEC = 4.5, is not supported in the present study. A revised VEC criterion is suggested, but the data 
supporting this trend is sparse and additional work is needed. These three ductility criteria are evaluated using 
only single-phase RCCA data. A new ductility criterion based on the RCCA yield strength ( σ
y
) is suggested from 
the present work, using both single-phase and multi-phase data. RCCAs with commercially relevant tensile 
ductility have σ
y 
≤ 1200 MPa, and alloys with compressive ductility of ≥ 30% (analyzed here to provide useful 
tensile ductility) have σ
y 
≤ 1500 MPa. Strengthening models to predict σ
y 
in RCCAs may thus be used to 
anticipate alloys that may also possess useable levels of ductility.

( Table 1 ); the reason is likely due to different concentration of inter -
stitial elements in the alloy. Thus, data from a single microstructure or 
processing condition may not indicate the true potential for RT tensile 
ductility. The majority of RCCA mechanical properties are measured in 
the as-cast condition, giving microstructures that are often characterized 
by features such as large or non-uniform grain sizes and dendritic 
segregation. These features can reduce both strength and ductility, and 
there are very few efforts to produce fine grained, equiaxed micro -
structures that may be expected to provide both good strength and RT 
ductility. As a result, current literature data may represent a lower 
bound on the capacity for ductility in RCCAs. Stated more directly, it 
cannot be concluded that an alloy with little or no ductility (tensile or 
compressive) in the as-cast condition is intrinsically brittle, and subse -
quent thermo-mechanical processing may produce different micro -
structures with significant ductility. Five classical strengthening 
mechanisms [ 12 – 15 ] provide a number of physics-based approaches to 
model strength, but there are still no established approaches to predict 
ductility as a function of composition and microstructure. 
Together, these features make it challenging to predict a priori which 
RCCA compositions and microstructures are likely to simultaneously 
possess attractive high temperature strength and RT tensile ductility. 
This knowledge is essential to guide ongoing efforts to explore RCCAs as 
potential high temperature structural alloys. In this report, we analyze 
the RT mechanical behavior of RCCAs using data in the literature [ 5 , 6 ], 
as well as some recent unpublished data collected by the authors. We 
consider the available tensile and compressive yield strength and 
ductility values and compare trends against known, phenomenological 
ductility criteria including the Pugh-Pettifor criterion, the valence 
electron concentration ( VEC ) and the Rice-Thomson criterion. The 
objective of this analysis is to provide new insights to guide efforts to 
identify and develop RCCAs that have both good strength and ductility 
at RT.

fracture and compression ductility (also called malleability) is identified 
as total compression strain to fracture. Compression deformation of 
several reported RCCAs was stopped after 50% compression strain with 
no evidence of fracture. In these cases, compression ductility is assigned 
to be > 50%. Plots of yield strength vs ductility are analyzed to illustrate 
trends for RCCAs as a class of materials, and ductility is plotted against 
the phenomenological ductility parameters G / B , VEC and Gb / γ 
(described below) to explore correlations and identify new insights. 
Only single-phase RCCAs are evaluated against these phenomenological 
criteria, since experimental values of G , B and γ for these alloys are not 
available and they are calculated using the rule of mixtures of the 
respective properties of the pure elements. It has been shown earlier that 
the values of the elastic moduli of single-phase RCCAs calculated using 
first principles and the rule of mixtures are almost similar [ 19 – 22 ]. 
There is no information in the open literature if the surface energy of 
single-phase RCCAs can be estimated using the rule of mixtures. How -
ever, the surface energy of solid solutions of binary refractory alloys has 
been reported to roughly follow this rule [ 23 ]. The rule of mixtures 
cannot be applied to multi-phase RCCAs, as the phases have different 
chemical compositions and some of them can be ordered. 
Where available, the tensile and compressive ductility values 
measured in the same alloy are evaluated. To address the possible 
variability in ductility and strength due to non-optimized microstructure 
and processing, the upper envelopes of ductility vs. strength compari -
sons are emphasized. This upper bound certainly represents the ductility 
that is possible for an alloy of a given strength, and even higher ductility 
may be achieved in the same alloy with better microstructure and im -
purity control via processing. 
3. Ductility criteria 
Pettifor and Pugh Criteria. A material ’ s ability to deform plastically 
is related to the nature of atomic bonds in the crystal structure. Non- 
directional, metallic bonds favor plasticity while covalent (directional) 
bonds result in a considerable decrease in plasticity and subsequent 
brittleness. In a theoretical study by Pettifor [ 24 , 25 ] a many-body po -
tential was derived, which explicitly included the angular (directional, 
covalent) character of the bonding orbitals. It was shown that angular, 
covalent bonding produces a negative Cauchy pressure ( C
12
 C
44
< 0), 
resulting in brittle behavior. The C
ij 
elastic constants can be difficult to 
obtain and are rarely available for experimental alloys, so this criterion 
is difficult to apply as a predictive tool to guide exploration of new al -
loys. In a phenomenological analysis, Pugh associated an alloy ’ s 
ductility with its shear modulus ( G ) and fracture to the bulk modulus 
( B ). He found that ductile materials typically have a low value of the 
ratio, G / B , while brittle materials have high G / B values [ 26 ]. In practice, 
the boundary between ductile and brittle materials is generally taken to 
be G / B ≅ 0 . 42 0 . 60, with the high bound for near isotropic materials 
and the low bound for highly anisotropic materials [ 27 ]. A recent study 
has shown that the Pugh and Pettifor criteria are equivalent for cubic 
materials [ 27 ], and so the Pugh-Pettifor criterion is used in the present 
analysis to explore trends in ductility of single phase RCCAs. 
Valence Electron Concentration criterion. Theoretical studies by Qi 
and Chrzan [ 28 ] showed that a transition from intrinsically brittle to 
ductile behavior in refractory metals of group 6 (Cr, Mo, W) can occur by 
alloying with group 4 elements (Ti, Zr, Hf). This alloying decreases the 
valence electron concentration ( VEC ) of these alloys, resulting in a shear 
instability of the BCC crystal lattice so that the alloys fracture by shear 
rather than cleavage. This information was later used by Sheikh et al. 
[ 29 ] to explain brittle and ductile behavior of single-phase BCC RCCAs. 
Analyzing limited data available at that time, they showed that 
single-phase RCCAs made from elements of groups 4, 5 and 6 were 
ductile if their VEC < 4.5 and were brittle when VEC ≥ 4.5. 
Rice and Thomson criterion. Rice and Thomson [ 30 ] calculated the 
stability of a sharp crack against emission of a blunting dislocation for a 
number of crystals and qualitatively found that crystals whose disloca -
tions have wide cores and small values of the parameter Gb / γ ( < 7.5 – 10) 
are ductile, while crystals with narrow dislocation cores and large values 
of Gb / γ are brittle. Here γ is the surface energy, G is the shear modulus 
and b is the Burgers vector. The width of the dislocation core generally 
increases with decreasing stacking fault energy, which may indicate that 
the value of this quantity is also important. A lower stacking fault energy 
means that generation of new dislocations becomes easier, however, 
cross-slip is more difficult.

4.2. Correlations between tensile and compression properties 
Only four RCCAs have been tested in both tension and compression. 
These are HfNbTaTiZr (in AC and A conditions, 8 – 20% tensile elonga -
tion), NbTaTi (18.5% tensile elongation), NbTiZr (14.2% tensile elon -
gation) and MoNbTi (4% tensile elongation). The first three alloys 
survive ≥ 50% compression strain without evidence of fracture. MoNbTi 
fractured after 30% compression strain [ 44 ]. Our unpublished results 
also indicate that some RCCAs that fracture after ≤ 10% compression 
strain have tensile fracture strain < 1%. From this limited information, it 
may be suggested that RCCAs with a minimum of 30% compressive 
strain may also possess commercially relevant levels of tensile ductility 
(~4%). However, this statement is based on a single data point, and 
additional data are needed to support this proposal.

5. Discussion 
The above analyses suggest that a significant fraction of the reported 
RCCAs are intrinsically ductile at room temperature. Microstructure and 
processing play critical roles in these comparisons as illustrated by the 
range in strength and ductility achieved for the single-phase HfNbTaTiZr 
RCCA produced by different methods. The majority of RCCAs in this 
study have been characterized in the as-cast condition and so it ’ s 
reasonable to expect some or many of these alloys may subsequently 
provide better ductile behavior through thermo-mechanical processing 
and microstructural refinement. This may be especially true for multi- 
phase microstructures, where careful control of the size, volume frac -
tion and distribution of brittle intermetallic phases may improve the 
measured ductility. Interstitial impurities can be another factor affecting 
strength and ductility of RCCAs. Although it is well known that inter -
stitial elements exert a dramatic influence on the strength, ductility and 
ductile-to-brittle transition temperature, interstitial levels are rarely 
measured and reported, and essentially never controlled in RCCAs [ 1 ]. It 
is further speculated that a more consistent control of microstructure

Previously developed phenomenological ductility criteria are eval -

Thomson criteria are generally consistent with the tensile and

transition from brittle to ductile behavior at a distinct value of VEC =
4.5, the current results suggest a trend that is spread across a range of 
VEC values. The data are still rather sparse and additional work is 
needed to further explore the possible connection between VEC and 
ductility in RCCAs.

The preceding analysis shows that some variability in ductility, 
perhaps a significant amount, might be accounted for by differences in 
parameters associated with fracture, such as the size of defects in the 
tested samples and other materials-dependent terms. No firm conclu -
sions can be made at present regarding the role of these mechanisms. 
Further, the preceding analysis addresses the native ability of a material 
to resist fracture, and other effects may also contribute to the observed 
variability. Many studies use as-cast material for evaluation, without the 
benefit of subsequent thermomechanical processing to control the de -
fects and microstructure. In addition to defects that may vary in size, 
number and type from one cast material to the next, other issues such as 
residual stresses, chemical segregation, impurity levels (especially in -
terstitials), and an inhomogeneous distribution of grain sizes may each 
contribute to variable results for both strength and ductility. Given the 
growing maturity of the field, future studies are encouraged to consider 
more rigorous control of defects, impurities and microstructure to better 
establish a mechanistic understanding of deformation and fracture in 
RCCAs. 
In addition to searching for clues to the physical basis for ductility in 
RCCAs, a main motivation for establishing a ductility criterion is to 
provide a predictive tool to guide the exploration and discovery of useful 
new alloys. Methods have been developed to predict the strength of 
complex, concentrated alloys (CCAs, which include high-entropy alloys) 
in general, and BCC-based CCAs (including RCCAs) [ 51 – 58 ]. Composi -
tion is included explicitly in these models, which may therefore be used 
to predict alloys that may possess desired levels of strength as well as 
useful ductility. Our analysis shows that none of RCCAs with tensile 
strength above 1200 MPa are ductile. Although some RCCAs with lower 
yield stress are reported to be brittle, perhaps due to some of the effects 
described above, all ductile RCCAs have yield stress below 1200 MPa. 
Therefore, identifying RCCAs with the tensile yield strength less than 
1200 MPa using available strengthening models considerably reduces 
the range of the compositions within which ductile RCCAs can be found. 
Studies to explore the efficacy of such an approach are suggested. 
Criteria for achieving good strength at high temperatures 
( > 1000 – 1200 
◦
C), thus extending application temperatures beyond 
conventional superalloys, have been suggested for RCCAs [ 2 ]. While 
multi-phase RCCAs have the highest strengths at low and intermediate 
temperatures, single-phase, BCC RCCAs currently display the highest 
strengths at high temperatures. Further, strength drops slowly with 
increasing temperature below 0.6 – 0.7 T
m
, but it decreases rapidly at 
higher temperatures. Thus, single-phase, BCC RCCAs with high T
m 
are 
currently the most likely to achieve the objective of extending the use 
temperature of structural alloys. Combining this with the finding of the 
current paper, alloys that simultaneously possess RT ductility and useful 
strength at 1000 – 1200 
◦
C and above may be characterized by a 
single-phase BCC structure, a RT yield stress not exceeding 
~1200 – 1500 MPa, and a melting temperature above ~1830 – 2180 
◦
C. 
Of course, multi-phase alloys may also be developed to possess both RT 
ductility and good high temperature strength, but much additional work 
is needed to identify two-phase alloys that can provide appropriate 
microstructural control and have the needed long-term microstructural 
stability at high temperatures. Such systems have not yet been identified 
and are suggested for future research. 
6. Summary and conclusions 
Comparison of the tensile and compressive mechanical properties of 
RCCAs against three phenomenological ductility criteria has been per -
formed. The current data are broadly consistent with the Pugh-Pettifor 
and Rice-Thomson criteria. The earlier suggestion that RCCAs with a 
valence electron concentration ( VEC ) of ≤ 4.5 are ductile and VEC > 4.5 
are brittle is not supported by the present analyses. Instead, the ductile- 
to-brittle transition seems to occur over a range of VEC values. The data 
for these comparisons are rather limited and additional work is still 
required. In addition to these previously suggested ductility criteria for 
RCCAs, we develop a new correlation based on yield strength ( σ
y
). 
Commercially relevant levels of ductility are correlated with tensile σ
y 
of 
≤ 1200 MPa and compressive σ
y 
of ≤ 1500 MPa. Different models exist to 
calculate RCCA strengths, providing a predictive method to anticipate 
room temperature ductility in RCCAs.
