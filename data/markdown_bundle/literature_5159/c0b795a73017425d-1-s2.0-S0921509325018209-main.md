ABSTRACT
The fabrication of refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) via laser-based directed energy deposition 
(DED-LB) using elemental powders often fails to achieve chemical homogeneity. This investigation of a NbTaV 
RCCA revealed the density-driven elemental segregation during DED-LB processing. We observed a chemical 
gradient with accumulating of Ta near the substrate and enriching of V in upper regions. This compositional 
evolution refined the grain structure and increased microhardness along the build direction. Despite the chemical 
heterogeneity, the as-deposited NbTaV RCCA exhibited a favorable mechanical profile, with a yield strength of 
~620 MPa and ~12 % elongation. A key finding was that remelting, intended to enhance chemical homogeneity 
of the alloy, increased its strength by ~10 % through microstructural transformations, not by eliminating the 
unmelted particles. In the referent Nb, remelting improved both strength and ductility due to elevating the 
content of interstitials (O, N). These findings demonstrate that while remelting is an effective strategy for 
enhancing the properties of single- and multi-element materials, advanced in situ control remains essential to 
manage segregation during the DED-LB processing of RCCAs from elemental powder blends.
1. Introduction
Complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) represent a relatively new class 
of multi-principal element materials which are interesting by their 
unique properties. Specifically, refractory-based CCAs (RCCAs) exhibit 
improved high-temperature strength [ 1 – 7 ] and strength retention abil -
ity [ 8 , 9 ], oxidation [ 10 – 13 ], wear [ 14 – 16 ], corrosion [ 15 , 17 – 19 ], and 
radiation [ 20 – 23 ] resistance, compared to conventional refractory al -
loys [ 9 , 24 ]. They also demonstrate promising fracture [ 8 , 25 – 27 ] and 
impact [ 28 ] toughness, malleability [ 25 , 29 , 30 ], or phase stability 
[ 31 – 33 ]. These features make them potential candidates for different 
applications in extreme environments.
However, the deployment of RCCAs is often hindered by economic 
and manufacturing constraints. Most of these alloys consist of expensive 
elements that pose limitations on the fabrication of parts having com -
plex geometry using traditional methods such as casting and machining, 
which are accompanied by substantial material wastage and time loss. In 
this context, additive manufacturing (AM) is viewed as an attractive 
method for synthesising RCCAs, enabling the production of final prod -
ucts with the required shape [ 34 , 35 ]. Among different AM techniques, 
Laser-Based Directed Energy Deposition (DED-LB) is of special interest 
since it offers a promising route for fabricating large-scale parts with 
precise compositional control [ 36 ].
To date, DED-LB has been applied for synthesis of numerous RCCAs 
[ 13 , 37 – 62 ], with predominantly successful depositions in the case of 
pre-alloyed powders [ 47 , 49 , 51 , 52 , 56 – 58 ]. Specifically, a defect-free

2. Materials and methods

NbTaV alloy samples. These powders were fabricated by Plasma

The sample deposition scheme is illustrated in Fig. 2 . A unidirec -
tional ’ snake ’ scanning strategy was employed for all samples. A key 
feature of this strategy was reverse surfacing: upon completing a layer, 
the deposition head began the next layer immediately from the endpoint 
of the previous layer ’ s track, without returning to the original starting 
point. This approach minimises non-productive travel time and helps 
manage thermal distribution. To mitigate excessive heat accumulation, 
a controlled interlayer pause of 50 s was enforced between the deposi -
tion of consecutive layers.
For samples processed with remelting, a specific layer-by-layer 
remelting strategy was implemented. The sequence for each layer was 
as follows. First, a single layer was clad using the standard parameters. 
Second, without moving to a starting point, this deposited layer was 
immediately remelted using the same laser power but without powder 
feeding. Third, after the remelting pass, the deposition head moved to 
the starting point of the remelting trajectory, which was identical to the 
end point of the previous deposition track. This ensured continuity and 
maintained the consistent ’ snake ’ pattern for the subsequent layer. 
Finally, the process repeated for the next layer. This strategy was 
designed to maximise local chemical homogenisation and dissolution of 
unmelted particles within each layer by applying a controlled secondary 
thermal cycle immediately after deposition.

3.1.2. EDS analysis of the NbTaV samples
Fig. 5 shows the concentration profiles of the NbTaV samples without 
( Fig. 4 (a)) and with remelting ( Fig. 4 (b)) along the BD-TD plane. A 
significant compositional gradient was observed in both samples, devi -
ating notably from the target equiatomic composition. The bottom re -
gions of the depositions were enriched in Ta (~60 at%) and Nb (~35 at 
%) but depleted in V (~5 at%). With increasing height, the Ta concen -
tration decreased linearly to approximately 35 at% at the top. The 
profiles of Nb and V were more complex: both elements increased in 
concentration towards the top, but V exhibited a minor increase up to 
~10 mm height before accelerating to ~20 at% at the top. The Nb 
content rose continuously until ~12 – 14 mm, reached a plateau at 
~45 – 50 at% for the next 3 – 5 mm, and then slightly decreased at the top. 
This final decrement was more pronounced in the sample with remelting 
( Fig. 5 (b)).
The strong compositional gradient and deviation from the equia -
tomic target composition were hardly connected with the morphology 
or flowability of the initial powders, as these parameters were close to 
the referent Nb (see Section 2 ). Furthermore, the Ta enrichment of 
bottom parts of the samples ruled out the factor of T
m
, which claims that 
the higher the T
m 
of a powder, the lower the probability of its deposition 
[ 38 – 40 ]. Rather, the inconsistency between the nominal and measured 
chemical composition of the NbTaV alloy was attributable to the sig -
nificant difference in the densities of the constituent elements: Nb (8.57 
g/cm
3
), Ta (16.68 g/cm
3
), and V (6.11 g/cm
3
). While the initial powder 
mixture had a composition close to the equiatomic one, its handling to 
the hopper could result in demixing. Thus, the denser Ta powder was 
likely settled to the bottom of the hopper and was consequently 
consumed first during the deposition process. Conversely, the lighter V 
powder remained at the top and was used predominantly in the later 
stages, leading to the observed vertical stratification.

To mitigate this density-driven powder segregation, one can be

spherical shape suggestive of an adhered, unmelted powder particle.

This spatial variation in grain morphology is likely influenced by a 
combination of thermal and chemical factors. In the initial layers adja -
cent to the Ti substrate, the high thermal conductivity of the substrate 
acts as a powerful heat sink, establishing a steep thermal gradient (G) 
that favors columnar grain growth. As the build height increases, 
progressive heat accumulation within the deposited wall reduces the 
thermal gradient, promoting a higher ratio of solidification rate (R) to G 
(i.e., a higher G/R ratio), which is a known condition for the transition to 
equiaxed growth. Concurrently, the chemical gradient, with increasing 
V content towards the top ( Fig. 5 ), widens the solidification interval and 
enhances constitutional supercooling, which further promotes equiaxed 
nucleation. Therefore, the more equiaxed structure at the top is a syn -
ergistic result of a reduced thermal gradient and an increased driving 
force for equiaxed nucleation due to solute enrichment.

Third, remelting effectively addresses local chemical heterogeneity 
within the melt pool but is incapable of rectifying the global, pre- 
existing segregation established during powder feeding. The remelting 
step provides additional thermal energy that promotes a more uniform 
redistribution of elements within the already-deposited layer, as evi -
denced by the homogenisation of V along TD in the BD-TD plane and the 
dissolution of most Nb particles ( Fig. 8 ). However, this process occurs 
after the density-driven stratification of powders in the hopper has 
already imposed a macroscopic Ta-rich (bottom) to V-rich (top) 
compositional gradient throughout the sample ( Fig. 5 ). The remelting 
fails to alter this global gradient because it does not involve the intro -
duction of new powder to correct the stoichiometry. Therefore, while 
remelting is a valuable strategy for improving microstructural homo -
geneity and dissolving certain unmelted particles at a local scale, it is an 
ineffective countermeasure against segregation rooted in the powder 
delivery system itself.
3.2. Mechanical properties
3.2.1. Microhardness
Next, we evaluated the local mechanical properties of the Nb and 
NbTaV samples. Fig. 10 (a) shows the microhardness profiles of the Nb 
and NbTaV samples without and with remelting along the BD-TD plane. 
The average microhardness of the Nb sample without remelting was 
~110 HV, and it showed small deviations along the BD-TD plane. 
Remelting increased both the microhardness, up to an average value of 
~125 HV, and its fluctuations along the height. The increment in 
microhardness could be connected with the solid solution strengthening 
induced by interstitials, namely O and N [ 9 , 73 , 74 ], the content of which 
increased by ~33 % after remelting ( Table S2 ). The source of these in -
terstitials is likely a combination of the initial powder feedstock 
( Table S2 ) and minor atmospheric contamination within the deposition 
chamber, despite the use of an argon atmosphere (see Section 2 ).
The microhardness of the NbTaV samples increased towards the top, 
from ~250 HV to ~340 HV, in the sample with remelting ( Fig. 10 (a)). 
This sample also showed slightly higher values on average, which could 
hardly be connected with the impurities, retaining their content almost 
unchanged ( Table S2 ), while its microhardness profile was smoother 
than that of the sample without remelting, where the magnitude of 
deviations could reach ~60 HV.
The observed ascending trend in the microhardness with height 
could be explained by an increase in V content ( Fig. 5 ), which is known 
as an effective solid solution strengthener [ 75 – 77 ]. To confirm this 
suggestion, we estimated the solid solution strengthening, according to 
the Maresca-Curtin model [ 70 ]. The calculated values of YS were then 
compared with the microhardness values, which were preliminary 
extrapolated from the trendlines, averaged for a certain local position 
correlated with the EDS area analysis ( Fig. 5 ), and converted into YS, 
following the well-known relationship [ 71 ] ( Fig. 10 (b)). The calculated 
YS values were in good agreement with the YS values recalculated from 
microhardness for the sample with remelting, while somewhat poorer 
agreement was found for the sample without remelting. The latter 
inconsistency, as well as steep microhardness deviations found in the 
sample without remelting, along with its lower microhardness compared 
to the sample with remelting, were probably due to a larger number of 
unmelted particles.
To deconvolve the concurrent microstructural contributions to the 
microhardness increment, the Hall-Petch effect was quantitatively 
assessed for the NbTaV sample with remelting as an example using a 
constant of k = 530 MPa μ m

data of Fig. 5 and Ref. [ 78 ]. The grain refinement from ~65 μ m at the 
bottom to ~30 μ m at the top accounts for a strength increase of ~31 
MPa, equivalent to a hardness increment of only ~10 HV. This repre -
sents a minor contribution of approximately 11.5 % to the total ~90 HV 
gradient. The influence of the texture is deemed negligible, since it 
remained weak and random (MUD < 1.7) along BD. Therefore, this ev -
idence confirms that solid solution strengthening from V is the dominant 
mechanism, accounting for the vast majority (~88.5 %) of the observed 
microhardness gradient.

3.3. Fractography
Fig. 12 shows fracture surfaces of the Nb and NbTaV samples without 
and with remelting after room-temperature tensile tests. Both Nb sam -
ples demonstrated developed necking and a fully ductile fracture mode, 
as found in the low-magnification images ( Fig. 12 (a and b)). The high- 
magnification images showed that fracture surfaces of the Nb samples 
contained profuse dimples with elongation. Adding to that, in the 
sample without remelting, signatures of delamination and void forma -
tion were exclusively observed ( Fig. 12 (a and b)). These features could 
explain the lower ductility compared to the sample with remelting 
( Fig. 11 ; Table 3 ).
In turn, the NbTaV samples exhibited less prominent necking, with a 
mixture of ductile and brittle fracture, as evidenced by low- 
magnification images ( Fig. 12 (c and d)). The high-magnification im -
ages revealed that the central parts of the cross-sections in both samples 
contained profuse zones of dimples alternated with occasional areas of 
river patterns, cleavage facets, voids, and secondary cracks. Meantime, 
the latter features of the brittle fracture dominated on the edges of the 
cross-sections.

processing parameters, in contrast to Nb. Indeed, the direct comparison 
of the side surfaces of the NbTaV samples without and with remelting 
revealed some distinct features. Specifically, the presence of unmelted 
particles had a minor effect on the plastic flow localisation and fracture 
behaviour, rather than the morphology of grains. This was confirmed by 
corresponding EDS elemental maps, which revealed no particle-induced 
cracks along particle/matrix interfaces in the sample without remelting, 
hinting at their good compatibility with the matrix ( Fig. 14 (a)). 
Furthermore, the polygonal-shaped fine grains in the sample without 
remelting appeared to provide more homogenous load transfer, as 
judged from the relatively homogeneous distribution of GNDs ( Fig. 14

strength ( Fig. 11 (c and d); Table 3 ). In turn, the heterogeneous micro -

shaped grains found in the sample with remelting ( Fig. 14 (b)) gener -
ated more obstacles, including back-stress [ 87 ], for dislocation motion 
that increased the strength ( Fig. 11 (c and d); Table 3 ). Meanwhile, this 
layered microstructure simultaneously limited the abilities to relieve 
stress by plastic deformation due to inhomogeneous load transfer be -
tween the adjacent layers which led to the appearance of lateral cracks 
in-between the layers as a response to tensile loading ( Fig. 14 (b)), 
resulting in slightly reduced elongation ( Fig. 11 (c and d); Table 3 ).
4. Conclusions
This study presents the first systematic investigation of the DED-LB 
processing of an equiatomic NbTaV RCCA from elemental powder 
blends. While the targeted equiatomic composition was not achieved 
due to density-driven powder segregation, this work is novel in its 
detailed analysis of how a remelting strategy influences the 
composition-structure-property relationships across a resulting 
gradient, with the preferred location of heavier Ta at the bottom and 
lighter V at the top. In terms of structure and local properties, this

These results demonstrate that optimising properties in DED-LB- 
processed RCCAs consisting of elements with highly different densities 
requires more advanced strategies than an additional remelting step. 
Future efforts must therefore integrate real-time compositional moni -
toring and advanced powder feed systems to actively manage chemistry, 
as these factors are as critical as melt pool control for achieving 
compositional uniformity. Ultimately, a holistic approach is needed to 
simultaneously ensure complete particle melting, actively mitigate 
density-driven segregation, and maintain target stoichiometry.
