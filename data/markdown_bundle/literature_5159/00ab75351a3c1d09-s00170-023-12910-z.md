Abstract
Surface integrity is considered to be a significant factor in evaluating surface qualities. A wide range of applications of 
nickel-based superalloys can be attributed to a number of features such as mechanical and chemical characteristics at elevated 
temperatures, high durability and ductileness, great resistance to corrosion, high melting point, thermal shock, thermal 
fatigue, and erosion. However, the practical performance of the component particularly the fatigue life is critically influenced 
by the machined surface finish of Ni-based superalloys. The present review article provides the most recent information on 
various surface integrity properties while machining Ni-based superalloys. The surface integrity aspects contain the surface 
topography including machined surface defects (plucking, metal debris, feed marks, surface cavities, smeared material, 
grooves and laps, cracking, carbide particles, and redeposited materials) and surface roughness; the metallurgical phase 
consists of plasticity, grain refinement and orientation, and white layer formation, and mechanical characteristics comprise 
the residual stress and strain hardening. The impact of various cutting parameters, the cutting environment, and cutting tool 
materials have been carefully explained on surface metallurgy and mechanical characteristics. Moreover, the influence of 
surface integrity on the fatigue life of machined components has been studied.

Surface integrity is the combination of various features 
such as surface texture (surface roughness and machined sur-
face defects), metallurgical states (plastic deformation, grain 
orientation, and white layer formation), and mechanical 
characteristics (microhardness, residual stresses) [26, 27]. 
Moreover, it directly affects the functionality and life span of 
a machined product by controlling tribological phenomena 
such as containing body wear and friction, lubrication effec-
tiveness, stress corrosion, and fatigue crack formation [26]. 
Figure 3 depicts the several unique surface integrity charac-
teristics of the machined parts which have been discussed in 
later sections of the current review paper [28].
In current days, the investigation of the surface integ-
rity of nickel-based superalloys has become a hot topic. 
This is mainly caused by the association between the sur -
face integrity of the machined surfaces and the fatigue 
performance of critical aero-engine components [29– 31]. 
These components including turbine blades, discs, and 
rotors are primarily consist of nickel-based superalloys 
and frequently experience extreme fatigue conditions [32, 
33]. Surface integrity includes an important component 
called surface topography. It is essential in establishing 
the machined component’s fatigue strength. The surface 
integrity of the component is negatively impacted by a 
number of surface topographic features, including surface 
roughness and machined surface defects [34, 35]. Surface 
roughness is significant element influencing the fatigue 
life of machined components. Various machining meth -
ods lead to micro-level irregularities including tool marks, 
scratches, and notches can cause to high stress and initi-
ate fatigue cracks [36, 37]. In addition, increase in sur -
face roughness can lessen the component’s load carrying 
capacity and leading to reduced fatigue life. However, a 
careful selection of machining parameters and use of cut-
ting tools can decrease the surface roughness and improve 
the fatigue performance [38].
In comparison to surface topography, the main factors 
influencing the fatigue life of the machined component 
are its microstructure and mechanical characteristics [35]. 
Residual stresses are one of the significant surface integrity 
factors influencing the fatigue performance of machined 
components. Various features including phase transforma-
tion, thermal gradient, and plastic deformation perform 
essential role in production of residual stresses during 
machining process. These stresses work as confined stress 
concentrators can dramatically shorten the fatigue life of 
machined components, encourage crack initiation, and has-
ten crack propagation [39– 41]. High compressive residual 
stresses are considered useful for fatigue performance, as 
they counter the applied load, whereas tensile residual stress 
can shorten the fatigue life [40]. Nickel-based superalloys 
possess intricate microstructure, which play a significant 
role in high temperature mechanical characteristics. More-
over, machining can bring variations in the microstructure 
near the machined surfaces, leading to phase changes, grain 
refinement, and development of recrystallized zones [42, 
43]. These microstructural variations may influence the 
fatigue performance of machined components. In addition, 
grain refining can increase the hardness and advance the 
fatigue resistance, whereas phase changes and recrystal-
lization can result in softening and shortened fatigue life. 
Employing the suitable machining techniques and heat 
treatment processes can reduce detrimental microstructural 
changes and increase fatigue resistance [44].

The current review seeks to inform the reader by evaluat-
ing surface integrity, factors that affect the surface integrity

aforementioned, the current assessment is divided into parts

allurgy (plastic deformation, grain orientation, and white layer

Most of the metals including copper, iron, molybdenum, and 
chromium easily alloyed with nickel. The properties of the 
resulting alloys can be reformed with the inclusion of the 
nickel, which can be applied to achieve desirable features 
including better corrosion resistance, increased temperature, 
or the reduced coefficient of thermal expansion. Chemical 
composition is the most common way to classify Ni-based 
superalloys. Regarding the surface integrity aspects, Inconel 
718 continues to be one of the most studied grades of Ni-
based superalloys [46–54]. Some of the Ni-based superalloys 
with their chemical composition of major elements, proper-
ties, and applications are summarized in Table  1 and 2.
Different grades of Ni-based superalloy characteristics 
are normally influenced by varying the ratio of alloying ele-
ments. Numerous elements including chromium, molybde-
num, tungsten, niobium, and tantalum are usually employed 
to strengthen the grain boundaries by precipitating carbides 
along them. However, Al and Cr mainly transmit resistance 
to oxidation. The solution hardening of these superalloys is 
caused by elements including Fe, Cr, Nb, Co, W, Ta, Mo, and 
Al offered in both γ and γ′ forms [23, 72]. Figure 5 provides a 
summary of the function of various alloying elements.
Cutting factors including cutting speed, feed speed, cutting 
speed, depth of cut, and various cutting environments (dry 
machining and wet machining) in the machining of nickel-
based superalloys are the key factors affecting various elements 
of surface integrity. Therefore, increasing the surface quality 
of nickel-based superalloys requires the concerned choice of 
optimum machining parameters, cutting conditions, and cut-
ting tool material. The present review offers the recent findings 
regarding the impact of the cutting factors (cutting parameters, 
cutting environment, and tool material) on machined surface 
integrity of Ni-based superalloys together with surface finish 
(surface defects, surface roughness), surface metallurgy (plastic 
deformation, grain size, and white layer), and mechanical char-
acteristics (work hardening and residual stresses).

3.1 Plastic deformation
During cutting, the processed material is subjected to 
extremely immense heat and mechanical stresses. They 
generate metallurgical changes, phase transformation, and 
residual stresses, which lead to a significant impact on the 
surface texture of the machined surface and beneath the 
surface of the material [6 , 73]. Using the undefined cut-
ting parameters in case of surface integrity may result in 
significant plastic deformation on the machined surface in 
the machining of nickel-based superalloys [73]. In addi-
tion, locked residual stresses which may be compressive or 
tensile stresses caused by non-homogeneous plastic defor -
mation are very common [74]. Numerous pieces of litera-
ture have described that machining time and tool failure is 
directly related to change in plastic deformation in terms 
of cutting parameters [75].
M’Saoubi [76] investigated the surface integrity while 
drilling Ni-based superalloys including Inconel 718, Was-
paloy, RR1000, and Alloy 720Li. However, these grades 
of Ni-based superalloys comprise excellent mechanical 
properties at elevated temperatures; therefore, they are 
more vulnerable to larger plastic deformation thickness 
and greater tensile stresses under high drilling environ-
ments. Kwong et al. [74] drilled Ni-based superalloy CG 
RRR 1000 at a rotation speed of 1061 RPM to 1592 rpm 
and the value of feed rates in the range of 0.08 mm/rev to 
0.1 mm/rev. The metallurgic investigations in the axial 
direction following drilling operations revealed that the 
worn tools caused intense material drag and fractures 
along the direction of hoop, as shown in Fig.  6. In the 
comparison of new tools, the depth of material drag with 
worn tools was 50% greater.

3.2 Grain refinement and orientation
In a machined surface and beneath the surface, the grain size 
and orientation can be easily modified by changes in thermo-
mechanical stresses bringing the cutting environments and 
extremely high tool failure effect into account. Zhou et al. 
[83] parted material’s surface into various zones compris-
ing the refabrication zone, plasticity zone, and total/mass 
material. The progression of cutting tool wear leads to an 
increase in thermomechanical stresses, causing an enhance-
ment in both the recreation zone and the plastic deformity 
zone. Figure  10 depicts the influence of tool failure on the 
microstructure change while employing the new and semi-
aged tools up to VBmax = 0.15 mm and the aged tools up to 
 VBmax = 0.3 mm. In addition, Fig. 10d shows the magnified 
recrystallization area employing used tools, where the grain 
sizes of this region confined between the range of 200 and 
300 nm were found smaller than the grain sizes of 2–20 µm 
in bulk material.
Figure 10 provides an excellent illustration of these zones. 
However, it is worth mind that by employing randomly 
selected cutting conditions, the formation of a deformed 
layer is more obvious. Whereas applying the suitable 
machining conditions may prevent such deformed subsur -
face microstructure. ESBD is the most progressive charac-
terization technique that is most suitable for the examination 
of machining-induced deformation. Figure  11 depicts that 
zone 1 has equally distributed misorientation angles across 
the grains, whereas according to various studies, zone 2 is 
the area of plastic deformation with the largest amplitude, 
elongated grains, and slip bands in the direction of machin-
ing. The mechanical qualities of the material are impacted 
by the expansion of this region [83–85].
M’Saoubi et  al. [86] studied phase transformation, 
machined defects, and mechanical properties while machin-
ing Inconel 718. It was found that a compressed fold of plas-
tic deformation occurs beneath the surface at a distance of 
1 to 1.5 mm. Moreover, the value of average grain sizes 
in distorted layers was found between 30 and 80 nm, and 
a prominent development in grain size was observed as a 
result of tool wear, mainly at high cutting speeds.
In order to improve the optimization of material charac-
teristics, it emphasizes the need for a greater understanding 
of the underlying mechanism, development of advanced 
machining methods, and the combination of computational 
modelling and experimental research for improved selection 
of material properties. Moreover, the newest developments 
in grain refinement techniques and the grain orientation in 
material science are thoroughly covered. Whereas research-
ers and scientists can use it as a valuable source to enhance 
the functioning and performance of material by precisely 
controlling grain size and orientation.
3.3 White layer formation
When subjected to strong thermomechanical stresses, the 
machined surface layer exhibits distinct behaviors from the 
bulk material, which may be seen as white under an opti-
cal microscope and is referred to as the “white layer” [85]. 
The white layer has a rigid and brittle structure due to the 
microstructural alteration and is normally deeper beneath 
the surface and causing the expansion and propagation of 
machined surface cracking [87]. However, before using the 
final product, the white layer must be eliminated from the 
part, as it affects the various surface integrity characteristics 
of the final component [88, 89]. In addition to the white 
layer, it can also occasionally be found in the form of a dark 
layer that can range in thickness from 20 to 40 µm depend-
ing on the processing circumstances and is also known as 
a ductile layer. Furthermore, developing a ductile layer at 
the machined surface affects product reliability and func -
tionality [90].
Various studies suggested white layers were connected 
to grain refining owing to plastic deformation, phase 
change, and chip particle adhesion [87]. Osterle and Li 
[91] discovered that a collection of nanoscales equiaxed 
grains of 50 to 100 nm made up the white layers which 
were connected with material melting and instant quench-
ing. Another study revealed that the formation of white 
layer was significantly influenced by quenching transforma-
tions of the material’s machined surface from an austenitic 
to a martensitic phase [92].
Soo et al. [93] looked at how the machining process 
affected the surface finish of nickel alloy RR1000. The 
findings show that the material acquired by drilling with-
out using cutting fluid has comparatively thick white layers 
on the subsurface as displayed in Fig.  12, where the white 
layer covered more than 90% of the surface and stretched 
a maximum depth of 15 mm in both radial and feed direc-
tions, respectively. Moreover, deformation of microstruc-
ture prolonged up to 10 mm beneath the white layer was 
also detected. Burr size at the entrance and exit of the hole

deformation. Imran et al. [101] have noted this pattern of 
increasing white layer thickness with increasing feed rates.

slip lines, indicating severe plastic deformation. A slip zone 
is created by the buildup of dislocations within the deformed 
grains of the machined surface. High cutting speed of up 
to 100 m/min and low feed rate result in an increase in the 
thickness of this slip zone. Energy dispersive spectroscopy

increasing the feed and Vc resulted in further refining of the

ability to minimize the thickness of the plastic deformation 
zone [114]. Furthermore, distinct observations to investigate 
the impact of different cutting fluids were made. However, 
more research is required for determining the impact of cut-

Ni-based superalloys [120].
The literature review specifies that the selection of cutting 
environment significantly effects the surface metallurgy in 
the machining of nickel-based superalloys. Dry machining 
is simple and environment friendly method but it is typically

cooling is an extensively adopted cutting environment, but

approach, enhances tool life, and provides better surface fin-
ish, while reducing its negative effects on the environment. 
Cryogenic cooling considers to be a promising technique 
for attaining better surface metallurgy, whereas the practical 
implementation and cost effectiveness of cryogenic cooling 
method need more research inquiries.

4.1 Work hardening and microhardness
The machined surface of a material seems to increase defor-
mation resistance and significantly decrease plasticity when 
machining Ni-based superalloys. This process is known as 
strain hardening, which has been linked to the impacts of 
thermomechanical stresses [6]. In addition, the thermophysi-
cal qualities of the workpiece material may cause the soften-
ing effect in small areas of machined surface [122]. Numer-
ous researchers have discovered that Ni-based superalloys 
undergo elevated temperatures and pressure while machin-
ing, producing a work-hardened plane whose stiffness is 
higher as compared to base material [59, 62, 63, 123, 124]. 
The work hardening phenomenon is clearly demonstrated 
by the fact that once the material has been machined, the 
surface is stiffer than the bulk of the material and the micro-
hardness gradually decreases toward the bottom of the mate-
rial [73, 125].
The wear resistance and fatigue life of each product 
mainly depend on the hardness of surface and subsurface of 
the material. Therefore, it always consider to be a significant 
part in surface integrity characteristics [126]. According 
to certain research, the main factors influencing the hard-
ness of the material were grain size and plastic deforma-
tion, whereas the factors affecting the softening phenom -
enon were dynamic recovery, dynamic precipitation, and 
cavitation formation [ 127]. Umbrello [ 59] investigated the 
surface integrity of Inconel 718 under dry machining. The 
results indicated that in comparison to bulk material hard-
ness, surface hardness was higher in each experiment. In 
addition, with growing the feed rate and cutting speed, the 
ratio of HV0.05max to HV0.05initaial was increased. Therefore, 
the higher value of feed rate and cutting speed allowed the 
workpiece to achieve greater hardness difference.
Microhardness, which is frequently measured in micron 
range, is the hardness of a substance at a very small scale. 
The Vickers hardness test, which includes pressing a pyrami-
dal diamond indenter into the surface of the material and 
measuring the size of the resulting indentation, is one exten-
sively used technique for technique for determining micro 
hardness. The applied load and the indentation’s surface 
area are used to calculate the Vickers hardness number 
(HV) [128]. Several factors including the machining pro-
cess, machining parameters, cutting environment, tool geom-
etry, and tool damage all influence the microhardness of the 
machined layer and subsurface. The machined layer exhib-
its a spike in firmness relative to bulk hardness that can be 
attributed to rapid heating and cooling [127]. Ramoni et al.

4.2 Residual stress
The machining procedures produce residual stress, which 
is regarded as a component of surface integrity. Residual 
stresses could offer a possible danger to various features of a 
part including its strength, corrosion wear, fracture initiation 
and propagation, and fatigue failure as shown in Fig. 23 [6]. 
Therefore, residual stresses must be taken into consideration 
during the machining process for a long-lasting, strong, and 
reliable finished product [6, 134].
Tensile residual stresses at the workpiece’s outermost lay-
ers are normally unwanted because they might lead to early 
corrosion and fatigue failures. In contrast, the compressive 
residual stresses at their superficial layers enhance the com-
ponent’s fatigue and corrosion resistance [6].
The mechanism of residual stresses is conceptually 
described in Fig.  24 [ 134]. The difficult procedure of 
mechanical loads and thermal loads during the cutting 
results in heterogeneous plastic deformation, which is the 
prime reason for initiating residual stresses [135– 137]. 
Moreover, phase change is alternative consideration of 
the origin of residual stresses due to mechanical loads and 
thermal gradient because mechanical load led to compres-
sive residual stress whereas thermal load typically causes 
tensile residual stress [123, 138]. The heat produced while 
machining is generated by friction, and plastic deforma-
tion describes the thermal phenomena. However, residual 
stresses can only be created by the heat that influences the 
workpiece [139].

4.5 Effects of cutting tool material
The choice of tool material in the machining process pre-
sents a crucial role in determining the mechanical charac-
teristics, particularly work-hardening and residual stress, 
which have a substantial influence on the machinability 
and the performance of machined components. The coated 
tool employed in the machining of nickel-based superalloys 
leads to a reduction in the thickness of the deformed layer 
and offers an opportunity to prevent work-hardening [100]. 
Mechanical deformation including tool wear and other fac-
tors has an impact on this phenomenon. In comparison to 
the uncoated tool, the coated tool shows excellent tool wear 
resistance. In addition, the coated tool could potentially 
experience more work-hardening than the uncoated tool 
if its thermal conductivity is lower [164]. Zhou et al. [58] 
found during the machining of Inconel 718 that utilizing a 
coated CBN tool resulted in a higher microhardness value as 
compared to an uncoated CBN tool. Madariaga et al. [165] 
analyzed the work-hardened chart while turning of Inconel 
718. It was observed that the depth of the subsurface com-
pressive peak is less than the work-hardened layer meas-
ured. In alternative study, Thakur et al. [166] investigated 
the microhardness during the machining of Inconel 825 by 
employing uncoated and coated carbide tools under various 
Vc. It was described that microhardness was decreased in 
surface and subsurface regions while using coated carbide 
tools, as illustrated in Fig. 33.
Residual stress can be affected by the thermal conductivity 
of a cutting tool. Lower thermal conductivity will make the 
thermal influence more dominant and increase the residual 
stress. It was observed in the machining of Inconel 718 where 
using a mixed alumina ceramic insert resulted in high tensile 
stress in comparison to CBN or PCBN tools [164]. On the other 
hand, a PCBN tool with high thermal conductivity efficiently 
dissipated heat, causing the induction of surface compressive 
stress [138]. Additionally, a coated tool with a larger edge 
radius caused more significant mechanical induced deforma-
tion, resulting in higher deformation depth [56]. Same results 
were found in other study [167], as illustrated in Fig. 34.
Nevertheless, the effect of coatings on residual stress varied; 
in contrast to coated tools, an untreated tool exhibited higher 
surface tensile stress when machining of RR1000 nickel alloy 
[168]. This can be ascribed to alterations in workpiece material, 
tool coating properties, or machining conditions.
This literature review has identified two major issues 
with coated tools: the delamination of the coating owing 
to poor strength and low thermal conductivity leading to 
higher levels of surface flaws, surface finish, and tensile 
stress. However, the development in multifunctional or 
complex nanocomposite coatings has considerably increased 
the performance of coated tools. These advanced coatings 
can mitigate surface roughness and surface flaws by reduc-
ing built-up edge (BUE) and friction between the chip-tool 
and workpiece-tool interface. In addition, they effectively 
resist tool failure, plowing, plastic deformation, and surface 
flaws including grooves, cavities, and lateral flow. Although 
the coating can increase surface roughness with a slight 
increase in edge radius, it can assist reduce harmful surface 
tensile residual stress. By selecting the appropriate coating 
method, it is possible to effectively prevent or reduce the 
development of a white layer. Whereas achieving the desired 
surface integrity, the low thermal conductivity of ceramic 
tools makes them an appropriate selection for the high-speed 
machining of nickel-based superalloys.

5 Conclusions and future work
Surface integrity is the primary determinant of a machined 
component’s surface quality, which ultimately affects its per-
formance during its intended use. A comprehensive analysis 
of the latest advancements in surface integrity of Ni-based 
superalloys reveals several key findings, including identified 
research deficiencies and potential future research paths.
Researchers have observed a close relationship between 
surface roughness and thermal and mechanical deformation 
of the workpiece surface or subsurface. These effects also 
lead to surface irregularities, strain hardening, and residual 
stress. Therefore, exploring the possible connection among 
these factors is of great interest. In the context of cryogenic 
machining, investigators have mainly ascribed the decrease 
in surface roughness to the phenomenon of surface hard-
ening caused by the application of cryogenic liquid. More 
study is needed to determine the effect of surface topography 
such as surface roughness, process constraints, and surface 
qualities on the fatigue strength of nickel-based superalloys.
When machining Ni-based superalloys, surface defects 
including deformed grains, cracking, surface drag, feed 
marks, debris, surface cavities, surface plucking, carbide 
particles, and redeposited materials are commonly observed. 
These defects are often the result of using improper machin-
ing parameters and cutting tool conditions. To mitigate these 
problems, it is recommended to use optimum cutting param-
eters and the most effective cutting fluid during the machin-
ing process.
According to the review, there are three distinct zones that 
are formed during the machining. The initial zone referred 
to as the white layer is mainly affected by heat and contains 
nano-crystalline grains. The second zone is the plastically 
deformed zone, which has deformed grains. The third (final) 
zone is the bulk material, which remains unaffected by any 
deformation. Cutting speed mostly affects the white layer 
thickness, whereas the feed rate has more impact on the 
plastically deformed zone. It is highly advised to carefully 
investigate the cutting constraints, tool characteristics, and 
cutting environments on the formation of white layer.
The findings indicate that when the cutting parameters 
are elevated, there is a consensus that the work hardening 
tendency also increases. However, the specific pattern of dis-
similarity may be altered due to thermal softening at higher 
cutting speed. Establishing a connection between work hard-
ening and residual stress is very crucial. Compressive stress 
induced hardening may be good for the component, whereas 
increased tensile stress on the surface may cause hardening 
and possibly fatigue failure. Further study is needed to fully 
understand the thermal softening process. It is also advised 
employing nano-indentation to mitigate the edge effect and 
confirm the occurrence of thermal softening.
Based on the major observations, it has been found that 
increasing cutting parameters causes the surface residual 
stress transfer from compressive residual stress to tensile 
residual stress. However, there have been few studies with 
different outcomes. Furthermore, increasing the cutting 
speed leads to deeper compressive peak at subsurface 
regions. It is recommended to carefully choose the cutting 
parameters, coating material, and cutting environments to 
enhance the residual stresses. Applying post-machining 
surface treatments such as shot peening can assist a com-
ponent, reach the necessary degree of compressive stress, 
and increase its fatigue strength.
It is frequently observed that applying cutting fluids 
enhances the surface integrity in dry machining by reduc-
ing the thermal effect. However, a comprehensive assess-
ment is required to evaluate the effectiveness of alterna-
tive techniques including high pressure cooling, MQL, and 
cryogenic (LN2 and LCO2) cooling methods, in efficiently 
changing various aspects of surface quality employing 
various cutting techniques.

be enhanced with tool coatings due to development in
