Introduction 
The correlation between microstructure and stress softening during low cycle fatigue tests 
at high temperature (> 950°C) has been the subject of extensive studies for various nickel based 
superalloys (1-6). The situation appears very complex since many parameters, some of which are 
interdependent, affect the development of the mJcrostructure (volume fraction of %~ precipitates, 
temperature, misfit stresses, test orientation, strain rate, frequency, applied load, distance from 
the fracture surface...). The general tendency is to relate cycling softening to coarsening of the 7' 
precipitates and the resulting dissolution of the interfacial dislocation networks. While the 
presence of superlattice stacking faults is well established for some alloys deformed at 
intermediate temperature (7), the situation at high temperature is not so clear. 
In this paper, microstructures resulting from low cycle fatigue tests at imposed stress and 
imposed strain are compared to those resulting from earlier monotonic tensile and compressive 
tests where the formation of superlattice stacking faults, responsible for the deformation, has 
been explained and related to the asymmetry of deformation under tensile and compressive load 
(8, 9). 
Exoerimental Procedure 
Low cycle fatigue tests have been performed at 950°C with a frequency of 0.25 Hz on 
<001> single crystals of an AM1 superalloy (Ni with wt% of 6.5Co, 7.5Cr, 5.3 A1, 5.5W, 8Ta, 2Mo, 
1.2Ti) The 7' precipitates after solution and precipitation heat treatments are cuboidal with a 
volume fraction of about 65% and average size of 450nm. Two different types of test have been 
carried out as follows: 
- imposed stress, five cycles at 940 MPa (R=0) giving a final plastic deformation of 0.6% 
- imposed strain, 200 cycles with a total deformation of 2.3 % and a final plastic deformation of 
1.6%. The stress conditions for the first five and last cycles are given in the following table :

The initial cycle was set at R=0. The stress amplitude remained relatively constant throughout

Slices were cut parallel to {111} planes and thinned down by electropolishing for

Burgers vectors of dislocations both in the matrix (fcc structure) and in the T' precipitates (L12 
structure) are defined within the paper using the FS/RH convention (12) and using the 
Thompson notation (13). The nature of faults (intrinsic superlattice stacking fault (S-ISF) or 
extrinsic superlattice stacking fault (S-ESF)) was determined by analysing the Burgers vector of 
the dislocation bounding the fault or the contrast in bright field of the outermost fringe in 
relation to the sense of the diffracting vector g (14). 
Exnerimental Results 
Fig. 1 shows a typical area observed in the specimen deformed with imposed stress. The 
deformation appears homogeneous with shearing of T' precipitates but no "fatigue bands" are 
observed. The shearing process of the T' precipitates involves mainly superlattice stacking faults 
as shown in Fig. la. The detailed analysis of the observed faults reveals a mixture of S-ISFs and 
S-ESFs. An example of the analysis is given in Fig. lb and lc for a group of three faults situated 
in the T plane (111) and denoted A,B and C. In Fig. lb, the faults A and B have a dark outermost 
fringe while C has a light fringe. The opposite contrast is observed for the reverse g in Fig. lc. 
Hence the faults are of opposite type with A and B determined to be S-ISF and C an S-ESF. 
Fig. 2 exhibits a typical area of the specimen deformed under imposed strain. Several 
points may be noticed : 
- deformation is quite homogeneous. 
- no "fatigue bands" similar to those reported in the literature (2,3) have been observed 
- very few superlattice stacking faults have been observed 
- dislocations are mainly located in the matrix forming reasonably dense networks located at the 
T/y interface as shown at A on Fig. 2 
- dislocations pairs are very seldom observed but an example of shear of a ~/precipitate by two 
pairs of a/2 <110> dislocations is given in Fig. 3 
In conclusion for this specimen, deformation is located in the matrix, precipitates are generally 
not sheared and networks of matrix dislocations are well established in some places. 
Discussion 
Our bright field observations show two different types of microstructure depending upon 
the test conditions. 
Under conditions of imposed stress at 940 MPa, even after only five cycles, there has been 
extensive shearing of T' precipitates producing superlattice stacking faults and the analysis 
reveals a mixture of S-ISF and S-ESF. Such an observation is similar to that reported previously 
for this alloy at the same temperature but under "dynamic" tension conditions (9) : the strain

rate was about 3 x 10 -4 s -1, the imposed stress was 990 MPa and the final plastic deformation 
was 1.4%. The two tests are similar in terms of temperature and maximum applied stress but in 
the present experiment the stress is not applied continuously (varying between 0 and 940 MPa) 
and, quite typically (2), the effective strain rate (about 10 -2 s - 1) is higher than that in the 
tension test. Despite this difference in the strain rate, we may explain the formation of the 
observed population of mixed faults in a similar way to that reported previously (9), where it 
was shown that superfaults were created by nucleation of a Shockley dislocation on the trailing 
partial of a dissociated a/2 <110> matrix dislocation which had entered the precipitate forming 
an antiphase boundary (APB). The nature of the resulting superfault (S-ISF or S-ESF) depends 
on the Burgers vector of the trailing partial i.e. whether or not inversion of the partials occurs 
when entering the precipitate. This last point depends upon the balance between two effects : - 
the energy barrier for crossing the interface by elementary Shockley partials; - the effect of the 
applied stress on the dissociation width of matrix dislocations. In dynamic tension, the mixture 
of faults was related to the fact that, despite the tendency for inversion of the partials to be 
promoted by the sense of the load (giving S-ISF), the high strain rate combined with the high 
applied stress may overcome the energy barrier and allow the first partial to enter the 
precipitate directly without inversion leading to an S-ESF. In the fatigue experiment under 
imposed stress, the stress is varying between zero and tension at 940 MPa which is very close to 
the stress applied during the dynamic tension test and a similar explanation applies. We may 
also assume that, due to the cycling of the load, locally some part of the specimen may 
experience compressive stress but the value of the resolved force on individual partials would be 
relatively low. Even if such internal compressive stresses were of sufficient magnitude to cause 
the generation of faults, they would be of the S-ESF type (9). 
Under conditions of imposed strain after 200 cycles, the microstructure observed is quite 
different. The deformation appears homogeneous with the beginning of the formation of 
dislocation networks and barely any shearing of precipitates (faults or dislocation pairs). If we 
compare the first five cycles of the two fatigue tests presented in this paper, the strain rate, the 
temperature and the deformation axis are similar but two features of the imposed strain test are 
different: 
- the maximum applied stress decreases from 975 MPa to 910 MPa while it is 
constant at 940 MPa for the imposed stress test. 
- the specimen goes into compression from the first cycle. 
The creation of faults requires the presence of matrix dislocations inside the precipitates and the 
nucleation of a Shockley dislocation. Even though the load is decreasing in the imposed strain 
test, the applied value is still sufficient to nucleate the Shockley partial (faults are created at 
intermediate temperature and at a lower stress (8)). The fact that the specimen goes into 
compression as from the very first cycle under imposed strain appears to be a significant factor 
in determining the microstructure developed in the specimen. Network formation in the matrix 
may be promoted by the movements of dislocations in the matrix during load reversal. Such 
networks locked at the T/7' interface prevent dislocations entering precipitates and that may 
explain why almost no shearing of precipitates occurs during the imposed strain test up to 200 
cycles and may also be responsible for the absence of softening. The absence of softening also 
suggests that coarsening of the ~ precipitates has not been significant during the 13mn 20s 
period of the test. 
Summary and Conclusions 
Despite the similarity in stress rate and maximum applied stress of the two fatigue tests 
of the AM1 single crystals specimens reported here, they have produced significantly different 
deformation microstructures.

In the five cycle imposed stress test the presence of a mixed population of S-ISF and S- 
ESF's is readily explained in relationship to previous dynamic tension tests and the cycling of 
the load from 0 to 940 MPa appears to have had little consequence as both S-ISFs and S-ESFs 
were observed after the dynamic tension test (9). 
The first few cycles of the imposed strain test are very similar to the imposed stress test in 
terms of maximum load, strain rate and temperature but no faults were observed after 200 
cycles. It is considered unlikely that this difference results from the difference in the number of 
fatigue cycles (5 and 200) i.e. faults being produced in the early cycles and disappearing during 
late cycles. Hence, these results suggest two things : 1) that the movement of dislocations under 
the reversal of stress which occurs even during the first cycle is very effective in forming 
networks which prevent dislocations entering the "~ precipitates and 2) that, at 950 MPa and 
950°C, a time at stress equivalent to a few cycles is required for the matrix dislocations to enter 
the ~/ precipitates, promoting the nucleation of Shockley dislocations and leading to the 
formation of faults. Otherwise faults should have been produced during the first cycle of the 
imposed strain test at the stress applied is 975 MPa.
