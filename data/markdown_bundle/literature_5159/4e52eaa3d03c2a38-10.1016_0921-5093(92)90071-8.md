Abstract 
The three-phase field incorporating the fl (NiAI), fl' (Ni2AITi) and y' {Ni~AI) phases in the Ni-AI-Ti system was 
accurately determined. A number of alloys within that field, with or without boron, chromium or iron additions, were 
compression-tested for plastic deformability and for strength at room temperature and up to 8(1(I °C. It was tk)und that the 
presence of a substantial fraction of the relatively ductile y' phase in semicoherent epitaxy with fl and fl' conferred several 
per cent of compressive plasticity on these alloys. The strength of the best alloys remained impressively high up to about 
650 °C. Results of a TEM investigation of the behaviour of dislocations at room temperature in the fl and fl' phases of 
some of the three-phase alloys are outlined, and the process of "dislocation injection" from a more ductile into a less 
ductile phase is proposed as a general phenomenon. 
l. Introduction 
In 1976, it was reported that the ordered Heusler 
phase, fl'-Ni2A1Ti (Strukturbericht, L2t) has an excep- 
tionally high creep resistance, and that polycrystalline 
two-phase structures consisting of fl' and fl (B2-NiAI) 
in parallel orientation (a b.c.c, analogue, so to speak, of 
a conventional y'/~s superalloy) had even better creep 
resistance than either fl' or fl separately [1, 2]. How- 
ever, this striking discovery was not followed up 
extensively because of the catastrophic room tempera- 
ture brittleness of the fl'/fl alloys. (One group [3] 
claimed to have made polycrystalline single-phase 
Ni,AITi + B plastically deformable in compression, but 
this has not been reproduced.) An attempt to enhance 
the ductility of fl/fl' by grain refinement or by TiB~ 
dispersion [4] was not successful. Recently, efforts have 
also begun to strengthen fl (NiAI) by a dispersion of 
various NiA1X (Laves) phases [5]; these materials are 
also brittle. 
Since a combination of fi with fl' does not at all 
enhance the ductility of the intrinsically brittle fl poly- 
crystal, other combinations have been assiduously 
explored. In particular, fl+ ~' combinations (where 
y' =Ni3Al ) have been examined by several investiga- 
tors, using various processing methods: (1) An early 
(1972) study by Russell and Edington [6] established 
that "rod-like" precipitates of y' in fl in a Ni-36at.%Al 
alloy greatly enhanced tensile ductility and impact 
resistance at room temperature; microscopy in 
that investigation was at the optical level only. 
(2) Moskovic, in 1978 [7], again found ductility in a 
Ni-30.3at.%Al, 6.6at.%Cr alloy containing 7' precipi- 
tates. In both these studies, it was suggested that the 7' 
precipitates hinder crack nucleation and propagation. 
(3) Khadkikar et al. [8] prepared a fl/y' alloy by hot 
extrusion and found some signs of tensile ductility. It 
was found to be vital to prevent the formation of a 
martensitic intermediate phase, Ni~AI~ [9~, because of 
its extreme brittleness. (4) Directional solidification of 
a NiToAl.~ alloy [10] produced single crystals of fl 
containing aligned 7' fibres and this material had 8% 
tensile ductility at room temperature (a polycrystalline 
material with this structure would, of course, be much 
less ductile). The same group [10, 111 also established 
the beneficial effect of certain kinds of surface coatings 
in enhancing ductility. 
These results suggest that there is scope for ductility 
not only a single-phase fl alloy, but even, in principle, a 
two-phase epitaxial fl/fl' "superalloy", without neces- 
sarily weakening the alloy drastically, by precipitating 
from the microstructure a strong but intrinsically more 
ductile ordered constituent, preferably g'. This is a 
radically different strategy from another which is at 
present in vogue, i.e. the introduction of a soft, dis- 
ordered, ductile phase (typically, a pure metal) into a 
brittle intermetallic, enhancing the ductility [12]; in at 
least one recorded instance of that approach, that of 
iron precipitated from fl-Ni(A1,Fe), the creep resis- 
tance as well as the ductility is raised [13]. Several 
studies (e.g. refs. 14 and 15) have exploited the ducti- 
lizing effect of disordered (f.c.c.) y phase in a matrix of

viability of our strategy of attempting to use an ordered 
second phase as an agent of ductility. 
The research of which an outline account is 
presented here was specifically aimed at exploiting the 
remarkable creep resistance of the rift' combination in 
a Ni-AI-Ti alloy, while hoping to overcome the brittle- 
ness by having 7' present as a third phase in equi- 
librium. We preferred to use the rift' combination as a 
basis, rather than a fl/NiAIX combination, since the 
creep performance of that second category of alloys is 
not sufficiently well established; however, later, it 
would be worthwhile to examine that family as well in 
conjunction with 7' phase. At the time the research was 
begun, in early 1989, it was not clear whether ductiliza- 
tion by 7', if feasible at all, would depend sensitively on 
the intrinsic ductility of the 7' component; accordingly, 
it was decided to include some examination of quater- 
nary Ni-AI-Ti-Fe and Ni-A1-Ti-Cr alloys, since 
chromium and, especially, iron in solution are known 
to be ductilizers of 7' (probably by reducing the order- 
ing energy of that phase). Since the shape and extent of 
the three-phase fl + fl' + 7' field in the Ni-A1-Ti system 
has hitherto been uncertain, widely different forms 
having been reported in the literature [17-20], a re- 
determination of that field at 900 °C was a crucial part 
of the project, and we describe this first. A temperature 
of 900 °C was selected since equilibration times were 
judged to be too long at lower temperatures; thus 
900 °C is a suitable temperature for equilibrating alloys 
for service at lower temperatures. 
2. Three-phase field 
Three alloys were made up, homogenized and 
equilibrated at 900°C as described in Section 3; 
their compositions were ones thought likely to be in the 
three-phase field at 900 °C. Analytical electron micros- 
copy, with proper corrections for absorption (which is 
crucial for aluminides involving heavy elements such as 
nickel and iron), was used to determine the compo- 
sitions of the r, fl' and 7' phases, and the procedure 
was calibrated by analysing a well homogenized 
Ni-26at.%Al specimen. As it turned out, one of the 
three alloys (C, D and G in Fig. 1) was close to an edge 
of the three-phase triangle. Ideally, of course, the corre- 
sponding phases of all alloys lying within the three- 
phase field should have the same compositions and this 
was indeed found to be approximately so. 
Figure 1 shows the three-phase field as determined 
in our work; the circles represent our estimate of the 
degree of uncertainty, taking results from the three 
alloys in combination. The boundaries of the fl, fl' and 
7' fields as shown in Fig. 1 are consistent with the 
literature [19], but taking for the 7' single-phase loop 
results from a recent study by Willemin and Durand- 
Charre [21]. The triangle in Fig. 1 is quite different 
from the triangles previously estimated, on the basis of 
inadequate data. 
In this study we joined forces with N. Saunders of 
Surrey University, who developed a CALPHAD 
model for this three-phase field. By fitting one thermo- 
dynamic parameter, he was able to secure excellent 
agreement between computed and experimental 
versions of the three-phase triangle, and then he was 
able to use the model thus optimized to calculate 
freezing temperatures and solidification sequences. An 
example of such a computed sequence, for an alloy of 
the useful composition Ni60A12sTil2 (C in Fig. 1), is 
shown in Fig. 2. For all the alloys of interest in our 
study, solidification was calculated to be complete in 
the range 1290-1330 °C. 
Full details of both the experimental and the 
theoretical aspects of this work are being published 
elsewhere [22].

5. Mechanical properties 
A substantial number of ternary and quaternary 
alloys, most without boron but some boron-doped, 
have been compression-tested at room temperature, 
about 600 °C (there are some doubts about the exact 
temperature with some of the 600 °C tests) and 800 °C, 
and a few were also tested at intermediate tempera- 
tures. We discuss first our findings concerning room 
temperature deformability, since this was the key 
aspect of the whole enterprise, and then the tempera- 
ture variation of flow stress. 
5.1. Plastic deformability at ambient temperature 
The compositions of the ternary base alloys which 
were tested are shown in Fig. 1. 
Alloys F and J, respectively close to the fl' corner 
and close to the 50/50 fl/fl' composition, were both so 
brittle that it was impossible to machine compression 
test specimens. Some tests were also done on fl/fl' 
specimens, in both as-cast and extruded conditions, 
obtained from elsewhere, whose compositions lay on 
the NiAI-NiTi join and which contained roughly equal 
proportions of the two phases: these could be 
machined but showed essentially zero room-tempera- 
ture deformability, in either condition. As expected 
therefore, in the absence of y' phase, no room tempera- 
ture deformability even in compression was attainable. 
Alloys B, E and G all lie close to the fl/y' edge of the 
three-phase triangle. Compressive deformabilities at 
room temperature of 11%,, 6°/,, and 9.5% were 
obtained for these alloys respectively. This thus con- 
firmed the findings of Russell and Edington and of 
Moskovic in the 1970s as to the ductilizing effect of y' 
precipitates (though their alloys contained no titanium). 
Finally, alloys C, D and H (IM), and 1 and 2 (PM), 
lay within the three-phase triangle. Their compressive 
room temperature deformabilities are 9°/,,, 3.5% and 
13% respectively for the IM alloys, and 9.5% and 11% 
respectively for the PM alloys. It should be noted that 
the three IM alloys have matrices consisting of fl, fl' 
and Y' respectively. (It should be added that alloys C 
and H have hitherto only been made up with boron- 
doping; hardly any systematic comparison of the same 
alloys with and without boron has been done; however, 
such as it is, such comparison suggests a slight, possibly 
not significant, enhancement of ductility by B.) Alloys 
1 and 2 both have fl matrices. The results for these five 
alloys indicate, as expected, that y' was the matrix that 
offered the best deformability, followed quite closely 
by fl, with poor deformability for a fl' matrix even when 
that was leavened by a large volume fraction of y' pre- 
cipitate. 
Iron additions had no detectable effect on deform- 
ability of the alloys in question, while chromium 
additions mostly reduced deformability, though alloy E 
became more deformable with a chromium addition.

Figure 12 shows a series of photographs, obtained by 
means of the straining stage, of a pair of linked disloca-

small fl' particle 125]. The dislocations are pure screw, 
with a (111) Burgers vector, which is not the normal 
Burgers vector that has been observed in this phase; 
normally (110) dislocations predominate. (Each partial 
dislocation has a Burgers vector equal to [111 ] of the 
small (/3) unit cell; an undissociated (111) dislocation in 
the giant unit cell of the Heusler phase would have a 
Burgers vector of 2[ 111] referred to the small unit cell.) 
Such dislocations move slowly, in a jerky manner, while 
many of their neighbours remain pinned. We do not 
know what distinguishes mobile from immobile dis- 
locations of the same type. Mobile dislocations were 
also seen to stream from a crack tip in the fl' phase [25] 
and this has clear implications for the transition from 
brittle to ductile behaviour in this phase. 
The other clear result of these studies is that the 
semicoherent interface between y' and/3 (we have not 
yet got round to examining y'/fl' interfaces) is a source 
of glissile dislocations. Figure 13 shows dislocations 
streaming into the/3 phase from such interfaces, the 7" 
phase on the other side already being rather full of 
dislocations. Figure 14 shows an entire 7" lath, at some- 
what lower magnification, with dislocations emerging 
from the interface and spreading through both the /3 
phase and the fl' particles embedded in it. Many of the 
dislocations in these pictures were found to have (111)

have in the past been expected to be less glissile in the 
B2 superlattice than the more usual (100) dislocations. 
In Fig. 8, above, we saw that the semicoherent interface 
itself in a lightly deformed sample contains extrinsic

7. Conclusions 
We can draw two kinds of conclusion from this 
research, one specific to this family of alloys, the other 
of a general nature. 
7.1. Specific conclusions 
(1) Camballoys are rendered plastically deformable 
at room temperature, at least in compression, to a 
modest degree, by the presence of semicoherent y' 
phase in epitaxy with the matrix. That ductilization is 
much more pronounced for a/3 matrix than for a /3'

8. Prognosis 
The interesting mechanical properties of the Camb- 
alloys, coupled with their modest density (in the range 
6.6-6.9 gcm ') suggest that, after suitable develop- 
ment of quaternary alloying and processing, they may 
possibly have some future role in aero-engines.
