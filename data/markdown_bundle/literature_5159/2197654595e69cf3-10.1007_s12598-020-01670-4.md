Creep prediction model for nickel-based single-crystal superalloys
considering precipitation of TCP phase
Xiao-Yan Wang, Jia-Jia Wang, Cheng-Jiang Zhang, Wen-Wei Tong,
Bo Jiang, Guang-Xian Lu, Zhi-Xun Wen* , Tao Feng*
Received: 8 March 2020 / Revised: 26 May 2020 / Accepted: 26 November 2020
/C211Youke Publishing Co., Ltd. 2021
Abstract The microstructure of nickel-based single-crys-
tal (SC) superalloys has a pivotal inﬂuence on their creep
properties. The addition of the Re element not only
enhances the long-term creep properties of nickel-based SC
superalloys, but also results in the formation of a topo-
logically close-packed (TCP) phase which is a harmful and
brittle hard phase. Here, high-temperature creep interrup-
tion tests of a nickel-based SC superalloy that contains
4.8 wt% Re were performed under various temperatures
and stress conditions, and the evolution of microstructure
during creep was observed by scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM). The volume fraction of the TCP phase was
also extracted to explore the mechanism that controls the
impacts of the TCP phase on the creep properties.
According to the microstructure evolution mechanism, the
inﬂuence of the TCP phase was attributed to the initial
damage and critical shear stress of the material. A creep
performance prediction model for nickel-based SC super-
alloys considering the precipitation of the TCP phase that is
based on the crystal plasticity theory and a modiﬁed creep
damage model was established. The simulation curves ﬁt
well with the experimental results and the errors between
prediction creep life with test results are within 5%.
Keywords TCP phase; Nickel-based single-crystal
superalloys; Microstructure evolution; Creep performance
prediction; Crystal plasticity theory
1 Introduction
Nickel-based single-crystal (SC) superalloys are currently
the preferred materials for turbine blades due to their high
strength and good structural stability at high temperatures
[1–3]. Turbine blades need to operate under extreme
environments, which poses a huge challenge for the per-
formance of SC materials [ 4, 5]. The addition of the kernel
element ‘ ‘Re’ ’ profoundly improves the mechanical prop-
erties of SC superalloys, resulting in the so-named ‘ ‘Re
effect’ ’ [6–8]. However, due to the low diffusion coefﬁcient
of Re, it segregates and causes precipitation of the needle-
like topologically close-packed (TCP) phase that is rich in
Re during long-term creep at high temperatures [ 9–11].
The TCP phase appears as a hard and brittle inclusion, for
which precipitation leads to a signiﬁcant decrease in creep
life [12, 13]. Studying the inﬂuence mechanism of the TCP
phase on creep properties and establishing a life prediction
model that considers the TCP phase have important theo-
retical and engineering implications regarding the devel-
opment of improved aerospace materials.
In-depth studies on the evolution of the TCP phase
during creep and its inﬂuence on creep performance have
been conducted [ 14–16]. Experiments show a strong
mutual coupling relationship between the quantity of the
TCP phase and the test states [ 17, 18]. The TCP phase is

In this study, a variety of creep tests under different
temperatures and stress conditions were carried out with a
nickel-based SC superalloys that contains 4.8 wt% Re, and
the morphology evolution of the TCP phase during creep
was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to
reveal the inﬂuence mechanism of the TCP phase on the
microstructure evolution and macroscopic creep durability
of nickel-based SC superalloys. Importantly, the volume
fraction of the TCP phase as a characteristic parameter that
indicates material degradation was extracted, and a creep
life model of nickel-based SC superalloys that considered
the inﬂuence of the TCP phase was established based on
the crystal plasticity theory and the modiﬁed creep damage
model.

3.2 Effects of TCP phase on creep properties of nickel-
based single-crystal superalloys
The creep test results are recorded in Table 2 and the creep
curves under different temperatures and stresses are plotted
in Fig. 7 to study the effects of the TCP phase on the creep
properties of the nickel-based single-crystal superalloys.
The test results indicate that the creep curves are
exponential and that there is no marked initial creep stage,
but only a steady creep and accelerated creep stage. As the
test stress increased, the time in the steady creep stage
decreased and the velocity increased. For example, at
980 /C176C, the stress increased from 200 to 250 MPa, and the
creep rate increased by 9.1%. However, when the stress
was increased from 250 to 300 MPa, the creep rate
increased by 37.64%. After the steady creep stage, the
specimen subjected to a greater stress entered the accel-
erated creep stage at a higher creep rate until fracture
failure occurred. Under the same test stress, the increase in
temperature causes the creep performance to drop sharply.
Under the same stress condition at 200 MPa, the creep life
at 980 /C176C was 55.6 times that at 1100 /C176C, and the rapid
growth of the TCP phase at high temperatures is one of the
reasons for this. Combining the volume fraction evolution
of the TCP phase with the creep curve, it can be seen that
the TCP phase grows rapidly during the steady creep stage,
and then the growth rate decreases as the creep process
progresses.
The qualitative energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)
concentration proﬁles of the chemical elements in the c, c
0
and TCP phases and the main element distributions in the
nickel-based SC superalloys at 1050 /C176C and 200 MPa are
shown in Fig. 8. The c phase is mainly composed of Co,
Cr, W and Re, and most of the Al, Ta, and Ni are dis-
tributed in the c0 phase, in which the contents of Ni and Ta
are relatively small. Compared with the second-generation
nickel-based SC superalloy, the increase in the Re element
content in the nickel-based SC superalloy reduces the
segregation free energy of the superalloy, which causes the
TCP phase to precipitate, grow, and form an irregular two-
phase interface during high-temperature creep. The pre-
cipitation of the TCP phase causes the consumption of the
main solid solution strengthening elements Re and W in the
c phase, weakens the solid solution strengthening effect of
the two phases, and causes the dissolution of the adjacent c
matrices, which deteriorates the long-term performance of
the nickel-based SC superalloys during high-temperature
creep. The contents of Cr and Co at the interface of c and c
0
phases increased slightly, indicating that Cr and Co atoms
may be related to interface segregation [ 19].
The lateral cracks and the surrounding microstructure of
the creep failure specimens are shown in Fig. 9. A number
of micro-cracks perpendicular to the direction of the stress
can be distinctly observed. These micro-cracks often
nucleate from the internal inclusions or microstructure
defects of the specimens under the action of creep stress,
then further expand and join to form macroscopic cracks,
which ultimately lead to the fracture of the specimens. The
c/c
0 two-phase microstructure near the micro-cracks
appears as a ‘ ‘wave-like’ ’ structure, and a topological
inversion phenomenon can be observed. Figure 9c
demonstrates the crack propagation during pre-fracture,
which is not a simple tear but a crack that extends with a
roughly zigzag shape. However, the subsequent crack
unsteadily extends as a simple tear on both sides of the
crack tip. Figure 9d[ 13] shows that the creep crack
nucleates from the TCP phase, and after the crack nucle-
ates, its propagation path coincides with the growth
direction of the TCP phase. The TCP phase has a topo-
logical close-packed structure, causing it to have a very
high hardness, coordination number, and space utilization,
which hinders dislocation slip, forming stress concentration
and leading to creep crack nucleation and propagation.

damage model
The nickel-based SC superalloy is composed of a c
0 pre-
cipitated (reinforced) phase and a c matrix phase, wherein
the c0 phase accounts for * 65% by volume and is uni-
formly distributed as cubic particles in the matrix c phase.

divided into three stages. In the ﬁrst stage, dislocations
enter the c channel and begin to propagate, and the soft c
phase undergoes viscoplastic ﬂow around the hard c
0 phase.
An increase in the dislocation density leads to a reverse
stress that hinders further dislocation slip. At this stage, the
creep strain rate decreases and the material hardens. In the

4.2 Creep prediction model considering TCP phase
In a high-temperature environment, the TCP phase is
highly susceptible to precipitation in nickel-based SC
superalloys during creep. The TCP phase consumes two-
phase solid solution strengthening elements and breaks the
inerratic c/c
0 microstructure; it is a hard-brittle phase that
can originate cracks and act as an expansion channel, all of
which have considerable effects on the creep behaviors of
nickel-based SC superalloys. The traditional creep consti-
tutive and damage models do not consider the inﬂuence of
the TCP phase; thus, they do not accurately predict the
creep performances of turbine blade materials working in
high-temperature environments. In this study, based on the
mechanism whereby the TCP phase inﬂuences the creep
performance, a creep performance prediction model con-
sidering the TCP phase is established.
The creep damage of nickel-based SC superalloys is
dominated by the resolved stress and resolved strain rate,
and the inﬂuences of the TCP phase on these two aspects
should be considered comprehensively in models [ 41].

5 Conclusion

brittle TCP phase precipitates along the [110] orientation
on the {111} crystal plane in the dendritic stem region, cuts
the c0 strengthening phase, and destroys the regular raft-
like structure, which causes material damage. In the late
stage of creep, the TCP phase dissolves and spheroidizes.
The TCP phase is rich in matrix phase forming elements,
which weakens the solid solution strengthening effect and
causes the dissolution of adjacent matrix phases. After the
