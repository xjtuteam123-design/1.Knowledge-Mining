Molecular dynamics simulations are performed to study mechanical characteristics and homogeneous 
plastic inception of CoCrCuFeNi high-entropy alloy at various temperatures under uniaxial tension. It 
is found that the elastic modulus and ultimate tensile strength increase with temperature decreasing. 
A notable softening effect is observed at the elastic deformation stage caused by the decrease of the 
interatomic force gradient. Extrinsic stacking faults and deformation twins are extensively observed, 
which are formed via intrinsic stacking faults overlap.

1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are metallic materials that can crys-
tallize as a single phase with substantially high entropy because 
of mixing equally or near equally of ﬁve or more principal ele-
ments. HEAs have triggered intensive research activities in mate-
rials science and engineering due to their distinctive mechanical 
properties such as high fracture resistance, tensile strength, and 
ductility at cryogenic and/or elevated temperatures. In 2012, Zhang 
et al. found that, when the temperature decreased from 298 to 
77K, the yield strength and fracture strength of the AlCoCrFeNi 
HEA increased by 29.7% and 19.9%, respectively, at the cost of triv-
ial ductility decrease [1]. In 2013 and 2014, Gali et al., Otto et 
al., and Gludovatz et al. proved that the ductility of the CoCr-
FeMnNi HEA did not vary inversely with yield strength. On the 
contrary, its yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elonga-
tion to fracture increased with decreasing temperature [2–4]. In 
2011, Senkov et al. reported that the VNbMoTaW HEA had a yield 
strength of 1246 MPa and a ductility of 1.5% at room temperature. 
Over the temperature range of 600 to 1000 ◦ C, while the mate-
rial can maintain a high yield strength ranging from 842 to 862 
MPa, signiﬁcantly outperforming conventional superalloys such as 
Inconel 718 [5,6]. In 2015, Seiﬁ et al. reported that the toughness 
of the AlCrFeNi2Cu HEA at 200 ◦C was higher than that of the ma-
terial at room temperature [7]. The outstanding characteristics of

HEAs at cryogenic and/or elevated temperatures qualify themselves 
as potential candidates to meet demanding requirements for ap-
plications in extreme environments, such as the low-temperature 
superconducting, nuclear, and aerospace industries.
Most of the investigations on the mechanical properties and 
microstructure of HEAs are carried out based on experiments. 
As a powerful complement of experiment, molecular dynamics 
(MD) simulation can provide a deep insight into the atomic struc-
ture dynamically, which is challenging to be captured experi-
mentally. In 2016, Meraj and Pal reported a MD study of Ni-
WCuFeMo HEA under tensile and compressive strains [8]. In 2017, 
Sharma and Balasubramanian reported a MD study of Al0.1 CoCr-
FeNi HEA under tensile loading using a hybrid embedded atomic 
method (EAM)-Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential to describe the in-
teratomic interactions of the alloying elements [9]. In 2016 and 
2019, Li et al. reported deformation behaviors of AlCrFeCuNi and 
Al0.25CoFeNiCu0.75 HEAs under tensile and compressive strain us-
ing MD simulation [10]. To date, only a few of MD studies have 
been reported on HEAs due to the absence of potentials.
To study the mechanical properties of HEAs under tension, a 
freshly developed EAM potential is employed to mimic the in-
teratomic interactions of a CoCrCuFeNi HEA. The potential was 
developed to resemble face-centered cubic (FCC) near-equimolar 
mixtures of Co-Cr-Cu-Fe-Ni [11]. The tensile responses and yield-
ing behaviors of the CoCrCuFeNi HEA in the temperature range 
from 77 to 500 K under the strain rate of 1×108 are stud-
ied.

2. Methods
The quinary monocrystalline CoCrCuFeNi HEA is equimolar with 
20400 atoms for each element, constituting a total of 102000 
atoms. The size of the workpiece is 71×75×214 Å3 . The three ori-
entations of the workpiece are in x-<001>, y-<110>, and z-<110>. 
Periodic boundary conditions are imposed in all directions. Three 
models are constructed by randomly position different alloying 
atoms at FCC lattice sites. Fig. 1 shows one of the models with 
a random seed, which is denoted by Seed01. The other two mod-
els with different seeds are denoted by Seed02 and Seed03, which 
can be found in the supplementary material. The interatomic in-
teractions are described using the EAM potential [11]. The ﬁdelity 
of MD simulations strongly depends on the accuracy of the inter-
atomic potential that applied to describe interactions. The potential 
provided by [11] has been proved that, with different random dis-
tribution of the ﬁve elements, it results in an equilibrium phase 
with FCC structure at 1 and 300K. In the present study, the poten-
tial is also tested at temperatures of 77, 200, 300, 400, and 500 K 
with the different distributions. The results conﬁrm that the poten-
tial can stabilize the CoCrCuFeNi HEA with an FCC phase at these 
temperatures. To further examine the potential, a quench simula-
tion from liquid state to solid state on Seed01 is performed. To 
realize a complete melting, the model is initialized at 3000 K un-
der an isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 0 MPa pressure for 
100 ps. Then the model is quenched to 77 K at a cooling rate of 
0.49 K/ps with a time step of 1 fs. The common neighbor analysis 
(CNA) of the quenched structure is shown in Fig. 2a. It is observed 
that 69.9% atoms are arranged in FCC structure, indicating that FCC 
is the major crystalline phase of the CoCrCuFeNi HEA after quench-
ing. The radial pair distribution function (RDF) of the quenched 
structure is shown in Fig. 2b. The pattern represents a typical FCC 
phase dominated crystalline structure. There are 17% atoms ar-
ranged in hexagonal close packed (HCP) structure and 13.1% atoms 
in amorphous. This is because the cooling rate in MD simulation is

To perform uniaxial tensile simulations, the energy minimiza-
tion using the conjugate gradient algorithm is carried out with 
energy tolerance of 10−12 and force tolerance of 10−12 on Seed01. 
Then the model is equilibrated using NPT ensemble at a pressure 
of 0 bar and a temperature of 77, 200, 300, 400, or 500 K. After 
equilibrium, the model is deformed in uniaxial tension (z direc-
tion) at a constant strain rate of 1×108 with a stress-free condition 
in the other two (x and y) directions under the NPT ensemble. The 
same process is also performed on Seed02 and Seed03. For refer-
ence, tensions of a pure Cu model under the same conditions with 
the same EAM potential are performed.
All simulations are carried out using the Large-scale Atomic/
Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) [12]. Visualiza-
tion and statistical analysis of the structure are performed using 
OVITO software [13]. RDF, CNA and Dislocation Extraction Algo-
rithm (DXA) are employed to identify lattice structure and distor-
tion [14–16].

The yield stress required for homogeneous dislocation nucle-
ation is deﬁned as UTS. At higher temperature, the lattice has 
higher entropy, which leads to the higher vibration amplitude of 
atoms as compared with that of the lattice at lower temperature. 
Therefore, bond breakage can readily occur at a higher tempera-
ture, resulting in a lower UTS. The elastic modulus is the slope 
of the linear portion of a stress-strain curve. Herein, the elastic 
modulus of all models are calculated using the data of the linear 
regimes (ε < 0.02) in terms of linear regression. Results show that 
the elastic modulus decreases with the temperature increases. This 
is because, with the temperature increasing, the atomic thermal 
motion becomes more intensive and thus the bonding energy be-
tween atoms becomes lower. Hence, the lattice of the HEA is more 
easily to deform with temperature increasing, leading to the de-
crease of the elastic modulus.
The nonlinear elastic response (0.02<ε<yield strain) of the 
stress to the tensile strain is known as elastic softening, which is 
originated from the decrease of the interatomic force gradient un-
der tension [17]. Atoms of a lattice have lowest energies when they 
are located at equilibrium positions and the ﬁrst neighbor spacing 
of two atoms can be deﬁned as equilibrium interatomic spacing 
(EIS). Applying tensile strain on the lattice inevitably increase the 
interatomic spacing as well as the cohesive energy, leading to at-
traction between the atoms and that is the origin of the stress. 
Using MD simulation, we calculate the variation of the cohesive 
energy per atom of the HEA as a function of interatomic spacing 
as shown in Fig. 4a. The EIS obtained is 3.553 Å as indicated by 
the dash lines in Fig. 4, which is very close to the lattice param-
eter obtained through equilibration at 1 K in [11]. The interaction 
spacing could be higher than EIS, meaning the HEA crystal is in 
tension. The ﬁrst derivative of the cohesive energy to the inter-
atomic spacing is the interatomic force as shown in Fig. 4b. The 
second derivative of the cohesive energy is the interatomic force 
gradient as shown in Fig. 4c. The force gradient decreases under 
tensile strain, which implies that the stress increasing rate de-
creases with the tensile strain, explaining the elastic softening of 
the CoCrCuFeNi HEA under tensile strain.
The temperature dependences of mechanical characteristics of 
the CoCrCuFeNi HEA revealed by MD simulations are very similar 
to those of traditional BCC and FCC metals [18,19]. However, the 
absolute values of the elastic modulus and UTS are higher than 
those of experimental values from the previous study on CoCr-
CuFeNi HEA[20]. This is because a perfect single crystal structure is 
assumed and a higher strain rate is employed in MD simulations.

In the present study, twin structure with at least four atomic lay-

be seen that ESF and DT are all evolved from ISF.
Fig. 6 reveals the formation mechanisms of ISF, ESF, and DT. For 
example, the ISF in Fig. 6a is formed by the emission of a leading 
1/6[211] Shockley partial dislocation from the interior of the lat-
tice. The ISF transforms the sequence ABCABC of the FCC stacking 
to an HCP stacking in such a way that a B layer following an A
layer becomes a C layer. The ESF is formed by the emission of an-
other 1/6[211] partial dislocation on the parallel slip plane. Hence, 
the ABCAC sequence becomes an ABCBA stacking, describing a twin 
structure. In Fig. 6b, after the formation of an ESF through the 
sliding of a leading 1/6[112] partial dislocation, another 1/6[112] 
partial dislocation occurs on the neighboring plane of the preex-
isting ESF, leading to the formation of a DT. Therefore, for the 
defect-free CoCrCuFeNi HEA, twins are homogeneously nucleated 
in the lattice by successive emissions of Shockley partial disloca-
tions on adjacent (111) planes.

ing mechanism of the CoCrCuFeNi HEA, generalized SFEs (GSFEs) 
are calculated on three MD models with different random seeds, 
which are denoted by SFE01, SFE02, and SFE03 as shown in Fig. 7.

which is higher than that of pure Ni, Cu, and other common FCC 
metals [21], indicating a very high twinning tendency of the CoCr-
CuFeNi HEA.
4. Conclusions
MD simulations are employed to investigate homogeneous 
yielding behaviors of a monocrystalline CoCrCuFeNi HEA under 
uniaxial tension. By sampling different temperatures and atoms 
distributions, these simulations examine effects of temperature on 
mechanical characteristics and homogeneous yielding mechanisms 
of the CoCrCuFeNi HEA. The ultimate elastic modulus and tensile 
strength increase with temperature decreasing because the higher 
atomic thermal vibration decreases the bonding energy at higher 
temperatures. Extensive ESFs and DTs are formed via ISFs overlap. 
ESFs and DTs tend to occur in the CoCrCuFeNi HEA, indicating high 
tendency of a mechanical twinning mediated plastic deformation 
due to the very high twinnability.
