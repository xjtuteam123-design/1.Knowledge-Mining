abstract
To address the challenges associated with preparing thin strips of difﬁcult-to-deform ma-
terials e such as low processing efﬁciency and yield, poor shape, and subpar performance e
our research team developed an on-line warm rolling experimental machine featuring
hydraulic tension. We used the Ti e6Ale4V alloy as our experimental material and con-
ducted warm rolling experiments at various temperatures, thoroughly examining the
intricate relationship between microstructure evolution and mechanical properties. Our
ﬁndings indicate that warm rolling causes a clear reﬁnement of the a phase and b phase
microstructures, while intensifying dislocation entanglement. These changes signiﬁcantly
enhance the strength of the Ti e6Ale4V alloy, though Total Elongation Limit (TEL) decreases.
After evaluating mechanical properties, surface quality, and energy consumption associated
with warm rolling, we determined the optimal warm rolling temperature for the Ti e6Ale4V
alloy to be 650
/C14C. At this temperature, the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS), Yield Strength
(YS), and TEL were 1325 MPa, 1164 MPa, and 8.0%, respectively, maintaining good ductility.
The impressive performance of the Ti e6Ale4V alloy sheet is mainly attributed to the
strengthening effects of ﬁne grain and dislocation, while the introduction of some ﬁne
recrystallization enhances ductility. High-performance Ti e6Ale4V alloy thin strip was
successfully prepared by on-line warm rolling with hydraulic tension, which provided a new
idea for the preparation of thin strip of difﬁcult-to-deform materials.

research [ 4e6]. In recent years, along with the rapid develop-
ment of aerospace and microelectronics, the demand for high-

parts to super-large complex structural parts and crucial
components of large containers [ 7,8]. Examples include rocket

Severe plastic deformation (SPD) processes can yield ul-
traﬁne grain microstructures, signiﬁcantly enhancing mate-
rial strength [ 10,11]. Current SPD methods primarily include
high-pressure torsion (HPT), friction stir processing (FSP),
equal channel angular pressing (ECAP), and accumulative roll
bonding (ARB) [ 12e14]. ARB is a method capable of continu-
ously producing ultra-ﬁne grain structures in thin plates and
has seen extensive use with Al, Mg, Ti, and their composites.
It's viewed by most scholars as the most suitable SPD method
for industrial production [ 15e17].However, titanium alloys,
characterized by high deformation resistance, poor oxidation
resistance, and low elastic modulus, often suffer from insuf-
ﬁcient deformation during the ARB process. Additionally, ARB
requires a sequence of process steps that include surface
polishing, spray lubrication for isolation, welding, hot rolling,
cutting, and pickling. The disadvantages of these numerous
steps e such as low production efﬁciency and yield rate, poor
surface quality, and high costs e seriously limit the further
development of the ARB process [ 18].
The Ti e6Ale4V alloy, whose primary crystal structure is
hexagonal close-packed (HCP), has a limited number of slip
systems that can coordinate deformation at room tempera-
ture. This limitation results in poor room temperature
ductility and signiﬁcant cold deformation work hardening
[19e21]. When rolling by the single-sheet method, the cold
rolling reduction rate is less than 25%, necessitating
numerous intermediate annealing and pickling processes
[22]. Cracks and other defects are prone to occur during cold
rolling, further impacting the quality of the products.
Therefore, cold rolling is unsuitable for processing thin strips
of difﬁcult-to-deform metal materials. Warm rolling, an
important processing technology for hard-to-deform metal
thin strips, is widely applied in magnesium alloys [ 23]. By
heating the rolled piece, the microstructure is softened,
ductility is increased, and deformation resistance is reduced.
Liu et al. studied the AZ31 magnesium alloy at different
warm rolling temperatures. When the deformation temper-
ature reaches 230
/C14C, more slip systems are activated;
ductility is signiﬁcantly improved; and the reduction rate for
each pass can exceed 30%. When the rolling temperature is
increased to 280
/C14C, the single-pass rolling reduction rate can
reach 50%, and the total rolling reduction rate can be as high
as 90%. They successfully prepared the magnesium alloy thin
strip [ 24]. In recent years, the warm rolling of titanium alloys
has begun to garner attention. For example, Murty et al.
successfully prepared high-strength Ti e6Ale4V alloy bars by
multi-pass warm rolling at temperatures of 650
/C14C, 700 /C14C,
and 750 /C14C. They obtained optimal mechanical properties
when the rolling temperature was 650 /C14C, with the ultimate
tensile strength and yield strength being 1299 MPa and
1191 MPa, respectively, and the total elongation was 10% [ 25].
Ikeda et al. studied the effect of warm rolling in the two-
phase zone on the aging behavior and mechanical
properties of the Ti e15Moe5Zre3Al alloy to improve the
balance between strength and ductility [ 26]. Chao et al. per-
formed symmetric and asymmetric warm rolling on
Tie6Ale4V alloy, whose initial microstructure was
martensitic, and successfully prepared an equiaxed ultraﬁne
grain microstructure with an average grain size of
110e230 nm [ 27].
While progress has been made in the warm rolling of ti-
tanium alloys, the process traditionally requires multiple
rounds of heating and preservation in a furnace, which is
essentially off-line warm rolling. This approach introduces
various challenges in practical production settings. For
instance, temperature control is a critical issue in
manufacturing thin strips of difﬁcult-to-deform metal ma-
terials. Thin strips are subjected to contact heat transfer, air
convection, and radiation heat dissipation due to their in-
teractions with the rolls, transfer roller channels, and other
surfaces. As the strips are transferred from the heating
furnace to the rolling mill, rapid temperature drops and sig-
niﬁcant heat losses can occur. The large temperature differ-
ence between the rolls and the strip can lead to an uneven
and inaccurate sheet temperature during warm rolling,
which may subsequently impact the material 's performance.
Additionally, after each warm rolling cycle, the strip must be
returned to the heating furnace for re-heating and heat
preservation before the next rolling step. This process greatly
reduces processing efﬁciency and increases processing costs.
Moreover, thin strips require high dimensional accuracy and
superior surface quality. Although off-line warm rolling can
achieve certain thickness reductions, with increased rolling
reduction, defects like uneven shaping, bending, and warp-
ing can occur. To address these challenges, our research
team developed on-line warm rolling experimental equip-
ment featuring hydraulic tension. We conducted experi-
ments with Ti e6Ale4V alloy hot rolled plates at various
warm rolling temperatures. By using on-line warm rolling
with hydraulic tension, we obtained high-performance
Tie6Ale4V alloy thin strips with excellent surface quality
and regular shape. This study also explores the internal
relationship between microstructure evolution and proper-
ties of the materials. Our research offers a novel technique
for fabricating thin strips of difﬁcult-to-deform metal mate-
rials and provides crucial processing parameters and theo-
retical guidance for industrial applications of on-line warm
rolling of Ti e6Ale4V alloys.

2.1.1. Hydraulic tension device
The hydraulic tension device is situated on both lateral sides
of the quad-roll reversible mill, exerting tension on the test
sample via the hydraulic clamps intrinsic to the tensioning
system, thereby accomplishing the rolling process in tandem
with the mill 's operation. The hydraulic clamps serve dual
roles: providing sample securing and facilitating electrical
conduction. In an effort to inhibit any slippage or inadvertent
dislodgement of the secured sample during the rolling pro-
cess, the clamping jaws undergo a specialized treatment.
These jaws, constructed from copper, heat the specimen by
conducting electrical current through it while in a secured
state. The hydraulic clamp interfaces with the hydraulic ten-
sion apparatus through a tension sensor. This tension is
calibrated by a computer system, and the veracity of the
tension is gauged by a tensiometer. Through the imple-
mentation of a servo valve and a proportional pressure-
reducing valve, a consistent tension control of the rolled
samples is actualized.
2.1.2. Heating system
Managing the temperature of the sample deformation zone
within an appropriate processing window is Essential for the
success of warm rolling. Various factors during the rolling
process, such as the rolling speed, deformation amount, roll-
ing piece temperature, and roll temperature, inﬂuence the
temperature of the sample deformation zone. Since the rolling
speed and rolling deformation amount affect the rolling efﬁ-
ciency and cannot be easily modiﬁed, the primary parameters
that can be adjusted online are the rolling piece temperature
and roll temperature. Therefore, the heating technology for
the sample and the roll is a critical aspect of warm rolling.
The heating system primarily consists of a specimen
heating system and a working roll heating system. The spec-
imen heating system, as shown in Fig. 3, is mainly composed
of a heating transformer and a power regulator. The power
regulator adjusts the voltage on the primary side of the
transformer within a range of 0 e380V. The output voltage on
the secondary side of the transformer is 0 e20V. The clamps of

4. Discussion
4.1. Microstructure evolution at different warm rolling
temperatures
Through the analysis of the microstructural evolution and
mechanical properties of the Ti e6Ale4V alloy at varying
warm rolling temperatures, observe that the principal pro-
cesses occurring during warm rolling are grain reﬁnement and
dislocation accumulation. Consequently, the key strength-
ening mechanisms are the reﬁnement of grains and disloca-
tion strengthening [ 25,27,33]. Both the grain size and the
dislocation density are inﬂuenced by the rolling reduction rate
and the warm rolling temperature [ 34]. In this study, the
rolling reduction rate remains constant, hence the grain size
and dislocation density primarily correlate with the warm
rolling temperature. Different warm rolling temperatures
result in distinct degrees of grain growth and dislocation
accumulation. As the warm rolling temperature increases,
there is a tendency towards more substantial grain growth
and less dislocation accumulation. Therefore, selecting an
optimal temperature range during the warm rolling process is
crucial.

4.2. Dynamic recrystallization behavior at different
warm rolling temperatures
Materials subjected to extensive deformation typically yield
higher strength, though this often results in compromised
ductility, particularly in the case of cold rolling and SPD
[42e44]. This phenomenon primarily arises because ﬁne grain
strengthening and dislocation strengthening boost the mate-
rial's strength, while high dislocation defect densities and
ultra-ﬁne grains detract from its ductility [ 45,46]. Thus, to
enhance ductility following extensive deformation, subse-
quent heat treatments are usually implemented. For instance,
brief annealing may slightly sacriﬁce strength to introduce a
suitable proportion of recrystallized grains, optimizing the
balance between strength and ductility [ 47e49].
The principal distinction between warm rolling and cold
rolling lies in the former 's provision of greater storage energy,
potentially prompting DRX when grain reﬁnement and dislo-
cation accumulation reach a critical threshold. This study

with the trends in mechanical properties detailed in Section

rising warm rolling temperature, the dislocation density and
degree of dislocation entanglement gradually decrease, lead-
ing to a reduction in strength. Concurrently, the proportion of
DRX and TEL increase.

grain reﬁnement and dislocation strengthening collectively
enhance the material 's strength, while the induced partial
DRX augments its ductility. At a warm rolling temperature of
550

at its nadir due to the minimum storage energy available.
Consequently, strength peaks at this temperature while TEL
dips to its lowest level. On the other hand, at a warm rolling
temperature of 750
/C14C (WR-750 /C14C), the maximum storage
energy available for DRX yields the highest proportion of DRX,
albeit at the expense of weakening the strengthening effect of
dislocation accumulation and grain reﬁnement. As such,
strength dips to its lowest level while TEL peaks at this tem-
perature. In actual production scenarios, factors beyond the
strength-ductility balance of the material must be considered,
including the surface quality of the warm-rolled sheet. As
observed in Fig. 5(d), at WR-550

compared to 750
/C14C, the warm rolling process is more energy-
efﬁcient. Thus, taking into account mechanical properties,
surface quality, and energy consumption during warm rolling,
the optimal on-line warm rolling temperature for the

650
