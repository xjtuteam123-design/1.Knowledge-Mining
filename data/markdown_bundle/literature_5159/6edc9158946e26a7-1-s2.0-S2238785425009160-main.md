ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing enables the creation of complex geometries, particularly in the production of Nb alloys 
for aerospace applications, which are challenging to process using conventional methods. In this study, TiC 
nanoparticles were selected as nucleants and assembled onto Nb powders. Using laser-directed energy deposition 
(L-DED) technology, we successfully achieved crack-free Nb- x TiC alloys (where TiC is a nanoparticle and x = 0, 
1, 2 and 3 wt%, and referred as to Nb – 0TiC, Nb – 1TiC, Nb – 2TiC and Nb – 3TiC alloys, hereafter). The micro -
structural characteristics, hardness, tensile properties at 1000 
◦
C and deformation behavior of the Nb- x TiC alloys, 
as well as the crystallographic orientation relationship of the Nb-carbide ( α -Nb
2
C) couple, have been thoroughly 
investigated. The microstructure consists of near-equiaxed Nb matrix and α -Nb
2
C carbides (0.3 – 1.5 μ m in width, 
0.4 – 5 μ m in length), which are found both along grain boundaries and within the grain interior. The L-DED Nb- 
x TiC alloy exhibited a high plastic elongation of 16.3 % while the ultimate tensile strength increased significantly 
to 318 MPa at 1000 
◦
C as the TiC content increased from 0 % to 2 wt%. This outstanding combination of high- 
temperature strength and ductility at 1000 
◦
C can be results from the synergistic effect of the high-density 
tangled dislocation pile-up around the α -Nb
2
C precipitates, the presence of SF networks and L-C locks within 
the α -Nb
2
C, the movable mixed dislocations and the kink bands within the Nb grains.
1. Introduction
Among the refractory metals, niobium (Nb) and its alloys (e.g., C103 
(Nb-10Hf-1Ti) and Nb521 (Nb – 5W – 2Mo – 1Zr)) have been widely 
employed for its suitability in high-temperature applications within the 
hot-gas exhaust systems of launch vehicle and the thrusters of spacecraft 
altitude control systems due to its comparatively low density (8.57 g/ 
cm
3
), elevated melting point (2467 
◦
C), relatively superior high- 
temperature corrosion resistance, and exceptional high temperature 
strength [ 1 – 4 ]. These alloys primarily rely on solid solution strength -
ening (via refractory elements sucn as Hf, W, and Mo) and second phase 
strengthening (through carbides like ZrC, NbC and HfC) to enhance 
mechanical performance [ 5 ]. However, their high-temperature strength 
deteriorates significantly above 1000 
◦
C due to rapid coarsening of 
precipitates and dislocation recovery [ 6 ]. Moreover, the incorporation 
of heavy alloying elements (e.g., W, Ta and Hf) further increases density 
(e.g., Nb521: ~8.9 g/cm
3
), which compromises their suitability for 
weight-sensitive aerospace applications [ 7 ]. While the Nb alloys can be 
processed using conventional hot forging and machining techniques, the 
fabrication of complex geometries and cost-effective components re -
mains challenging due to the high strength and poor oxidation at 
elevated temperatures [ 8 , 9 ].
In contrast, additive manufacturing (AM) offers a comprehensive 
solution for producing the Nb alloys components directly, facilitating 
the creation of intricate geometries while reducing costs and improving 
efficiency [ 10 ]. The current literature on fabrication of the pure Nb and 
Nb alloys utilizing additive manufacturing techniques encompasses 
methods such as laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) [ 4 , 9 , 11 – 20 ], electron 
beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) [ 7 , 21 , 22 ], and laser directed energy 
deposition (L-DED) [ 23 , 24 ]. Most of the additively manufactured Nb 
alloys mentioned above exhibited excellent geometric precision and 
high-quality surface finishes. Furthermore, Philips [ 9 ] and Awasthi [ 12 ] 
have reported that the performance of the C103 components produced 
via LPBF is comparable to, or even exceeds, that of equivalent wrought

elevated thermal stresses often lead to the formation of cavities and hot 
cracks, while nonequilibrium solidification and epitaxial growth tend to 
produce large columnar grains, resulting in anisotropic mechanical

manufacturing large structural components composed of valuable ma -

powder feeding nozzle in L-DED overcomes the spatial constraints 
associated with powder beds or chambers [ 27 ]. However, many aspects 
of the L-DED Nb alloys, including their microstructure and mechanical 
properties, remain inadequately understood and require further 
investigation.
The interface, especially its orientation relationship (OR) and atomic 
structure, plays a critical role in influencing the properties of second- 
phase strengthening materials [ 28 – 30 ]. For instance, the interface can 
mediate the nucleation of dislocations in adjacent phases [ 30 ]. 
Furthermore, the interface significantly impacts the overall mechanical 
response of materials, including aspects such as the plastic-strain re -
covery and plastic instability, due to the varied mechanisms of dislo -
cation interaction with the interfaces and/or the second-phase [ 29 ]. For 
the case of the Nb alloys doped with carbide, the carbides usually 
introduce highly dense inter-phase boundaries. Especially, the mor -
phologies and size of precipitates in the AM samples experience varia -
tions during layer-by layer heating and cooling cycles, leading to the 
precipitate sizes that are usually an order of magnitude smaller 
compared to the precipitates in as-cast Nb alloys [ 7 , 23 ]. To our 
knowledge, the interface between the precipitates (especial carbide) and 
the Nb matrix in the AM Nb alloys has not been systematically studied. 
Gaining a better understanding on characteristics of the interface is both 
scientifically and technologically important for development of the 
high-performance AM Nb-based alloys strengthened by the 
second-phase.
Our very recent work firstly reported an effective strategy involving 
the introduction of TiC nano-powders to control microstructure of Nb by 
restricting directional grain growth, which resulted in the crack-free and 
near-equiaxed grains in the Nb- x TiC alloys fabricated using L-DED 
technology [ 23 ]. The TiC nanoparticles decomposed during L-DED 
processing, thus introduced both Ti and C into the Nb matrix, while Ti 
may solidify into Nb and toughen Nb and C can react with Nb and 
readily form a dispersed precipitation-strengthening α -Nb
2
C phase [ 23 ,
31 ]. Consequently, a balanced tensile strength and ductility in the 
L-DED Nb alloys at room temperature was achieved through this syn -
ergistic effect of the TiC nanoparticle doping [ 23 ]. However, no avail -
able data on the high-temperature mechanical behavior of the Nb alloys 
processed by L-DED have been reported so far, which is critical for their 
intended applications in nuclear and aerospace industries.
While our previous work (Ref. [ 23 ]) established the feasibility of TiC 
nanoparticle addition for grain refinement and room-temperature 
property enhancement in L-DED Nb alloys, this study focuses on three 
key advancements: (1) a systematic characterization of 
high-temperature (1000

pile-ups, kink bands, and stacking fault networks – phenomena that were 
absent in earlier studies. These findings provide valuable insights for the 
further exploration of high-performance L-DED Nb alloys for applica -
tions at elevated temperatures.
2. Materials and experimental methods
2.1. Materials and L-DED fabrication
High purity (99.9 %) spherical Nb powder, with a particle size range 
of 50 – 150 μ m ( Fig. 1 a and b), was used as the starting material. Addi -
tionally, the TiC nanoparticles with the average size of 200 nm ( Fig. 1 c) 
were utilized as the additive. To prepare the TiC-doped Nb feedstock, 
pure Nb powders were mixed with varying TiC nanoparticle contents of 
1 wt%, 2 wt% and 3 wt%) using a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP4, Nanjing 
Nan Da Instrument Plant, China) operating at 200 rpm for 4 h. The TiC- 
doped Nb alloys were hereafter referred to as Nb – 0TiC, Nb – 1TiC, 
Nb – 2TiC, and Nb – 3TiC alloys, respectively. As shown in morphologies 
of the Nb/TiC composite powders ( Fig. 1 d – f) and corresponding energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental maps ( Fig. 1 g
1

the small TiC nanoparticles have been uniformly distributed and 
adhered to surface of the Nb powders.
Nb ingots without and with the TiC nanoparticles addition were 
produced using a 6 KW optical fiber laser processing system. The central 
schematic diagram of Fig. 2 represents an overview of the L-DED. 
Rectangular ingots with the dimension of 36 mm (length) × 15 mm 
(width) × 22 mm (height) were built on Ti6Al4V substrate. The 
deposited layer was parallel to XY plane, and the building direction was 
parallel to Z axis. All the Nb- x TiC alloy ingots were fabricated using 
optimized L-DED parameters (laser power: 800 W, scanning speed: 800 
mm/min), resulting in a nearly defect-free microstructure with fine 
near-equiaxed grains [ 23 ].
2.2. Microstructure characterization
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was recorded using an X-ray 
diffractometer (D/MAX-2500, Rigaku, Japan), which was operated at 
40 kV and 200 mA. The microstructures of all the specimens were 
characterized by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Apreo S 
LoVac, FEI Co, Ltd). Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was per -
formed using field emission SEM (Apreo S LoVac, FEI Co, Ltd) to analyze 
the grain structure, while the EBSD samples were prepared by standard 
mechanical grinding and polishing, followed by a final step chemo- 
mechanical polishing for 8 h in a vibratory polisher (VibroMetTM 2, 
Buehler, USA). The transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100 
F, Japan) was utilized to conduct bright-field TEM image and the 
selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis, which was operated 
at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. The ion milling technique was used 
to prepare TEM samples. To avoid contamination, plasma cleaning of 
the TEM samples was done prior to their examination using TEM. To 
calculate the strain field of the interface, the Geometric Phase Analysis 
(GPA) plug-in was utilized in the Digital Micrograph software.
2.3. Mechanical properties tests
The nano hardness and Young ’ s modulus were measured using Nano 
Indenter (UMT-2, Bruker, USA) at a maximum load of 50 mN. The 
nanoindentation measurements were conducted at various positions on 
the sample surface using a microhardness tester (FALCON511, Inno -
vatest, Netherlands) with a load of 300gf and a dwell time of 15s at room 
temperature, and the average of five nano hardness values was recorded 
as the final hardness. Dog-bone tensile specimens with gauge di -
mensions of 18 mm (length) × 3 mm (width) × 1.7 mm (thickness) were 
machined from the rectangular ingots. Tensile testing at 1000

3. Results
3.1. TiC nanoparticle effect on microstructure of the L-DED Nb-xTiC 
alloys
The inherently high ratio of temperature gradient to solidification 
rate in L-DED, usually results in microstructures that are predominantly 
columnar, regardless of the material type. These columnar structures 
arise through epitaxial growth from the previous layer and extend across 
numerous sequential build layers [ 32 ]. As the real-time temperature 
decreases and the volume fraction of residual liquid diminishes during 
solidification, the resultant thermal contraction and volumetric solidi -
fication shrinkage within these boundaries of the large columnar grains 
lead to the formation of cavities and hot tearing cracks [ 26 ]. These de -
fects may extend along the entire length of the columnar grain and 
propagate through additional intergranular regions, as shown in 
Fig. 2 a
2
. For the L-DED processed sample, the considerable cooling rate 
can induce substantial residual thermal stress, which can also aggravate 
the formation and propagation of cracks. Fig. 2 a
3 
shows the BSE images 
of the YZ-cross-section of the L-DED Nb – 0TiC sample, where 
micro-cracks along the grain boundaries are clearly visible, as indicated 
by the red arrows.
However, grain refinement is considered an effective strategy to 
mitigate crack initiation and propagation [ 33 ]. Refined grains enhance 
the capacity of a liquid phase to infiltrate dendrite channels and manage 
volumetric shrinkage and thermal contraction during AM processes.

4.2.2. Kink band
Besides dislocation slipping, the plastic deformation through kinking 
is also a common mechanism in the BCC metals. Kink bands are typically 
activated in regions of highly localized plastic deformation and initiated 
by intense dislocation slip. This occurs when the active slip systems 
locally rotate the bands within grains toward more favorable Schmid 
factor directions [ 53 ]. As a result, the kink band involves a 
stress-relaxation process that induces local crystallographic reor -
ientation through vigorous dislocation activity, thereby enhancing 
plasticity through strain softening [ 54 ]. To further understand how kink 
bands enhance plasticity, particularly at 1000 
◦
C we analyzed the EBSD 
results of the side plane adjacent to the fracture position of the Nb – 2TiC 
alloy, as presented in Fig. 10 . A typical inverse pole figure (IPF) map and 
the IPF of two grains containing kink bands are constructed in Fig. 10 a 
and b, respectively. A distinct change in the crystallographic orienta -
tions (indicated by inconsistent color variations) is evident between the 
deformation traces and the Nb matrix. Comparison indicates that the 
lamellar deformation traces propagate distinctly within a single grain

5. Conclusions
Our research proposes a novel machining solution that effectively 
improves hardness and high-temperature strength in niobium additive 
manufacturing using TiC nanoparticles (with 0, 1, 2 and 3 wt% addi -
tions) decorated powder feedstock. The microstructure features, high- 
temperature tensile properties and deformation behavior of the L-DED 
Nb – 2TiC alloys have been investigated. The main conclusions are drawn 
as follows: 
(1) The L-DED Nb- x TiC alloys, characterized by crack-free, near- 
equiaxed grains and consisting of the Nb matrix and α -Nb
2
C 
carbides, exhibit a substantial enhancement in microhardness at 
room temperature, increasing from 78.2 HV
0.3 
in the as-cast Nb to 
141.1 HV
0.3 
in the Nb – 3TiC alloy. The submicron-scale α -Nb
2
C 
carbides (0.3 – 1.5 μ m in width, 0.4 – 5 μ m in length) precipitate 
both along grain boundaries and within the grain interior.
(2) The specific OR between the α -Nb
2
C phase and the Nb matrix in 
the L-DED Nb – 2TiC alloy has been determined. HRTEM analysis 
reveals a semi-coherent interface between the α -Nb
2
C phase and 
the Nb matrix.
(3) The L-DED Nb – 2TiC alloy shows the best combination of strength 
and ductility at 1000 
◦
C due to the α -Nb
2
C carbide precipitates 
and the refined Nb grains. Specifically, the σ
0.2

Nb – 0TiC alloy.
(4) Post-deformation analyses reveal that strengthening of the L-DED 
Nb – 2TiC alloy at 1000 
◦

the high density tangled dislocations around the α -Nb
