Structure and properties of Fe–Al–Ti intermetallic alloys
U. Prakash 1, G. Sautho/C128 *
Max Planck Institute fu¨r Eisenforschung, Postfach 140444, 40074 Du ¨sseldorf, Germany
Received 17 July 2000; accepted 7 August 2000
Abstract
Structure and mechanical properties of Fe–Al–Ti alloys in the composition range Fe–(10–27.5) at.%Al–(10–30) at.%Ti have
been studied. The alloys exhibited a wide range of microstructure comprising a-Fe, Fe 2Ti (Laves phase) and/or Fe 2AlTi (Heusler
phase) depending on the composition. Their room temperature hardness, Young’s modulus, ratio of plastic to elastic work and
yield strength were determined. Elevated temperature yield strength and creep properties of these alloys were also studied. It was
observed that signiﬁcant increase in strength and hardness may be achieved in alloys containing two or more phases. The com-
pressive creep behaviour of these alloys followed the power law in creep in the temperature range 873–1073 K. Creep stress expo-
nents in the range 3–6 were observed, suggesting dislocation creep to be the operative mechanism. Apparent activation energy for
creep was found to be in the range of 400 to 600 KJ/mol. # 2001 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Structure and properties of alloys in the Fe–Al–Ti
system have recently been investigated [1–3]. The studies
have focussed primarily on the Ti-rich alloys in the sys-
tem. Also Heusler type Fe
2AlTi (L2 1) and B2 Fe–(Al,
Ti) alloys with attractive combinations of Young’s
modulus, yield stress and creep resistance were reported
[1]. In the present work structure and properties of some
more iron-rich Fe–Al–Ti alloys are reported.
2. Experimental procedure
Seven alloy compositions (Table 1) were selected for
the present study. Alloy ingots each weighing 200 g were
prepared by levitation melting high purity (99.99%)
constituent elements in an argon atmosphere. The
ingots were chill cast into copper moulds of 20mm dia-
meter. Samples measuring 5510 mm were machined
using electro discharge machining (EDM) from the
alloy ingots for mechanical testing. Compression tests
were carried out on alloy samples at room temperature,
873, 1073 and 1273 K at a strain rate of 10
ÿ4/s. The
compression creep behaviour was studied by creep tests
with stepwise increased load on individual specimens.
The creep tests were carried out at 873, 973 and 1073 K.
All the tests were carried out in air.
Samples for optical and scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) were machined by EDM and mounted in bake-
lite. The samples were mechanically polished to 1 mm
grade diamond powder ﬁnish. Optical microscopy sam-
ples were etched using an etchant comprising 68% gly-
cerine, 16% HF and 16% HNO
3 by volume. For SEM,
the samples were lightly etched after polishing. This was
done to achieve improved contrast during SEM obser-
vation. The samples were studied in a CAMSCAN SEM
equipped with an EDAX detector.
The alloy hardness was determined on etched optical
metallography samples with a Fischerscope H100 sys-
tem with a Vickers indenter, using loads in the range of
0.4 mN to 1 N and indentation rates below 1 mm/s. The
computer controlled system measures the indentation
depth continuously as a function of applied load during
loading and unloading. This allows the determination of
Young’s modulus, universal hardness (from the inden-
tation depth under load), plastic hardness (from the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Constitution and microstructure
The phases observed in various alloys are listed in
Table 1 and correspond to those previously reported for
these compositions [1–3]. The isothermal section of Fe–
Al–Ti phase diagram at 1073 K along with the alloy
compositions studied is shown in Fig. 1. The micro-
structure of these alloys is shown in Fig. 2. Except for
alloy 5, all the other alloys contain two or more phases.
The phases present are identiﬁed in the respective
micrographs. The alloys can be classiﬁed as follows:
1. Those (alloys 1 and 2) containing a-Fe+Fe
2Ti
(Laves phase) phases: a-Fe is the matrix phase in
Alloy 1 while Fe 2Ti is the major phase in alloy 2.
2. Those (alloys 3 and 4) containing a-Fe+Fe2Ti+
Fe2AlTi (Heusler phase) phases: a-Fe is the matrix
phase in the alloy 3 whereas Fe 2Ti phase is the
major phase in alloy 4.
3. Alloy 5 is a single phase alloy containing the
intermetallic Fe 2AlTi.
4. Those (alloys 6 and 7) containing Fe 2Ti+Fe2AlTi,
Fe2Ti being the major phase in alloy 6 and
Fe2AlTi the matrix phase in alloy 7.
These alloy compositions were selected to represent
possible phase combinations containing intermetallics in
Fe-rich alloys in the Fe–Al–Ti system. For the two-
phase alloys, the compositions of the phases have been
marked on the ternary section shown in Fig. 2. This
enables drawing of tie lines in the two-phase ﬁelds. For
alloys 1 and 2 containing a-Fe+Fe
2Ti, the composi-
tions of the two phases are similar to those given by the
intersections of the tie lines with the respective phase
ﬁelds. However, in alloys 6 and 7 containing Fe

phase boundaries, indicating that these compositions do
not correspond to equilibrium at 1073 K.
Eutectic morphologies were observed in alloys 1–4,
suggesting that after the formation of the primary phase
interdendritic liquid solidiﬁed as eutectic. In the case of
alloys 1 and 2, the eutectic observed was a-Fe+Fe
2Ti.
In the case of the three-phase alloys (alloys 3 and 4), the
eutectic phases were Fe 2Ti+Fe2AlTi in alloy 3 and a-
Fe+Fe2Ti in alloy 4, respectively. The primary phase
also changed from a-Fe in the case of Alloy 3 to Fe 2Ti

3.2. Hardness and yield strength
The hardness measurements on these alloys allowed
the determination of Young’s modulus E and the ratio
of plastic work to elastic work (RPE) for the indenta-
tion as well as the hardness [4]. The results obtained are
shown in Fig. 3. The Young’s modulus is just above 200
GPa for the alloys studied. The hardness, however,
changes with the alloy composition and is lowest (Fig.
3) for the single-phase Fe
2AlTi alloy (alloy 5). This alloy
also exhibits the highest RPE. The alloys exhibiting
higher hardness have a correspondingly lower RPE. The
lower hardness of the single-phase alloy is also reﬂected
in the low room-temperature yield strength (Fig. 4). The
alloy shows an increase in yield strength when tested at
873 K. This is similar to the increase in yield strength
observed for binary Fe
3Al alloys with DO 3 structure [5].
The other alloys have a higher room-temperature yield
strength with a sharp decrease in yield strength with
increasing in test temperature. All the alloys exhibited
very low (50 MPa or less) yield strength at 1273 K (Fig.
4). The mechanical properties of alloy 5 have been stu-
died previously [1] and the only major di/C128erence
observed is the relatively higher value of RPE reported
in the previous work. This may be attributed to the heat
treatment given to the alloy by Gorzel et al. [1].
There is a good correspondence between the room
temperature yield strength and hardness, with harder
alloys also exhibiting higher yield strength. It would
appear from the present results that signiﬁcant increase
in strength may be achieved if two or more phases are
present in the alloy. At elevated temperatures increased
plasticity was observed in all the samples tested. Alloys
6 and 7 were found to be very brittle. It is worth noting
that these alloys do not contain the a-Fe phase and are
composed entirely of the intermetallics Fe
2Ti and
Fe2AlTi. The test samples of alloy 7 broke during EDM
or subsequent handling. Consequently, only hardness
tests could be carried out for this composition. Alloy 6
did not exhibit any plasticity at room temperature and
failure occurred because of cracking at low stresses in
the alloy samples. The single-phase Fe
2AlTi alloy sam-
ples were successfully machined by EDM and tested.
The relatively brittle behaviour of alloys 6 and 7 may
thus be attributed to the presence of the Laves Phase
Fe
2Ti and the absence of a-Fe.

4. Conclusions
Structure and properties of Fe–Al–Ti alloys were
investigated. The alloys exhibited a wide variety of
microstructures comprising a-Fe, Fe 2Ti and Fe 2AlTi
phases in the composition range Fe–10–27.5%Al–10–
30%Ti studied. It was observed that signiﬁcant increase
in strength and hardness may be achieved if two or
more phases are present in the alloy. The compressive
creep behaviour of these alloys was found to conform to
the power law in creep for the temperature range 873–
1073 K. The creep stress exponents obtained from creep
data were found to be in the range of 3–6, suggesting
dislocation creep to be the operative mechanism in these
alloys. The apparent creep activation energy was found
to be in the range 400–600 KJ/mol and was approxi-
mately double the reported values for activation energy
for di/C128usion reported for Fe in Fe–Al alloys. This dif-
ference may be attributed to possible changes in micro-
structure during testing.
