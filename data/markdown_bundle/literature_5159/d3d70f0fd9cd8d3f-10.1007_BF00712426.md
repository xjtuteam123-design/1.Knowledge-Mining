HIGH-STRENGTH STEELS AND ALLOYS 
STRUCTURE AND PROPERTIES OF THE HIGH-TEMPERATURE- 
STRENGTH ALLOY KhN62BMKTYu (I~P-742) 
E. A. Maslenkova, S. B. Maslenkov, Yu. V. Solov'ev, 
and A. V. Logunov UDC 669.14.018.44 
The high-temperature strength, mechanical properties, and structure of the KhN62BMKTYu alloy are investigated 
after quenching and aging under various conditions. It is shown that the strength properties and impact strength 
depend on the quenching temperature, whereas the plastic properties are almost independent of them. It is deter- 
mined that the KhN62BMKTYu alloy has a good combination of mechanical and high-temperature strength proper- 
ties. 
The alloy KhN62BMKTYu has been developed at the VIAMe in collaboration with the "l~lektrostal," factory and other 
organizations. This alloy differs from the alloy KhN73MBTYu in terms of the high aluminum, niobium, and molybdenum 
contents as well as additional doping with cobalt and lanthanum (Table 1). Rare-earth elements and boron are added to the alloy 
in calculated amounts. The high-temperature strength of the KhN62BMKTYu alloy is due to the formation of complex alloying 
of the solution with nickel, chromium, and cobalt and the large amount (-35%) of the strengthening phase Ni3(Ti, AI, Nb). 
Increase in the high-temperature strength of the alloy on account of its complex alloying undoubtedly leads to higher 
resistance to deformation and contraction in the temperature range of a sufficient amount of plasticity, which makes it difficult 
to treat it under pressure. For example, whereas the KhN73MBTYu alloy satisfactorily deforms upon free forging by hammers in 
the temperature range 1180-1000°C, the alloy KhN62BMKTYu, which contains larger amounts of Ti, A1, and Nb has a narrower 
range of hot plasticity (1140-1070°C) and can be deformed only by pressing or extrusion. 
In order to attain the requisite properties in large forgings of an alloy of the KhN73MBTYu type with a high content of 
the strengthening 7'-phase, it should be highly clean in terms of the impurities, gases, and nonmetallic inclusions, and should 
have a minimum amount of segregated inhomogeneities. In this connection, such alloys are melted in a two-stage process, viz., 
vacuum induction melting + vacuum arc remelting. The properties of the alloy KhN62BMKTYu were determined using samples 
tangentially cut out of an experimental forging 600 mm in diameter. The forging was prepared by drawing and upsetting an ingot 
400 mm in diameter. The total degree of deformation was equal to --80%. 
The forging was heat treated under the conditions: quenching from 1150°C (8 h) in air + quenching from 1050°C (4 h) 
in air + aging at 850°C for 8 h and at 730°C for 32 h followed by air cooling. The main phases present in the structure of the 
alloy after the heat treatment were as follows: 
1) y-solid solution which contained (in %): 58.0 Ni, 24.0 Cr, 11.0 Co, 4.0 Mo, 0.6 Nb, 0.5 Ti, and 0.3 AI; 
2) the strengthening ?,'-phase of the Ni3(Ti , A1, Nb) type contained (in %): 69.0 Ni, 2.4 Cr, 4.7 Co, 1.2 Mo, 3.1 Nb, 7.0 
Ti, 12.0 A1, 0.1 Fe, and 0.4 Si; 
3) MC-type carbides containing (in %): 64-68 Nb, 15-17 Ti, and 5-7 Mo; 
4) M6C-type carbides containing Ni, Co, Mo, Cr, Nb, and Ti; 
5) titanium nitrides; 
6) two lanthanum-containing phases: an intermetallic compound of the NisLa type, in nickel was partially replaced by 
cobalt, chromium, and aluminum in amounts of 2.6, 1.5, and 1% respectively, and an Ni(La, Ce)P phosphide containing 61.3% 
(La + Ce), 13.2% P, and the rest Ni.

Until recently, it was thought that the main features of the rare-earth elements, which control their state in high- 
temperature strength nickel alloys, are: 1) high chemical affinity to oxygen, sulfur, and nitrogen, which leads to the formation of 
refractory oxides, sulfides, and nitrides and 2) the ability to form refractory compounds with low-melting nonferrous-metal 
impurities [2]. Application of pure charge materials and vacuum techniques for melting made it possible to establish that the 
form, in which lanthanum occurs in the investigated alloy, is determined not only by its affinity to the harmful elements but also 
by the fact that it forms stable intermetallic compounds with nickel. It was determined that, at 0.021-0.073% La and <0.005- 
0.013% P content, a phosphide phase is formed in the structure. Its greatest amount corresponded to the minimum phosphorus 
content. If lanthanum is more than 0.025% and phosphorus is more than 0,005% in the alloy, then an intermetallic phase NisLa 
appears along with the phosphide Ni(La, Ce) in the structure of the alloy.

It is well known that the first high-temperature heating of high-temperature strength alloys is carried out for producing 
a homogeneous solid solution and grains of a certain size which does not change upon further heat treatment. After cooling in 
air, the alloy KhN62BMKTYu has a higher hardness than after quenching in water (Table 4), which indicates that decomposition 
occurs in the y-solid solution already during the cooling of the alloy in air. The average size of the strengthening particles of the 
y'-phase precipitated is --80 nm. 
During the second quenching, a sPecific structure of the alloy is formed which produces a combination of high strength 
and high-temperature strength. The second quenching almost does not have any effect on the mechanical properties at 20°C and 
the hardness of the alloy when both the quenches were carried out by cooling in air. However, when the second quenching was 
carried out in water, it substantially decreased the hardness (Table 4). Such an effect can be explained by the fact that, in those 
samples that were quenched from 1150°C in water, the supersaturated solid solution intensively decomposed during the subse-

It was determined that, during the high-temperature aging at 850°C, the hardness of the samples increases up to 36 
HRC during the first hour of the aging. It decreases to 35 HRC in about four hours and then it stays at this level until the 
completion of the aging (Table 5). In the water-quenched samples, the structure is observed to stabilize already after two hours 
of aging. Aging at 850°C increases the amount of the 7'-phase and gives rise to growth of the fine particles to 50 nm, which 
helps increase the strength properties but it decreases the plasticity and impact strength of the alloy (see Table 4). 
Investigation of the effect of the second quenching at 730°C on the hardness of the alloy showed that the samples had 
almost identical hardness values that were independent of the method of cooling the sample after high-temperature heating 
(1150 and 1050°C). The hardness increased somewhat upon going from the first high-temperature aging at 850°C to aging at 
730°C for one hour. Increase in the aging time at 730°C to 32 h increased the hardness of the alloy, which indicated that there 
was some increase in the amount of the 7'-phase.

Quenching from 1050°C was an incomplete process and it was unindicative of the values of properties of an alloy since, 
according to the data of [5], which were obtained by DTA, the temperature range of dissolution of the )"-phase is equal to 
1090-1140°C. In the structure of the samples air-quenched from 1050°C coarse and fine particles of the )"-phase precipitated, 
which both coalesced during heating and conversion of the metal. The presence of such particles promotes increase in strength 
but it decreases the plasticity of the alloy. 
The effect of the quenching temperature on the mechanical properties of the alloy KhN62BMKTYu determined in the 
temperature range 400-900°C is presented in Fig. la. With increasing test temperature, both the strength and plastic properties 
of the alloy quenched from the temperature range 1050-1150°C (i.e., above and below the dissolution temperature of the 7'- 
phase) decreased. The lowest values of the ultimate tensile strength and the apparent flow stress are found to occur in the 
operational temperature range 600-800°C among those alloy samples that were quenched from the highest temperature. The 
impact strength of the alloy varied in a similar manner. Its values were higher, the greater the amount of the )','-phase dissolved 
during the course of heating before quenching (Fig. la). Apparently, the amount of the strengthening by the Ni3(Ti, Al, Nb) 
phase precipitating out during testing was inadequate for retaining the strength at the initial level. Decomposition of the ),-solid 
solution decreased plasticity and embrittlement in the alloy. 
The strength properties of the aged alloy are presented in Fig. lb. It can be seen that the greatest mechanical properties 
are attained after quenching from ll00°C followed by two quenchings. 
Upon testing in the temperature range 400-700°C, the plastic properties of the alloy are practically independent of the 
heat-treatment conditions. They either only slightly decrease or do not vary at all (see Fig. 1). Above 700°C, the properties 6 
and g~ determined in quenched or quenched and aged samples vary in different ways. Increasing test temperature of the quenched 
samples leads to a strong decrease in the plastic properties whereas, in the quenched and aged samples, it leads to a significant 
increase in them, which indicates the beginning of the coagulation process of the ),'-phase. The values of the plastic properties 
are independent of heating temperature before quenching. In contrast to plasticity, the impact strength of the alloy is directly 
related to the dissolution temperature of the phase Ni3(Ti , AI, Nb). The values of a 1 increase during coagulation of the particles 
of the primary strengthening phase (Fig. lb).
