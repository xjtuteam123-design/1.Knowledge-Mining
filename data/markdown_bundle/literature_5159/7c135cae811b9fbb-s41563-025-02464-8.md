The energy efficiency of heat engines (gas and steam turbines) for electricity 
production and propulsion is determined by the Carnot cycle and scales 
with operating temperature. Commercial nickel- and cobalt-based 
superalloys melt near 1,500 °C and rapidly lose mechanical strength beyond 
1,000 °C. Refractory metals melt well above 2,000 °C but have i nh er-
ent m an ufacturability challenges that are barriers to adoption, such as 
high ductile-to-brittle transition temperatures. Using density functional 
theory-guided design, we demonstrate tailored local lattice distortions 
that promote phase-stable, non-equiatomic refractory concentrated 
solid solutions with both high ductility and strength. We exemplify this 
for single-phase, body-centred cubic Nb4Ta4V3Ti that exhibits castability, 
excellent room-temperature tensile yield strength (∼1 GPa) and ductility 
(approaching 20% uniform strain), and exceptional high-temperature 
tensile strength (500 MPa at 1,000 °C). These findings illustrate a path for 
designing materials that hold great potential for advancing next-generation 
technologies such as Generation IV fission reactors, first-generation 
fusion-plasma reactors, and more efficient gas turbines for electricity 
generation and propulsion.
Multi-principal-element alloys (MPEAs) are generally defined as consist-
ing of concentrated amounts of three or more elements with no one 
element representing a molar majority. In these concentrated solid 
solutions, solute atoms of differing atomic radius and composition 
can induce lattice distortions and increased lattice friction resulting in 
extraordinary strength1–3. Many MPEAs based on refractory and other 
transition metals have been shown to exhibit extraordinary mechani-
cal properties4–8. Refractory MPEAs with body-centred cubic (bcc) 
crystal structure are ideal candidates for use in power generation and 
aerospace applications due to their intrinsically high melting tempera-
tures and excellent softening resistance at temperatures far beyond 
those that would melt commercial superalloys 9–13. However, a key 
limitation is that most of them exhibit a ductile-to-brittle transition 
at temperatures above room temperature, resulting in limited or zero 
tensile ductility, or the inability to absorb damage under mechanical 
strain conditions that drive crack opening and propagation. This makes 
refractory alloys extremely difficult to manufacture, highly susceptible 
to fracture and of limited practical use in devices such as combustion

A bcc phase fraction of 1 implies that the electronic-structure calcula-
tions do not find a thermodynamically stable solid-solution phase at 
0 K that is fcc or hexagonal close packed (hcp). We limited the search 
to disordered solutions with fcc, bcc and hcp lattices to make calcula-
tions practical, although the computational methods are applicable 
to (and practical for) other lattices and ordered phases. As we have 
shown in an earlier manuscript35, it is possible to identify MPEA com-
positions that will segregate to meet a stable (minimum free energy) 
combination of two or more phases; this is a compelling route for fur-
ther tailoring high-temperature mechanical properties, such as creep 
resistance, for refractory MPEAs. The transition from composition A 
(equiatomic NbTaTiV) to composition B (Nb 4Ta4V3Ti1) corresponds 
to a substantial decrease in LLD from 0.142 to 0.060 and an increase 
in unstable stacking-fault energy (USFE) from 575 to 657 mJ m−2, indi-
cating enhanced ductility and resistance to dislocation glide (higher 
strength). As shown in Fig. 1b, the interstitial charge density ρ0, which 
directly correlates with alloy strength26, increases from Ti-rich (∼0.20 
electrons per Å3) to Nb–Ta-rich (∼0.25 electrons per Å3) regions, con-
sistent with higher USFE values for the equiatomic composition. The 
shift from equiatomic to the exemplar non-equiatomic (specifically, 
composition B) retains phase stability while improving strength, high-
lighting the role of electronic structure and lattice distortion in tuning 
mechanical properties.
We then used a more computationally intensive search algorithm 
to identify composition-dependent reductions in LLD, optimizing the 
strength–ductility balance, often dictated by the interplay between 
LLD and USFE, both of which have been predicted to influence disloca-
tion mobility. The LLD trend is illustrated in Fig. 1c for a subset of data 
with varying titanium concentrations in (Nb 4/11Ta4/11V3/11)1−xTix. As the 
titanium content increases, the LLD rises non-linearly. At low titanium 
concentrations (x < 0.2), the LLD remains relatively small (<0.1), sug-
gesting a more uniform atomic arrangement that enhances ductility21. 
Despite a high Ti–Ti SRO (>0.05)36 (D.B. et al., manuscript in prepara-
tion), which discourages excessive lattice strain, this structured atomic 
environment maintains a favourable balance for ductility and strength. 
However, the LLD increases rapidly with increasing Ti concentration, 
promoting strain hardening but also leading to brittleness if excessive. 
Simultaneously, titanium clustering, the dominant type of SRO in this 
case (Supplementary Fig. 2a), decreases monotonically and approaches 
zero at high titanium concentrations (Fig. 1c ), indicating increasing 
atomic randomness. The selected non-equiatomic composition bal -
ances two atomistic mechanisms that may promote ductility, that is, 
reducing lattice irregularities (low LLD) and promoting titanium-rich 
cluster formation (high SRO).
In bcc metals, at homologous temperatures below ∼0.2, strength 
is predominantly governed by screw dislocations due to a high Peierls 
barrier37, which scales as σP = USFE/b, where b is the Burgers vector. The 
USFE is correlated to the activation energy for dislocation slip, with 
higher values indicating higher strength and lower ones facilitating slip 
and improved plasticity38–40. As shown in Fig. 1d, USFE and ρ0 decrease 
nearly linearly from ∼650 to ∼200 mJ m−2 and from ∼0.25 to ∼0.20 
electrons per Å3 as a function of titanium concentration, in contrast to 
higher lattice friction (effective Peierls stress41) associated with large 
LLDs. For Nb4Ta4V3Ti, a USFE ≈ 657 mJ m−2 ensures high lattice friction 
(strength) without introducing lattice distortions that can impede 
dislocation glide. Unlike many other concentrated solid solutions, 
Nb4Ta4V3Ti reveals a pathway for achieving high strength with small 
lattice distortions. This underscores the importance of tuning the LLD 
and ρ0 to optimize the mechanical properties of bcc RMPEAs. The LLD 
(indicator of ductility), USFE (dislocation-mediated strength) and SRO 
(impacting strength and ductility) are directly linked to the electronic 
structure in metals21,26,36,42. Although a comprehensive framework for

Environmental effects are particularly complicated with refrac -
tory metals because they also exhibit high solubility for interstitial 
non-metal species, including oxygen, nitrogen and carbon. These 
smaller atoms can dissolve into and diffuse through the space between 
the metal atoms that form the crystal lattice, and can accumulate 
along grain boundaries and other structural defects, causing lat -
tice strain and reducing the activation energy required for cleavage, 
leading to embrittlement 14,15. Conversely, oxygen additions in some 
refractory MPEAs have been shown to promote the formation of 
ordered oxygen complexes, a form of chemical short-range order -
ing (SRO), resulting in simultaneous increase in strength and ductil -
ity by promoting dislocation shear through non-planar slip 16–18. For 
example, metal-oxide, metal-carbide and metal-nitride precipitates 
have been demonstrated to result in extraordinary strength retention 
and creep mitigation at high temperatures in superalloys and some 
refractory alloys, like WC3009 (refs. 19,20). However, a key enabler 
of precipitation-strengthened nickel- and cobalt-based superalloys, 
which are multiphase materials, is the presence of a face-centred 
cubic (fcc) matrix that is softer and more ductile than the interme -
tallic precipitates and imparts strength and creep resistance; the 
lower melting temperature of the softer phase is a limiting factor to 
their high-temperature use. Our work provides a demonstration of 
these requisite properties for the ductile phase, historically nickel- or 
cobalt-based, in a refractory-based composition, establishing a foun-
dation for the design of higher-performance multiphase refractory 
alloys with far greater performance. Ductility indicators for general 
solid-solution alloys, such as valence-electron concentration (VEC), 
Pugh’s ratio (B/G, ratio of bulk to shear modulus) and Rice’s criterion, 
have been proposed to predict ductility in refractory MPEAs, although 
these have shown limited accuracy in comparisons with experimental 
results21–25. We implemented an electronic-charge-based design strat-
egy to identify refractory MPEA compositions with both high strength 
and ductility21,26 by enhancing the interstitial electronic charge density 
(ρ0, the interstitial electron charge count normalized by interstitial 
volume)26 and lowering the local lattice distortion parameter (LLD, 
a weighted average of atomic displacements derived from the opti -
mally disordered structure 21), respectively. The ρ 0 is a quantitative 
(scalar) measure of strength26. A reduction in LLD is associated with 
larger asymmetry in the interstitial charge distribution, postulated 
in recent work to promote a multiplicity of dislocation pathway and 
enhance ductility in bcc metals21,27,28. Lattice distortions also increase 
the activation energy for dislocation movement and can thus contrib-
ute to higher strength, but are historically associated with a reduction 
in ductility29–32.
For theory-guided design, we performed a rapid scan of equia -
tomic refractory four-element composition space and identified the 
quaternary NbTaTiV as a suitable candidate for refinement, having 
both ductility (LLD ≈ 0.142) and strength (ρ0 ≈ 0.231 electrons per Å3). 
This equiatomic composition was experimentally verified to have high 
room-temperature tensile yield strength (702 MPa) and uniform strain 
(12.5%) in an as-cast single bcc phase33. We then mapped phase stability 
and ρ0 for this alloy family in the non-equiatomic composition space, 
enabled by a combined machine learning (ML) and density functional 
theory (DFT, Green’s function KKR-CPA) method, which rely on rapid 
calculations of estimated phase fraction 34, composition-dependent 
formation energy34 and rule-of-mixtures ρ0 from tabulated elemental 
values26 for the bcc crystal structure, as an initial guide. A summary of 
key results is presented in Fig. 1a,b via pseudoternary phase diagrams, 
with Nb–Ta reported in aggregate because they have interchangeable 
effects on phase stability (formation energy) and ρ0.

The brief overview of the alloy design process led to the selection 
of Nb4Ta4V3Ti1 as a target composition for experimental validation 
because it offers an exceptional combination of low LLD (0.06 versus 
equiatomic 0.142), Ti–Ti clustering decreases but does not vanish 
entirely, and USFE and ρ 0 fall within an optimal range, while avoid -
ing phase instability. Although this composition does not maximize 
strength or ductility alone, it strikes a balance—offering sufficient 
plasticity resistance and controlled lattice distortion for enhanced 
toughness. This trade-off is critical for structural applications requir-
ing both mechanical reliability and durability.
Castings of Nb4Ta4V3Ti1 were prepared (Fig. 2a) using plasma arc 
melting from high-purity elemental feedstock and homogenized in 
vacuum at 1,400 °C for 24 h. A single-phase bcc structure with a large 
grain size (Supplementary Table 1) was observed in both as-cast and 
homogenized specimens (Supplementary Fig. 4). Remarkably, we 
were able to roll the homogenized castings at room temperature up to 
∼63% thickness reduction without cracking (Fig. 2a). Despite the use of 
high-purity feedstock and a high-vacuum furnace, the oxygen concen-
tration in the homogenized samples were in the 100s of ppm. Castings 
were annealed again at 1,400 °C for 24 h on a bed of yttrium shavings, 
a strong oxygen getter, to reduce dissolved oxygen via solid diffusion, 
with a reduction in oxygen concentration from 100s of ppm to a few 
10s of ppm (Supplementary Table 1). The homogenized low-oxygen 
castings were either slowly (furnace) cooled or quenched in an oil 
bath to vary chemical SRO. Several tensile bars were prepared from 
the above specimens with various combinations of SRO and oxygen 
concentration, and representative room-temperature tensile test 
results are presented in Fig. 2b. Additional tensile curves and proper-
ties can be found in Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 1. 
The baseline condition with low oxygen (31 ppm) and reduced SRO 
resulted in a tensile yield strength σy ≈ 0.93 GPa and an ultimate tensile 
strength σUTS ≈ 1.04 GPa with an average uniform strain εu of ∼15.5%. This 
corresponds to ∼30% and ∼20% improvement in σy and εu, respectively, 
compared with data available for equiatomic NbTaVTi33. An increase in 
interstitial oxygen from 31 to 420 ppm resulted in a substantial increase 
in strength (∼0.19 GPa) at the cost of reduced ductility, as expected. 
However, the use of a slow furnace cooling rate resulted in a notable 
combination of high σy (∼1 GPa), σUTS (1.11 GPa) and an exceptional uni-
form strain εu of ∼18.2%. In the case with low oxygen concentration, the 
grain size was sufficiently large that it is not expected to make a mean-
ingful contribution to strength via the Hall–Petch mechanism; for the 
case with finest grain size (37 µm diameter), we estimate ΔσH-P ≈ 20 MPa 
based on a Hall–Petch coefficient of 240 MPa· µm1/2 reported for the 
Ti–Zr–Nb–Hf–Ta system49,50.

Fracture morphology of the highest and lowest ductility cases 
clearly show a transition from microscale necking to intergranular 
fracture (Fig. 2c ), indicating a possible tendency of grain bound -
ary weakening due to oxygen segregation. While the yield strength 
increased for the high-oxygen case, indicating that some of the oxygen 
is in solution, it is likely that oxygen segregation at grain boundaries 
plays an important role. Further systematic investigation is needed to 
determine how oxygen affects intra- and intergranular strengths; that 
is, is the dominant factor a strengthening of the lattice that exceeds 
the activation energy for grain boundary cleavage, or are boundaries 
rapidly weakening below the strength of the lattice? Recent work has 
shown that addition of non-metal species such as boron can compete 
with oxygen at grain boundaries, substantially mitigating oxygen 
embrittlement of grain boundaries51. The surface of a fractured tensile 
specimen was polished and further analysed using electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD) and electron channelling contrast imaging (ECCI), 
as shown in Fig. 2d,e, revealing deformation kinks, deformation twins, 
a high density of parallel and intersecting slip bands, and dislocation 
forests. The remaining matrix shows a dense dislocation network 
arising from plastic deformation. A misorientation of 60° indicates 
that the numerous narrowly spaced twins (∼2 µm) correspond to the 
{112}<111> system (Supplementary Fig. 6). Kinking is characterized 
as crystal rotations of arbitrary angles which can effectively accom -
modate dislocation movement to relieve stress concentrations and 
improve ductility. Kink bands are bounded by two approximately 
parallel boundaries composed of geometrically necessary edge dis -
locations in the same direction as the Taylor axis52,53. A more detailed 
analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7) of the kink bands revealed the predomi-
nant activation of the {112}<111> slip mode, consistent with previous 
similar investigations12,54,55. Atom probe tomography (APT) was used 
to identify departures from a random solid solution in the atomic-scale 
distribution. Although atomic clustering tendencies were shown to 
be statistically significant in the case of furnace-cooled material, 
such clustering was not statistically meaningful in the oil-quenching 
condition. Specifically, APT of the furnace-cooled casting confirms 
the presence of titanium-rich clusters, a form of chemical SRO, as 
predicted via DFT calculations. Figure 3a shows an ion map reconstruc-
tion (on the left) from the furnace-cooled sample, which is uniform in 
colour, as expected for a random or near-random solid solution. The 
frequency distribution of both samples also confirms no significant 
deviation from randomness (Supplementary Fig. 8). An aggregate 
analysis of the cluster radial distribution function (RDF; see Supple -
mentary Information for details 56,57) in the magnified box (Fig. 3b ) 
shows the concentration of each element as a function of the radial 
distance from titanium-centred lattice points. This analysis reveals 
titanium-rich clusters with an approximate diameter of 1 nm in the 
slow-cooled case, but not in the rapidly cooled (oil-quenched) case. 
Cluster composition analysis (Fig. 3a) confirms substantial elemental 
partitioning within these clusters (titanium enrichment) as compared 
to the average bulk composition of the entire reconstruction. Although 
systematic investigations are needed to further elucidate the impact 
of the titanium-rich clusters on the activation energies for screw/edge 
dislocation movement58, we hypothesize that the localized titanium 
(and less impactfully, vanadium) enrichment may result in further 
reduction of LLD in the matrix (Fig. 1c), and their combined influence 
leads to the observed increase in both strength and ductility by intro-
ducing higher-energy barriers for their long-range motion.

Figure 4a,b presents results of high-temperature tensile testing of 
samples with low oxygen contents, in some cases after furnace cool-
ing (with SRO) and in others after quenching (no SRO), as noted. A few 
models have been proposed for estimating the strength of single-phase 
MPEAs, and while each has its own underlying assumptions that gov-
ern predictive accuracy, most are based on the Fleischer 62 approach 
whereby solid-solution strengthening arises from differences in atomic 
size and/or a mismatch in elastic modulus. These models are used 
to extrapolate beyond dilute concentration limits 63. Senkov et al.64 
explored their validity for room-temperature strength predictions, 
and others65–67 have proposed alternative models that can account for 
temperature effects. A temperature-dependent strength prediction 
for Nb4Ta4V3Ti1 was made using the model proposed by Maresca and 
Curtin59 based on the edge dislocation predominance in bcc MPEAs 
and was compared to our data with remarkably good agreement for 
cases where chemical SRO was not present (at 20 °C and 1,300 °C). 
This is a notable validation because we are comparing predictions of

Notably, the subnanometer-sized titanium-rich cluster (a mani -
festation of SRO) in the slow-cooled case resulted in notable increases 
in both strength and uniform ductility, which differs from previous 
work on refractory MPEAs showing a limited contribution or a coun-
terproductive effect of (in some cases nearly negligible amounts of) 
SRO on mechanical behaviour68,69. SRO is generally accompanied by 
smaller lattice distortions, which is true in our case (Fig. 1c ). The for-
mation of regions with SRO has been proposed to reduce the efficacy 
of lattice distortions on dislocation trapping, promoting both screw 
and edge dislocation glide, leading to a strength decrease68. However, 
for our alloy, which was designed for minimized lattice distortion, 
the formation of titanium-rich clusters (SRO) clearly resulted in both 
higher strength and ductility, potentially by changing the dislocation 
shear mode16,17.
Substantial effort has been dedicated to elucidating pathways 
for predicting and improving mechanical behaviour in concentrated 
solid solutions. A rapidly growing body of theoretical and experi -
mental data highlights the importance of edge dislocation activa -
tion and increased mobility in enabling strength retention at high 
temperatures12,27,59,60. Baruffi et al.70 provided an extensive comparison 
of proposed and identified strengthening mechanisms in bcc MPEAs, 
and they show that edge-based strengthening can be comparable to 
or exceed screw dislocation effects if the atomic-size misfit parameter 
δ is larger than 3.5%. Our Nb 4Ta4V3Ti alloy is deliberately designed 
to have a low LLD (higher ductility), and thus a low misfit param -
eter (3.34% for the fully random case), implying screw-controlled 
strength, which is generally correlated with rapid strength reduc -
tion at high temperatures due to the unpinning of cross-kinks 66. 
The high tensile strength of our alloy up to 1,300 °C clearly deviates 
from this hypothesis of screw-dominated deformation, support -
ing the continued importance of edge character at high tempera -
tures. The remarkable agreement between our experimental data 
and the Maresca–Curtin model based on edge dislocation mobility 
(Fig. 4b) further supports this claim. The equiatomic NbTaTiV has a 
larger LLD but an even smaller δ (3.27%), yet edge dislocations were 
experimentally observed via in situ neutron diffraction and TEM to 
be active at elevated temperatures 61,71. The exposure to prolonged 
high-temperature environments, above 1,300 °C, would result in 
homogenization of the observed chemical SRO that is imparted dur -
ing slow cooling; this probably occurred in the time frames of our own 
high-temperature experiments. While the increase in strength at room 
temperature due to SRO is small, there may be opportunities to tailor 
composition to promote retention of SRO at higher temperatures, 
or to introduce secondary phases, as a route for mitigating creep 72. 
These data suggest that reduced lattice distortions and/or misfit 
parameters do not necessarily imply weakening of edge dislocation 
activity, or a reduction in strength. Rather, a framework based on 
electronic-structure methods such as the one described here is an 
effective pathway for tailoring composition in refractory MPEAs for 
high combined strength (high ρ 0) and ductility (low LLD), including 
high-temperature strength retention.

Methods
Machine learning and DFT-based alloy design
bcc phase stability. The bcc phase fractions were calculated using 
a pretrained machine-learning model34 that was developed by us for 
quantitative estimation of phase fractions of fcc, bcc and intermetal-
lic phases in high-entropy alloys. The model uses an ensemble of 150 
neural networks trained on a diverse dataset of MPEAs that span a 
compositional space of 24 elements including 3d transition metals, 
refractory metals and main-group elements. The alloy compositions 
were parameterized using a set of 18 physical and thermodynamic 
parameters and the trained model was validated using experimen -
tal, DFT and CALPHAD results. The pseudoternary plots (Fig. 1a and 
Supplementary Fig. 1) were created by predicting the bcc phase frac-
tions over the entire compositional space using a step size of 1 at.%.
T otal-energy calculations were performed using DFT within the 
Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), using the projector aug-
mented wave method 81, 82 and the Perdew–Burke–-Ernzerhof 
exchange-correlation functional 83 within the generalized gradient 
approximation framework. A 500 eV kinetic energy cut-off and a 
5 × 5 × 5 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh44 were used. Geometry optimi-
zations used the conjugate-gradient method with a 10 −5 eV energy 
convergence threshold and a force criterion of 0.01 eV Å−1. Supercell 
random approximates (SCRAPs) of 16–120 atoms per cell were gener-
ated via a hybrid cuckoo search method84 to model Nb–Ta–V–Ti MPEAs. 
LLD evaluations for stoichiometric NbTaTiV across various supercell 
sizes were tested for finite-size effects, which were found negligible 
beyond 60 atoms. For non-stoichiometric cases, 120-atom SCRAPs 
were required for accurate atomic pair correlations. No significant 
energy or LLD changes were observed beyond this size21. The dimen-
sionless LLD metric, which is based on electronegativity differences in 
localized chemical environments and combines atomic-scale displace-
ments due to local lattice distortions with a weighted average of 
valence-electron count, is a reliable predictor of ductility in 
RMPEAs as described in our previous work 21. The USFE for the 
{110}<111> slip system was calculated using the rule of mixture, 
that is, USFEMPEA = ∑iciUSFEi , where i  = Nb, Ta, Ti or V, and ci is the 
elemental composition85.
SRO calculations. We used a DFT-driven machine-learning framework 
for predicting the Warren–Cowley SRO parameters in complex concen-
trated alloys36,86 (D.B. et al., manuscript in preparation). The methodol-
ogy developed relies on the hypothesis that the manifestation of SRO is 
governed primarily by the atom-pair interactions and that simultane-
ous knowledge of the interaction tendencies of all possible atomic pairs 
in a given alloy can point towards the SRO parameters36. This has been 
implemented by expressing each alloy as a unique three-dimensional 
feature stack that encapsulates the physical and thermodynamic prop-
erties of all atomic pairs. Variational autoencoders are then used to map 
this three-dimensional feature stack to the two-dimensional SRO array 
that contains SRO parameters for all binary pairs within the alloy. The 
machine-learning model, comprising an ensemble of autoencoders, 
is shown to be capable of reliably classifying the type of SRO (ordering 
or clustering) for each binary pair within an alloy.
Materials synthesis
The Nb4Ta4V3Ti1 alloy ingots, weighing ∼120 g, were fabricated from 
high-purity (>99.9 wt%) elements by arc melting in an argon atmos -
phere on a water-cooled cooper hearth. The as-cast ingots were 
homogenized in a high-vacuum furnace (<10 −5 torr) at 1,400 °C for 
24 h, followed by furnace cooling over a 30-min period. Plates with 
dimensions 6 × 10 × 20 mm3 were cut from the as-homogenized ingots, 
and further cold-rolled to a 63% thickness reduction. Recrystallization 
annealing was performed on the cold-rolled plates at 1,200 °C for 24 h, 
followed by oil quenching. Part of the quenched plate was further 
annealed at 1,400 °C for 24 h along with yttrium shavings to reduce 
oxygen concentration, followed by oil quenching. The concentrations 
of oxygen and nitrogen were determined by an inert gas fusion method.
Mechanical testing methods
Rectangular dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with a gauge geom-
etry of 6 × 3 × 0.5 mm3 were sectioned from bulk solid-solution speci-
mens using electrical discharge machining. All sides of the specimens 
were mechanically ground up to 1,200-grit SiC paper before being 
tested. Ambient-temperature uniaxial tensile tests were conducted on 
a zwickiLine table-top testing machine using a 6-mm gauge laser exten-
someter at a strain rate of 10 −3 s−1. For elevated-temperature tensile 
tests, subscale tensile samples with a cross-section of 0.75 × 0.75 mm2 
and a gauge length of 4 mm were used. T o obtain shiny and oxide-free 
specimens, the tests were conducted in a vacuum chamber under a 
vacuum level of <10−7 torr. More information on the details of speci -
men preparation and the ultrahigh-temperature test methodology 
can be found in ref. 78.
High-temperature hardness tests were carried out on an Rtec 
universal MFT-5000 tribometer system in air using a diamond Vickers 
indenter. The surface of the samples for high-temperature hardness 
tests were ground and mechanically polished to a mirror finish. T o 
ensure thermal stability, a 10-min hold time was applied when the 
test temperature was reached before tests. The indentations are deep 
enough at each temperature for the influence of the oxidation layer to 
be negligible. Room-temperature nanoindentation measurements for 
subsequent TEM analyses were performed with a diamond Berkovich 
tip on a Hysitron TI-980 triboindenter. Each indentation was created 
under load control to a force of 5,000 µN with a constant strain-rate 
loading function (target strain rate, 0.1 s−1). The indentation spacing 
was 20 µm, large enough to prevent interactions between neighbouring 
indentations. The specimen surface was prepared by standard grinding 
and polishing procedures, up to 0.05-µm colloidal silica to minimize 
residual surface damage.

Microstructural characterization
Microstructural investigations including backscattered electron imag-
ing, EBSD and energy dispersive spectrometry analysis were conducted 
on an FEI T eneo LoVac scanning electron microscope (SEM). ECCI 
was carried out to characterize the deformation substructure after 
room-temperature tensile test using an FEI Nova NanoSEM 230 SEM. 
Specimens were ground and polished under standard procedures to 
a mirror finish.
An FEI Titan G2 80-200 scanning transmission electron micros-
copy with a Cs probe corrector and ChemiSTEM technology (X-FEG 
and SuperX energy dispersive spectrometer with four windowless 
silicon drift detectors) operated at 200 kV was used for microstruc -
ture characterization in the vicinity of a nanoindentation. The TEM 
specimen was prepared in cross-section by a FIB. For dislocation analy-
sis, an electron probe with a beam convergence angle of <5 mrad 
was used. Bright-field scanning transmission electron microscopy
