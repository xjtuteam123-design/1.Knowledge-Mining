ABSTRACT
The experimental studies of the parameters of hot electric forging were carried out on indus-
trial heat resistance ( α + β), (near- α), and ( α″ + β) titanium alloys. It was established that
simultaneous fast Joule heating with a high-density electric current and an initial temperature
of hot intense plastic deformation are important parameters for the electric forging of heat
resistance titanium alloys. The heating rate dependence on electric current density and
thermophysical properties of metal, as well as deformation temperature dependence on axial
compression stress and mechanical strength of heated metal under compression as the main
parameters of electric forging, were chosen. To calculate the processing parameters, we
investigated the evolution of the thermophysical properties and mechanical strength of se-
lected titanium alloys at a heating rate of 50°C ·s
−1 at a temperature up to 1,200°C.
Microstructural evolution and the phase transformation temperature were determined using
optical and scanning electron microscopes. The results show that the grinding efficiency of the
coarse-grained microstructure depends on the increase of the heating rate, and the formation
of uniaxial ultrafine alpha-phase grains depends on an increase of the compressive stress or
decrease in the deformation temperature. It can be argued that the compressor blades made of
titanium alloys for aircraft turbojet engines had increased ductility, stability, and high-cyclic
fatigue strength, as well as increased operating life at ambient temperatures. The results
can be used to model and calculate the rational parameters of electric forging to improve
the operational properties of the final products.
Keywords
titanium alloys, Joule heating, phase transformation, microstructural evolutions,
plastic deformation, high cycle fatigue, life extension, turbojet engines

Introduction
The most important property of titanium alloys is low thermal conductivity, followed by thermal diffusivity, high
chemical activity, phase transformation, high specific strength, high strength-to-density ratio, limited possibility
of cold deformation, and so on. 1 For example, the thermal conductivity and thermal diffusivity of heat-resistant
titanium alloys are about 15 times less than the thermal conductivity of aluminum alloys and about 5 times lower
than the thermal conductivity of steels.
2 This leads to significant temperature differences across the cross section
of blanks during heating or cooling to the occurrence of internal stresses in the metal and cracking. During electric
contact heating, or Joule heating, 3 the hydrogen in titanium alloys is mobile inside the metal and can lead to
dangerous concentrations of hydrogen in different parts of workpiece and to red fracture during exploitation 4
while temperature increases. The titanium alloys contain a mixture of titanium and different chemical elements
for alloying. Among the elements, titanium can usually be alloyed with iron, aluminum, vanadium, and molyb-
denum.5 Heat-resistant titanium alloys have the highest degree of specific tensile strength and impact strength
with respect to the density among other structural metals and alloys at temperatures between 200 –550°C.5
Lightweight titanium alloys have an unusually high corrosion resistance in seawater. 5 Given the claimed features
of titanium alloys, they are used as structural materials in the manufacturing of compressor blades for aircraft
bypass turbojet engines.
6 During the operation of a turbojet engine, these components (compressor blades) are
heavily loaded under the action of combined stresses: centrifugal stresses during the rotation of the rotor, bending
stresses from compressed air, and torsional stresses caused by the flow of compressed air at operating temper-
atures. The operating temperatures of titanium alloys used for compressor blades in a turbojet engine are in the
range from −60°C to ∼650°C, respectively.
6 In these applications, titanium alloys typically must have high heat
resistance, high strength-to-weight ratio, and high-cycle fatigue (HCF) strength characteristics over their entire
service life. These improved properties of titanium alloys can be obtained during hot working by deformation and
subsequent isothermal or conventional heat treatment.
7–17 It is well known that the microstructure and poly-
morphic phase transformation of titanium alloys depend on the chemical composition and the heating temper-
ature. It is also well known that using fast Joule heating with a high electric current density, though using a low
electric current voltage,
13,18,19 an extremely high heating rate of up to ∼1,000°C·s−1 is ensured. With such param-
eters of the heating rate, the mechanisms of microstructure formation and physical and mechanical properties can
change significantly, and the temperature of the polymorphic ( α + β) → β-phase transformation increases. This
leads to an increase in internal stresses and a refinement of the coarse-grained lamellar microstructure with the
formation of a fine microcrystalline structure in titanium alloys. For example, this phase transformation time of a
titanium alloy is 14 s for isothermal heating in an electric furnace. During Joule heating, the phase transformation
time decreases from 1.3 s at a heating rate of ∼100°C·s
−1 to 0.5 s at a heating rate of ∼250°C·s−1. With an increase
in the heating rate, the temperature of the α-martensitic transformation increases of titanium alloys.
Consequently, the temperature of the polymorphic phase transformation depends on the heating rate and is
associated with a decrease in the mutual diffusion of atoms. For example, it was shown that the limiting temper-
ature of hot processing rises by ∼60°C for ordinary alumina close to α-phase at a heating rate of ∼100°C·s
−1.I ti s
well known that, unlike steel, the preheated microstructure of titanium with a large β-phase cannot be improved
only by heat treatment. 13 This structure can only be changed as a result of the following deformation at temper-
atures of the ( α + β)-phase or only by melting a titanium alloy. A decrease in the heating gradient in the titanium
alloy during electric forging (EF) is associated with the provision of effective conditions for the energy and
thermophysical properties of the contacting metals in the electrodes and with improved machining properties
of the workpiece surface. In this case, the temperature in the deformation region depends on the effective
uniaxial compressive stress and the loss of strength of the rapidly heated metal. In contrast to the isothermal
deformation strength, the ultrashort temporary strength during rapid heating of titanium alloys (during EF) is
much higher.
3,13,18 It is important to understand that during EF, the actual temperature at which the deformation
starts depends mainly on the uniaxial compressive stress. In addition to the EF process in titanium alloys,
other hot processing techniques such as forging, stamping, isothermal forging, additive manufacturing, or

Results and Discussion
THERMODYNAMIC PROPERTIES OF TITANIUM ALLOYS
The thermodynamic properties of titanium alloys were tested within the range of 20°C to 1,200°C and were
determined at heating rate of 50°C ·s−1 of different special samples. It should be highlighted that in the scientific
literature, the thermodynamic properties are usually measured at room temperature. 1,5 In this study, measure-
ments of changes in thermodynamic properties were specified with rapid Joule heating of titanium alloy
samples to 1,200°C. The results of measurements at such temperatures and temperature increase are shown
in figure 2A–F. Later, these properties are used to explain in support of the processing characteristics of titanium
alloys, as well as to calculate the EF process parameters. As can be seen in figure 2A the density of titanium alloys
under investigation linearly decreased with increasing temperature to the minimum values (for [ α + β]t i t a n i u m
alloy ∼4,400 kg·m−3) at the temperature when phase polymorphic transformation starts, and then this temper-
ature sharply increased at a heating temperature of 1,000°C and eventually it decreases. As can be seen, the
density of titanium alloys is about 2 times lower than the steel and about 3.8 times lower than that of pure
tantalum. The heat conductivity ( fig. 2 B) linearly increased about 5 times in this temperature range, at an in-
creasing temperature rate of 50°C ·s−1. The heat diffusivity of titanium alloys ( fig. 2C) is 3.5 times higher com-
pared with steel and 15 times higher than pure aluminum. The heat diffusivity ( fig. 2 C) and the mass heat
capacities ( fig. 2 D) linearly increased to the temperature of 700°C. In the field of phase transformation, these
parameters have contradictory signs. The specific heat capacity ( fig. 2D) increases with increasing temperature
and increases two times near the temperature range of the polymorphic phase transformation. This means that
this phase transition occurs with heat absorption and the process is endothermic. It is common knowledge that
the electrical resistance of titanium alloys is 6 times higher than that of steel and 20 times higher than that of
aluminum alloys.

Conclusions
A large number of experiments using industrial equipment, laboratory installations, and parameter calculations
were carried out to determine the features of microstructure evolution, phase composition, and mechanical-
physical property evolution during rapid Joule heating and hot intense plastic deformation in situ of heat-resistant
titanium alloys. The following main conclusions can be drawn from this experimental work:
(1) It has been established experimentally that during electric forging, the temperature of the onset of de-
formation mainly depends on the axial compressive stress, and the metal heating rate depends on the
electric current density.
(2) With an increase in the heating rate, the temperature of polymorphic phase transformation of titanium
alloys increases.
(3) It was established that implementing the optimal electrical current density for heating and using axial
compressive stress for deformation allowed increasing the ductility and strength of titanium alloys.
