Effect of W on formation and properties of precipitates in Ni-based
superalloys
Zhi-hua Gong 1,2 • Yuan-yuan Ma 1 • Han-sheng Bao 2 • Gang Yang 2
Received: 14 April 2020 / Revised: 4 October 2020 / Accepted: 10 October 2020
/C211China Iron and Steel Research Institute Group 2021
Abstract
The formation and properties of precipitates in wrought Ni-based superalloys with different W contents during long-term
exposure to high temperatures were investigated. The scanning electron microscope, transmission electron microscope, and
chemical phase analysis were used to investigate the formation and properties of precipitates. It is found that with
increasing W content, the quantity and thermal stability of MC carbide in Ni-based superalloys increased, while the
quantity of M
23C6 carbides decreased. As the results show, W has a higher partition coefﬁcient in c0- and c-matrix, and the
addition of W promotes the precipitation of c0 phase. W content has no signiﬁcant effect on the morphology, size, crystal
structure, and coarsening rate of c0 precipitates. The inﬂuence of W content on high-temperature tensile and creep
properties of the alloys was investigated. The results showed that W content has no obvious inﬂuence on the high-
temperature yield strength, but the elongation and area reduction decreased signiﬁcantly when the addition of W was more
than 4 wt.%. Because of the similar volume fractions of c0 phase, the creep fracture strengths in the tested alloys with lower
W concentrations were not signiﬁcantly different after long-term exposure at 700 /C176C.

1 Introduction
Because of the excellent high-temperature creep fracture
properties and stability, the precipitation hardened Ni-
based Waspaloy alloy is widely used for gas turbines,
especially for stationary components such as turbine disks,
blades, and fasteners [ 1–3]. Waspaloy alloy has been
selected as a material for the blades in advanced ultra-
supercritical (A-USC) coal-ﬁred power plants [ 4–6].
Waspaloy alloy is mainly strengthened by precipitation of
nano-sized c
0 particles that have a coherent interface with
c-matrices [ 7], with the main strengthening elements of
chromium (Cr), titanium (Ti), aluminum (Al), cobalt (Co),
and molybdenum (Mo). Waspaloy alloy is used for ﬂue gas
turbine blades with a maximum service life of more than
15 years at a temperature no more than 650 /C176C[ 8, 9].
Waspaloy alloy can be used at service temperatures over
850 /C176C, but the service life in this condition is usually only
a few thousand hours. There is no record of long-time
service at 700 /C176C and above.
Tungsten (W), which could improve the formation of c0
phase and reduce the stacking fault energy of the alloy, is
added to improve the creep fracture strength in some kinds
of Ni-based superalloy for Bao et al. [ 10] reported that the
creep fracture property of Waspaloy alloy can be improved
by adding some W.
For improving the creep fracture strength of the alloys,
we produced and tested three alloys based on Waspaloy
alloy with different W contents, and our goal is to study the
effect of various concentrations of W on the formation and
mechanical properties of precipitates of alloys that might
be used as blades with long-term exposure to temperatures
from 700 to 750 /C176C.
However, excess amount of W added to the alloy can
promote the precipitation of harmful phases such as r and
l and affect the high temperature creep rupture strength
[11]. The main carbides of Waspaloy alloy are MC and

2 Materials and methods
Three Ni-based alloys with different W contents (alloy 1,
alloy 2, and alloy 3) were produced in a vacuum induction
furnace and were cast into 25-kg ingots. The ingots were
re-heated to 1170 /C176C and forged into bars with diameters of
18 mm. Table 1 shows the main chemical compositions of
the three alloys.
Before the thermal exposure and property tests, the
alloys were subjected to a standard heat treatment that
involved heating at 1080 /C176C for 4 h (with oil cooling),
followed by heating at 845 /C176C for 24 h (with air cooling),
and ﬁnally heating at 760 /C176C for 16 h (with air cooling).
For simulating service conditions, the long-time aging
experiment was done after the standard heat treatment. The
different samples were heated at 700 /C176C for 1000, 3000,
5000, or 10,000 h or heated at 750 /C176C for 3000 or 5000 h.
Then, the formation of microstructures and carbides and c
0
evolution were investigated with an optical microscope
(Leica), a scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4300),
and a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Jeol-
2100F). The qualitative and quantitative analyses of the
precipitates after long-term exposure were studied by
chemical phase analysis and X-ray diffraction (Pert MPD).
The high temperature tensile tests with an INSTRON
5582 tensile tester (tensile speed is about 0.25–3.00 mm/
min) were carried out, and the impact tests with a JB-30
Charpy tester were conducted at room temperature. The
samples after exposure were polished for carbide obser-
vation with a solution prepared with 200 mL HCl, 10 g
CuCl
2, and 200 mL ethanol, and samples were electro-
polished for c0 observation in an electrolyte containing
40 vol.% HNO 3 and 60 vol.% water at 2.5–3.5 A and
25 /C176C.
Samples were reduced by hand to less than 50 lmi n
thickness for TEM observation and then electro-polished in
a double jet electro-polishing equipment with an electrolyte
containing 92 vol.% ethyl alcohol and 8 vol.% perchloric
acid at 65 mA or 25 V at -10 to -20 /C176C.
3 Results and discussion
3.1 Formation of carbides
It has been reported that there are two types of MC phases
in Ni-based Waspaloy alloys [ 11, 17]. One is called pri-
mary MC carbide, which has relatively large particles and
forms during smelting. It has an irregular distribution both
inside the grains and on grain boundaries as shown in
Fig. 1. The size of granular primary MC carbides is
5–15 lm. The interface structure of MC phase and c-ma-
trix is disordered, and some holes occur around MC car-
bides. The morphologies of MC phase did not change after
exposure for 10,000 h at 700 /C176C, as Fig. 1b shows. The
secondary MC carbides formed during the heat-treatment
process were nano-sized, as shown in Fig. 2b. There were
very few these carbides, and they could only be observed
by TEM. Figure 1c, d shows the energy dispersion spectra
(EDS) of MC carbide and M
23C6, respectively.
The mass fractions of MC phase in the alloys with dif-
ferent W contents after long-term exposure at 700 and
750 /C176C obtained by chemical phase analysis are displayed
in Fig. 3. It can be seen that more MC separates in the alloy
with higher W content. W and Mo are refractory elements
and part of them can enter MC phase. Table 2 lists the
structural formulas obtained by chemical phase analysis of
MC after exposure for 10,000 h at 700 /C176C. It can be seen
that W and Mo are constituent elements of MC, and W
atom proportion in MC increases with increasing W addi-
tion. The promotion of the precipitation of MC by W may
explain why as more W is added, W concentration in MC
increases, which provides a greater driving force for the
formation of MC. The mass fractions of MC phases
increase slowly with increasing high temperature exposure
time, which can lead to ﬁne secondary MC precipitation at
high temperature (Fig. 2b).
Figure 3 also shows that the precipitation peaks of MC
phase are different in alloys with different W contents.

Figure 6 shows M

different W contents obtained by chemical phase analysis
after long-term exposure at 700 and 750 /C176C. M
23C6 mass

can be seen that fewer M 23C6 carbides precipitated in the
alloy with high W content, and thus, W hindered the pre-
cipitation of M 23C6. The main reasons are as follows. (1)
W promotes the precipitation of MC, which causes more
carbon atoms to be consumed. (2) Excessive addition of W
results in the formation element of M
23C6, e.g., Cr, dif-
fusing more difﬁcultly. Therefore, the precipitation of
M
23C6 is hindered.

3.3 High temperature properties
High temperature property curves of alloys with different
W contents at 700 /C176C after standard heat treatment are
shown in Fig. 11. Figure 11a shows that the tensile
strengths of alloy 1, alloy 2, and alloy 3 are 1090, 1110,
and 1190 MPa, respectively. As W content in the alloy
increases from 1.97% to 7.92%, the change in tensile
strength is not obvious, but further increase in W content
increases the tensile strength signiﬁcantly. The yield
strengths of alloys with different W contents are essentially
the same. Figure 11b shows the ductility and area reduction
of alloys with different W contents. It can be seen that the
alloy 1 and alloy 2 have better elongation and area
reduction than alloy 3.
The fracture graphs of alloy 1 and alloy 3 after high
temperature tensile tests at 700 /C176C are shown in Fig. 12.
The fracture images consist of characteristics of cleavage
fracture and ductile fracture. The fracture images of alloy 1
are shown in Fig. 12a, b. There are some cavities and
planes in Fig. 12b, which indicates that cracks initiated
from small cavities and grain boundaries. The report has
shown that some precipitates like initial d phase increase
the density of nucleus of microvoids and promote the
formation of microvoids during the hot tensile deformation.
The small cavities are the places where the cracks initiate,
and the planes are related to cleavage plane [ 24–26]. There
are some dimples in the fracture surface.
A comparison of Fig. 12b, d shows that the fracture
surface of alloy 1 has more dimples than that of alloy 3, so
that alloy 1 has greater plasticity than alloy 3.
Figure 13a, b shows the metallographic structures of the
holding side and the deformation side of the high temper-
ature tensile samples, respectively. The microstructure of
the holding side kept the original austenite morphology
during the test, but some carbides precipitated in the grains
and at grain boundaries. Figure 13b shows the micrograph
of the fracture zone of samples for the tensile test. It shows
that the grains were distorted, and some cracks occurred at
the grain boundaries. The main reason is that the carbides
precipitated at the grain boundaries where they were
arranged in chains. This morphology weakens the pinning
effect of the precipitation on the grain boundary, so that the
grain boundaries become channels for crack propagation.
The work described in Sect. 3.1 showed that more MC
phases precipitated in alloy 3 than in the other two alloys,
and these precipitated phases partially appeared at the grain
boundaries (see Fig. 1), which promotes the formation and
propagation of cracks.

4 Conclusion
The increased addition of W will promote the precipitation
of MC phase. MC phase with higher W content has a better
thermal stability during high temperature exposure. The
addition of W hinders the precipitation of M
23C6. The
addition of W promotes the precipitation of c0 phase for W
has high partition coefﬁcients for c0 and c. The effect of W
content on the high temperature yield strength of the alloy
is not obvious. There has no signiﬁcant difference in creep
fracture properties between alloy 1 and alloy 2, but alloy 1
has lower W content and better hot working performance
and is less expensive than alloy 2. Therefore, alloy 1 is the
best candidate for A-USC steam blades.
