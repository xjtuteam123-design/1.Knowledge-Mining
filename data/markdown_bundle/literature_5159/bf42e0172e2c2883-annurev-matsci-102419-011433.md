Abstract
The mechanical properties of superalloys are strongly governed by the re-
sistance to shearing of ordered precipitates by dislocations.In the operating
environments of superalloys, the stresses and temperatures present during
thermomechanical loading influence the dislocation shearing dynamics,
which involve diffusion and segregation processes that result in a diverse
array of planar defects in the orderedL1
2 γ′precipitate phase. This review
discusses the current understanding of high-temperature deformation
mechanisms of γ′ precipitates in two-phase Ni-, Co-, and CoNi-base
superalloys.Thesensitivityofplanarfaultenergiestochemicalcomposition
results in a variety of unique deformation mechanisms, and methods to
determine fault energies are therefore reviewed. The degree of chemical
segregation in the vicinity of planar defects reveals an apparent phase
transformation within the parent γ′ phase. The kinetics of segregation
to linear and planar defects play a significant role in high-temperature
properties.Understanding and controlling fault energies and the associated
dislocation dynamics provide a new pathway for the design of superalloys
with exceptional properties.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Background
With exceptional mechanical properties and resistance to environmental degradation up to tem-
peratures corresponding to 85% of the melting point, Ni-base alloys operate under severe ther-
momechanical conditions in aircraft engines and land-based energy generation turbines (1–3).
These alloys’ high resistance to deformation,particularly under creep and fatigue conditions,de-
rives from the presence of a high volume fraction of coherent orderedL12 intermetallic phase.
Recently, Co-base and CoNi-base alloys, also strengthened by high volume fractions of theL12
phase, have been under development (4–9). The typical microstructures of these alloys consist
of two phases: orderedL12 γ′precipitates (often cuboidal with an edge length between 0.3 and
0.5 μm and a volume fraction of>60%) coherently embedded within aγ matrix phase [a face-
centered-cubic (fcc) solid solution]. These materials typically contain a high number of alloying
elements; examples are given inT able 1. The highest temperature resistance is achieved when
thesematerialsaregrownassinglecrystals(SXs)bytheBridgmantechnique(Ni-SXandCo-SX)
(10–13).Since SXs represent good model materials for fundamental plasticity studies,this article
primarily focuses on SX variants.
