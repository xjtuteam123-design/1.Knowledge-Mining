1. Introduction
Structural materials for high-temperature components in aerospace, 
nuclear energy, and defense applications are subject to increasingly 
stringent performance requirements [ 1 ]. Components operating in 
ultra-high-temperature environments, such as hot-end structural com -
ponents in hypersonic vehicles and the combustion chambers of liquid 
rocket engines, are required to retain sufficient mechanical strength 
above 1600
◦
C, with peak service temperatures approaching 2200
◦
C, 
while also maintaining adequate room-temperature deformability for 
processing [ 2 , 3 ]. However, the long-standing conflict between 
high-temperature strength and room-temperature damage tolerance has 
severely constrained the development and deployment of such materials 
[ 4 , 5 ]. Ni-based superalloys, the workhorse materials for 
high-temperature applications, typically have maximum use tempera -
tures below 1200
◦
C and thus cannot satisfy ultra-high-temperature de -
mands [ 6 ]. Refractory Ta- and W-based alloys, although possessing 
extremely high melting points, undergo pronounced softening at 
elevated temperatures because of accelerated atomic diffusion [ 7 , 8 ]. 
Carbon/carbon (C/C) composites and ultra-high-temperature ceramics 
(UHTCs) suffer from catastrophic oxidation and intrinsic brittleness with 
poor thermal-shock resistance, respectively [ 9 – 11 ]. Refractory 
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), benefiting from multi-principal-element 
lattice distortion, have emerged as promising alternatives owing to

In carbide-strengthened refractory medium- and high-entropy alloys, 
the second-phase carbides typically adopt two crystal structures: hex -

Compared with MC-type carbides such as TaC and HfC, M
2

(for example, Ta

to the presence of metallic bonding in M
2

shear modulus and more active slip systems than in MC carbides, 
whereas MC-type carbides usually provide superior high-temperature 
strength [ 26 – 30 ]. Previous studies on Ta-W-based refractory 
medium-entropy alloys (RMEAs) containing carbides have shown that 
divorced eutectic microstructures comprising a BCC-M
2
C network 
formed at low carbon content (C:Ta < 0.25) deliver the best balance 
between high-temperature strength and room-temperature plasticity, 
although their resistance to high-temperature softening remains limited 
[ 27 , 31 ]. Incorporating Hf facilitates the preferential formation of 
MC-type carbides via strong Hf-C bonding [ 32 , 33 ], thereby markedly 
enhancing the high-temperature strength of the divorced eutectic alloy. 
Moreover, multicomponent (Hf,Ta)C carbides can deform via the for -
mation of stacking faults, thereby preventing significant impairment of 
the overall alloy plasticity [ 34 , 35 ]. These metal-carbide divorced 
eutectic alloys exhibit an interpenetrating composite morphology [ 36 ], 
in which primary carbides solidify into a three-dimensional continuous 
network that percolates throughout the metallic matrix. The ceramic 
skeleton bears the majority of the load, whereas the metallic matrix 
bridges and blunts cracks in the carbides, enabling cooperative 
strengthening and yielding excellent high-temperature strength while 
retaining plasticity. Rapid cooling can further induce the secondary 
precipitation of plate-like MC carbides from supersaturated carbon in 
the BCC matrix, forming coherent interfaces that strengthen the alloy 
[ 37 , 38 ]. Such MC carbides can relieve local stress concentrations 
through shearing associated with stacking-fault formation, thereby 
avoiding brittle fracture and enabling alloys with good 
room-temperature compressive plasticity and outstanding 
high-temperature strength.

2.2. Microstructural characterization and mechanical property testing
Samples for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis were sequentially 
ground with coarse-to-fine grit sandpapers, followed by electrolytic 
polishing to eliminate surface stress and strain layers. Samples for 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron channeling contrast 
imaging (ECCI) were prepared by standard mechanical grinding and 
polishing procedures, and finally etched in an acid solution (hydroflu -
oric acid: nitric acid: deionized water = 1:2:2) for 30 s. Electron back- 
scattered diffraction (EBSD) samples were subjected to mechanical 
grinding and subsequent mechanical vibratory polishing. Transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) samples were first mechanically ground 
down to 80- μ m-thick foils and then processed by ion thinning.
XRD was performed using a Bruker D8 diffractometer (Bruker D8, 
Bruker Corporation, Germany) to identify the crystal structures of the 
alloys. The scan range was from 30
◦
to 120

at a rate of 2
◦

XRD experiments were performed using a high-temperature chamber 
equipped with a tungsten heating strip (HTK 2000 N, Anton Paar GmbH, 
Austria). The microstructures and chemical compositions were exam -
ined using SEM (SUPRA55, ZEISS Corporation, Germany; SEM5000, 
CIQTEK, China) coupled with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS). 
ECCI experiments were conducted using a dedicated backscattered 
electron (BSE) detector attached to a field-emission SEM (JSM-7900F, 
JEOL, Japan). EBSD experiments were conducted using a SEM (JSM- 
7001F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an EBSD detector. The EBSD step 
size was 0.6 μ m, while the accelerating voltage was 20 kV, and the 
working distance was 16 mm. EBSD data were analyzed using OIM 
Analysis software. Additionally, the microstructure and crystallography 
of the alloys were characterized by TEM (JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan; 
Spectra 200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

3.3. Fracture morphology and microstructure of the alloy after 
compressive deformation
As shown in Fig. 8 , the fracture surface of the room-temperature 
compressed specimen exhibits a pronounced lamellar fracture 
morphology. The carbides do not display the typical brittle fracture 
features commonly associated with ceramic phases (e.g., flat cleavage 
facets or intergranular separation). Instead, their fracture surfaces are 
dominated by lamellar delamination accompanied by wrinkled tearing 
features, giving rise to a MAX-phase-like laminated fracture appearance.

are also observed, suggesting that these carbides retain a certain degree 
of plastic deformability even at room temperature. These observations 
suggest that the M

possess a certain capability for deformation accommodation at room

compression ( Fig. 8 ) shows that the fracture surfaces of the carbides

and wrinkled tearing features are observed, giving rise to a laminated

dense dislocation structures are observed within the skeletal M

carbide network can blunt and bridge locally cracked carbide segments

purely carbide-controlled brittle failure process. As a result, the skeletal

mation. Under external loading, the local stress at these coherent or

strain transfer between the carbides and the matrix [ Fig. 11 (a)]. During

5. Conclusion
In this study, a divorced-eutectic carbide-reinforced refractory 
medium-entropy alloy, Re
0.1
Hf
0.25
Ta
1.85
W
0.2
C
0.25
, was developed to 
achieve a balance between high-temperature strength and room- 
temperature compressive plasticity. The main conclusions are summa -
rized as follows: 
(1) The alloy exhibits a multiscale carbide-reinforced microstructure 
consisting of a BCC metallic matrix, a continuous three- 
dimensional M
2
C-HCP carbide skeleton, and dispersed plate- 
like MC-FCC carbides. The skeletal M
2
C carbides are mainly 
Ta
2
C-based carbides with Hf substitution, while the plate-like MC 
precipitates are identified as multicomponent (Hf,Ta)C carbides.
(2) The alloy achieves a true yield strength of 1329 MPa and a true 
compressive fracture strain of 19.6% at room temperature, and 
retains a true yield strength of 760 MPa and a true compressive 
strength of 910 MPa at 1450
◦
C, demonstrating an excellent bal -
ance between room-temperature compressive plasticity and 
elevated-temperature strength.
(3) The skeletal M
2
C carbides exhibit a certain degree of plastic 
deformation capability, contributing to strain accommodation 
during compression. The dispersed plate-like MC-FCC pre -
cipitates further act as dislocation barriers and accommodate 
local strain through stacking-fault-mediated slip.
(4) The combination of a continuous load-bearing M

shearable MC plate-like carbides provides an effective strategy 
for mitigating the strength – plasticity trade-off in carbide- 
strengthened refractory alloys.
