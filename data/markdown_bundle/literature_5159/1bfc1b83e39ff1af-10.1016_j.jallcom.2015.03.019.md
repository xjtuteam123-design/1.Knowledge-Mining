abstract
The mechanical properties of powder metallurgy (P/M) Ti–6Al–4V alloys, produced from prealloyed pow-
ders by hot isostatic pressing, were evaluated over the intermediate temperature range of 300–500 /C176C. A
fully equiaxed or lamellar structure was observed depending on the processing temperature.
Microstructural changes were attributed to the beta-transus temperature, predicted as 977 /C176C. As the test
temperature increased from 300 to 500 /C176C, the strength of the Ti alloys decreased and their elongation
increased, regardless of their initial microstructure. Over the entire temperature range tested, the
strength and ductility of equiaxed phase morphologies were higher than those of the lamellar structure.
In addition, ﬁner equiaxed structures exhibited higher strength and lower ductility than coarser
morphologies. These results were attributed to the initial microstructures of the P/M Ti–6Al–4V alloy
samples, as conﬁrmed by evaluating their fracture features.

1. Introduction
In military applications, titanium alloy has attracted more
attention than other metallic materials with regard to its capability
to balance the ‘iron triangle’: i.e., performance, payload and protec-
tion [1]. It is attributed to the high strength-to-weight ratio and
durability under extreme conditions, in addition to its overall
superior corrosion resistance and exceptional elevated tempera-
ture performance [2]. However, despite these favorable properties,
titanium is currently utilized mostly in niche applications due to
its high raw material and manufacturing costs [3–5]. For example,
in the titanium alloy airframe fabrication process for the Boeing
787 aircraft, approximately 83% of the titanium is discarded in
the form of machining chips [6]. Several near-net shape (NNS) fab-
rication technologies (e.g., powder injection molding (PIM), hot
isostatic pressing (HIPping), powder forging (P/F), additive manu-
facturing, and cold isostatic pressing (CIP)/sintering) have been
incorporated with powder metallurgy (P/M) approaches to
improve efﬁciency and cost [7–9].
Of these fabrication processes, HIPping is beneﬁcial for large-
scale production of complex-shaped structural components [9].
To apply this process in industrial ﬁelds, the optimal HIPping
parameters and their inﬂuence on the properties of the ﬁnal pro-
duct must be identiﬁed. Several studies have investigated the
effect of the processing parameters (e.g., temperature, and pres-
sure) on microstructural and mechanical properties of Ti alloy at
room temperature [10–13]. Zhang et al. [13] improved the fatigue
properties of P/M Ti alloy by controlling the processing conditions.
In addition, numerical simulation studies have attempted to pre-
dict the changes in geometric shape of Ti alloy powders during
HIPping [14–16].
It is well known that the usage of titanium alloy has an upper
temperature limit of 500 /C176C for its safe service temperature range,
due to the substantial loss in mechanical strength and severe oxi-
dation embrittlement that occur at elevated temperatures [17].
However, the full potential of P/M titanium alloy products in real
applications at intermediate temperatures (approaching /C24 500 /C176C)
has yet to be determined.
This study was performed to investigate the inﬂuence of
HIPping temperature on the microstructures and mechanical prop-
erties in the intermediate temperature range of P/M Ti–6Al–4V
alloys. Earlier studies of alloys prepared from various types of pre-
alloyed powders indicated that their microstructures are closely
correlated with their mechanical properties at room temperature.
In the present investigation, P/M Ti alloy components were pre-
pared from plasma atomized powder, and the mechanical proper-
ties were evaluated in the intermediate temperature range of 300–
500 /C176C. The inﬂuence of the microstructure on the mechanical

2. Experimental
Prealloyed Ti–6Al–4V powders were prepared by a plasma atomization process,
and were of high purity compared with powder produced using other techniques
[18] or the standard values (AMS4998) [19]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
images revealed that the powder particles were spherical ( Fig. 1(a)). Fig. 1(b) shows
optical microscopy (OM) images of the microstructure of the powders, revealing an
alpha-prime (martensite) phase, which was attributed to rapid solidiﬁcation during
atomization. The mean particle size was 28.08
lm, and Tables 1 and 2 describe the
shape and quality of the powders and their detailed characteristics, respectively.
The general HIPping process was conducted as follows. The prealloyed powders
were placed in a custom-designed stainless steel capsule for the HIPping procedure
(capsule diameter: 25 mm; capsule height: 100 mm); stainless steel was chosen
due to its inert properties with respect to the raw powder material, Ti alloy. The
vessel underwent degassing at 200 /C176C to remove vapor and contaminants from
the powder. After degassing, crimping and welding of one end of the stem were car-
ried out until the vacuum level in the capsule reached 10
/C0 2 Torr. The samples were
then heated to 880, 930 and 980 /C176C, and pressurized up to 1000 bar, at a heating
rate of 5 /C176C min/C0 1 in phase. Hereafter, the HIPped specimens are designated as
HT-1, HT-2 and HT-3, corresponding to processing temperatures of 880, 930 and
980 /C176C, respectively. Following HIP, the containers were removed and the speci-
mens were machined via an electron beam discharge process. Given a theoretical
density of is 4.43 g/cm
3 for Ti–6Al–4V, the relative density of the HIPped samples
was measured using Archimedes’ principle. The microstructure and crys-
tallographic orientation of the specimens were analyzed by OM and electron
backscattered diffraction (EBSD), respectively.
Cylindrical tensile specimens were prepared from HIPped billets by a machining
process based on the ASTM E 8M standard [20]. Tensile tests were carried out at
room temperature and over the intermediate temperature range of 300–500 /C176C
under and atmosphere of Ar, using a testing machine with an attached resis-
tance-heated furnace (Instron, Norwood, MA). The temperature was controlled by
a thermocouple placed in contact with the specimens. The testing conditions fol-
lowed ASTM E 8M [20] and E 32 [21] speciﬁcations for room temperature and
intermediate temperature measurements, respectively. Following testing, the frac-
tured surface and its cross-sectional area were examined using SEM and OM,
respectively.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructures of HIPped samples
As described in the previous section, the prealloyed powders
were HIPped between 880 and 980 /C176C. For all temperature condi-
tions, the relative densities of the HIPped samples were within
99.9 wt.% of the theoretical density. Fig. 2 shows the microstruc-
tural evolution with processing temperature; two different
microstructures developed over the processing temperature range.
HT-1 and HT-2 specimens, corresponding at 880 and 930 /C176C,
respectively, revealed equiaxed and lathlike alpha phases with
intergranular beta phases, as shown in Fig. 2 (a) and (b), respec-
tively. In addition, as the HIPping temperature increased from
880 to 930 /C176C, the alpha phases were coarsened from 3.90 to
5.42 lm. In contrast, the samples HIPped at 980 /C176C (HT-3) exhib-
ited fully lamellar structures, composed of the alpha lamellae,
alpha colonies and alpha layers at beta grain boundaries. The
individual microstructures of the HIPped specimens evolve as fol-
lows. For fully equiaxed phase morphologies (HT-1 and HT-2),
according to Zhang et al. [13], equiaxed alpha grains are mainly
generated at previously existing particle boundaries and at grain
boundaries inside the powders, a lathlike structure forms in the
central region of the particles. In this case, considerable deforma-
tion occurred locally at the boundaries, resulting in recrystalliza-
tion and the creation of newly equiaxed alpha phases. Note that
the original microstructure of powders was alpha prime marten-
site due to rapid cooling during processing ( Fig. 1 (b)). This initial
structure transforms into the equiaxed alpha phase at particle
boundaries and lathlike one at non-boundaries regions. Thus, the
newly created phases would coarsen with increases in the
HIPping temperature. In contrast, in fully lamellar structures
(HT-3), the alpha phase nucleates initially at beta grain boundaries,
leading to continuous alpha layers along the grain boundaries. In
addition, the alpha phase creates continuous alpha layers that
grow into the beta grain; this process is referred to as sympathetic
nucleation and growth. As a result, individual alpha plates form,

appear to be associated with the beta transus temperature.

beta phase (BCC) at higher temperatures to the alpha one (HCP)
at lower temperatures. It is well known that such a transformation
is strongly inﬂuenced by alloying and interstitial elements.

sus temperature for a given alloy composition. Sha et al. [22] calcu-
lated the temperature of the specimens using a linear regression

3.2. Mechanical properties of HIPped samples at intermediate
temperatures
Fig. 4 shows the tensile behavior of the HT-2 specimen over the
test temperature range of 300–500 /C176C; as the temperature
increased, the strength of the sample decreased and the elongation
increased. The response of the HT-2 sample with increasing tem-
perature may be associated with thermally activated processes
that assist in deformation and reduction of strength at elevated
temperatures. Fig. 5 shows the yield and tensile stresses as a func-
tion of the test temperature for fully equiaxed (HT-1 and HT-2) and
lamellar structures (HT-3). Irrespective of their initial microstruc-
tures, the yield and tensile stresses declined continuously as the
temperature increased from room temperature to 500 /C176C; for
example, the yield strength of HT-1 specimens decreased from
845 to 420 MPa as the temperature increased to 500 /C176C. In general,
over the entire temperature range tested, ﬁne equiaxed
microstructures (HT-1) exhibited higher yield and tensile stresses
than coarse equiaxed structures (HT-2) or lamellar phase morphol-
ogy (HT-3), demonstrating the hardening effect resulting from
grain size reﬁnement. However, the difference in the stresses
became less pronounced as the temperature increased, so the tem-
perature dependence was weaker at higher temperatures.

In contrast, elongation increased continuously with increasing
test temperature for all specimens as illustrated in Fig. 6 . Above
400 /C176C, the slope of the strain versus temperature curves increased,
compared with that observed between room temperature and
400 /C176C. These observations indicated a stronger temperature
dependence of the fracture strain at higher temperature. In addi-
tion, the ductility of the fully equiaxed structures was higher than
that of the fully lamellar samples. Microstructures with ﬁner
equiaxed morphologies exhibited a slightly lower strain than those
of coarser ones; this result was attributed to the nucleation and
growth of voids during deformation. According to Schwalbe et al.
[23] and Kanamori et al. [24], most of the voids in Ti alloys nucleate
at the interface between alpha and beta phases, suggesting that
increasing the interface areas would lead to an increase in the
number of voids and prohibition of their growth. Therefore, reﬁned
microstructures may exhibit higher strength and lower ductility
compared with coarser structures. In this study, the HT-1 specimen
with a ﬁne equiaxed structure had a larger interface area than the
HT-2 sample with coarser phase morphology. Thus, during defor-
mation, void formations in the HT-1 specimens may have become
activated resulting in higher strength and lower ductility than HT-
2. Moreover, since incremental temperature increase promotes
void growth, the fracture strain increased with the test
temperature.
The tensile stress and elongation at ambient temperature of
various Ti–6Al–4V alloys are shown in Fig. 7. Compared to the data
MIL-STD [25] provided, ﬁne equiaxed microstructures (HT-1) exhi-
bit higher strength and elongation over entire test temperature
ranges, unlike HT-2 and HT-3. This indicates that smaller equiaxed
alpha phases might be beneﬁcial to apply in high-temperature
structural components. Moreover, it was also found that the
mechanical properties of powder injection molded (PIMed) Ti–
6Al–4V alloys [26] were lower than those of HT-1. This might be
due to the residual porosity of the PIMed alloys. Consequently,
we conﬁrmed the possibility of the usage of the alloy, HT-1, devel-
oped by our studies in elevated temperature conditions.
3.3. Fracture microstructures of HIPped samples
Fig. 8 shows the low-magniﬁcation fractographs of HIPed speci-
mens tested at 300 /C176C. These fractographs exhibited cup-and-cone
fracture, which was divided into two regions: dimple structures in
the central region (A) and a shear-lip region (B). The ductile ‘cup-
and-cone’ fracture can develop under various conditions. For
example, many ﬁne cavities form in the region where necking
begins, at the point of plastic instability. The cavities grow and
coalesce into a central crack. This crack grows in a direction per-
pendicular to the axis of the specimen until it approaches the sur-
face of the specimen (formation of the ‘cub’ part). Subsequent
propagation of the crack occurs along with localized shear plane
formation at 45 /C176with respect to the axis (formation of ‘cone’ part).
The P/M titanium parts exhibited ductile fracture via crack growth,
which was accompanied by void coalescence. In addition, the for-
mation of dimple structure was related to the number and size
of voids.

4. Conclusions
We investigated microstructural changes in HIPped P/M Ti–
6Al–4V alloys with various HIPping temperatures, and the effects
of such changes on their mechanical properties in the intermediate
temperature range from 300 to 500 /C176C. The major results can be
summarized as follows.

(1) The alloys exhibited fully equiaxed structures (HT-1 and HT-
2) or lamellar phase morphologies (HT-3), depending on the
HIPping temperature. It is found that the microstructural
changes were strongly associated with the beta transus tem-
perature, btr, predicted as 977 /C176C based on the chemical com-
position of the powders used in this study. Thus, the transus
temperature was dependent on the purity of the alloys.
(2) The yield and tensile strengths of the alloys declined and the
elongation increased with increasing test temperature from
300 to 500 /C176C. Over the entire temperature range, the
equiaxed phase morphologies (HT-1 and HT-2) exhibited
higher strength and ductility than the lamellar structures
(HT-3). Moreover, in the intermediate temperature range,
the alloy with a ﬁner equiaxed structure (HT-1) exhibited
higher strength and lower ductility than the coarser samples
(HT-2).
(3) It is found that the alloys showed ductile ‘cup-and-cone’
fractures. For the equiaxed phase morphologies (HT-1 and
HT-2), typical dimple structures were observed and their
size increased with increasing phase size. In contrast, the
fully lamellar structures showed a mixture of ﬁne and coarse
dimples and cracking, indicating lower ductility compared
with equiaxed ones. Moreover, the fracture mechanism
was associated with void nucleation and growth. Such void
behavior was conﬁrmed by cross-sectional examination of
the fractured specimens.
