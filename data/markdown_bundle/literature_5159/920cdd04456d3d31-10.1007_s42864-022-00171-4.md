Abstract
Heat-resistant ferritic stainless steels are widely used in many high-temperature applications such as power plants, automo-
tive exhaust manifolds and solid oxide fuel cell interconnects due to their low price, low coefficient of thermal expansion, 
high thermal conductivity, high thermal fatigue resistance, high creep performance and excellent corrosion resistance. 
High-temperature strength, formability, high-temperature oxidation resistance and creep performance are the main evalua-
tion criteria for the application. With the development of relevant industries, higher requirements are proposed for the per -
formance of ferritic stainless steels. Therefore, the development of a new generation of heat-resistant ferritic stainless steel 
has received extensive attention. In this presentation, we summarized the research progress of heat-resistant ferritic stain-
less steels including high-temperature strength, formability, high-temperature oxidation resistance and creep performance. 
Meanwhile, some suggestions are given for alloy composition design and microstructure optimization. The future research 
direction of heat-resistant ferritic stainless steels also prospected.

1 Introduction
With the development of metallurgical technologies, the 
properties of ferritic stainless steels, such as toughness and 
corrosion resistance, have been significantly improved by 
reducing the carbon and nitrogen content to a relatively low 
level [1–3]. Ferritic stainless steels possess excellent weld-
ability, electrical conductivity, corrosion resistance, ther -
mal conductivity and thermal fatigue performance [4 –6]. 
Compared with austenitic stainless steels, ferritic stainless 
steels contain little or no Ni, so their cost is lower. Low-
cost ferritic stainless steels are widely used in household 
appliances, architectural structures, automotive exhaust sys-
tems, solid oxide fuel cells and key components in industries 
[7], accounting for 40–50% of total stainless steel filed [8 ]. 
Therefore, ferritic stainless steels have the potential to be 
major economical stainless steels.
To protect the environment, severe environmental laws 
on automotive exhaust emissions have recently been imple-
mented worldwide. To meet the emission standards, automo-
tive manufacturers have made great efforts to enhance fuel 
efficiency and clean exhaust gas. For example, the exhaust 
gas temperature was increased to 950–1150 °C to meet the 
emission standard [9 ]. The exhaust manifold is the com-
ponent closest to the engine and as such, it is repeatedly 
subjected to high exhaust gas temperatures. Excellent heat-
resistant properties, especially thermal fatigue resistance, 
are necessary for this component [9 , 10]. In addition, basic 
research and more technology-oriented research have been 
conducted using iron-based alloys specially designed for 
solid oxide fuel cell applications to replace commercially 
expensive ceramic interconnects. The operation temperature 
of the solid oxide fuel cell interconnect between 600 and 
900 °C necessitates good mechanical strength and creep 
resistance in high-temperature service, as well as a low 
coefficient of thermal expansion [11– 13]. Ferritic stainless 
steels are superior to austenitic stainless steels in cyclic oxi-
dation resistance and thermal fatigue resistance due to the 
low coefficient of thermal expansion [14–16]. Furthermore,

The grain boundary region contained a large number of

be efficiently increased via precipitating a high density of 
fine intergranular precipitates. Fine intergranular precipi-

thus slowing down the creep rate. The other strengthening 
mechanism commonly used to increase the high-tempera-
ture strength is solid solution strengthening. Furthermore, 
some studies have indicated that at elevated temperatures, 
the effects of precipitation strengthening are smaller and 
solid solution strengthening is stronger [10, 22]. The high-
temperature strength of ferritic stainless steels is mainly con-
tributed by precipitation strengthening and solid solution 
strengthening. At a certain elevated temperature, solid solu-
tion strengthening is time-independent and the precipitation 
strengthening effect will be reduced due to the coarsening 
of precipitates. As a result, the coarsening of precipitates 
is primarily responsible for the degradation in strength at 
elevated temperatures.

enhanced high-temperature strength of Nb alloyed ferritic

of Nb and the precipitation strengthening of Nb-based Laves 
phase precipitates and Nb(C, N) precipitates [25–27]. Nb is 
known to be the most potential Laves phase forming element 
[28]. The precipitation of Laves phase can inhibit the grain 
boundary migration and avoid excessive grain growth during 
high-temperature aging.

Alloy design with Nb, Ti and Mo additions is a good way to 
improve the high-temperature strength of ferritic stainless

Adding W to ferritic stainless steel can enhance the 
creep rupture strength due to the precipitation strengthen-
ing of the Fe2W Laves phase and fine MC carbides [32]. 
The addition of W can also improve the high-temperature 
strength and creep properties of Nb-alloyed ferritic stain-
less steel [ 12, 20]. That is to say, the combined addition 
of Nb and W can effectively enhance the high-temperature 
strength of ferritic stainless steels. As shown in Fig.  5, the 
high-temperature tensile strength of Nb-containing ferritic 
stainless steels increases with the W content. The higher 
tensile strength of Nb-W alloyed steels at elevated tem-
peratures results from the formation of Laves phase and 
the solid-solution strengthening effect of W. Laves phase 
preferentially precipitated at the grain boundaries and was 
uniformly dispersed within the grains after aging. Com-
pared to Mo and W, Nb had a higher diffusion coefficient 
to participate in forming Laves phase, which accelerated 
the coarsening of Laves phase. Therefore, Nb was mainly 
responsible for the coarsening of Laves phase particles in 
ferritic stainless steels containing Nb, Mo and/or W during 
aging, especially at higher temperatures [20]. According 
to the above results, it is suggested that the content of 
Nb should be reduced to suppress the fast coarsening of 
Laves phase.
Crofer 22 H steel is also a typical example of the com-
bination of Nb and W addition to ferritic stainless steel 
to increase the high-temperature strength, and it is usu-
ally used as an interconnector in solid oxide fuel cells. 
Crofer 22H steel was developed by adding 0.51 wt.% Nb 
and 1.94 wt.% W to Crofer 22 APU. As shown in Fig.  6, 
Crofer 22H steel has better high-temperature strength than 
Crofer 22APU steel, which is mainly attributed to the pre-

the dramatic coarsening of Laves phase. Kuhn et al. [13] 
investigated the effects of Nb, W and Si addition on the 
properties of Crofer 22 APU, and they found that small 
additions of Nb, W and Si greatly increase the creep resist -
ance. This can be attributed to the combination of solid 
solution strengthening and precipitation strengthening by 
Laves phase.

3 Formability
Compared to austenitic stainless steels, ferritic stainless 
steels have lower formability, which limits their applica-
tion. Therefore, much research work has been carried out 
to improve the formability of ferritic stainless steels. The 
formability of ferritic stainless steels can be improved by 
increasing the plastic strain ratio (r -value) and decreasing 
anisotropy Δr , which are closely related to γ -fiber recrys-
tallization texture [33– 35].
Representative methods of controlling γ-fiber recrystal -
lization texture include lowering the contents of C and N 
[36], adding stabilizing elements [37– 39], optimizing the 
hot rolling process [40– 42], cold rolling process [43, 44] 
and annealing process [45, 46]. Interstitial C and N atoms 
have been found to be detrimental to the deep drawa -
bility of ferritic stainless steels during the deformation 
process [6 , 47]. Furthermore, one major disadvantage of 
ferritic stainless steels is their low intergranular corro-
sion resistance, which is caused by Cr depletion zones 
next to grain boundaries due to the formation of chro -
mium carbides or nitrides [37]. Lowering the contents of 
C and N is an effective way to avoid the aforementioned 
drawbacks. Yazawa et al. [36] investigated the effects of 
C content and N content on the r -value, and they revealed 
that r-value increased with decreasing C and N contents. 
It is further observed that the improvement in formability 
is more pronounced with the reduction in C content, while 
the reduction in N has relatively little effect. However, it 
should be noted that decreasing C and N content may lead 
to a reduction in the number of effective nucleation par -
ticles during solidification. As a result of the reduction in 
the number of nucleation particles, the grain in ultra-pure 
ferritic stainless steels became coarser, which was detri-
mental to ridging resistance [48, 49]. It is suggested that 
C content should be reduced as much as possible, while 
N content should be maintained at an appropriate level.
The other way to eliminate the negative effects of C and 
N in ferritic stainless steels is to add stabilizing alloying 
elements Ti and Nb [37–39]. It is widely acknowledged that 
the addition of Ti and/or Nb to ferritic stainless steels can 
improve deep drawing by consuming interstitial C and/or 
N atoms [ 37]. Du et al. [ 39] found that the formability of 
SUS430 stainless steel can be improved by adding stabiliz-
ing elements Ti and Nb, this is because the addition of Ti 
and Nb promotes the development of {111} texture. SUS430 
stainless steel is usually used to manufacture automobile 
exhaust system. Shu et al. [50] investigated the effect of Ti 
addition on the formability of Nb-bearing ferritic stainless 
steel. They found that the recrystallization γ -fiber shifted 
from {111} 〈110〉 to {111} 〈 112 〉 due to Ti addi-
tion, resulting in higher r-value and lower Δr -value. That 
is, Nb + Ti dual stabilized stainless steel has better form-
ability than Nb single stabilized stainless steel. During dif-
ferent stages of recrystallization, a comparison was made 
between the Nb stabilized ferritic stainless steel 430Nb and 
the Nb + Ti stabilized ferritic stainless steel type ASTM 439 
[51]. The result shows that the Nb + Ti stabilized ferritic 
stainless steel displayed better drawability compared with 
the Nb stabilized ferritic stainless steel due to the lower 
amounts of C and N.
Besides Ti and Nb, other alloying elements such as Al, 
Sn, Cr, etc. are also added to ferritic stainless steels to 
improve formability. Al is suitable as a heterogeneous nucle-
ating particle to increase the proportion of equiaxed grains 
in the casting structure and to refine the microstructure due 
to the formation of Al2O3 precipitates [52]. The steel modi-
fied with 0.16 wt.% aluminum, according to Zhang et al. [2], 
possessed outstanding formability due to the appearance of 
the strong {111} γ-fiber texture. Sn addition can inhibit the 
growth of recrystallized grains and strengthen the intensity 
of γ-fiber located at {111} 〈112〉, thus the formability of 
Sn-alloying ferritic stainless steel was excellent [8]. Increas-
ing Cr content can also improve the formability of ferritic 
stainless steels, which can be attributed to the constraint 
of some slip systems by Cr addition [53]. Liu et al. [54] 
revealed that W addition improved the formability of 444 
ferritic stainless steel. This is because W addition acceler -
ated the transformation of rolling texture to stable compo-
nents, benefiting the formation of recrystallized γ -fiber tex-
ture, and also restrained the deviation from the exact γ-fiber 
after cold rolling and annealing. The W-added 444 ferritic 
stainless steel can be used in automotive exhaust pipes.
Besides adjusting the chemical composition, optimizing 
the production process can also improve the formability of 
ferritic stainless steel. For example, Zhang et al. [40] inves-
tigated the effect of finisher entry temperature on the form-
ability of an ultra-purified 21 wt.% Cr ferritic stainless steel.

Nb + Ti dual-stabilized 15 wt.% Cr ferritic stainless steel 
with and without hot band annealing. They discovered that 
the final sheet with hot band annealing had higher intensity 
of {111} 〈112〉 γ-fiber and higher drawability. Bai et al. 
[46] investigated the effect of hot band annealing on the 
formability of an Sn-bearing ferritic stainless steel, and they 
found the final sheet with a hot band annealing temperature 
of 950 °C had the best formability. This is because the steel 
sheet is comprised of a large number of uniform and equi-
axed 〈111〉 //ND recrystallized grains in this case.

Cr-2 wt.% Mo ferritic stainless steel was achieved when cold 
rolled reduction was 85% [43]. The effects of tandem rolling 
and reversible cold rolling on the formability of SUS436L 
steel were studied by Wang et al. [44]. It was discovered 
that the strip produced by tandem rolling had an enhanced 
intensity of γ -fiber texture and excellent formability due 
to the larger shear strain caused by the larger working roll 
diameter. It has been found that the formability of ferritic 
stainless steels can also be improved by means of intermedi-
ate annealing during cold rolling [55]. This is due to the fact 
that intermediate annealing during cold rolling resulted in a 
weaker, more typical bcc rolling texture with fewer texture 
gradients, and the subsequent recrystallization annealing led 
to the more {111}//ND γ-fiber texture.
4 High‑temperature oxidation behavior
Ferritic stainless steels are frequently employed in high-tem-
perature situations, such as automotive exhaust systems and 
solid oxide fuel cell interconnects, and they must be resistant 
to high-temperature oxidation. During the oxidation process, 
a thin layer of chromium oxide forms on the surface of fer -
ritic stainless steels, which can inhibit oxidation reactions. In 
general, Cr2O3 scales can give protection up to a maximum 
of roughly 1000–1100 °C for long-term usage under isother-
mal conditions [56]. The key to achieving excellent oxida-
tion resistance is establishing a continuous external layer of 
thermodynamically stable and slow-growing oxide scales. 
There are a number of factors that can result in the degra-
dation of the oxide scale, including temperature, exposure 
time, oxygen partial pressure, water vapor in the atmosphere, 
and formation of volatile Cr-containing species [9 , 57–59]. 
Researchers are aiming to increase the high-temperature oxi-
dation resistance of ferritic stainless steels by optimizing 
alloy design and developing anti-oxidation coatings [16, 60].
It was found that the addition of Ti increased the thick -
ness of the Cr2O3 layer and significantly decreased the grain 
size of Cr2O3. The oxidation resistance limit temperature 
was enhanced by Ti addition, which arose from the oxygen-
getter effect of Ti atoms [61]. However, it is reported that a 
high amount (1 wt.%) of Ti in the Cr2O3 matrix generated 
excess ionic defects and reduced the oxidation resistance 
[62]. Considering that Al2O3 scales had a lower growth rate 
and better thermodynamic stability than Cr2O3 scales, Al 
was added to ferritic stainless steels to improve the high-
temperature oxidation resistance. Xu et al. [63] revealed that 
the high-temperature oxidation resistance of 15 wt.% Cr fer-
ritic stainless steel at 1000 °C was improved by Al addi-
tion. Furthermore, the high-temperature oxidation resistance 
increased as the Al content increased. The porous Cr2O3-rich 
outer layer is replaced by a more compact Al2O3-rich outer

Precipitates in ferritic stainless steels must also be taken 
into account during alloy design. The precipitates formed 
near the oxide/metal interface influenced reactant diffusion, 
the oxide scale growth mechanism, and the failure mecha-
nism. Laves phase forming elements such as Nb, Mo and W 
were added to ferritic stainless steels to increase the high-
temperature oxidation resistance. It has been discovered that 
the oxidation rate of Fe-22Cr-0.5Mn ferritic stainless steel 
at 800 °C in ambient air was reduced by up to 2 wt.% Mo 
addition [65], which is important for the application of solid 
oxide fuel cell interconnect. This is because Mo addition 
increased the activation energy and suppressed the inward 
diffusion of oxygen. When the Mo content was greater than 
4 wt.%, however, the oxidation rate increased after 300 h 
because the evaporation of Mo reduced the stability of the 
 Cr2O3 scale. Shu et al. [66] found that Mo could promote 
the precipitation of Laves phases, which are distributed near 
the oxide-metal interface. These Laves phases hindered the 
external diffusion of iron, chromium, and manganese cati-
ons from the matrix and prevented the internal diffusion of 
oxygen, resulting in a lower oxidation rate. Nb acted simi-
larly to Mo to promote the precipitation of Laves phases 
at the oxide-metal interface [ 67]. The Laves phases at the 
oxide/metal interface inhibited cation transport to the oxide 
scale. Thus, the high-temperature oxidation resistance was 
improved by Nb addition. Ali-Löytty et al. [68] discovered 
that the formation of Cr-Mn spinel oxide on Cr2O3 can be 
prevented because Mn diffusion to the oxide film is impeded 
by the Laves phase. It has been found that the high-temper-

can hinder the inward growth of oxide film. It is worth not-

of Laves phase precipitated at the oxide-metal interface, 
resulting in a significant reduction in the adhesion of the 
oxide scale.

effective for improving long-term creep strength. However, 
it has been argued that subgrains are the key to improving 
the creep performance of 9-12Cr steels [77].
The creep performance of heat-resistant ferritic steel is 
often improved by optimizing chemical composition. For 
example, the effect of carbon content on the creep per -
formance of 9Cr steels was studied by Abe et al. [79]. As 
illustrated in Fig.  11, the minimum creep rate of 0.002C-

and the creep rupture life of 0.002C-9Cr steel is around 10 
times that of 0.078C-9Cr steel. This means that reducing 
the carbon content of 9Cr steels can extend the creep life. 
The fine and thermally stable MX carbonitrides along lath 
and block boundaries are responsible for the excellent creep 
performance of 0.002C-9Cr steel. Abe and Nakazawa [80] 
found that the addition of W increased the creep strength 
of 9Cr steel, and the creep strength increased linearly with 
W content up to 3 wt.%. The contribution of W to creep 
strength came from solid solution strengthening, precipi-
tation strengthening and sub-boundary strengthening. The 
addition of B to 9Cr steel was found to increase its creep 
strength, due to the B addition reducing the coarsening rate 
of M23C6 carbides near the prior austenite grain boundaries 
[78]. Lee et al. [81] investigated the influence of Nb content 
on the creep strength of high-Cr martensitic heat-resistant 
steels. It has been revealed that through MX carbonitrides 
strengthening, the short-term creep strength was improved 
with increasing Nb content. However, the long-term creep 
strength of high-Nb steel was lower than that of low-Nb steel 
due to the formation of coarsened Z-phase. Therefore, some 
unfavorable phases should be avoided when optimizing the 
chemical composition.
It should be noted that the microstructure of conven-
tional 9–12Cr heat-resistant steels is tempered martensite, 
which is thermodynamically metastable at high-temperature 
long-term service [82] and therefore inevitably evolves into 
recrystallized ferrite [77, 83]. Conventional 9–12Cr steels 
have a maximum working temperature of 620 °C and are 
not capable of meeting the high-temperature requirements 
of 650 °C in the Generation IV power plants. Chromium 
contents higher than 15 wt.% are necessary to reliably reduce 
steam oxidation at 650 °C [84]. Based on the above back -
ground, fully ferritic, intermetallic phases strengthened 
stainless steels with 15 wt.% Cr were developed [85– 88]. 
Regarding the effects of C content and N content on creep 
performance of 15Cr ferritic steel, Toda et al. [85] concluded 
that appropriate additions of N or a combination of C and N 
can improve creep strength, while excess addition of C and 
N resulted in a reduction in long-term creep strength. The 
effects of W and Co on the creep strength of 15Cr ferritic 
heat-resistant steels were investigated by Toda et al. [86], 
and it was found that Co addition can effectively improve 
the short-term creep strength by promoting the precipitation 
of fine M23C6 carbides, whereas W addition can effectively 
improve the long-term creep life by the formation of inter -
metallic compounds. Shibuya et al. [ 87] studied the creep 
properties of 15Cr ferritic steel with different Ni and Co 
contents and compared them with T92 steel. As shown in 
Fig. 12, the creep rupture lives of 15Cr ferritic steels are 
significantly higher than that of T92 steel. This demonstrates 
the feasibility of employing full ferrite microstructure with 
intermetallic compound strengthening to improve creep life.

in oxidation resistance at 650 °C [89]. Based on the com-

insufficient oxidation resistance at 650 °C [89–93]. 17–23Cr 
ferritic stainless steels have excellent creep performance,

6 Summary and prospects
This review describes research advances in high-temperature 
strength, formability, high-temperature oxidation resistance 
and creep performance of ferritic stainless steels. These 
aforementioned studies show that the addition of alloy -
ing elements can effectively improve the high-temperature 
properties. The precipitation strengthening of Laves phase 
is critical to high-temperature strength. While inhibiting the 
growth of precipitates, these elements can lead to less deg-
radation in high-temperature strength. The formability of 
ferritic stainless steels can be improved by reducing the C 
and N content, adding stabilizing elements and optimizing 
production processes such as hot rolling, cold rolling and 
annealing processes. Optimization of chemical composi -
tion is the main way to improve high-temperature oxidation 
resistance. Alloying elements or Laves phases distributed 
near the steel/oxide interface inhibit the inward diffusion of 
oxygen and modify the morphology of the oxide film, thus 
reducing the oxidation rate. It is the key to improve the creep 
performance of heat-resistant ferritic stainless steels to sup-
press the coarsening of M23C6 carbides, MX precipitates and 
intermetallic compounds through composition optimization. 
The optimized combination of properties could be obtained 
by specifying steel compositions with careful control of 
minor alloying additions.
Currently, the high-temperature strength, formability, 
high-temperature oxidation behavior and creep behavior of 
ferritic stainless steels are mostly studied through experi-
ments, which cost a great deal of time and money. In the 
future development of novel heat-resistant ferritic stainless 
steels, machine learning, first principles and genetic algo-
rithms can be used as appropriate to shorten development 
time.
