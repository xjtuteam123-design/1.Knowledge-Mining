1. Introduction 
Materials with excellent high-temperature properties are in high 
demand within the aerospace and steam turbine industries [ 1 ]. 
Although Ni-based superalloys have exhibited good high-temperature 
properties and are widely used, some issues still need to be resolved. 
For example, the operation temperature of Ni-based superalloys should 
be lower than 1573 K due to the limitation of the melting point of the 
alloy [ 2 , 3 ]. At present, a new way of alloying, termed complex 
concentrated alloys (CCAs), has attracted more attention for their 
excellent comprehensive performance. For their difference in entropy, 
CCAs are often divided into high-entropy alloys (HEAs), 
medium-entropy alloys (MEAs), and low-entropy alloys (LEAs) [ 4 – 6 ]. 
Unlike conventional alloys, HEAs were defined as alloys composed of 
five or more elements and the concentration of each element ranging 
between 5% and 35% (at. %) [ 7 ]. A single solid solution rather than 
intermetallic compounds is likely formed within the alloys with high 
configuration entropies. Novel alloy design methods exhibit many ad -
vantages, such as high strength [ 8 ], excellent corrosion resistance [ 9 ], 
high creep resistance [ 10 ], and excellent radiation resistance [ 11 ]. 
Some CCAs, which contain high melting point elements (such as Cr, 
Hf, Mo, Nb, Ta, Ti, V, W, and Zr), have been designed using the concept 
of HEAs, named refractory HEAs (RHEAs). Interestingly, it was found 
that RHEAs have better high-temperature properties than some com -
mercial Ni-based superalloys [ 12 ]. Therefore, some materials that have 
superior high-temperature performance should be explored. However, 
poor room temperature plasticity and a high density hinder the appli -
cations of RHEAs [ 10 , 13 ]. Numerous methods have tried to solve these 
issues. Gao et al. [ 14 ] tried to reduce the density of the alloys by adding 
lighter elements such as Cr into Cr
