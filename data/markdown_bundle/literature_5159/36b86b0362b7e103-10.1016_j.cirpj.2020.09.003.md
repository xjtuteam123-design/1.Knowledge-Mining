A B S T R A C T
Alloy 718 is a high temperature nickel base superalloy, broadly used in the aerospace sector due to its
exceptional strength at elevated temperature. It is more vulnerable to the segregation of alloying
elements and development of laves phase in fusion zone (FZ) owing to the high heat input in Tungsten
Inert Gas (TIG) welding which drastically deteriorates the yield strength and ductility of welded joints. To
obviate this problem, a newly emerged InterPulsed TIG welding process, principally characterized by
electromagnetic constriction and high frequency pulsation of arc, was employed to make the joints of
thin Alloy 718 sheets. The three-dimensional (3D) response surface graphs were developed using
Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to identify the InterPulsed TIG welding parameters giving
maximum yield strength and elongation. The yield strength and elongation of joints were predicted from
the parametric mathematical models. The mathematical relationships were also developed to predict
yield strength and elongation from volume fraction (%) of laves phase. The InterPulsed TIG welded joints
exhibited a maximum of 94.45% and 82.89% of base metal yield strength and elongation. It is attributed to
the grain reﬁnement in FZ and substantial evolvement of laves phase in ﬁner discrete morphology.

Introduction
Alloy 718 is a precipitation hardened high temperature nickel-
based superalloy which is playing a major role in the advance-
ment of high thrust jet engine technology [1]. The CF6 engine of
Boeing 787 makes 34% use of Alloy 718 due to its exceptional
strength and weldability amongst other types of high tempera-
ture alloys [2]. It exhibits excellent resistance to creep and stress
rupture, high temperature strength and greater resistance to
thermal corrosion and oxidation [3]. It can function at elevated
temperature of 650 /C14 C for extended period of time, which proves
its suitability for gas turbine engine applications such as discs,
afterburners, high-pressure turbine blades and thrust reversers
[4]. It shows good endurance to strain aged cracking owing to its
slower aging dynamics [5]. Its yield strength is raised mainly by
the precipitation of primary gamma (g’) and secondary gamma
(g’’) phases favoured by alloying of Ni with Nb, Ti and Al [6]. The
ongoing research and development for enhancing the service life,
performance and efﬁciency of jet engine, reduced repairing and
maintenance cost has extended the usage of Alloy 718 [7]. Most of
the high temperature alloys are subjected to an accelerated creep
rate at 30– 40 percentage of their melting point. In this manner,
Alloy 718 shows better resistance to creep deformation which is
above the 70% of their melting point i.e. up to 1050 /C14 C. It proves its
viability for usage in modern high-thrust jet engines [8].
In aerospace industries, the fabrication and service repairing of
mechanical parts is mainly accomplished by Tungsten Inert Gas
(TIG) welding process to produce clean, precise and high quality
joints. This process is cost-efﬁcient, shop ﬂoor friendly and ensues
good control on welds compared to other types of fusion welding
processes. It does not comprise any particular operating conditions
like need of vacuum chamber and can be applied for any type of
onsite repairing and maintenance job. However, the high heat

welding thin sheets of metal [9]. The weldability of Alloy 718 is
principally constrained by the segregation of alloying elements and
development of coarse networked laves phase in fusion zone (FZ)
owing to the high heat input conditions in TIG welding [10]. The
intermetallic laves phase is a hard and brittle constituent having
topologically closed packed (TCP) structure which does not permit
plastic ﬂow along with the ductile austenitic matrix [1 1]. It endows
rapid initiation and growth of microcracks leading to premature
fracture of welded joints. This increases the expenses related with
the service repair and maintenance of mechanical components.
Alloy 718 is also more sensitive to microﬁssuring in HAZ owing to
the grain boundary liquation under the conditions of high heat
input [12]. This minimizes the strength and elongation of joints
drastically.
The problem of high heat input in TIG welding mainly refers to its
low energy density because of its wider arc column. It causes
dissipation of heat input on the outermost ﬂare of TIG welding arc
and more loss of heat to the atmosphere through the wider
cylindrical bell-proﬁled arc column. This also minimizes the density
of charged particles striking at the arc-workpiece interface which
aids in the need of high heat input to obtain full penetration of joints
in TIG welding [13]. To mitigate this problem, an advanced variant of
TIG welding process, known as InterPulsed TIG (IP-TIG) welding, was
practised for joining Alloy 718 sheets. It is principally featured by
electromagnetic constriction and high frequency pulsation of the TIG
welding arc (20 kHz) which signiﬁcantly curtails the heat input
conditions to attain full joint penetration during welding [14]. Fig. 1
reveals the difference between typical InterPulsed TIG and normal
TIG welding arc [15]. In InterPulsed TIG welding, InterPulse current is
superimposed on welding current to introduce an electromagnetic
ﬁeld. It gives rise to electromagnetic arc constriction force, acting in a
direction perpendicular to the welding arc column and thereby
compresses the arc [16]. The electromagnetic constriction of arc
endows localized fusion of joint. The InterPulsed TIG arc column
increases the concentration of charged particles striking the joint
seam per unit area. It also mitigates the loss of heat input to the
surrounding atmosphere owing to the smaller surface area of the
narrower InterPulsed TIG arc column. Thus, InterPulsed TIG welding
process necessitates comparatively less amount of heat to obtain full
penetration in joint than normal TIG welding process [1 7]. In this
study, InterPulsed TIG welding process was employed to lower the
segregation of alloying elements and substantial laves phase growth
in Alloy 718 welds for enhanced yield strength and ductility of joints
to comply with the requirements of advanced high-thrust jet
engines.
The high competition in aerospace sector is forcing OEM’s to
modify and advance their manufacturing methods for avoiding
costly machining and casting operations. These issues can be
overcome by the redesign and manufacturing of jet engine parts
and welding them by suitable advanced processes such as tungsten
inert gas (TIG) welding, laser beam welding (LBW) and electron
beam welding (EBW) [18]. Thus, it needs the development of
techniques to maximize and predict the strength and elongation of
joints. The welding parameters exhibit greater control on the
microstructural evolution of welds and can be optimized to
maximize the yield strength and elongation of joints. Response
surface methodology (RSM) is one of the main applications of DOE,
which can be utilized for ﬁnding the input parameters giving the
maximum yield strength and elongation [19– 21]. Taguchi method
offers a cost-effective statistical analysis. They are comparatively
more sophisticated to plan and comprise easier computational
analysis. However, the objective of Taguchi is always to maximise
the S/N ratio of the response and cannot be used to develop the
empirical relationships for predicting the joint performance [22].
The studies on welding of Alloy 718 are mainly presented on
tungsten inert gas (TIG) [23– 25], laser beam (LB) [26– 28] and
electron beam (EB) [29– 31] welding processes. Some studies are
reported on magnetic oscillated arc and pulsating current in TIG
welding to maximize the strength of Alloy 718 joints [32,33].
Nevertheless, these studies are principally associated with the
inﬂuence of welding processes on the microstructure and
performance of welded joints. There is a major gap with respect
to the systematic statistical study on maximizing the yield strength
and elongation of Alloy 718 joints. Furthermore, the studies
published on the InterPulsed TIG welding process for making the
joints of thin Alloy 718 sheets are few. The microstructural
evolution and substantial laves phase growth depend on heat input
and can be monitored by the selection of optimum welding
parameters. So, the aim of this study is to maximize the yield
strength and elongation of Alloy 718 joints for jet engine
applications. The empirical relationships were formulated to
predict the yield strength and elongation of Alloy 718 joints
incorporating InterPulsed TIG welding parameters.

Identiﬁcation of InterPulsed TIG welding parameters
The arrangement of InterPulsed TIG welding machine (IE175i;
VBC, UK) is displayed in Fig. 3. The InterPulsed TIG welding
parameters (welding current, welding speed, InterPulse current
and frequency) were identiﬁed using “one factor at a time” method
of DOE in previous investigations which exhibits substantial effect
on the tensile strength and microstructure of Alloy 718 joints
[13,14,16].
Development of experimental matrix and fabrication of welded joints
Extensive trial runs were done on the base metal utilizing
several combinations of InterPulsed TIG welding parameters to ﬁx
the viable working limits to produce defect free welds and for
developing experimental matrix. Central Composite Design (CCD)
was utilized for developing the response surfaces. It contains 30
experiments, four parameters and ﬁve levels (+//C0 α, +//C0 1 and 0)
where α = 2(k/4) and k is the number of independent parameters.
Table 2 shows the workable range of InterPulsed TIG welding
parameters for producing the joints of Alloy 718 sheets. Table 3
shows the variables and levels utilized for the development of
experimental matrix. Table 4 shows the 4 variable - 5 levels CCD
matrix formulated by Design Expert 8.0 software. The coded
conditions – 2 and +2 sign display the minimum and maximum
level of the variables. The butt welds were made successfully
utilizing InterPulsed TIG welding machine according to the
predetermined series of experimental runs.
Tensile testing
The tensile specimens were extracted from the Alloy 718 sheets
in transverse direction using wire-cut electric discharge machining
(W-EDM) and tested according to ASTM standard of E8M-05. The
engineering stress-engineering strain curves were recorded. From
these curves 0.2% offset yield strength values were derived for each
specimen. The tensile elongation was measured with 50 mm gauge
length. Fig. 4 shows the dimension of the tensile specimen.
Microstructure
The cross-section of welded samples was polished to 1 mm
ﬁnish and etched with Kalling’s reagent. Optical microscope was
utilized to reveal the microstructure of fusion zone. The volume
fraction (%) of laves phase was approximated using ImageJ
software.

predominant inﬂuence on yield strength and ductility of joints.

undesirable as it promotes segregation of alloying elements in
interdendritic areas of FZ and causes the growth of laves phase in
coarser networked morphology.

and arc wave form. The pulsing characteristics of InterPulse

pulsation of InterPulsed TIG welding arc. InterPulse current pulses

building time is completed, the electromagnetic constriction of arc
is relieved suddenly. Since the electromagnetic ﬁeld is super-

motion is produced in the molten weld metal around the axis of
tungsten electrode causing stirring of the melt and diminishes the
segregation of solute constituents during weld solidiﬁcation. The
convective force and molten metal motion also rise which ensues
signiﬁcant supercooling and increased nucleation rate. It provides
dendritic structure reﬁnement in FZ. There is an electromagnetic
constriction and release of arc at a high frequency of InterPulse
Current (up to 20 kHz) during welding. It causes disruption in the
weld thermal cycle and increases thermal oscillation in the weld
pool. The cyclic thermal oscillation due to the electromagnetic
constriction and release of InterPulsed TIG arc can cause remelting
and breaking-off the growing dendrites. The fragmented dendritic
pieces act as nucleating sites during solidiﬁcation and grain

oscillation of beam in EB welding process. In comparison to the
above ﬁndings, InterPulsed TIG welded Alloy 718 joints revealed
94.45% and 82.83% of base metal yield strength and elongation
which is higher (20– 30%) than normal TIG welding process and
equivalent to LB and EB welding process. It refers to the lower laves
phase evolvement in FZ.
The engineering stress-engineering strain curves of welded
joints are displayed in Fig. 17. The joints with high volume fraction
of laves phase revealed lower yield strength and elongation.
Whereas the joints with lower volume fraction of laves phase
exhibit higher yield strength and elongation. It mainly refers to the
solidiﬁcation conditions (heat input and cooling rate) during
welding. The faster cooling rate encountered in InterPulsed TIG
welds leads to ﬁner dendritic structure in FZ. The evolvement of
ﬁner dendritic grains in FZ minimizes the interdendritic zones
which are privileged locations for solute segregation and
substantial growth of laves phase. The dispersion of hard and
brittle laves phase in ductile solidiﬁed matrix imparts reinforcing
strength. This makes it difﬁcult for the movement of dislocations
and increases the strength of joints. The lower strength of the
joints is attributed to the coarse dendritic grain structure of FZ and
laves phase growth in thicker and networked form. The
evolvement of coarser laves phase depreciates the strengthening
constituents from the matrix and drops the yield strength of joints.
Also being hard and brittle intermetallic compound, laves phase
reduces the plastic ﬂow of the matrix and provides rapid crack
initiation and growth at lower strain energy. This signiﬁcantly
drops the elongation of joints.

Limitations of the yield strength and elongation equations
(a) All the welded joints must be free from defects such as
microcracks, porosity, inclusions, incomplete penetration,
deeper concavity and undercuts.
(b) The developed equations can predict the yield strength and
elongation accurately depending on the accuracy of laves
phase estimation from the microstructure.
(c) It does not take in account the inﬂuence of secondary phases
such as Nb carbides on the prediction of yield strength and
elongation of joints.
(d) The morphological features of laves phase can limit the
application of yield strength and elongation equation.
Conclusions
1 The 3-dimensional response surface graphs were developed for
the InterPulsed TIG welded thin Alloy 718 sheets to ﬁnd the
optimal welding parameters rendering the maximum yield
strength and elongation using a statistical approach of RSM.
2 The yield strength and elongation of welded joints can be
predicted accurately using the developed parametric mathe-
matical models with a conﬁdence level of 95% and % error of
<4.5.
3 ANOVA test results disclosed that welding current and welding
speed extend predominant inﬂuence on yield strength and
elongation of Alloy 718 joints, followed by InterPulse frequency
and current.
4 The joints fabricated using welding current of 65 A, InterPulse
current of 50 A, InterPulse frequency of 4 kHz and welding speed
of 60 mm/min exhibited higher yield strength of 548 MPa.
5 The joints fabricated using welding current of 65 A, InterPulse
current of 50 A, InterPulse frequency of 12 kHz and welding
speed of 70 mm/min yielded higher elongation of 31.50%.
6 The grain reﬁnement in fusion zone and development of laves
phase in ﬁner discrete morphology are mainly responsible for
improving yield strength and elongation of joints which is 20–
30% higher than normal TIG welding process. It is mainly
attributed to the electromagnetic constriction and high fre-
quency pulsation of InterPulsed TIG welding arc.
7 The yield strength and elongation of Alloy 718 joints are
inversely proportional to the volume fraction (%) of laves phase.
The mathematical relationships were developed to predict the
yield strength and elongation of welded joints from volume
fraction (%) of laves phase and validated using statistical
technique of ANOVA.
