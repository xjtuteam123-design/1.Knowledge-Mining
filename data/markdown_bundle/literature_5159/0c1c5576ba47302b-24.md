Most attempts to improve the properties of high entropy alloys (HEAs) involve the exploration of non- 
equimolar compositions, addition of alloying elements, and/or manipulation of the microstructure. Al- 
ternatively, this work reports on intentionally doping HEAs with non-metallic species to precipitate co- 
herent, ordered phases in order to maximize hardness. A refractory MoNbTaW HEA was synthesized via 
cryogenic mechanical alloying and doped with nitrogen by using liquid nitrogen as the cryogen. Overall, 
the doping strategy was successful as multiple nitrogen-rich secondary phases were observed. In partic- 
ular, a unique ordered laminar phase was developed and identiﬁed as tetragonal (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N nitride 
via aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Another complex (Nb,Ta) 2 CN 
carbonitride was also identiﬁed. The growth behaviors of the ceramic phases were studied using long 
term aging treatments of up to 100 hours at 1200 °C. A second MoNbTaW alloy was also prepared via 
high-energy ball milling, without nitrogen, by using liquid Ar as the cryogen. A comparison of the two al- 
loys’ microstructures and properties conﬁrm that intentional formation of complex nitride phases greatly 
enhanced the hardness of mechanically alloyed MoNbTaW by up to 3-4 GPa.

1. Introduction 
There are currently many ongoing efforts to develop the next 
generation of advanced materials that could potentially replace Ni- 
based super alloys for extreme applications. One class of materi- 
als that show promise are refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) 
[1–3] . Comprised of four or more refractory elements (e.g. Hf, Mo, 
Nb, Ta, Ti, V, W, Zr) ranging in concentrations between 5-35 at.%, 
RHEAs have been shown to exhibit strengths in excess of 400 MPa 
at temperatures up to 1600 °C [4–6] . Notwithstanding, considerable 
efforts have been made to further enhance the mechanical proper- 
ties of RHEAs by incorporating additional alloying elements [ 4 , 6–
12 ] and/or applying complex processing and/or deformation tech- 
niques to modify microstructures [13–22] . 
A speciﬁc approach to improve the thermomechanical per- 
formance of HEAs is precipitation strengthening via incoherent 
and/or coherent ordered phases, similar to L1 2 - γ’ Ni 3 (Al,Ti) pre- 
cipitates in Ni-based super alloys [23–27] , θ and β precipitates 
in Al alloys [28–32] , and nanoscale oxides in oxide dispersion

stance, additions of Al and Ti in Ni-based HEAs form ordered L1 2 
and B2 phases that improve hardness [38] or strength and ductility

strengthened by, for example, imitating γ- γ’ microstructures, sim- 
ilar success has not been demonstrated in RHEAs. 
Considering the diﬃculties in controlling coherency of metallic 
phases in RHEAs, an alternative design strategy to improve me- 
chanical performance is to intentionally precipitate ordered ce- 
ramic phases by doping with non-metallic elements (e.g., C, N, 
O). The viability of incorporating non-metallic elements (e.g. C, N, 
O) into RHEAs has previously been demonstrated by arc-melting 
and/or mechanical alloying [ 21 , 42–44 ]. Oxygen, in particular, has 
been shown to form ordered oxygen complexes that enhance 
strength and ductility [ 42 , 43 ]. While no evidence of ordered ni- 
trides was observed in these experiments, one particular experi- 
ment, in which cryogenic mechanical alloying was employed, re- 
ported on the formation of various complex nitrides which pre- 
cipitated upon annealing at 10 0 0 °C. These nitride phases were

also potentially maintaining reasonable ductility (e.g., high entropy 
metals). 
This work aimed to develop a nanocrystalline composite con- 
sisting of high entropy refractory metallic phases combined with 
ordered complex nitride phases that achieves superior mechanical 
properties. A secondary goal was to evaluate the atomic strength- 
ening mechanisms associated with ordered nitride phases. There- 
fore, two distinct alloys were synthesized via cryogenic mechanical 
alloying. One material was milled using liquid nitrogen to incorpo- 
rate N and induce nitride formation, while the second alloy was 
milled with liquid argon to minimize N additions for comparison. 
Both alloys were then subjected to long term annealing treatments 
to evaluate the formation and growth behavior of complex nitrides. 
Microstructures were characterized via aberration-corrected scan- 
ning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Vicker’s microin- 
dentation was performed to measure the effect of the complex ni- 
trides on hardness and ultimately to determine the optimal heat 
treatment. Overall, this study reports on the effect of successful 
nitrogen doping on the formation of coherent, nitride phases and 
their effect on the mechanical properties of mechanically alloyed 
MoNbTaW. Furthermore, the stability, microstructural characteris- 
tics, and strengthening mechanisms of the coherent, ordered ni- 
tride phase will be discussed. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Bulk Sample Preparation 
Two refractory HEAs with intended compositions of 
Mo 25 Nb 25 Ta 25 W 25 were synthesized via high-energy cryogenic 
mechanical alloying. High purity elemental powders (Alfa Aesar, 
99.9 metals basis) were loaded into tool steel SPEX mixing vials 
with tungsten carbide ball bearings. The ball to powder ratio 
was 10:1. A modiﬁed SPEX 80 0 0M mill was used to mechanically 
alloy the powders for 8 hours at cryogenic temperatures. One 
alloy was milled using liquid nitrogen to intentionally introduce N 
while the second alloy was milled using liquid argon to minimize 
incorporation of N. The two alloys will hereafter be referred to as 
HEA-N 2 and HEA-Ar. Once mixed, 3 mm compacts were produced 
by uniaxially pressing loose powder using a tungsten carbide die 
with an applied force of 3 GPa. Lastly, compacts were sealed in 
quartz ampules backﬁlled with Ar gas and annealed at 10 0 0 °C for 
1, 100, and 1000 hours or 1200 °C for 100 hours. Samples were 
furnace cooled at a rate of ~3 °C/min. 
2.2. Hardness Testing 
Vicker’s microhardness testing was completed on as-milled and 
annealed compacts using a Buehler Tukon 1202 indenter. At least 
15 indents were taken from polished surfaces using a load of 50 
grams and dwell time of 10 seconds. Indents were applied in 
the center of the largest particles to minimize contributions from 
porous areas. 
2.3. Microstructural Characterization 
Quantitative x-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) via 
scanning electron microscopy was conducted to determine con- 
centrations of metallic species. A FEI Scios dual-beam focused ion 
beam (FIB) instrument was operated at 30 kV and equipped with 
an octane elite silicon drift detector (SDD) to collect a minimum 
of 20 spectra from polished compacts. Quantitative x-ray analy- 
sis was performed using an approach described by Newbury et al. 
[46–48] . An additional MoNbTaW alloy with a known composition, 
conﬁrmed by Luvak Laboratories Inc., was used as a reference ma- 
terial. Non-metallic species concentrations were measured by Lu- 
vak Laboratories, Inc. via combustion infrared detection (ASTM E 
1019-18) and inert gas fusion (ASTM E 1019-18 & ASTM E 1447- 
16). Ar concentration was not quantiﬁed due to detectability limi- 
tations. 
Backscattered electron images of polished, as-milled and an- 
nealed compacts were acquired using the FIB instrument operated 
at 10 kV. Thin specimens for aberration-corrected scanning trans- 
mission electron microscopy (STEM) were prepared using FIB in- 
situ lift-out methods [ 49 , 50 ]. Specimens were mounted on either 
Mo or Si grids (Ted Pella). Primary extraction and thinning were 
performed with a 30 kV Ga ion beam. The Ga ion voltage was re- 
duced to 5 kV during the ﬁnal thinning steps to reduce ion beam 
damage. A Fischione 1040 NanoMill with an Ar ion beam oper- 
ated at 900 eV was used as a ﬁnal milling processes to remove 
Ga implantation. STEM was conducted using a JEOL ARM-200CF in- 
strument operated at 200 kV. X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS) maps were collected using a JEOL Centurio 100 mm 2 sili- 
con drift detector. Bright ﬁeld (BF), high-angle annular dark ﬁeld 
(HAADF), and low-angle annular dark ﬁeld (LAADF) images were 
simultaneously collected. Fast Fourier transforms (FFTs) and inten- 
sity proﬁles were extracted from STEM HAADF images to facili- 
tate phase identiﬁcation. JEMS microscopy simulation software was 
used to simulate, match, and index the FFTs. Lastly, Visualization 
for Electronic and Structural Analysis (VESTA) software was used 
to interpret atomic structures.

3.4. Effects of Nitride Formation on Hardness Mechanisms 
The hardness values for the as-milled and annealed HEA-N 2 and 
HEA-Ar alloys are shown in Fig. 10 . The horizontal line in each data 
set represents the median, the top and bottom limits of the boxes 
represent the 25 th and 75 th percentiles, and the caps represent the 
minimum and maximum values. The median hardness of as-milled 
HEA-N 2 (14.1 GPa) and HEA-Ar (13.4 GPa) were, on average, 300% 
harder than as-cast MoNbTaW (4.5 GPa). There were also measured 
differences in hardness upon annealing at 10 0 0 °C between HEA- 
N 2 and HEA-Ar; annealing HEA-N 2 consistently increased hardness 
up to 26% as compared to the as-milled condition, whereas HEA- 
Ar did not experience an increase in hardness. However, upon an- 
nealing at 1200 °C for 100 hours, both alloys experienced a drop 
in hardness by about 50%. Considering similarities in grain size, 
this direct comparison of the HEA-N 2 and HEA-Ar indicates that 
nitride formation signiﬁcantly improved the hardness in the an- 
nealed HEA-N 2 alloys. 
Considering that HEA-N 2 after annealing at 10 0 0 °C for 10 0 0 
hours exhibited the maximum hardness amongst all the alloys, 
a thin section was extracted underneath an indent to evaluate 
deformation mechanisms. Fig. 11 a shows the thin section un- 
der the indent and Fig. 11 b demonstrates that separate deforma- 
tion mechanisms were observed for the HEA BCC phase and the 
complex nitride MoTaN. Most notably, a series of parallel planar 
defects (marked by black arrows in Fig. 11 b) were observed in 
(Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N while multiple dislocations were observed in the 
HEA BCC phase (marked by the dashed yellow circles). Fig. 11 c 
shows an atomic-resolution STEM-HAADF image of a planar defect 
observed in the (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N nitride and Fig. 11 d shows an in- 
verse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) of the same region in Fig. 11 c. 
The IFFT was reconstructed using the (0-12) reﬂections. The yellow 
arrows mark the continuous shift in atomic planes. Interestingly, as 
shown by Fig. 11 c and d, the coherent phase boundary between the 
HEA and (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N phases acted as a barrier, stopping the 
planar defect (or shear plane) in its path. This behavior indicates 
that the shear mechanism is indeed unique to the (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N 
phase despite the coherency between the two phases and further- 
more suggests that the coherent phase boundaries may play a sig- 
niﬁcant role in strengthening. In contrast, Fig. 11 e shows a STEM- 
HAADF image of an edge dislocation in the HEA BCC grain, which 
is more clearly identiﬁable in the IFFT that was reconstructed using 
the (110) crystallographic plane as shown in Fig. 11 f. Overall, based 
on these results, the deformation mechanisms were distinctly dif- 
ferent between the two prominent phases in HEA-N 2 . 
4. Discussion 
4.1. Complex Carbonitride and Nitride Formation 
Two alloys in this work were synthesized in which one was in- 
tentionally designed to contain high amounts of N (HEA-N 2 ) and 
the other alloy (HEA-Ar) was designed to contain signiﬁcantly less 
N. The goal was to strategically form coherent nitride phases that 
increase the overall hardness of the material. A direct comparison 
of the HEA-N 2 and HEA-Ar alloy compositions indicates that HEA- 
N 2 contained ~24 at% nitrogen while HEA-Ar contained ~1 at%. In 
addition, one complex carbonitride and one complex nitride pre- 
cipitated in HEA-N 2 upon annealing and exhibited distinct orien- 
tation relationships with each other and the main HEA BCC phase. 
More speciﬁcally, (Nb,Ta) 2 CN and (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N formed after an- 
nealing at 10 0 0 °C. The presence of the complex carbonitrides and 
nitrides, (Nb,Ta) 2 CN and (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N, in HEA-N 2 , coinciding 
with the absence of nitrides/carbonitrides in HEA-Ar, is directly at- 
tributed to the use of liquid nitrogen during milling. Overall, the

According to a theoretical stability map of inorganic ternary 
metal nitrides, MoTaN has been previously observed as a 
metastable nitride that is stabilized under extreme conditions (i.e. 
high pressure) [56] . It has been experimentally veriﬁed that MoTaN 
can be synthesized at 1100 °C in an atmosphere having an elevated 
nitrogen pressure equal to P N2 = 300 bar [57] . Although atmo- 
spheric pressures encountered by the alloys in this experiment did 
not likely approach 300 bar, it is generally accepted that alloys 
formed by severe plastic deformation processes (i.e., mechanical al- 
loying) encounter extraordinarily high amounts of strain. Therefore, 
it is suggested that high amounts of strain induced by mechani- 
cal alloying and the incorporation of nitrogen from milling with 
liquid nitrogen both enabled the formation of the relatively un- 
common MoTaN-type phase. Overall, considering the extreme con- 
ditions necessary to form MoTaN (i.e., temperatures close to the 
melting point and pressures up to 10 0 0 bar), this work has demon- 
strated that cryomilling with liquid nitrogen is a viable and al- 
ternative method to synthesize ultrahard transition metal nitrides 
that are otherwise unstable. 
4.2. (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N Thermal Stability 
Although mostly amorphous in the as-milled condition, 
Fig. 2 and Fig. 7 indicate 1 hour annealing treatments of HEA-N 2 
at 10 0 0 °C resulted in crystallization of 15-25 nm HEA BCC grains 
as well as the coherent, complex ceramic phases (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N 
and (Nb,Ta) 2 CN. Considering that the (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N phase is po- 
tentially metastable, as previously discussed, long term annealing 
treatments up to 10 0 0 hours at 10 0 0 °C were performed to as- 
sess the thermal stability and growth of (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N. It was 
observed that (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N likely nucleated on grain bound- 
aries (i.e. heterogenous nucleation) and propagated towards the 
center of primary HEA BCC or (Nb,Ta) 2 CN grains via an edge 
growth mechanism analogous to twin growth in FCC metals (e.g. 
Cu). Furthermore, the layered (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N grains were sta- 
ble upon annealing at 10 0 0 °C for 10 0 0 hours. Thus, while pre- 
viously considered to be metastable in other material systems, 
this work suggests that (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N is thermodynamically sta- 
ble within nanostructured composites. The primary difference of 
the (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N phase after annealing for longer times, how- 
ever, was that (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N completely transformed within pri- 
mary grains after annealing for 10 0 0 hours. Otherwise, in alloys 
annealed for shorter times, individual grains consisted of at least 
(Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N and either the HEA BCC or (Nb,Ta) 2 CN phases. An- 
other critical result was that upon annealing at 1200 °C for 100 
hours, the ordered phase was no longer observed. Thus, this work 
has shown that the stability range of the (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N phase 
does not extend up to 1200 °C.

4.3. Effect of (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N on Mechanical Properties 
The microhardness of two alloys with the same intended bulk 
compositions (Mo 25 Nb 25 Ta 25 W 25 ) were compared in this study. In 
the as-milled condition, both alloys showed ~350% improvements 
in hardness as compared to as-cast MoNbTaW, most likely due to 
the much smaller crystallite sizes in the as-milled alloys [1] . Upon 
annealing at 10 0 0 °C for 1 hour, an increase in the hardness was 
observed in both alloys and further annealing at 10 0 0 °C for ex- 
tended times resulted in either an increase or no change in me- 
dian hardness. A decrease in hardness in either alloy was not ob- 
served until signiﬁcant grain growth occurred after annealing at 
120 0 °C for 10 0 hours. Similar trends in hardness have been re- 
ported in nanocrystalline Ni-W and MoNbTaW whereby anneal- 
ing an initially amorphous microstructure results in an initial in- 
crease in hardness. Most studies attribute this behavior to crys- 
tallization. Moreover, it is argued that further annealing induces 
grain growth resulting in corresponding decreases in hardness of 
up to 50% [ 21 , 58–62 ]. While similar trends in hardness were ob- 
served for HEA-N 2 and HEA-Ar, their hardness values differed con- 
siderably. More speciﬁcally, HEA-N 2 consistently exhibited a hard- 
ness 3-4 GPa greater than that of HEA-Ar. Overall, these hard- 
ness results highlight the beneﬁcial effects of (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N and 
(Nb,Ta) 2 CN on improving mechanical properties of MoNbTaW. Ad- 
ditional experiments aimed at evaluating the effect of complex ce- 
ramic phases on the ductility (i.e., tensile testing, nanoindentation, 
cantilever toughness testing, etc.) should be conducted to evaluate 
the potential of these alloys being used for extreme applications. 
Extensive atomic-resolution characterization was performed 
on deformed materials to rationalize the increased hardness of 
HEA-N 2 . Here, it was conclusively determined that HEA-N 2 was 
strengthened by coherent and complex nitride phases. From a 
macroscopic perspective, HEA-N 2 contained a much higher volume 
fraction of nitride phases which likely increased the overall hard- 
ness considering that refractory nitrides are harder than refractory 
metal phases. In this case, the intrinsic hardness of MoNbTaW, the 
primary HEA BCC phase in HEA-N 2 , has been reported to be ~4.5 
GPa [ 1 , 13 ]. While no experimental mechanical data could be found 
for the speciﬁc nitrides observed in this study, ﬁrst-principles cal- 
culations estimate the hardness of MoTaN to be ~19 GPa, with the 
high hardness of MoTaN resulting from the highly directional co- 
valent bonds between tantalum and nitrogen [ 53 , 56 ]. The speciﬁc 
strengthening mechanism of the complex (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N nitride 
observed in this work was that it deformed via a cleavage pro- 
cess, similar to many other brittle ceramics. Meanwhile the HEA 
BCC phase deformed via dislocation motion. The energy required to 
form a cleavage plane is most typically much higher than the en- 
ergy required to form dislocations and thus, the improved hardness 
of HEA-N 2 is due to the presence of the complex nitride phase(s). 
Of additional importance is the lattice mismatch between the 
ceramic and BCC phases, which results in a strengthening elastic 
strain energy at the phase boundaries. Analysis of the atomic res- 
olution image in Fig. 11 c provided that a misﬁt of f = 0.113 ex- 
ists between the HEA phase and (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N lattices, respec- 
tively. This misﬁt was measured between the (0-12) shear plane in 
MoTaN and (110) in BCC. The magnitude of misﬁt measured here 
indicates that the interface between these two phases is either a 
highly-strained coherent interface or semi-coherent interface based 
on the criteria used to describe coherent interfaces in thin ﬁlms 
[63] . Overall, in this particular example, the 0.113 planar misﬁt pro- 
hibits planar defects from translating between the HEA phase and 
(Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N, which subsequently strengthens the alloy. 
5. Conclusions 
The main goal of this work was to develop refractory HEAs 
that contain coherent nitride phases in order to maximize 
hardness. Two alloys with intended nominal compositions of 
Mo 25 Nb 25 Ta 25 W 25 were synthesized via high energy cryogenic me- 
chanical alloying. One alloy was milled with liquid nitrogen to in- 
corporate N and promote secondary nitride phase formation as a 
mechanism to increase overall hardness. High temperature anneal- 
ing treatments were completed followed by aberration-corrected 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) characterization 
and Vicker’s microhardness indentation. Overall, it was determined 
that the optimized heat treatment was annealing at 10 0 0 °C for 
10 0 0 hours in Ar atmospheres to achieve a peak median hard- 
ness of 17.8 GPa, an almost 450% improvement compared to its cast 
relative. Atomic-resolution characterization determined the hardest 
alloy contained coherent (Nb,Ta) 2 CN and (Mo,W)(Nb,Ta)N ceramic 
phases that deformed via a cleavage plane mechanism, as com- 
pared to dislocation-based deformation that is observed in HEA 
metallic phases. The overarching result of this work was the com- 
plete determination of processing-structure-property relationships 
pertaining to strategic incorporation of N into refractory HEAs, 
identiﬁcation of heat treatments to precipitate and grow coherent 
ceramic phases, and establishment of annealing conditions to ef- 
fectively age-harden nanocrystalline microstructures up to 24 GPa 
as a maximum observed hardness. Furthermore, this work demon- 
strates that cryogenic mechanical alloying is an adaptable tech- 
nique to form complex ceramic phases that would otherwise re- 
quire extreme conditions of temperature and pressure.
