ABSTRACT
As a unique directional solidification microstructure of Ni superalloys produced by laser powder bed fusion (L- 
PBF), the crystallographic lamellar microstructure (CLM) exhibits a strong texture with < 001 > and < 110 >
orientation, thereby has potential application in aero-engine turbine blades. Nevertheless, in-depth mechanical 
properties studies remain lacking. In this study, comparative studies are conducted to reveal the unique defor -
mation and strengthening mechanism of the CLM and DSM (directionally solidified microstructure) under room 
and high-temperature tensile conditions. However, substantial differences are observed in grain size, the number 
of grain boundaries, geometrical necessary dislocation (GND), elemental segregation, and crystallographic 
texture. The tensile results showed that the specimen with CLM exhibited an outstanding strength-ductility 
synergy due to the presence of high-proportioned < 110 > texture and unique < 001 > and < 110 > boundary. 
The regularly distributed < 001 > grains can serve as strong obstacles for slip bands and cracks propagating in 
< 110 > grains. Moreover, the < 110 > texture together with < 001 > and < 110 > boundary can elevate the local 
flow stresses and result in the activation of profuse deformation mechanisms, such as Planar slip bands, Stacking 
faults (SFs), and deformation twins. This work provides cross-scale insights into the deformation mechanisms and 
underscores the potential of CLM for developing high-temperature structural applications in Ni superalloy.
1. Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys are well-known engineering materials for 
producing aircraft engine blades, gas turbine combustors, and other 
complex high-temperature components [ 1 , 2 ]. To pursue higher oper -
ating temperatures in Ni-based superalloys, a strong texture micro -
structure (like directionally solidified or single crystal microstructure) 
usually needs to be achieved due to the well-aligned crystal orientation 
and less transverse grain boundaries [ 3 ]. Ni superalloy exhibits a 
face-centered cubic (FCC) crystalline structure with a preferential 
< 001 > crystallographic growth direction [ 4 ]. Hence, the strong texture 
can be theoretically obtained by precisely controlling the heat flow and 
aligning it to < 001 > direction [ 5 ]. Although conventional casting 
techniques (such as the Bridgman method) have realized directional 
solidification, there are still certain limitations regarding machining 
precision, efficiency, and flexibility. Therefore, building highly complex 
geometric structures is still a prominent challenge that traditional 
casting techniques encounter [ 6 , 7 ].
Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF), one of the mainstream additive 
manufacturing technologies, possesses the unprecedented capacity to 
fabricate high-precision superalloy parts (resembling multi-cavity hol -
low structure blades) attributed to its ultrafine spot size and digit control 
technique [ 8 , 9 ]. In the L-PBF process, the inherent high thermal 
gradient (10

various printing parameters, such as laser power, scanning speed, laser 
spot size, scanning strategy, etc [ 12 , 13 ]. In 2021, a unique crystallo -

activation in CLM superalloy remains unclear. The high-temperature 
loading may trigger distinctive deformation mechanisms due to rapid 
dislocation slip and atomic diffusion. According to previous reports

which may trigger back stress strengthening to improve the material ’ s 
mechanical properties further. Therefore, the above characteristics 
inherent to CLM imply it is worth further exploring in high-temperature 
loading conditions.

strengthening mechanism and deformation behavior. This work pro -

promising paradigm for overcoming the strength-ductility trade-off in L-

DSM specimen at room and elevated temperature (1000

Both specimens exhibit higher yield strengths (YS) and uniform elon -
gation (UE) at room temperature than their elevated temperature 
counterparts. Other superalloys have also reported a similar phenome -
non due to the weakens the metal bonding force with increasing tem -
perature [ 19 , 27 , 28 ]. Especially the decrease in ductility presumably due 
to its very high volume fraction of γ ′ (51 %) and high γ ′ solves 
(~1197 
◦
C). According to conventional materials science, the mechan -
ical properties tend to have a strength – ductility trade-off, i.e., an inverse 
relationship between strength and ductility [ 29 ]. However, as shown in 
Fig. 4 a, the CLM specimens exhibit a higher YS and UE compared to DSM 
at both room and elevated temperature conditions, indicating syner -
gistically enhanced strength-ductility. Fig. 4 b shows the relationship 
between strain hardening rate and true strain of the two types of sam -
ples. Both alloys within the elastic-plastic transition stage depict the 
up-turn tendency of the strain-hardening rate. Previous work [ 30 , 31 ] 
revealed that this upturn was derived from strain-hardening recovery 
and benefits ductility. Contrary to decreasing monotonically in classical 
materials, the up-turn characteristics of strain-hardening rate generally 
appeared in alloys, such as gradient structured materials, hetero -
structure strength alloys, and twinning/transformation-induced plas -
ticity alloys [ 32 – 34 ].
As shown in Fig. 4 b, the CLM alloys exhibit a more significant upturn 
compared with DSM, whether at room temperature or elevated tem -
perature testing. After the elastic-plastic transition stage, a similar strain 
hardening level of the two types of the samples was observed in the room 
temperature tensile process. Under elevated temperature conditions, an 
apparent strain-softening phenomenon with a negative strain-hardening 
rate was presented. This can be understandably attributed to the 
weakens the metal bonding force mentioned above, which contribute to 
the cross-slip and annihilation of dislocations. It is noteworthy that CLM 
possesses a slower strain-softening rate compared to DSM counterparts 
( Fig. 4 b). This observation implies that CLM alloys could be strength -
ened at elevated temperatures via an extra strengthening mechanism in 
the plastic deformation stage.
3.3. Tensile deformation behavior
According to the above results, the as-built CLM and DSM specimens 
feature a mixed texture with different proportions and distributions of 
< 001 > -oriented and < 110 > -oriented grains. In addition, the different 
deposition conditions also affected other solidification microstructures, 
as measured from EBSD maps and summarized in Table 3 . These initial 
microstructures determine the deformation behavior at the room and 
elevated temperature, as summarized in Table 4 .

As illustrated in Fig. 4 a, the CLM shows higher mechanical proper -
ties, including yield strengths and fracture ductility. Meanwhile, a sus -
taining strain-hardening level is usually relevant to the delay of necking 
in the tensile process ( Fig. 4 b). Therefore, the typical fracture feature 
was investigated to elucidate further the failure difference of both 
texture types of specimens ( Fig. 7 ). Fig. 7 a exhibits fracture morphology 
of CLM alloys in room temperature tensile. Plentiful slip bands with a 
uniform distribution were observed in < 110 > -oriented grains. These 
slip bands initiate and propagate along the tensile direction with a 45
◦
angle for an FCC crystal structure, which had the largest critical resolved 
shear stress to activate the slip system [ 20 , 36 ]. It should be noted that 
these slip bands can ’ t propagate through the < 001 > -oriented grains due 
to the difference in crystallographic orientation.
A different fracture behavior was observed in DSM samples. The 
DSM specimens show an irregular mixed texture ( Fig. 3 b), weakening 
the ability to hinder slip band propagation. Consequently, the unre -
strained slip bands could induce cracks and facilitate fracture. Thus, the 
fracture surface was inclined ~45
◦
to the tensile direction ( Fig. 7 b). In 
high-temperature tensile testing, the intergranular regions usually un -
derwent more severe plastic deformation than their transgranular 
counterparts. This phenomenon can be attributed to a relatively disor -
dered atomic arrangement in grain boundaries and easy sliding caused 
by thermal activation. Therefore, both the CLM and DSM specimens 
exhibit mechanical properties deterioration caused by these intergran -
ular cracks, as seen in Fig. 7 c and d. However, in CLM alloys, < 001 > - 
oriented grains effectively restrained intergranular cracks propagation 
and avoided local catastrophic fracture ( Fig. 7 c). In comparison, for the 
DSM specimens, one can see that intergranular cracks were more 
directly linked to the failure region ( Fig. 7 d). The longer HAGB in DSM 
increased accessible paths for grain boundaries-induced cracks propa -
gation and facilitated necking instability. Thus, the CLM alloys exhibit a 
higher ductility compared with DSM alloys.

5. Conclusion
In this work, the crystallographic lamellar microstructure (CLM) in 
L-PBF-produced Ni superalloys is investigated by experimental obser -
vations of microstructure, tensile properties, and deformation behavior. 
For comparison, another set of samples was fabricated with similar total 
volume fractions of < 001 > / < 110 > mixed texture, i.e., DSM samples. 
The two types of alloys exhibit different proportions and arrangements 
of < 001 > and < 110 > grains, leading to various mechanical properties 
and strengthening mechanisms. The main conclusions are summarized 
as follows: 
(1) The unique CLM exhibits a strong texture with orderly alternating 
< 001 > -and < 110 > -orientated layers. Compared to DSM, the 
CLM possess some inherent solidification characteristics 
including: larger grain size, lower HAGB and LAGB numbers, 
lower initial dislocation density, and stronger local chemical 
segregation. The grain-scale microstructure difference and dy -
namic strain partitioning contributes to CLM alloys show 
enhanced strength-ductility synergy relative to DSM alloys.
(2) Although various traditional strengthening mechanisms benefit 
DSM alloys in the room- and high-temperature tensile, texture- 
related strengthening plays a dominant role in CLM alloys. The 
asynchronous deformation of < 001 > and < 110 > oriented layer 
induced strain gradient and produced HDI strengthening, which 
enhanced yield stress and strain-hardening ability. In addition, 
the regularly alternate < 110 > and < 001 > texture layer syner -
getic strengthening increased ductility through manipulate the 
initiation and propagation of planar slip bands.
(3) The abundant deformation mechanism can be activated in CLM 
alloys under room temperature tensile, including Planar slip 
bands (PLBs), Stacking faults (SFs), L-C locks, and SFs networks. 
Under high-temperature conditions, these SFs attract Co and Cr 
elements, which lower the local stacking fault energies (SFEs). 
The element ’ s diffusion facilitates the formation of deformation 
twins and leads to a more significant performance gap than room 
temperature.
