abstract
The effect of Pt –aluminide bond coat on the mechanical properties, namely tensile and creep, of a Ni-
base single crystal superalloy has been investigated. Tensile properties of both uncoated and bond coated
specimens were evaluated in the temperature range of room temperature (RT)-1 100 °C. Creep testing
was carried out using four combinations of temperature and stress, viz. 850 °C/500 MPa, 982 °C/240 MPa,
1038 °C/138 MPa and 1 100 °C/90 MPa. By and large, the presence of bond coat reduced the tensile
strength of the superalloy at all temperatures. However, the coated alloy exhibited a higher ductility than
the uncoated alloy in the above temperature range. The lowering of strength in the coated alloy can be
explained in terms of the signi ﬁcantly lower strength of the coating as compared to that of the substrate,
which caused a decrease in the strength of the coating-substrate composite structure. The higher duc-
tility of the coated alloy, especially at elevated temperatures, can be attributed to the surface protection
provided by the oxidation resistant bond coat to the substrate. The creep properties (strain-to-fracture
and creep life) of the coated alloy were somewhat inferior to those of the uncoated alloy at all test
temperatures. This can be ascribed to the low strength of the bond coat at high temperatures and also to
the microstructural changes that took place in and near the coating during the creep test.

1. Introduction
Superalloy components operating in hot sections of aviation
gas turbine engines are exposed to oxidizing environments. In
order to combat high temperature oxidation damage, these com-
ponents are provided with protective coatings. Platinum-modi ﬁed
diffusion aluminide coatings (also called as Pt –aluminide coatings)
provide excellent oxidation resistance to nickel-base superalloy
components such as blades and nozzle guide vanes that operate in
high temperature sections of advanced gas turbine engines [1,2].
The oxidation resistance of Pt –aluminide (Pt –Al) coatings is de-
rived from the formation of an adherent and protective alumina
scale on coating surface during high temperature exposure [3–5].
The improved scale adherence achieved because of the presence of
Pt [3,4,6] reduces the incidence of scale spallation under cyclic
heating and cooling conditions, typically encountered in gas tur-
bine environments [7,8]. As a result, the effectiveness of these
coatings against high temperature oxidation exposure is greatly
enhanced.
Notwithstanding their excellent oxidation resistance, Pt –Al
coatings are reported to degrade the mechanical properties of
substrate superalloys [9–17]. Such degradation is caused by the
brittle
β-NiAl phase which is the primary constituent phase of the
coating and has a high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature
(DBTT) typically above 650 °C [12]. Effect of aluminide coating on
various mechanical properties such as tensile [9–11,13], creep
[9,11,14], high cycle fatigue [15] and thermo-mechanical fatigue
[16,17] of various superalloys has been reported. For example, Veys
and Mervel [1 1]have reported that the application of a diffusion
aluminide bond coat did not appreciably affect the strength of the
single crystal alloy CMSX-2 up to about 1050 °C. However, it led to
a drastic reduction in the room temperature (RT) ductility of the
alloy, although at higher temperatures ( 4850 °C), the coated alloy
exhibited very good ductility values [12]. Alam et al. [13] have
reported the tensile properties of Pt –Al coated directionally soli-
diﬁed (DS) CM-247LC superalloy, evaluated at a test temperature
of 870 °C. They evaluated the properties in as-coated condition as
well as after subjecting the samples to cyclic oxidation in air at
110 0°C. In as-coated condition, the presence of bond coat was
found to cause about 8 –10% drop in the strength of the substrate
alloy, although it did not appreciably affect the ductility [13]. The
coated alloy exhibited a signi ﬁcantly higher ductility as compared
to the uncoated alloy after a cyclic oxidation exposure of 750 h at
110 0°C [13]. There have been limited studies where the effect of
aluminide coatings on the creep and stress rupture behavior of
superalloys has been examined [9]. Most of these studies conclude

substrate superalloys has not been examined in a systematic
manner. In the present study, the effect of a high activity Pt –Al
bond coat on the tensile and creep properties of a single crystal
superalloy alloy has been investigated. Detailed microstructural
and factography examination of the tested samples has been car-
ried out to understand the tensile and creep behavior of the above
superalloy in both uncoated and bond coated conditions.
2. Experimental details
As mentioned earlier, the substrate material used in the present
study was a ﬁrst generation nickel base single crystal superalloy
having a nominal chemical composition of (in wt%) Ni –6.5Co–
7.8Cr–5.7 W–5.2Al–7.9Ta–1.1Ti–2.0Mo. This superalloy was avail-
able in the form of 12 mm diameter rods having the single crystal
orientation of o0014 along the length of the rods. The single
crystal orientation of the rods was determined by using a Laue
XRD system (Make: Proto XRD, Canada) and the rods that were
used for mechanical testing had the above o 0014 orientation
with a deviation not exceeding 10 degrees. All the rods were so-
lutionized at 1300 °C for 3 h. Some of the solutionized rods were
then given a two-step aging treatment, which consisted of 9 h at
110 0°C followed by 20 h at 870 °C, to achieve the required
γ–γ′
structure. Both the solutionizing and aging treatments were car-
ried out in a vacuum furnace. The fully heat treated rods were used
for preparation of uncoated tensile and creep specimens using a
computerized CNC machine. Tensile and creep specimens were
prepared as per ASTM standard. The remaining solutionised rods
were used for preparation of coated specimens. To achieve this,
tensile and creep specimens were machined from the solutionized
rods. Subsequently, they were electroplated with an approxi-
mately 5 mm thick Pt layer. The Pt plated samples were given a
diffusion treatment in vacuum at 1 100 °C for 5 h. The specimens
were then pack aluminized at 850 °C for 5 h in an argon atmo-
sphere. Subsequently, the aluminized samples were given a post-
aluminizing diffusion treatment at 1 100 °C for 4 h in vacuum to
complete the coating process. Finally, the coated samples were
aged at 870 °C for 20 h in vacuum. It may be mentioned that the
above mentioned coating treatments were selected in such a way
that the substrate alloy simultaneously received the required two-
step aging, as mentioned previously. Other details of Pt –Al coating
formation can be found elsewhere [19].
Both uncoated and Pt –Al coated specimens were tensile tested
at different temperatures, namely room temperature (RT), 870,
1 000 and 1 100°C, using a Walter Bai universal testing machine. All
testes were carried out at a cross head speed of 1 mm min
/C0 1.A t
least two samples were tested under any given condition for en-
suring consistency in the results. Tensile creep testing of uncoated
and coated specimens was carried out in air under constant load
conditions using four combinations of temperatures and stress,
namely 850 °C/500 MPa, 982 °C/240 MPa, 1038 °C/138 MPa and
110 0°C/90 MPa. For measurement of creep strain, the ex-
tensometer was mounted on the specimen ridges and two linear
variable differential transducers (LVDTs) were attached to the ex-
tensometer outside the hot zone of the furnace. The average of the
strain values measured by the two transducers was used for
plotting the creep curve. Creep data was collected at regular time
intervals using a data acquisition system. All the creep tests were
conducted till fracture of the specimens.
Detailed microstructural examination of the specimens and
fractography studies were carried out using an optical microscope
and a Leo 440I scanning electron microscope (SEM) operating at
20 kV. For metallographic observation of coating cross-section, the
samples were ﬁrst plated with a layer of Ni using sulfamate bath to
prevent edge-rounding during polishing operation. Subsequently,
the sample cross-sections were polished using standard metallo-
graphy techniques. Hardness variation across the coating thickness
was recorded by using a LEICA micro-hardness tester a load of
25 g. Hardness was taken on the polished cross-section of the
coated alloy in as-coated condition.

4. Discussion
4.1. Tensile behavior
The increase in strength at 870 °C observed in case of uncoated
alloy is typical for superalloys containing high volume percentage
of γ′, typically 450% [29,30]. The slight increase in YS of such
superalloys at intermediate temperatures of around 800 °C is due
the anomalous ﬂow behavior observed in the alloys involving
cross-slip of screw dislocations in γ′-Ni3Al phase from octahedral
{1 1 1} slip planes to cube {001} planes, which is not typically ob-
served in f.c.c metals [31,32]. The cross-slipped segments, known
as Kear –Wilsdorf locks [33], are immovable as they cannot move
without trailing anti-phase boundaries (APBs) behind them.
Therefore, they resist the movement of dislocations on these new
crystallographic planes {001} which leads to an increase in ﬂow
stress of the alloy in the intermediate temperature range, as ob-
served in the present case ( Fig. 4 (a)).
In case of coated samples, the substrate alloy underwent ad-
ditional high temperature exposure, namely 5 h at 850 °C (alu-
minization treatment), as compared to the uncoated alloy. Alam
et al. [13] have studied the effect of such additional high tem-
perature exposures due to coating formation on the tensile prop-
erties of the substrate alloy in case of Pt –Al coating on direction-
ally solidi ﬁed (DS) Ni-base superalloy CM-247LC [13]. The sub-
strate CM-247LC superalloy in the coated alloy was exposed to the
additional exposures at 830 °C for 5 h (aluminizing) and at 1080 °C

As previously mentioned, strengthening at intermediate tem-
peratures was not apparent in the coated alloy (Fig. 4(a)). Alam et al.
have reported that Pt –Al coatings are much weaker than the sub-
strate alloy on which they are deposited [22]. For example, YS and
UTS of a Pt –Al bond coat have been determined to be approximately
200 and 300 MPa, respectively, in the temperature range of 800 –
900 °C. These values were much lower than the corresponding YS
and UTS of the Ni-base substrate superalloy CM-247LC, which were
approximately 882 and 975 MPa, respectively [13]. Assuming a si-
milar large difference in strength between the present bond coat and
the substrate single crystal alloy, it can be expected that the presence
of the bond coat would cause some degree of weakening of the alloy.
Based on the studies by Hook and Hirth [34] and Sreeramamurthy
et al. [35], the coated tensile specimen can be considered as an iso-
axial bi-crystal which is constituted of the annular coating and the
inner unaffected substrate. When this bi-crystal is subjected to uni-
axial stress in the elastic range during tensile test, local stress build-
up at coating/substrate interface occurs because of the incompat-
ibility in elastic moduli between the substrate and the coating. The
magnitude of this stress would depend on the extent of incompat-
ibility and the magnitude of the applied stress. It has been reported
that both YS and elastic modulus for Pt –Al bond coats are much
lower than that of the underlying substrate. Similarly, the elastic
modulus of the coating was also lower than that of the uncoated
alloy. Thus, an elastic incompatibility at the coating/substrate would
develop during tensile loading. It appears that, at 870 °C, the weak-
ening caused by the coating is higher than the strengthening caused
by the anomalous ﬂow behavior of the substrate. As a result, the
coated alloy exhibited a decrease in strength as the test temperature
was increased from RT to 870°C( Fig. 4(a)), which was unlike the case
in uncoated alloy. The drastic drop in strength of the alloy beyond
870 °C in both uncoated and coated conditions was as a result of
activation of slip on several additional crystallographic planes in the
substrate alloy as well as a decrease in the resistance of screw dis-
locations to cross slip at higher temperatures.
By and large, both YS and UTS of coated single crystal alloy
were about 5–28% lower than those of the uncoated alloy at all the
test temperatures. The general lowering of strength of the alloy in
presence of the coating can be ascribed to the much lower
strength of the coating as compared to that of the substrate, as
mentioned previously [13,22], which leads to a reduction in the

As previously stated, PtAl bond coats have high DBTTs, typically
in the range of 650 –750 °C [12]. Therefore, these coatings are
brittle at temperatures below their DBTTs and develop cracks
under tensile loading [12]. The present bond coat also developed
cracks all along the gauge length of the tensile specimens tested at
lower temperatures ( Fig. 5 (a)). Some of these cracks penetrated
into the substrate and caused a certain amount of reduction in
ductility, as found at RT ( Fig. 5(a)). At temperatures above its DBTT,
however, the bond coat exhibits ductile behavior [12] and, as ob-
served in this as well as previous studies [12], is able to deform
with the substrate without cracking during the tensile test ( Fig. 5
(b)). Coating cracks observed close to the fracture end ( Fig. 5 (b))
were actually caused because of plastic strain localization in the
neck region prior to failure. Thus, unlike its in ﬂuence at RT, the
bond coat did not cause any reduction in the ductility of the coated
AM1 alloy at higher temperatures, i.e. at and above 870 C. It has
been shown in one of our previous studies that high temperature
oxidation damage to the surface of a superalloy can cause severe
loss in its ductility [36]. For example, Alam et al. [36] have re-
ported a reduction in ductility of uncoated CM-247LC superalloy
from 10.6% to 3% after an exposure of 100 h at 1 100 °C in air. Al-
though the duration of exposure of the specimens to high tem-
perature during tensile testing in the present case would be
comparatively much shorter, some degree of oxidation damage at
the surface and consequent loss in ductility can be expected in
case of uncoated alloy. When the Pt –Al bond coat was present, it
protected the surface from oxidation-related damage. Because of
such protection, the coated specimens exhibited a higher ductility
than their uncoated counterparts at high temperatures ( Fig. 4 (b)).
Thus, the enhanced high temperature ductility of single crystal
alloy observed in the presence of Pt –Al coating can be ascribed to
the combined effect of higher ductility of the coating and the
oxidation protection it provided to the substrate alloy. Similar ef-
fect of oxidation resistant Al
3Ti coating on the ductility of a near α
Ti-alloy Titan 29A has also been reported [37].
The microstructure and fractography examination of the tested
tensile specimens, as described earlier, suggest that failure in the
uncoated alloy at RT was initiated by nucleation of voids in the
matrix and micro-cracks in coarse eutectic γ′ in the interior of the
specimen. The voids grew and coalesced under continued de-
formation to form micro-cracks. The micro-cracks (caused by void
coalescence and cracking of eutectic
γ′) then linked up leading to
the eventual failure of the tensile specimen. In case of coated
samples at RT, although the through-thickness cracks in the brittle
coating formed during early stages of tensile loading, their inward
penetration was arrested by the relatively ductile substrate ( Fig. 5
(a)). The ﬁnal failure of the specimen occurred in a similar manner
as mentioned for the uncoated specimen, i.e. by outward propa-
gation of the crack formed by linking-up of the micro-cracks
generated in the interior of the substrate. At high temperatures
(870 °C and higher), the failure in both uncoated and coated alloy
occurred primarily by void nucleation and their coalescence, ty-
pically observed in a ductile material.

4.2. Stress rupture behavior
The reasons for the inferior creep behavior of the superalloy in
presence of the Pt –Al coating can be explained as follows. Firstly,
as mentioned earlier, this coating was considerably weaker than
the substrate and therefore, virtually non-load bearing at all creep
test temperatures [22]. As a result, there was a loss in the load
bearing cross-section of creep specimens in presence of the coat-
ing. This led to some degree of degradation in the creep properties
of the coated alloy. Secondly, the degradation in the micro-
structure of the coating and that of the substrate close to coating/
substrate interface also contributed to the deterioration in creep
performance. The degradation in coating microstructure occurred
because of interdiffusion of various elements, including Ni, Al and
Pt, between the coating and the substrate during prolonged high
temperature creep exposure. During this exposure, there was also
loss of Al towards the formation of an oxide scale on the coating
surface [38]. The microstructural degradation manifested in the
form of increase in the thickness of the IDZ of the coating, for-
mation of blocky γ′ phase inside the coating and also formation of
topologically closed packed (TCP) phases in the substrate adjacent
to the coating. The above degradation can be seen in the coating
microstructures corresponding to various failed creep samples, as
presented in Fig. 14(a)–(b). Similar microstructural degradation of
Pt–Al bond coats has also been reported in several other studies
[39,40]. EDS analysis of the TCP phases revealed that they were
rich in W, Mo and Cr ( Fig. 14(c)). The formation of TCP phases is
not desirable because their formation depletes the superalloy
matrix of the above elements which are required for solid solution
strengthening. These phases are also very brittle and have sharp
needle-like shape ( Fig. 14(a)–(b)). Therefore, they can cause pre-
mature crack initiation and the resultant deterioration in the stress
rupture properties of the alloy [41,42]. Thus, the degradation of
microstructure in and near the coating has also contributed to the
lowering of the creep performance of the coated alloy. Thirdly, the
presence of coating promoted circumferential surface cracks in the
necked region late in the tertiary creep regime due to the presence
of a brittle oxide layer that formed on the coating surface during
creep test as shown in Fig. 4 (b). These surface cracks not only
reduced the load bearing cross-section, but also promoted their
early link-up with creep cracks propagating from interior of the
specimen, leading to its premature failure. All the above three
factors contributed to the observed reduction in the creep per-
formance of the present single crystal superalloy in presence of
the coating. The highest performance drop observed at 1 100 °Ci s
consistent with the highest extent of microstructural degradation
the coating underwent during creep exposure at this temperature,
as evident from Fig. 14(b).
Apart from the above microstructural changes observed in the
coating and its vicinity, the bulk substrate microstructure also
degraded during creep test under all four stress/temperature
combinations. The original cuboidal γ′ precipitates underwent
rafting or directional coarsening perpendicular to the stress axis,
as typically illustrated in Fig. 14(d) during testing at 850 °C. For-
mation of rafted γ′ structure during creep deformation is a well
reported phenomenon in nickel-base superalloys containing high
volume percents of γ′ (typically 450%) [23]. The orientation of γ′
rafts depends on the type of mis ﬁt (positive/negative) between γ
matrix and γ′ precipitates as well as the sense (tensile/compres-
sive) of applied stress. The perpendicular orientation of the rafts to
the applied tensile stress in the present superalloy suggests that
there was a negative mis ﬁt between γ matrix and γ′ precipitates
[43–47]. Similar rafting also occurred in the uncoated alloy during
creep testing.

From the present study, it is clear that the mechanical prop-
erties such as tensile and creep of the substrate single crystal su-
peralloy underwent a certain degree of degradation in presence of
Pt–Al bond coat. Similar observations have also been reported in
other studies [17,18].P t –Al bond coats are typically used as a part
of thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) deposited on Ni-base superalloy
components such as blades and nozzle guide vanes of advanced
gas turbine engines [48,49]. Typically, TBCs are bi-layer coatings
consisting of an outer ceramic layer (7% yttria stabilized zirconia or
7YSZ) that provides thermal insulation and an inner bond coat
which provides oxidation protection to the superalloy substrate
[48,49]. Despite the fact that the bond coat causes some degree of
degradation in the mechanical properties of the substrate, TBCs
are essential to enable the superalloy components to operate at
much higher temperatures in a gas turbine engine than would be
possible in uncoated condition. Therefore, the knowledge of the
extent of degradation caused by the bond coat in the mechanical
properties of the substrate superalloy would be very helpful for
the component of gas turbine engines.
5. Conclusions
In the present study, the effect of a Pt –aluminide bond coat on
the tensile and creep properties of a Ni-base single crystal alloy
has been examined. Properties of the alloy were evaluated in un-
coated as well as bond coated conditions. Tensile properties were
evaluated in the temperature range of RT-1 100 °C and the creep
properties evaluated using four temperature/stress combinations,
namely 850 °C/500 MPa, 982 °C/240 MPa, 1038 °C/138 MPa and
110 0°C/90 MPa. Application of the bond coat resulted in a de-
crease in the alloy strength (YS and UTS) at all temperatures. This
lowering of strength can be ascribed to the much lower strength of
the coating as compared to that of the substrate alloy. The coated
alloy exhibited higher ductility than the uncoated alloy because of
the surface protection provided by the oxidation resistant Pt –Al
coating to the substrate, especially during testing at elevated
temperatures. Both coated and uncoated tensile samples failed at
RT by mixed mode fracture with the fracture surface showed both
dimples and cleavage facets. At higher temperatures, however,
they failed in a ductile mode primarily showing dimples. Several
changes in the microstructures of the substrate and the coating
occurred due to the combined effect of creep deformation and
thermal exposure. These include rafting of the original cuboidal
γ′
precipitates of the substrate alloy perpendicular to the stress axis,
increase in the thickness of IDZ of the coating, formation of γ′
phase in the coating and needle shaped TCP phases at the coating/
substrate interface. The presence of bond coat led to a certain
degree of deterioration in the creep performance of the alloy,
namely 7 –23% deterioration in time-to-rupture and 10 –20% in
strain-to-fracture. Factors responsible for such deterioration in
creep properties include the loss in load bearing cross-section of
the specimen due to the low strength of the coating at creep
temperatures and the microstructural degradation in the bond
coat and the substrate caused during creep tests. Creep fracture in
both uncoated and coated alloy occurred essentially by nucleation,
growth and coalescence of voids formed at eutectic
γ′ regions
inside the substrate.
