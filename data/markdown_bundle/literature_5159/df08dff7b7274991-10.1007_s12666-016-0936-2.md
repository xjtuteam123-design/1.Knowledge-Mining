Sintering of Tungsten and Tungsten Heavy Alloys of W–Ni–Fe
and W–Ni–Cu: A Review
N. Senthilnathan 1 • A. Raja Annamalai 1 • G. Venkatachalam 1
Received: 1 March 2016 / Accepted: 19 July 2016
/C211The Indian Institute of Metals - IIM 2016
Abstract Tungsten is a refractory metal possessing good
mechanical properties of high strength, high yield point,
and high resistance to creep. Therefore, tungsten and its
alloys are used in many high temperature applications. Due
to the high melting point, they are generally processed
through powder metallurgy method. The powders are
compacted using die pressing or isostatic pressing. The
compacts are sintered in a sintering furnace to achieve high
density, thereby, making the metal suitable for further
processing. This article reviews the recent research ﬁndings
of consolidating tungsten and its alloys (W–Ni–Fe and W–
Ni–Cu), from preparation of powder alloys to sintering of
the compact. The advances in sintering are based on the
objective of achieving good densiﬁcation of the metal at
lower temperature and at faster rate. The use of microwave
sintering and spark plasma sintering techniques resulted in
signiﬁcant reduction in sintering time and producing
products of good mechanical properties.

1 Introduction
Tungsten has the highest melting point (3420 /C176C) and
lowest thermal expansion coefﬁcient of all metals. It
belongs to the group of refractory metals and is also
characterized by high modulus of elasticity, good thermal
stability and relatively high density [ 1]. Tungsten does not
occur naturally in its pure metallic state, but is found in the
form of minerals, such as, wolframite and scheelite. The
ore concentrate is chemically processed to produce
ammonium paratungstate (APT), which is an intermediate
compound in the production of pure tungsten metal powder
[2]. The APT is chemically reﬁned further to obtain high
purity tungsten powder of different particle size and size
distributions. Tungsten, in its raw form is hard, brittle and
difﬁcult to work. But, pure tungsten, retains the hardness
and is malleable enough to work easily [ 3]. Due to its high
melting point, tungsten metal powder is consolidated
through powder metallurgy technique. As the tungsten
powder is hard and deforms less under pressure, it is
generally blended with a binder or with small amounts of
other materials that provide the desired properties to the
ﬁnal product [ 5]. The powder is compacted into billets
using a uniaxial or biaxial die press or by isostatic pressing
using hydrostatic pressure. The compacted part, which is
known as green compact, is heat treated using a sintering
technique. During the sintering process, the compact
becomes more denser. The sintered part may be further
subjected to metal working operations and heat treatment,
depending on the requirement of the ﬁnal ﬁnished product.
1.1 Application of Tungsten Alloys
Due to the excellent physical and chemical properties,
tungsten and its alloys ﬁnd applications in various ﬁelds
such as automotive, medical, aerospace, electronics etc. As
tungsten retains its strength at elevated temperatures and
has high melting point, it is used in high temperature
applications, such as light bulb, cathode ray tube and
vacuum tube ﬁlaments, heating elements, rocket engine

1.2 Tungsten Based Alloys
The type of tungsten alloy is selected based on the appli-
cation and requirement of the ﬁnished product. Some of the
important alloys are highlighted here. The addition of
rhenium to tungsten offers greater ductility and better creep
resistance. Tungsten-Rhenium alloy also has higher
recrystallization temperature and better weldability and
corrosion resistance. They are used as a material for ther-
mocouples to measure temperatures of the order of
2600 /C176C in high temperature furnaces, combustion cham-
bers of turbo engines and reactors. The common alloys for
thermo elements are W–5Re/W–26Re and W–3Re/W–
25Re. They are also employed in high temperature appli-
cations such as ignition tubes of rocket engines and ther-
mionic emitters in spacecraft [ 5]. Tungsten-Titanium alloys
(W–10Ti) are used as sputtering target in the manufacture
of microelectronic devices. The addition of up to 25 % wt
of Titanium to tungsten provides good fracture toughness
and high densiﬁcation. They are also used as plasma facing
materials in nuclear energy systems [ 6, 7]. Tungsten-Cop-
per and Tungsten-Silver are two phase alloys, used to
combine the advantage of high hardness and wear resis-
tance of tungsten with high electrical and thermal con-
ductivity of copper and silver. They are employed in arcing
contacts of high and medium voltage circuit breakers, as
electrodes in electric spark erosion cutting machines and as
resistance welding electrodes. They are also used as base
plates and heat spreaders in radar technology, heat sink
material for electronic packaging. The W–Cu and W–Ag
alloys are generally produced by inﬁltration techniques and
at higher concentrations, they are solid state sintered after
blending and pressing. The high energy milling method is
found to improve the properties of the alloy further
[5, 8, 9]. Tungsten is also alloyed with chromium to form
protective oxide scales which offer protection from oxi-
dation [ 10, 11].
One of the important class of tungsten alloys are
Tungsten Heavy Metal Alloys (WHA). They are generally
two phase alloys with the tungsten grains contained in a
ductile matrix phase. The binder matrix includes a com-
bination of transition elements such as nickel, iron, copper
or cobalt. Due to the unique combination of high density,
mechanical strength, ductility, good machinability, cor-
rosion resistance and economy, they are employed in
many engineering applications. They are used in crank-
shaft balance weights in engines, absorbers and dampers
in drive trains, rotating inertia members, as precision tools
for machining, oscillating weights in watch industry, as
optical precision instruments, as a substitute for lead in
sports equipments, etc. They also have high absorption
capacity for X-rays and gamma rays and therefore, ideal
material for radiation shielding. They are also used in
defence applications such as kinetic energy penetrators
and anti-armour products [ 5, 12]. To obtain near theo-
retical density, dimensional stability, high hardness and
very high ductility of the alloy, different techniques have
been followed through powder metallurgy route. The
recent advances in consolidating the tungsten and tung-
sten heavy alloys of W–Ni–Fe and W–Ni–Cu have been
reviewed in this article.
2 Synthesis of Pure Tungsten
The tungsten powder is difﬁcult to compact due to its high
hardness and little deformation under pressure [ 5]. Two
major methods used for compacting the powders are die
pressing and isostatic pressing. In die pressing, the powders
are pressed in rigid dies using mechanical or hydraulic
presses. In isostatic pressing the powders are ﬁlled in
moulds of rubber or elastomers and subjected to hydro-
static pressing. The green density normally obtained for
consolidating pure tungsten is in the range of 55–65 % of
the theoretical density. Poster [ 13] summarized the factors
affecting the compaction of tungsten powders. Irregular
shaped particles give less dense green compact compared
to regular shaped particles. Also, ﬁner tungsten powders
produces less dense green compacts than coarser powders
due to increased friction between the particles while
applying pressure. Sintering of tungsten is done in the
temperature range of 2000–3050 /C176C under ﬂowing hydro-
gen using conventional sintering methods [ 14]. Higher
sintering temperature is needed to get higher densities at
shorter period of time. For example, more than 50 h is
required to obtain 92 % density with high strength for a
tungsten powder of 4 lm particle size at 1800 /C176C. But it
takes only 1–2 h of sintering to obtain the same density and
strength at 2400 /C176C[ 15]. The densiﬁcation during sintering
also depends upon powder particle size, green density,
compact size, sintering atmosphere, powder purity, heating
rate and thermal gradient.

The other developing sintering method used to consol-
idate and sinter metal powders is spark plasma sintering
(SPS), also referred to as ﬁeld assisted sintering technique

Graphite foils are used between the die and the punch, and
between the powder and the punch, for easy removal of the
sample. The powders are subjected to simultaneous appli-
cation of pressure and pulsed current. Zhipeng gao et al.

0.5 lm. The SEM image of the received tungsten powder

cobalt occupying the remaining percent [ 21, 22]. The

tungsten heavy alloys are generally liquid phase sintered
(LPS) in which the sintering temperature is maintained
above the lowest melting temperature of the constituent.
The densiﬁcation through LPS involves the following
stages of particle rearrangement, solution-reprecipitation
and solid-state sintering. In the rearrangement stage, the
liquid wets the W grains and densiﬁcation occurs through
capillary forces. The sharp edges of the W grains gets
dissolved in the molten matrix. The small grains saturate
and get reprecipitated on to the coarser W grains. This
mechanism in the second stage is known as Ostwald-
ripening mechanism. The continued densiﬁcation occurs
through the solution reprecipitation and solid skeleton
sintering that helps to eliminate residual pores as the grains
change shape and size.
3.1 Consolidation of W–Ni–Fe Alloy
In W–Ni–Fe alloy, the W grains get interspersed in the
FCC matrix of Ni–Fe–W solid solution. The solubility of
tungsten in the binder phase increases by about 16 at%
when the liquid forms. The melt spreads to ﬁll the small
pores and penetrates the grain boundaries. It also dissolves
the interparticle necks, resulting in the decrease of solid
liquid interfacial energy below the equilibrium value and
contributing to the densiﬁcation in the initial stage. From
the Fe–Ni phase diagrams, it is evident that iron and nickel
are mutually soluble over a wide temperature range. In the
Fe–Ni–W phase diagrams, ( cFe,Ni) acts as one of the main
phases between 800 and 1500 /C176C. The eutectic Ni–Fe–W
liquid phase forms at 1465 /C176C. The melting temperature of
the Ni–Fe matrix is well above the melting point of the
elemental nickel. The alloy system may also have other
phases due to the formation of intermetallics.

microstructure effects on the tensile properties of the
specimen. They found that the total elongation is decreased
with increasing tungsten content, but the ﬂow stress is
increased with increase in the content of tungsten. The

sintering of metal powders by Roy et al., the use of
microwaves to consolidate different metal samples gained
momentum. Upadhyaya et al. [ 33] compared the effect of

conventionally sintered compact. The elemental composi-

requisite proportions in a Turbula mixer for about 30 min.
The powder mixture is uniaxially pressed at 200 MPa in a

3, which corresponds to about 61 % of the the-

tensile strength. But both the samples attain [98.5 %
density.

Spark plasma sintering, a recent technique, is used to
sinter compacts in less than one hour. A pulsed direct
current is applied to the powders and simultaneously they
are subjected to a pressure of 30–80 MPa. Li et al. [ 37]
studied in detail the microstructure and mechanical prop-

SPS and conventional methods. The heating rate in SPS

temperature is determined by trial and error method
(Table 3). The sintering temperature is the reference tem-

squeeze out from the die cavity. The actual specimen
temperature remains the same (1460–1465 /C176C). In con-
ventional method, the powders are subjected to isostatic
cold pressing under a pressure of 250 MPa and liquid phase
sintered in hydrogen atmosphere.
A relative density of 0.987 is obtained through con-
ventional sintering. But in SPS process, it is about 0.95 for
all sintering conditions. The liquid phase sintering in SPS is
done only for a short period to avoid the squeezing out of
the melted matrix phase. Therefore, the solution-reprecip-
itation mechanism of the liquid phase is restrained. Hu
et al. [ 38] obtained a relative density of around 0.95 with a
solid state sintered microstructure, while sintering in SPS at
1320 /C176C by using a heating rate of 900 /C176C/min. Therefore,
the liquid phase formation discussed by Li et al. [ 37] does
not contribute to densiﬁcation, but gives a smoother and
rounded grain surface by dissolution of the sharp edges of
tungsten grains. The tungsten grains obtained from con-

long process time. The SPS process gives a much ﬁner
tungsten grains, as shown in Fig. 14. The contiguity of the

4 Conclusion
The spark plasma sintering is a new process to consolidate
tungsten and tungsten heavy alloys. The heating rate is
high and the sintering is completed within 20 min. By
controlling the parameters of the SPS process effectively,
such as, applied pressure, sintering temperature, heating
rate etc., it is possible to achieve a relative density of more
than 97 % of the theoretical density. For larger sample
diameters of 60 mm, hybrid spark plasma sintering gives
higher density and better microstructure than SPS. Micro-
wave sintering is also used extensively to sinter the tung-
sten alloys, due to 90 % overall reduction in process time,
and volumetric heating of the compacts compared to con-
ventional resistance heating method. The mechanical
properties of the sample and its microstructure also depend
on the powder particle size and its characteristics. Exper-
iments have been performed by several researchers on ﬁne
grain tungsten powders. The smaller particle size does not
necessarily lead to higher sintered density, though the
green density is more. The results depend on combination
of powder characteristics. The effects of additions of ele-
ments like cobalt, molybdenum, manganese on the tung-
sten heavy alloys are also being carried out, to reﬁne the
grain size and to improve the performance of the alloys
further.
