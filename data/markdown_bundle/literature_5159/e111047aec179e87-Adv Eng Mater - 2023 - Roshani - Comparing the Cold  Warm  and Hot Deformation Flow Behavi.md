Comparing the Cold, Warm, and Hot Deformation
Flow Behavior of Selective Laser-Melted and
Electron-Beam-Melted Ti –6Al–2Sn–4Zr–2Mo Alloy
Mehrad Roshani, Hamid Reza Abedi,* and Abdollah Saboori
1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are considered suitable candidates for high-
temperature strategic applications due to their low density,
low Young’s modulus, high thermal stability, and corrosion resis-
tance.[1] These alloys typically consist of the α phase, which pos-
sesses a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure and remains
stable at room temperature. However, at a speci ﬁc temperature
known as the transus temperature, a transformation occurs,
converting the alloy into the β phase with a body-centered cubic
(BCC) structure. [2,3] The combination of these two phases
imparts several unique properties to the
alloy, including excellent strength and frac-
ture toughness. Despite these promising
properties, the production of components
using titanium alloys presents several chal-
lenges. Casting of titanium alloys is known
to be expensive and time-consuming due to
their high melting point and chemical reac-
tivity. Additionally, the inherent low ther-
mal conductivity and elastic modulus of
titanium alloys contribute to poor machin-
ability, further increasing production
costs.[4,5] Therefore, it is crucial to explore
economical or near-net shape processes
that can reduce the production cost of these
alloys.[6]
In recent years, the fabrication of
near-net-shape titanium components using
layer-by-layer additive manufacturing (AM)
methods has garnered signi ﬁcant atten-
tion.[7] The appeal of these AM processes
stems from their ability to minimize mate-
rial waste and eliminate the need for post-
machining steps. Among the AM techniques, powder bed fusion
(PBF) processes, such as selective laser melting (SLM) and elec-
tron beam melting (EBM), have demonstrated their capability to
fabricate various types of titanium alloys, [8] including intermetal-
lic γ-TiAl.[9,10] The ultrahigh cooling rates experienced during
SLM and EBM methods result in appreciable microstructure
reﬁnement and material strengthening. However, it has been
reported that the rough surface of as-built PBF components,
comprising unmelted powders, can act as crack initiation cen-
ters, thereby deteriorating the material ’s mechanical proper-
ties.[11,12] Nevertheless, parts produced using the SLM method
exhibit higher speci ﬁc strength and hardness compared to those
fabricated through conventional methods. [2,4,5,13] Consequently,
several studies have focused on comparing the mechanical prop-
erties of SLMed products with parts produced through traditional
processes such as casting and forging. [14– 16] In contrast, it is well
documented that the as-built microstructure and performance of
components differ between the EBM and SLM processes due to
their inherent process variations. Therefore, this aspect should
be considered an important factor, particularly when producing
complex-shaped components. The necessity for such compara-
tive evaluations becomes more signi ﬁcant when targeting the
high-temperature mechanical properties of AM materials.

The cold, warm, and hot deformation ﬂow behavior and mechanical properties of
the Ti-6242 alloy produced by selective laser melting (SLM) and electron beam
melting (EBM) are compared. Hot compression tests are conducted over a wide
temperature range (100 –1000 °C) at a constant strain rate of 0.001 s /C01. The yield
and overall strength levels of the SLMed specimens are higher than those of the
EBMed specimens at all temperatures. A thermally stable region is observed for
SLMed specimens in the temperature range of 100 –700 °C, but the strength level
drops signi ﬁcantly (approximately 300 –500 MPa) at above 700 °C. The stability
region shifts to the lower temperature range of 100 –600°Cfor the EBMed
specimens, and strength starts to decrease at 600 °C. Both specimens experience
fracture in a cold-temperature regime but exhibit a high strain tolerance of
approximately 0.4. The SLMed samples display a completely brittle behavior at a
warm temperature regime, whereas, the EBMed specimens demonstrate better
formability, tolerating higher compressive strains. The softening fraction sub-
stantially increases at high temperatures, indicating safe domains for thermo-
mechanical processing of additively manufactured Ti6242 alloy.

mechanical properties between Ti6242 and Ti64 alloys. They
found that the SLMed Ti6242 alloy exhibited higher room-
and high-temperature strength and ductility compared to
SLMed Ti64 and as-cast Ti6242 alloys. Furthermore, Ti6242 parts
manufactured using EBM were found to have a lower content of
internal defects compared to those produced using SLM. [21]
Additionally, it was revealed that the ultimate tensile strength,
yield strength, and elongation to fracture of the specimens fab-
ricated via SLM were signi ﬁcantly higher than those of the EBM-
fabricated Ti6242S alloy at ambient temperature. [19,22]
It is evident that previous research has primarily focused on
investigating the mechanical properties of AM titanium alloys at
room temperature. These studies have explored the impact of
heat treatments on mechanical properties and have compared
the balance between strength and ductility with components pro-
duced through conventional methods. However, two crucial
aspects have been largely overlooked in the existing research.
First, the high-temperature mechanical properties of AM
materials have not been extensively studied. Second, there is a
lack of comparison regarding the mechanical performance of
parts fabricated using different PBF methods. Therefore, the
objective of the current work is to address these gaps by
conducting a comparative analysis of the ﬂow behavior of as-built
Ti– 6Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 2Mo alloy fabricated through SLM and EBM
techniques. This will be achieved through compression tests con-
ducted across the cold, warm, and hot deformation regimes. By
examining the material response under different deformation
conditions, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanical
performance of SLM and EBM Ti – 6Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 2Mo alloy
can be obtained, shedding light on its suitability for high-
temperature applications.
2. Experimental Section
2.1. Sample Production
In this study, gas-atomized Ti – 6Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 2Mo powder was
utilized to produce SLM samples. The powder had a particle size
ranging between 15 and 45 μm, with an average diameter of
32 μm. The SLM samples were built using a Concept-Laser
Mlab Cusing R machine. The laser power used was 100 W,
and the spot size of the laser beam was 50 μm. To ensure the
quality of the samples, speci ﬁc process parameters were
employed, which are presented in Table 1 as the optimized
process parameters for this study. Prior to the building process,
argon gas was purged into the chamber to maintain the oxygen
content below 0.2%, thus minimizing the in ﬂuence of oxygen on
the fabrication process.
In contrast, for the production of the EBM samples, a
gas-atomized spherical Ti – 6Al– 2Sn– 4Zr– 2Mo powder with a
particle size range between 45 and 106 μm, and an average diam-
eter of 75 μm, was employed as the feedstock material. The speci-
mens were fabricated using an Arcam-A2X-EBM machine in a
cylindrical form, with a height and diameter of 12 and 8 mm,
respectively. The Z-axis was considered as the building direction
for the EBM process. The fabrication parameters used for the
EBM process are presented in Table 2 . During fabrication, the
specimens were built vertically under an accelerating voltage
of 60 kV. The start plate used was made of stainless steel and
was preheated to 700 °C prior to the dispersion of the powders.
Throughout the EBM process, the temperature of the powder bed
was maintained at a minimum of 600 °C to ensure proper melt-
ing and consolidation of the powder particles.
Figure 1 illustrates the schematic layout of both the SLM and
EBM processes. The size and shape of the powders used in these
processes were crucial factors that impacted the mechanical
properties of the ﬁnal product. To achieve complete densi ﬁcation
and high-density parts, a wider range of particle sizes should be
employed. However, the speci ﬁc range of particle sizes required
might vary depending on the PBF method used. In this study, the
selected powder size ranges were previously utilized in similar
research studies. [13,23,24] AM of titanium alloys, particularly
through PBF processes, presented signi ﬁcant challenges related
to shrinkage and porosity, surface ﬁnish requirements, and tol-
erance speciﬁcations. In this study, the aim is to fabricate sound
components with full densi ﬁcation, high dimensional accuracy,
and acceptable surface roughness. To achieve this, the optimum
fabrication parameters for SLM and EBM were determined based
on the researchers ’ own experience and a comprehensive analy-
sis of the ﬁndings reported in previous studies. [8,19,25]

3.2. Flow Stress Behavior
The compressive true stress – true strain curves of the as-built
SLM and EBM specimens at various temperatures are shown
in Figure 3 a– d– f, respectively. It is evident from these ﬁgures
that as the temperature increases, there are noticeable changes
in the strength level, hardening and softening rates, and overall
ﬂow behavior of both EBMed and SLMed samples. It is observed
that the SLMed specimens exhibit higher strength values com-
pared to the EBMed specimens. Furthermore, a signi ﬁcant drop
in strength is observed at 700 °C for the EBMed specimens and at
higher temperatures for the SLMed specimens. This reduction in
strength amounts to approximately 300– 500 MPa. It is worth not-
ing that at temperatures ranging from 100 to 600 °C for SLMed
specimens (Figure 3a) and 100 – 500 °C for EBMed specimens
(Figure 3d), fracture occurs without signi ﬁcant softening.
However, the high amount of compressive strain, approximately
0.4, tolerated at such low temperatures is surprising. This behav-
ior may be attributed to the activation of nano-twinning within
the martensite phases, which warrants further microstructural
analysis for a deeper understanding. The presented graphs
highlight the distinct mechanical responses of the SLMed and
EBMed specimens at different temperatures. The strength levels,
hardening and softening rates, as well as the overall ﬂow behav-
ior, undergo signi ﬁcant changes with increasing temperature.
Additionally, the superior strength exhibited by the SLMed speci-
mens and the substantial drop in strength at elevated tempera-
tures emphasize the importance of temperature control and
optimization in the manufacturing process.
Furthermore, it is observed that at cold to warm temperatures,
particularly between 400 and 600 °C, the as-built EBM specimens
exhibit higher tolerance for compressive strains compared to the
SLMed specimens. As depicted in Figure 3b, the SLMed
samples at temperatures of 500 and 600 °C exhibit a completely
brittle behavior with negligible softening rate. In contrast, the
EBMed sample displays signi ﬁcant deformation and can sustain
appreciable strains. This difference in behavior may be attributed
to the activation of dynamic strain aging (DSA), which adversely
affects the formability of the material. DSA refers to the phenom-
enon where the material experiences a reduction in ductility and
an increase in strain hardening rate when deformed within a spe-
ciﬁc temperature range. The activation of DSA can lead to a more
brittle response in the material and a limited ability to sustain
plastic deformation. This observation suggests that the EBMed
specimens are more resistant to the detrimental effects of
DSA, allowing them to undergo larger plastic deformations at
these temperatures compared to the SLMed specimens.
Understanding and mitigating the effects of DSA is crucial
for optimizing the formability and mechanical properties of
materials, especially in the context of cold to warm temperature
regimes. Further analysis and investigation are warranted to
explore the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed
differences in behavior between the SLMed and EBMed speci-
mens under these speci ﬁc temperature conditions.
Figure 3 clearly illustrates that the ﬂow stress behavior of the
as-built EBM and SLM samples undergoes signi ﬁcant changes
with increasing temperature, up to approximately 600 °C for
EBM and 700 °C for SLM samples. In both cases, the hardening
stage gradually diminishes, and the softening fraction becomes
more prominent. The ﬂow behavior of the material after reaching
the peak stress is typically characterized by the strain rate sensi-
tivity (m), which is closely related to the material ’s ability to dis-
tribute strain within its microstructure. At higher temperatures,
the slip activity of non-basal systems becomes more pronounced.
This increased slip activity can be attributed to the enhanced
mobility of dislocations, resulting in higher m-values and a more
signiﬁcant softening fraction. The observed increase in m-values
and softening fraction is reasonable in light of the heightened
slip activity of non-basal systems at elevated temperatures. [27– 29]

pressive formability of both as-built EBM and SLM specimens
increases with temperature. This improvement in formability
can be attributed to two main factors: dynamic globularization

plastic strain, are key characteristics of specimens produced
through PBF methods.[37– 40] These features play a signiﬁcant role
in shaping the material ’s ﬂow behavior and formability at both
room and high temperatures. Understanding and leveraging
these characteristics are vital for optimizing the manufacturing
and thermomechanical processing of PBF-produced materials.

3.3. Peak Stress, Peak Strain, and Yield Stress
Figure 4 provides a comparative analysis of the effect of
temperature on the peak stress, peak strain, and yield stress
of specimens produced through SLM and EBM processes. It
is noteworthy that the SLMed specimens exhibit higher yield
strength and peak stress compared to the EBM samples. As
the temperature increases, it is observed that the yield stress
decreases, and the ultimate compressive stress is reduced for
both SLMed and EBMed specimens. However, a thermally stable
region can be identi ﬁed for the SLMed specimens in the temper-
ature range of 100 – 700 °C. Within this range, the yield strength
shows a bump hardening behavior, indicating an increase in
strength between 500 and 700 °C. This suggests the existence
of an optimum temperature range for the SLMed specimens,
where the material exhibits improved strength and stability.
Above 700 °C, the decrease in strength becomes more pro-
nounced, indicating the maximum temperature limit for service-
ability. For the EBMed specimens, the stability region is shifted
to a lower temperature range of 100 – 600 °C, and the strength
starts to decrease at 600 °C. This suggests that the EBMed speci-
mens have a narrower thermally stable region compared to the
SLMed specimens. The observed differences in yield strength
and peak stress between the SLMed and EBMed specimens
can be attributed to variations in the microstructure and defect
characteristics resulting from the different manufacturing
processes. The SLM process typically produces specimens with
a ﬁner and more homogeneous microstructure, contributing to
higher strength properties.
In general, as the temperature increases, the peak strain cor-
responding to the peak stress decreases. This behavior can be
attributed to the activation of softening mechanisms that
overcome the work hardening, leading to a reduction in the
strength level and causing the peak strain to occur at a lower level.
This implies better hot workability at higher deformation temper-
atures, as the material can sustain larger strains before reaching its
peak stress. For the EBM samples, the peak strain decreases
monotonically with increasing temperature. This indicates that
the softening mechanisms dominate over the work hardening,
resulting in a decrease in the material ’s capability to sustain large
strains as the temperature rises. However, in the case of SLM
specimens, the behavior is more complex. The curves represent-
ing the peak strain versus temperature exhibit changes in direc-
tion, and bumps are observed at temperatures of 200 and
700 °C. This suggests that the SLMed specimens are more suscep-
tible to a shift in the dominant deformation and restoration mech-
anisms with increasing temperature. The presence of these
bumps indicates variations in the material ’s response and behav-
ior, possibly due to the in ﬂuence of additional factors or compet-
ing mechanisms at those speci ﬁc temperatures. In the cold to
warm temperature regimes, the EBMed specimens achieve higher
peak strains compared to the SLMed specimens. This suggests
that the EBMed specimens have a higher capability for strain hard-
ening during compressive loading in these temperature ranges.
The ability to undergo signi ﬁcant strain hardening is bene ﬁcial
as it enhances the material’s capacity to absorb energy and deform
plastically, making it more resistant to failure.

achieved by SLMed and EBMed Ti6242 are also comparable to
those of additively manufactured Ti64 alloys and even compete
with extruded Ti64 alloy in solution-treated and aged states.
This indicates that the near-net shape SLMed and EBMed
Ti6242 parts have the potential to serve as replacement candidates
for various strategic applications. The exceptional strength-
to-weight ratios exhibited by these materials are particularly note-
worthy. This means that the high strength levels are maintained
while keeping the material lightweight, leading to a favorable com-
bination of strength and weight ef ﬁciency. Furthermore, the fact
that these high strength-to-weight ratios are maintained over a
wide temperature range highlights the high-temperature stability
of lightweight structures fabricated using SLM and EBM methods.
The ability to achieve such outstanding mechanical properties and
stability at elevated temperatures makes SLMed and EBMed
Ti6242 parts promising candidates for a range of applications.
These materials can offer signi ﬁcant advantages in terms of per-
formance, reliability, and weight reduction, which are particularly
valuable in strategic applications where strength, durability, and
thermal stability are essential considerations.
4. Conclusions
The comparison of cold, warm, and hot deformation behavior
between Ti-6242 alloy processed via EBM and SLM reveals
several key ﬁndings. The conclusions drawn from this work
are as follows. 1) The as-built SLM specimens exhibited higher
strength values compared to the EBMed specimens. This sug-
gests that the SLM process produces parts with higher initial
strength; 2) A signi ﬁcant drop in strength level was observed
for both EBM and SLM specimens. In the case of EBM, this drop
occurred at a temperature of 600 °C, while for SLM specimens, it
was observed at a higher temperature. This indicates a decrease
in strength with increasing temperature for both processing
methods; 3) Both EBM and SLM specimens demonstrated
high tolerance for compressive strain at cold temperatures, with
a strain of about 0.4 achievable. However, in the warm tempera-
ture regime, particularly between 400 and 600 °C, the EBM
specimens exhibited better performance compared to SLM
specimens. The SLM samples at 500 and 600 °C displayed a brit-
tle behavior, while the EBM samples were able to deform to con-
siderable strains; 4) The yield strengths of both EBM and SLM
samples experienced minor changes over a wide range of tem-
peratures. A thermally stable region was identi ﬁed for SLMed
specimens in the range of 100 – 700 °C, while for EBMed speci-
mens, it was recognized in the range of 100 – 600 °C. This indi-
cates that the mechanical properties of the materials remain
relatively stable within these temperature ranges; 5) The yield
stress, fracture strength, and peak stress of SLM specimens were
higher compared to the EBM specimens. In contrast, the EBM
specimens achieved higher peak strains and exhibited a higher
tolerance for fracture strain, indicating their superior formability;
6) Both EBM and SLM processes resulted in Ti-6242 alloy parts
with higher strength compared to conventional manufacturing
methods, while maintaining a reasonably low apparent density.
This highlights the advantage of AM techniques in producing
high-strength components with reduced weight.
These ﬁndings underline the potential of EBM and SLM pro-
cesses in fabricating Ti6242 alloy parts with superior mechanical
properties and formability, making them suitable for a range of
applications where high strength, low weight, and good deforma-
tion characteristics are crucial. considerations.
