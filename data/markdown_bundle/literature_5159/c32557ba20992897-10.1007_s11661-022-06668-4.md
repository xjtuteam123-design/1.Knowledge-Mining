Microstructure and Mechanical Properties
of a High-Strength Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu Alloy Fabricated
by Sintering and Hot Extrusion
MOJTABA NAJAFIZADEH, DELIANG ZHANG, ALIREZA MALDAR,
MANSOOR BOZORG, and JIAMIAO LIANG
A Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu (wt pct) alloy containing only low-cost alloying elements was fabricated by
vacuum sintering a blend of TiH 2, Al, Fe, and Cu powders at 1200 /C176C for 1 hour followed by hot
extrusion at the same temperature. The as-extruded alloy exhibited a microstructure consisting
of mainly a/b lamellar colonies and Ti
2Cu as a minor phase. The average colony size and lamella
thickness were 118 and 12 lm, respectively, and Fe and Cu were predominantly distributed in
the b lamellae. The as-extruded alloy had a high tensile yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) of 1248 and 1270 MPa, respectively, but a limited ductility (elongation to
fracture: 2.3 pct). Annealing at 750 /C176C for 4 hour caused the average colony size and lamella
thickness of the alloy to increase to 145 and 17 lm, respectively, and the volume fraction of the
b phase decreased with the annealing. These microstructural changes resulted in a slight
decrease of the YS and UTS to 1221 and 1253 MPa, but a clear increase of the ductility with the
elongation to fracture reaching 4 pct. This work demonstrates that a combination of relatively
low-temperature vacuum sintering, hot extrusion, and annealing can be eﬀectively utilized to
fabricate a low-cost Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu alloy with high strength and appreciable tensile ductility.

I. INTRODUCTION
TITANIUM and titanium alloys are widely used in
aerospace, chemical engineering, biomedical, automo-
tive, sport equipment manufacturing industries thanks
to their outstanding mechanical and other properties
such as high strength, good ductility, high fracture
toughness, good biocompatibility, and high corrosion
resistance.
[1–5] However, as engineering materials, the
total amount of titanium and titanium alloys used is
relatively small, being only a tiny fraction ( <0.2 pct) of
the amount of aluminum alloys. The most signiﬁcant
limitation for using a large quantity of titanium and
titanium alloys as engineering materials is the high cost
of titanium and titanium alloy products in the forms of
components and mill products such as plates, sheets,
and tubes. The reasons for the high cost include the
relatively high cost of Ti sponge and some alloying
elements such as V and Mo, and the complex metallur-
gical processing routes for producing titanium and
titanium alloy products. Various research endeavors
have been made to reduce the cost of titanium and
titanium alloy products, and one way of them is to use
inexpensive and readily available alloying elements or
master alloys in making the alloy.
[6,7] Another way is to
use powder metallurgy (PM) in conjunction with
blended elemental (BE) powders to produce titanium
and titanium alloy near-net-shaped components and
mill products. Furthermore, by employing the BE-PM
process, titanium alloys with a large variety of alloy
compositions and microstructural features can be fab-
ricated, and this advantage makes the BE-PM route
more attractive than traditional ingot metallurgy (IM)
processes and the PM routes utilizing pre-alloyed
titanium alloy powders.
[8] By pre-sintering of powder
blends at low temperatures and for a short time and
subsequent hot extrusion or forging, the cost of titanium
and titanium alloy can be further reduced, and the
processing time can be shortened. Good mechanical
properties can be achieved due to the ﬁne and uniform

with excellent mechanical properties at room tempera-

Recently, we fabricated a Ti-4Al-3Cu-2Fe (wt pct)
alloy by hot extrusion of a compact of TiH 2, Al, Fe, and
Cu powder blend, and demonstrated that the alloy had a
fully a/b lamellar microstructure with a high yield
strength (YS) of 1159 MPa, high ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) of 1240 MPa, and a relatively low
tensile ductility of 2 pct. [12] After a homogenization
treatment, the YS and UTS of the alloy were reduced
slightly to 1122 and 1214 MPa, respectively, and its
tensile ductility increased clearly to 4.2 pct. It was also
shown that this alloy had a great potential for biomed-
ical applications due to its high corrosion resistance.
[13]
Zadorozhnyy et al . fabricated a similar titanium alloy
with a composition of Ti-5Fe-5Cu-1.5Al, and showed
that the relatively high Fe and Cu contents and low Al
content made the microstructure of the alloy mainly
contain b phase.
[14] The UTS and tensile ductility of this
alloy are clearly lower than those of the Ti-4Al-3Cu-2Fe
alloy (1140 vs. 1214 MPa and 2 pct vs. 4.2 pct). Overall,
titanium alloys containing mostly inexpensive alloying
elements such as Fe and Cu can have mechanical
properties which are comparable with those of the other
titanium alloys containing much more expensive alloy-
ing elements such as V and Mo.
[15] Both Fe and Cu are b
phase stabilizing elements in titanium alloys, and adding
them to titanium alloys can increase strength, hardness,
and wear resistance, and improve their hot cracking
resistance and ductility.
[16,17]
The BE-PM process normally involves sintering com-
pacts of powder blends of Ti and alloying elements at a
temperature of 1300 /C176C or higher for two or more hours in
argon or vacuum to achieve required density and com-
positional homogeneity.
[18–20] The prolonged sintering in
addition to extended heating and cooling steps signiﬁ-
cantly reduces the production eﬃciency. In contrast, by
thermomechanical processing (i.e., extrusion, forging,
etc.) of sintered powder compacts titanium and titanium
alloy powders can be eﬃciently consolidated into
near-net-shaped components and structural members
within a time of 20 minutes or less. It has been demon-
strated that Ti-6Al-4V and Ti-4Al-4Sn-4Mo-0.5Si alloys
fabricated by this process in conjunction with elemental
powder blends or pre-alloyed powders have similar or
better mechanical properties than their counterparts
fabricated by ingot metallurgy.
[21–25]
Based on the above considerations, we combined
vacuum sintering of a blend of TiH 2, Al, Cu, and Fe
powders at a relatively low temperature of 1200 /C176C for a
short time of 1 hour and hot extrusion of the sintered
powder compact at the same temperature to fabricate a
Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu alloy, and investigated the microstruc-
tures and mechanical properties of the alloy before and
after annealing. The aim of the study is to ascertain
whether the combination of low-temperature sintering
and thermomechanical processing would lead to good
tensile properties of the low-cost Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu alloy,
and to elucidate the correlation between its microstruc-
ture and tensile properties.
II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
The raw materials are TiH
2 powder ( /C0 200 mesh,
purity: 99.5 pct), Al powder ( /C0 325 mesh, purity: 99.9
pct), Fe powder ( /C0 200 mesh, purity: 99.9 pct), and Cu
powder ( /C0 300 mesh, purity: 99.9 pct). The particle
morphologies of the TiH 2 powder were observed using

2 powder are D10: 9.2 lm, D50:
38.4 lm, and D90: 84.2 lm. The powders were heated at

tumbler milling machine.
The blended powder having a nominal composition
of Ti-4Al-2Fe-3Cu was die-pressed into a cylindrical
powder compact with a diameter of 26 mm, a height
of 25 mm, and a relative density of 87 pct. The
powder compact was sintered at 1200 /C176C for 1 hour in
a vacuum furnace to achieve a relative density of 96
pct. The nominal heating and cooling rates were both
programmed at 10 /C176C/min, but the cooling rate was
lower at temperatures below 500 /C176C due to the
limitation of furnace cooling. The sintered powder
compact was induction heated to 1200 /C176C, held at the
temperature for 5 minutes, and then extruded into a
circular rod with an extrusion ratio of 9:1. The
heating, holding, and extrusion of the sintered powder
compact were all performed within a glovebox ﬁlled
with argon. The extrusion cylinder and die were
heated to 500 /C176C before carrying out the extrusion
experiment. Part of the extruded rod was annealed at
750 /C176C for 4 hour in a glass tube furnace with a
vacuum of 10

III. RESULTS
A. Microstructure
The XRD patterns of the SE and ASE samples are
depicted in Figure 2. As shown in Figure 2, the major
phase in the SE sample was a-Ti, while the minor phases
were b-Ti phase and Ti 2Cu intermetallic compound.
However, after annealing, the Ti 2Cu phase disappeared,
TiO phase appeared, and the b/a peak intensity ratio
decreased, indicating a decrease in the fraction of b
phase.
As shown in Figures 3 and 4, the SE and ASE samples
had an a/b lamellar microstructure together with a
plates located at the prior b grain boundaries. As shown
in Figure 3(a), the SE sample was almost fully dense,
indicating that the residual pores in the vacuum sintered
compact of the alloy were closed during extrusion.
As conﬁrmed by EDS point analysis (Table II), the
dark lamellae were richer in Al than the bright lamellae,
and Fe and Cu were segregated into the bright lamellae.
Considering that Fe and Cu are b stabilizing elements,
the dark lamellae are a phase, and the bright lamellae
are b phase. The EDS elemental mapping (Figures 5(a)
and (b)) conﬁrmed the results of the EDS point analysis.
The signiﬁcant parameters of the lamellar microstruc-
ture related to the mechanical properties are the average
size of lamellar colonies ( C
G) and the average thickness
of the lamellae ( k). Their values were measured by image
analysis, and are shown in Table III.
Figure 6 presents the aggregation maps of a and b
phases in the microstructures of the SE and ASE
samples. Based on the image analysis of the maps, the
thickness distributions, average thicknesses, and volume
fractions of the a and b lamellae in the microstructures
were determined, and are shown in Figure 7 and
Table III. From Table III, it is clear that annealing
caused the average thicknesses of a and b lamellae to
increase and the volume fraction of b lamellae to
decrease.

IV. DISCUSSION
A. Microstructure
The study on the microstructures of the SE sample
shows that diﬀusion of Al, Fe, and Cu into the Ti
particles during sintering and extrusion results in the
formation of an a/b lamellar microstructure. The EDS
point analysis and elemental mapping of the microstruc-
ture of the SE sample shows that the a lamellae and
plates consist of only Al and Ti, while the b lamellae
consist of Al, Fe, Cu, and Ti. Siqueira et al .
[28] reported
comparable results conﬁrming that there was no Fe in
the a phase. This result conﬁrms that both Fe and Cu
have a low solubility in the a phase at temperatures close
to room temperature, in agreement with the Ti–Fe and
Ti–Cu binary phase diagrams.
[29] As can be seen by
comparing the XRD patterns of SE and ASE samples
and the aggregation of a and b phases in them, the
percentage of b phase in the microstructure decreases
after annealing despite the increase of Fe and Cu
contents in b phase and the b phase stabilization eﬀect
of Fe and Cu. This shows that the microstructure of the
SE sample is metastable due to the relatively high
cooling rate of the as-extruded rod after extrusion in
argon.
This work shows that annealing of the as-extruded
alloy at 750 /C176C for 4 hour causes a clear increase of the
lamellar colony size and the lamella thickness, suggest-
ing that some of the ﬁne a and b lamellae are not
stable and dissolve during annealing, allowing the a and
b lamellae with favorable crystallographic orientations
to grow.
[28] Overall, the lamellar structure is still quite
stable during annealing. The typical lamellar
microstructures of the SE and ASE samples are similar
to the microstructure of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy with a
similar thermal history.
[30–32] This shows that adding Fe
and Cu to a titanium alloy in the place of V does not
change the microstructural evolution path of the
