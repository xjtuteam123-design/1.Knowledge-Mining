ABSTRACT
In recent developments, Cu–Cr–Nb alloys have emerged as promising materi-
als offering a desirable balance between mechanical properties and electrical 
conductivity, rendering them suitable for intricate high heat flux applications. 
However, the laser additive manufacturing of Cu–Cr–Nb alloys has encountered 
notable challenges, primarily stemming from limitations associated with Cu–
Cr–Nb alloy powders. Recognizing the pivotal role of powder feedstock in addi-
tive manufacturing, this study exerts the utilization of ball milling techniques to 
tackle pertinent challenges. Specifically, a kind of powder obtained by mechani-
cal ball milling of elemental powders of Cu, Cr and Nb is employed to fabricate 
Cu–Cr–Nb raw materials, which exhibits a distinctive sesame ball structure and 
effectively mitigates the inherent high reflectivity of Cu powder. The resulting 
milled powder represents a notable departure from the conventional reliance on 
argon gas atomization for Cu–Cr–Nb alloy powder production. Then the study 
encompasses the fabrication of five distinct Cu–Cr–Nb bulk alloys, ranging from 
Cu–1Cr–0.5Nb to Cu–12Cr–6Nb, using laser directed energy deposition. The as-
printed Cu–Cr–Nb alloys exhibit commendable density, surpassing 99% based 
on visual analysis, and demonstrate mechanical properties akin to casts achieved 
through traditional melting and powder metallurgy methods. Notably, Cr2Nb 
particles were observed within all bulk alloys, with their size increasing pro -
portionally with the Cr + Nb content. Tensile strength demonstrated a positive 
correlation with Cr + Nb content, while elongation and conductivity exhibited an 
inverse relationship. These findings hold considerable promise for advancing the

Introduction
In light of the rapid advancements in contemporary 
technologies and industrial sectors, there exists a 
growing demand for sophisticated engineering mate-
rials endowed with exceptional mechanical proper-
ties [1]. Copper-based alloys emerge as indispensable 
materials in high-temperature applications character-
ized by intense heat flux, notably in aerospace and 
thermonuclear reactors, owing to their outstanding 
thermal conductivity [2 , 3]. Regenerative cooled liq-
uid rocket engines, for instance, necessitate copper-
based alloys that can concurrently realize high thermal 
conductivity and mechanical strength [4 ]. Notably, 
a limited selection of Cu-based alloys possesses the 
requisite attributes to meet these demanding cri-
teria, with examples including Cu–Zr, Cu–Ag–Zr, 
Cu–Cr–Zr and Cu–Cr–Nb alloys [ 4, 5]. Among these 
alloys, Cu–Cr–Nb alloys, initially developed by NASA 
Glenn Research Center [6], have garnered considerable 
attention due to their relatively superior performance 
[2]. In Cu–Cr–Nb alloys, the limited solubility of Cr 
and Nb in Cu, combined with their strong mutual 
affinity [6, 7], results in the formation of a random dis-
tribution of intermetallic Cr2Nb particles within the 
alloy matrix [8, 9]. These precipitates serve as strength-
ening phases, leaving behind a nearly pure Cu matrix 
[9, 10]. Consequently, Cu–Cr–Nb alloys with a Cr/Nb 
atomic ratio of 2:1 offer a compelling combination of 
strength and thermal conductivity, making them suita-
ble for applications demanding structural stability and 
efficient heat dissipation. While previous research has 
extensively investigated properties of Cu–8Cr–4Nb 
(at.%, the same as following) and Cu–4Cr–2Nb alloys, 
including conductivity, tensile strength, creep behav-
ior and fatigue properties [4 , 11, 12], the microstruc-
ture and properties of other Cu–Cr–Nb alloy com-
positions, such as Cu–12Cr–6Nb, Cu–2Cr–1Nb and 
Cu–1Cr–0.5Nb, remain largely unexplored.
Initially, Cu–Cr–Nb alloys were synthesized using 
conventional furnace melting and casting methods 
[13, 14], yielding relatively coarse microstructures and 
subpar properties due to the high melting temperature 
of Cr2Nb and the rapid growth of Cr2Nb particles [13]. 
Subsequent utilization of gas atomization facilitated 
utilization of Cu–Cr–Nb alloys in various applications requiring tailored perfor-
mance characteristics.

Material and methods
Powder pretreatment and sample preparation
This study encompassed the design and fabrica-
tion of five distinct alloys with varying Cr + Nb con-
tents: Cu–1Cr–0.5Nb, Cu–2Cr–1Nb, Cu–4Cr–2Nb, 
Cu–8Cr–4Nb and Cu–12Cr–6Nb. The raw materi-
als utilized in this research comprised commercially 
available spherical pure Cu powder, characterized 
by a diameter ranging from 53 to 150 μm, alongside 
mechanically crushed and fragmented pure Cr and 
pure Nb powders, each with particle sizes falling within 
the range of 5–10 μm. Preparation of these raw materi-
als involved precise weighing of pure Cu, Cr and Nb 
powders in accordance with the designed composi-
tion ratios. Subsequently, these powders underwent 
mechanical ball milling under an argon atmosphere, 
with the milling process conducted at a rotational speed 
of 250 rpm. Further details concerning the rationale for 
selecting this rotational speed are available in the sup-
plementary material. The planetary gear ball milling 
machine utilized, designated as the QM-3SP4-CL and 
manufactured by Nanjing University Instrument Co., 
Ltd., boasted a maximum capacity of four liters. To 
prevent overheating during milling, the process was 
periodically paused for 10 min every 30 min of milling 
time. Subsequent to milling, the milled powders were 
sieved through 80 mesh sieves to ensure the uniformity 
of powder feedstock. Given that contamination from 
extraneous materials, particularly elements such as Fe, 
Al and Si, significantly impacts the conductive proper-
ties of copper alloys, including Cu–Cr–Nb, two innova-
tive contamination mitigation strategies were employed 
to mitigate impurities. Notably, one approach involved 
the utilization of pure copper milling balls instead of 
conventional stainless steel or agate balls. This inno-
vative detail ensured that no foreign substances were 
introduced into the Cu–Cr–Nb alloy during the milling 
process, thereby preserving the alloy’s purity. Addition-
ally, despite the use of stainless steel vacuum tanks, a 
relatively low ball milling speed was adopted to further 
reduce the risk of contamination.
The milled powder was introduced into a coaxial 
powder feeding device, an integral component of the 
L-DED setup (Fig. 1). To emulate real-world addi-
tive manufacturing application scenarios and mini-
mize the introduction of impurities, Cu–0.6 wt.% Cr 
sheets served as substrates. These Cu–Cr substrates 
were 10 mm in thickness and were preheated to 80 °C 
to facilitate improved interface bonding and reduce 
thermal stress [31]. The L-DED process entailed the 
convergence of three infrared lasers at a single focal

Microstructure characterization and properties 
measurement
The study employed various analytical techniques to 
comprehensively evaluate the properties and charac-
teristics of the materials under investigation. Powder 
morphology was examined using a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM), while energy dispersive spectros-
copy (EDS) facilitated compositional analysis. To offer 
comprehensive information regarding the ball-milled 
Cu powder, we conducted SEM + EDS characterization 
observations on both the powder surface and pow-
der cross section. For surface observation, the powder 
sample was directly collected and spread on the scan-
ning electron microscope sample stage. Meanwhile, 
for cross-sectional observation, the ball-milled powder 
sample was embedded in resin material. Following the 
sample preparation process, which involved polishing 
to 1500 grit sandpaper and 0.5 μm diamond polishing, 
it was placed for observation on the scanning elec-
tron microscope sample stage. Laser reflectivity was 
assessed using an ultraviolet–visible near-infrared 
spectrophotometer, with subsequent conversion into 
absorbance, while disregarding other factors contrib-
uting to loss. Powder fluidity assessment involved the 
use of a Hall funnel, determining the falling time of 
50 g of powder discharged from the funnel.
Following the synthesis of the as-built alloys, sam-
ples were prepared for subsequent examination. Ini-
tially, these samples were sectioned and embedded 
in epoxy, followed by mechanical grinding using 
600, 1000 and 1500 grit sandpaper to achieve a mir-
ror finish. Optical microscopy (OM) imaging was then 
conducted to provide initial insights into the micro-
structure. To gain deeper insights into the microstruc-
ture, the samples underwent corrosion treatment by a 
solution of HCl and FeCl3, and their structures were 
further examined through SEM imaging. Phase iden-
tification within the as-built alloys was accomplished 
via X-ray diffraction (XRD). Further characterization 
of the specimens was performed using electron back-
scatter diffraction (EBSD) with a step size of 2.25 μm 
on a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-
SEM, FEI Quanta 450 FEG) equipped with an EDAX-
TSL system.
The electrical conductivity of the as-built alloys was 
assessed at room temperature using an eddy current 
conductivity meter. Vickers hardness testing was car-
ried out with a 200 g load and a 10 s dwell time, with 
a minimum of 5 repetitions for each alloy to mitigate 
measurement error. Tensile samples, oriented parallel 
to the laser scanning direction, were obtained using a 
low-speed wire electrical discharge machine from the 
Cu–Cr–Nb wall and the dimensional drawing of the 
tensile sample is in Figure S1. Each alloy underwent 
tensile testing with three replicate samples to establish 
consistent trends. Subsequent to the tensile tests, the 
fracture surfaces were subjected to further scrutiny 
through SEM analysis, enhancing the understanding 
of fracture characteristics and modes.

An investigation into the laser reflectivity of the 
five milled powders, spanning the wavelength range 
of 400–1600 nm, was conducted and compared with 
pure Cu powder (Fig. 6a). Remarkably, a substantial 
reduction in laser reflectivity was observed with the 
introduction of just 1 at.% Cr and 0.5 at.% Nb after 
the milling process. Moreover, it was evident that the 
degree of reduction in laser reflectivity was positive 
correlated with the Cr + Nb content. To provide a com-
prehensive understanding, we specifically assessed 
the laser reflectance of these powders at 1065 nm, 
a critical laser wavelength employed in the L-DED 
process (Fig. 6b). The results revealed an initial rapid 
decrease in laser reflectivity upon the introduction of 
small quantities of Cr and Nb. However, this decline 
somewhat leveled off as the concentration of these 
alloying elements increased further. For instance, 
with the addition of 1 at.% Cr and 0.5 at.% Nb to pure 
Cu, a notable decrease of 14.9% in laser reflectance 
was observed. This reduction could be attributed to a 
combination of factors because chemical composition, 
temperature, roughness/topography, oxide layers and 
contamination are the materials factors affecting laser 
absorption [ 38, 39]. Firstly, the roughened surface of 
the milled particles contributed to this decline by alter-
ing the optical properties of the surface. Brandau et al. 
[40] reported that a flat surface of the same material 
has lower absorbance than the powder surface. Addi-
tionally, the adherence of Cr and Nb to the Cu par-
ticle surface effectively diminished the uncoated Cu 
surface area, resulting in reduced overall laser reflec-
tivity for Cu. However, the extent of improvement in 
laser absorption rate diminished as the composition 
transition from Cu–8Cr–4Nb to Cu–12Cr–6Nb, where 
only a 3.8% enhancement in laser absorption rate was 
obtained. This trend could be elucidated by the satu-
ration effect, where increasing the concentration of Cr 
and Nb did not yield significant improvements in laser 
absorption once the surface of Cu particles was nearly 
entirely covered by Cr and Nb. Research by Simonelli 
et al. [41] showed that the satelliting effect could result 
in microstructural features similar to those of the refer-
ence samples deposited from Ti–6Al–4V pre-alloyed 
powders by influencing the energy absorbance and 
consequently the melting and solidification modes 
[29]. The reflection behavior of laser beam’s trans-
mission differed in different treated powder layers, 
with more gaps found in powder layers composed of 
the arrangement of coarsen and fine aggregates [37]. 
Therefore, more laser beam can be captured resulting 
in higher laser absorption by milling mixture of the 
Cr/Nb with Cu.

Conclusion
In this paper, we verified the feasibility of building 
Cu–Cr–Nb alloys by L-DED with mechanical milling 
pure metal powder. A sesame ball structure with Cr 
powder and Nb powder inlaid into the surface of Cu 
powder was obtained via mechanical milling. This 
structure effectively reduced the laser reflectivity of 
powder and maintained a well fluidity, which laid a 
foundation for the success of laser additive manufac-
turing. With the increase in Cr and Nb addition, the 
laser absorptance obviously increased and the fluidity 
of the powder gradually decreased.
A multimodal distribution of precipitates was 
obtained in the L-DEDed Cu–Cr–Nb bulk samples. 
 C r2Nb is in micron-scale and its size increases as the 
Cr + Nb content increases. Almost all Nb existed in 
the form of Cr2Nb while Cr existed in both Cr2Nb 
and Cr phases due to the deviation of Cr:Nb = 2:1 
in L-DEDed samples. Nano-scaled Cr precipitates 
formed during the complex heat treatment cycle in 
the L-DED process. The tensile strength and hard -
ness of the L-DEDed bulk samples increased with the 
increase in Cr + Nb content, while the tensile elon-
gation showed inverse tendency. All samples were 
ductile failure and show typical dimples.
This study not only presents a cost-effective and 
versatile approach for the laser additive manufactur-
ing of a range of alloys within the Cu–Cr–Nb system 
but also contributes valuable insights into the com-
plex relationship between alloy composition and the 
resultant material properties.
