ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing (AM) enables fabrication of components with tailored shapes and properties. TA15 (Ti- 
6Al-2Zr-1Mo-1V), a near- α titanium alloy, is widely used in aerospace applications due to its exceptional high- 
temperature strength. Current research primarily focused on studying the tensile properties of TA15 alloy at 
500 
◦
C. To elucidate the performance at higher temperatures, this study systematically investigated the room 
temperature and high-temperature (500 – 700 
◦
C) mechanical behaviors of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) TA15 
alloy with different microstructure. Bulk samples were built via LPBF and heat treated at 600 
◦
C and 800 
◦
C. In- 
situ high-temperature X-ray diffraction revealed the decomposition of martensite at 700 
◦
C. Microstructural 
analysis showed that columnar prior- β grains in the as-built condition contained martensitic α ′ laths. Heat 
treatment at 800 
◦
C (HT800) induced the formation of the equilibrium α and β phases. Tensile testing at 25 
◦
C 
and 500 – 700 
◦
C showed a decreased in strength with increasing temperature. Grain boundary softening occurred 
at 600 
◦
C, causing the boundaries to deform and slip first, which led to the onset of yielding. As a result, the 
HT800 sample with coarser grains exhibited a higher yield strength. The samples prepared in this study main -
tained relatively high strength even at 600 
◦
C, exceeding the strength tested at 500 
◦
C in other works. This 
suggests that LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy can serve at higher temperature.
1. Introduction
Additive manufacturing (AM) is revolutionizing the field of materials 
science and engineering. In this layer-by-layer fabrication technique, 
objects are constructed by depositing materials in a predetermined 
pattern as opposed to removing material by machining. The additive 
approach grants extensive liberty in designing components with com -
plex geometries and internal features [ 1 ]. AM further empowers mate -
rials scientists to fabricate alloys and composites with superior or 
customized properties by manipulating composition and microstructure 
during processing [ 2 – 4 ].
TA15 is a near- α titanium alloy that exhibits excellent strength, fa -
tigue resistance, and corrosion resistance while retaining adequate 
ductility and fracture toughness. These properties render TA15 a tech -
nically superior and cost-effective solution for challenging applications 
in the aerospace, automotive, chemical, and biomedical sectors [ 5 , 6 ]. 
The excellent high-temperature performance and creep resistance of 
TA15 make it an ideal choice for aerospace applications [ 7 – 10 ].
Currently, numerous researchers have conducted extensive studies 
on the tensile properties and deformation behavior of the TA15 alloy 
fabricated by AM at 500 
◦
C. Cai et al. [ 11 ] found that the tensile strength 
of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) TA15 alloy at 500 
◦
C gradually 
decreased with increasing heat treatment temperature, while the elon -
gation exhibited the opposite trend. Wu et al. [ 12 ] reported that the 
LPBF TA15 alloy exhibited superior tensile strength at high tempera -
tures compared to conventional TA15, due to its ultrafine grain size and 
high density of nanoscale twins. The tensile strength at 500 
◦
C reached 
990 MPa, while the elongation did not decrease significantly. Liu et al. 
[ 13 ] evaluated the effect of microstructure on high-temperature 
(500 
◦

discontinuous grain boundary α phase exhibited higher strength (827 
MPa) and elongation (20.7 %). Zhang et al. [ 14 ] conducted in-situ 
high-temperature tensile experiments at 500

high-temperature deformation mechanism of the LPBF TA15 alloy. Long 
slip bands were observed at low tensile strain. As the tensile strain 
increased, strain concentration was observed in some α / β phase inter -
face regions, which may lead to the formation of microcracks. Rizwan 
et al. [ 15 ] studied the tensile deformation behavior of laser melting

4. Discussion
4.1. Mechanism of high temperature strength
It is a common phenomenon that the strength of titanium alloys 
progressively decreases with increasing tensile temperature [ 17 , 32 – 34 ]. 
This behavior arises from the thermal activation of atoms, which reduces 
the resistance to dislocation motion and thereby promotes deformation. 
During tensile deformation, two competing microstructural evolution

mechanisms are involved: on the one hand, dislocation multiplication 
leads to work hardening effect. With the continuous accumulation of 
plastic strain, the dislocation density gradually increases and further 
develops into a highly entangled cellular substructure. On the other 
hand, dynamic recovery (DRV) and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) 
lead to softening. The rate and extent of these softening processes are 
closely related to the tensile temperature and strain rate, as can be seen 
in Figs. 9 and 10 . At higher thermal deformation temperatures, the 
intensified atoms thermal motion within the material facilitates the 
activation of dislocation recombination, merging and annihilation, 
thereby promoting the effective reduction in dislocation density [ 37 ,
38 ]. The DRV process can be triggered after the onset of plastic defor -
mation, and the hardening effect caused by dislocation accumulation 
can be alleviated by eliminating some dislocation networks. As the 
tensile temperature and strain reaches the critical value, DRX occurs, 
forming a new grain structure with few or no defects, which significantly 
alters the mechanical properties of the material, transitioning it from a 
hardened state to a relatively softened state [ 38 , 39 ]. For samples 
tensile-tested at 500 – 550 
◦
C, the relatively low temperature inhibited 
the DRV process from effectively counteracting the stress concentration 
caused by work hardening. Consequently, the sample underwent 
necking and fracture rapidly after reaching the stress peak ( Fig. 6 a – c). In 
contrast, at higher tensile temperatures (600 – 700 
◦
C), the enhanced 
dislocation mobility facilitated the DRV process. Moreover, DRX 
occurred after the flow stress reached its peak, during which the rate of 
dislocation annihilation surpassed that of dislocation generation, lead -
ing to a continuous softening characteristic in the tensile curve.
Generally, the yield strength of polycrystalline materials at room

This phenomenon is known as the inverse Hall-Petch relationship. The 
transition is closely related to the competition between grain strength 
and grain boundary strength, as illustrated in the schematic in Fig. 11 . At 
lower temperatures, the grain boundary strength exceeds the grain 
strength, and tensile deformation predominantly proceeds through 
dislocation slip within the grains. As the temperature increases, both 
grain boundary strength and grain strength decrease. However, the 
strength of the grain boundary declines more significantly due to its 
higher defect content and elevated atomic energy [ 40 ]. Thus, a critical 
temperature exists at which the grain boundary strength equals the grain 
strength, known as the equi-cohesive temperature (T
E
). It can be 
concluded that the T

◦

600

4.2. Cooperative plasticity mechanisms
The reason for the increase in plasticity with temperature during the 
tensile process of LPBF TA15 alloy can be explained by the following 
three factors. I) With the increasing tensile temperature, more slip sys -
tems are activated due to thermal energy and the reduction in critical 
resolved shear stress, facilitating easier slip [ 14 , 41 ]. Meanwhile, DRV 
and DRX operate as the strain reaches the critical value, which promotes 
the absorption of continuously generated dislocations through the for -
mation of subgrains and grain boundaries [ 32 ]. II) For samples subjected 
to tensile testing at 600 – 700 
◦
C, the grain boundaries lose their original 
barrier function due to softening. In contrast, weakened grain bound -
aries can absorb more dislocations and enhance their transmission to 
adjacent grains, significantly reducing stress concentration. During the 
high temperature tensile, the strain consists of two parts: 
dislocation-mediated plastic deformation and GB-mediated plastic 
deformation. Although the contribution of GB-mediated deformation to 
the overall strain is limited, it can improve the ductility by helping the 
deformation within the grains [ 40 ]. III) In-situ XRD patterns ( Fig. 1 ) and 
BSE images ( Fig. 3 ) indicate that a pronounced α →β phase transition 
occurs at 700 
◦
C. Owing to the greater number of slip systems, the β

5. Conclusions
In this work, the in-situ phase transitions and microstructural evo -
lution, as well as the room-temperature and high-temperature me -
chanical properties (500 – 700 
◦
C) of LPBF-manufactured TA15 titanium 
alloy were systematically investigated. The main conclusions are as 
follows. 
(1) In-situ XRD analysis revealed that the lattice constants increase 
with increasing heating temperature. When the temperature 
reached 700 
◦
C, martensite began to decompose.
(2) The as-built microstructure was characterized by fine martensitic 
α ′ laths within columnar prior- β grains. Annealing at 600 
◦
C 
resulted in negligible microstructural changes, while annealing at 
800 
◦
C led to the formation of the equilibrium α and β phases.
(3) High-temperature tensile test results showed the strength pro -
gressively decreased with increasing temperature, while total 
elongation increased. The strength variation trend across 
different microstructural samples showed a distinct turning point 
at 600 
◦
C, which is associated with grain boundary softening. 
Compared to TA15 alloys fabricated by traditional methods, HIP, 
SPS, and EBSM, the samples in this study exhibited higher 
strength across most temperatures.
