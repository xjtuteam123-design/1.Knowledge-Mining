ABSTRACT 
Dual-phase microstructures arising out of eutectic reactions offer several advantages: ease of casting, composite 
properties and tunable characteristic length scale of the phases. Eutectic high entropy alloys (EHEA) with a 
multi-component solution phase and a hard intermetallic phase are candidate materials to identify alternate 
high-temperature materials. Alloying elements can be chosen to improve the resistance to lamellar microstruc -
ture degradation and coarsening. In this work, we present the CALPHAD guided design of a hypo-eutectic high 
entropy alloy CoCrFeNiTa
0.395 
with primary dendritic FCC phase and fine eutectic (FCC solution + Laves phase) 
microstructure possessing good high-temperature mechanical properties. The primary FCC phase fraction is 0.42 
± 0.02. The interlamellar spacing of the eutectic is 0.69 ± 0.12 μ m. The alloy exhibited a balanced yield strength 
of 1303 ± 18 MPa, a fracture strength of 2237 ± 23 MPa and a fracture strain of 0.3 under room temperature 
compression testing. The mechanism for change in the lamellar morphology during deformation at temperatures 
beyond 0.8 T
E 
is explained schematically. Strain field distribution obtained by FEM simulation correlates well 
with the observed microstructure gradients in the deformed samples. The formation of nano-scale precipitates in 
the low strain rate deformation is attributed to thermal effects. High-temperature precipitation of two types of 
precipitates (L1
2 
ordered and Ni
3
Ta type) in the FCC-solution phase extended the useable temperature range of 
this alloy. 
1. Introduction 
Dual-phase multi-component alloys are used in a wide range of in -
dustrial applications. Examples include alloys such as alpha-beta tita -
nium alloy [ 1 ], dual-phase steels [ 2 ], duplex stainless steels [ 3 ] and 
Ni-base superalloys [ 4 ]. These alloys are designed using a base 
element in which two or more elements are alloyed to obtain the 
required dual-phase microstructure and mechanical properties. The 
approach of alloying multiple principal elements and exploring the 
centre of the phase diagram through the advent of high entropy alloys 
has shown promise in exploring alloys that resist microstructure 
degradation [ 5 ]. Accelerated development of high entropy alloys is 
being pursued using a combination of approaches such as CALPHAD [ 6 ], 
ICME [ 7 , 8 ] and machine learning [ 9 ]. Unique features attributed to high 
entropy alloys (HEA) include high configurational entropy of mixing, 
sluggish diffusion and lattice distortion [ 10 , 11 ]. As in conventional al -
loys, single-phase microstructures in HEAs are modified to develop 
alloys with engineering significance [ 11 ]. Mechanical properties of 
these alloys are improved by cold working [ 12 ], precipitation hardening 
[ 13 ] and second phase dispersion [ 14 ]. The review on metastability in 
high entropy alloys [ 15 ] has highlighted the importance of composi -
tional and structural metastability along with extended solid solutions in 
achieving an exceptional combination of mechanical properties. The 
idea of eutectic high entropy alloys (EHEA) consisting of alternate FCC 
and B2 phases in AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 
with good mechanical properties over a 
range of temperatures was inspired by Lu et al. [ 16 ]. 
The unique microstructural features of eutectic alloys arising out of 
cooperative growth of the two phases lead to in-situ composite struc -
tures. Eutectic alloys avoid solidification defects such as hot cracks, 
porosity, shrinkage and macro-segregation [ 17 , 18 ]. The length scale of 
the microstructural features of eutectic alloys can be tuned by control -
ling the processing conditions [ 19 ]. Minor addition of alloying elements 
can lead to microstructure refinement, improvement in strength and 
fracture toughness. EHEAs in CoCrFeNiNb

Ultrafine-grained duplex microstructure with a lamellar arrangement

annealing. Development of EHEA components using hot-working can 
take advantage of an integrated approach combining experiments and 
computer simulations [ 32 ].

2. Experiment and simulation details 
2.1. Experimental details 
The CoCrFeNiTa
x 
eutectic high entropy alloys were made using 
vacuum arc melting of pre-weighed 99.99% pure metals. A non- 
consumable tungsten electrode and vacuum chamber backfilled with 
Ar gas was used for melting. The alloys were re-melted five times by 
flipping after each melt cycle to achieve chemical homogeneity. Cylin -
drical samples were then made from the as-melted button using the 
suction casting method. Samples with a diameter of 6 mm and a height 
of 9 mm were sectioned from suction cast rods using wire EDM. Nano -
indentation tests were performed using Hysitron TI ® premier nano- 
indenter on a well-polished sample surface. Trapezoidal load function 
and a peak load of 5000 μ N was used to measure nano-hardness and 
reduced elastic modulus. The hot deformation of samples was performed 
using a thermomechanical simulator, Gleeble 3800 ® . The samples were 
heated to test temperature by resistance heating. A K-type thermocouple 
was welded to the sample working in a closed feedback loop to control 
the temperature and the heating rate. The isothermal hot deformation 
conditions used were 1073 K, 1173 K, 1273 K and 1373 K temperature at 
constant strain rates 0.001, 0.1 and 1 s
 1
. The samples were soaked for 5 
min at the test temperature to attain temperature homogeneity. Ni paste 
and graphite sheets were used at the sample-anvil interface to increase 
the electrical conductivity and lubrication. The heating rate used for 
deformation was 5 K/min. The heat treatment was carried out for the 
suction cast sample at a temperature of 1173 K for 15 min, 2 h and 4 h to 
verify the precipitation formation. The microstructures were studied in 
backscattered electron imaging mode using FEI Quanta 400 ® field 
emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) attached with Bruker ® 
EDS setup. Standard metallography techniques were used for all SEM 
samples. Xpert Panalytical ® X-ray diffraction (XRD) facility with Cu-K
α 
radiation was used for phase identification. The samples were sectioned 
parallel to the compression axis. Microstructure analysis and micro -
hardness measurements were performed at the centre of the sample. The 
TEM samples were prepared using standard polishing techniques. The 
final electron transparent section was made by Gatan ® PIPS argon ion 
milling. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using 
FEI ® Tecnai T20 and FEI ® Tecnai G
2 
30ST. The compositional ‘Z- 
contrast ’ images of the fine-scale phase distribution were obtained with 
electron beam scanning capability in the scanning transmission electron 
microscopy (STEM) mode of TEM operation and the attached High- 
Angle Annular Dark-Field (HAADF) electron detector. The elemental 
distribution was characterized using ‘spectrum-profile ’ and ‘drift-cor -
rected spectrum imaging ’ capabilities in STEM mode of TEM operation 
and the EDS composition analyzer. For these two STEM-EDS analytical 
techniques, an electron probe size of ~5 nm was used for collecting x-ray 
signals pixel by pixel during scan by maintaining a resolution of 10 nm 
between pixels and a short dwell-time of 4 s at every pixel to minimize 
the effect of any specimen drift. The quantitative area maps of elemental 
distribution in the fine microstructure of interest were generated with 
TEM Imaging and Analysis ® (TIA) software available with the TEM. 
2.2. Simulation details 
The phase fractions as a function of temperature and Scheil ’ s solid -
ification predictions for CoCrFeNiTa
0.395 
alloys were obtained using the 
TCHEA2.1 database available with Thermo-Calc ® software. The hot 
deformation of the CoCrFeNiTa
0.395 
alloy was simulated using FEM 
(Simufact ® ) software using the “ upsetting forming ” module. Strain rate 
control was ensured by controlling stroke velocity and time for total

materials database. The material physical constants were calculated 
using a “ rule of mixture ” approach. Microstructure gradients in the 
sample could be correlated with the predictions of the FEM simulation.

The secondary electron images of the fractured surface shown in 
Fig. 3 (a) indicate cleavage fracture characteristic of brittle materials. 
BSE micrograph of the fractured surface at high magnification ( Fig. 3 (b))

micro-cracks in the eutectic region. Formation of shear deformation

4. Discussion 
The rationale for the choice of alloying elements is as follows: Fe, Co, 
Cr and Ni (VEC:8, 9, 6 and 10 respectively) in equimolar composition 
stabilize FCC solid solution. Ta shows binary eutectic reaction with Fe, 
Co, Ni and Cr and favours the formation of Laves phase due to atomic 
size difference. High eutectic temperature ensures thermodynamically 
stable demixing of these two phases even at elevated temperatures. 
In the designed alloy, it is observed that at room temperature, the 
deformation of ductile dendritic phase through dislocation motion re -
sults in the formation of slip bands that are constrained within the 
dendrites. The shear bands formed in the adjacent fine eutectic also 
accommodate strain at the later stages of deformation. This observation 
is consistent with the deformation behaviour of eutectic composites 
reported in the literature. An in-situ composite microstructure with 
bimodal microstructure features is beneficial [ 40 ]. This work shows that 
alloys with such lamellar microstructural features incorporating a 
hard-intermetallic like Laves phase could retain their strength even at 
elevated temperatures. 
The high temperature (1073 – 1373 K) strength of this alloy is high 
compared to other EHEAs and single-phase HEAs [ 21 , 41 ]. Samples 
isothermally deformed at 1073 K and 1173 K do not show coarsening or 
loss of lamellar morphology inherited from cast structure and therefore 
explained the high strength retained at these temperatures. The soft -
ening or decrease in strength at higher temperatures ( > 1273 K) is due to 
the dynamic microstructural changes occurring in the sample. The 
microstructure shows coarsening and formation of the degenerated 
structure of Laves phase in the eutectic region. As the deformation 
proceeds at high temperatures, the formation of degenerated structures 
proceeds further. At this stage, the microstructure in the eutectic region 
cannot be described by FCC lamellae. Also, for samples deformed at high 
temperatures (1273 K and 1373 K) it is observed that recrystallization in 
the primary phase and the eutectic FCC takes place as seen in Fig. 6 (c) 
and (d), where dislocation free grains could be observed. 
The significance of soft - hard phase interface and lamellar micro -
structure for strengthening is also evident from other reports. Gao et al. 
[ 42 ] explained the high strength and ductility observed in AlCoCr -
FeNi
2.1 
EHEA based on back stress resulting from the interface between 
two phases, restricting the motion of dislocations. The ductile FCC phase 
undergoes deformation and strain hardening while the hard B2 phase 
strengthens the alloy. Thus, the lamellae interfaces provide Hall-Petch 
type strengthening to the eutectic alloys. Change in the morphology of 
lamellae during deformation and resultant softening is also reported for 
Ti – 6Al – 4V [ 43 , 44 ] and BCC + Laves in-situ composites [ 29 ]. Thus, loss 
of continuous lamellae interfaces in the microstructure results in soft -
ening at a higher temperature. 
The following mechanism may explain the formation of degenerated 
structures and the change in lamellar morphology ( Fig. 10 ). During the 
initial stage, the dislocation motion in the dendritic FCC phase allows 
plastic deformation. Accumulation of dislocations and formation of 
dislocation forest at the dendrite phase boundary results in strain 
hardening. At later stages, the eutectic region undergoes plastic strain 
borne by deformation in the eutectic FCC, whereas the Laves phase 
lamellae undergo fracture. Locally the eutectic colonies undergo 
bending/kinking ( Fig. 6 (a)). The dislocations accumulated in the FCC 
lamellae near the kinked region indicate deformation-induced bending 
of FCC lamella. Unlike the eutectic FCC, Laves phase lamellae in the 
kinked region appear to be broken. The change in morphology is further 
promoted by high-temperature conditions present during high- 
temperature compression. 
Application of strain results in the reduction of the length of laves 
phase lamellae due to fracture. The Laves phase alone may not have 
undergone significant plastic deformation or bending, but together with 
the FCC phase, which contributes to the ductility, it provides good 
strength. It must be noted that the strength-ductility combination and 
the failure mode of alloys in the studied alloy system are affected by

Cooperative growth of eutectic alloys during the solidification of this 
alloy allows a characteristic length scale of the hard and soft phases. In 
the cooperative growth regime, any perturbation of wavelength formed 
in lamellar microstructure in eutectic alloys does not grow. Therefore, 
such structures are stable against perturbations and show significant 
microstructural stability compared to other morphologies [ 45 ]. Martin 
et al. [ 45 ] discussed that the change in lamellar morphology can be 
explained by the loss of low surface energy orientation relationship, 
interface grooving due to polygonization and fracture of one of the two 
phases. The change in morphology of lamellar phases due to thermal 
effects is commonly attributed to mechanisms like fault migration, edge 
spheroidization, boundary splitting etc. In the present case, lamellar 
morphology change appears to be Laves phase lamellae fragmentation 
or fracture-related phenomena. Therefore, the thermal effects are likely 
to play a secondary role rather than the dominant driving force for 
lamellae morphology change within the short span of an isothermal 
compression test.

cantly high temperature ( > 0.8 T
E
). This study highlights that lamellar 
microstructural morphology is stable at reasonably high temperatures, 
even during simultaneous plastic deformation. The role of thermal ef -
fects justified the formation of nano-scale precipitates during high- 
temperature heat treatment. EHEAs consisting of an intermetallic 
phase and a multi principal element ductile solid solution are possible 
candidates for high-temperature applications. 
5. Conclusions 
The eutectic high entropy alloy with optimum properties was 
designed with CALPHAD guided experiments. This alloy shows superior 
high-temperature compressive properties compared to the EHEAs and 
single-phase alloys in the literature. The deformation mechanism was 
explained based on the deformed sample characterization. The high- 
temperature sample (1373 K) is characterized by the formation of 
recrystallized grains and coarsening of eutectic colonies. The change in 
lamellar morphology during high-temperature deformation at temper -
ature > 0.8 T
E 
was explained by kinking and bending in the eutectic 
region. The formation of fine-scale precipitates at 1173 K was observed 
and verified by heat treatment studies. The thick precipitates are of an 
L1
2 
ordered structure, and the thin precipitates are of Ni
3
Ta type. The 
designed alloy with nano-scale precipitation could exhibit stable 
lamellar microstructures and good strength over an extended range of 
elevated temperatures. The combination of spatial constraint by the 
hard phase of the fine lamellar microstructure and nano-scale precipi -
tation within the ductile high entropy solution-phase could be a means 
to design dual-phase alloys capable of high-temperature application.
