ABSTRACT 
A new constitutive creep model for Ni-base superalloys has been developed that links the instantaneous creep 
response to incremental microstructure changes occurring during creep. The structure starts with cuboidal γ ′ - 
particles embedded in the γ -phase, and transitions to a rafted structure. In the present Kocks-Mecking type of 
model, the dislocation density ρ is considered as a state variable that evolves during creep. The increase of ρ 
depends on the γ -channel width in the γ / γ ′ -microstructure. Recovery, i.e., the reduction of ρ , is modelled from 
thermally activated, mechanically assisted climb. The strain rate is also modelled from the very same recovery 
process. The model successfully describes creep curves in a range of temperatures and load stresses with a 
maximum of five physical parameters. Once the model has been calibrated, it can be used to predict creep curves 
for other temperatures, stresses, and microstructures if the channel width evolution is known. The model is even 
capable of making reasonable predictions when fitted only to a single experiment. Extrapolations to different 
alloys with moderately varying compositions are possible. 
1. Introduction 
There is a steady driving force for an increase of temperatures in gas 
turbines, in order to achieve high thermal efficiencies. Turbine blades 
are critical high temperature components which are made of single 
crystal superalloys [ 1 , 2 ]. They operate at temperatures close to the 
melting point of the Ni-base superalloys, where they must resist me -
chanical loading caused by high centrifugal forces. Creep, the time 
dependent plastic deformation of materials, is one central element of the 
overall load spectrum. In the present work we consider high temperature 
and low stress creep of superalloy single crystals (SXs). After processing, 
SXs exhibit a microstructure where γ ′ -cubes (ordered L1

cube edge length: ≈ 0.4 μ m, volume fraction: ≈ 70 %) are separated by 
thin γ -channels (crystal structure: f.c.c., average channel width: ≈ 0.1 
μ m, volume fraction: ≈ 30 %). It is well known that the microstructures 
of SXs evolve during creep. The physical nature of elementary defor -
mation mechanisms governing creep depend on the stress temperature 
regime. At low temperatures and high stresses planar faults play a 
central role [ 3 – 5 ]. In contrast, at high temperatures and low stresses 
individual dislocation reactions are more important and rafting, the 
well-known directional coarsening of the γ ′ -cuboids is a characteristic 
feature [ 6 – 11 ]. In Ni-base SX the lattices of the γ ′ - and γ -regions are 
coherent, but the lattice constants of the phases differ. During < 100 >
tensile testing, a negative misfit between the two phases is at the origin 
of the formation of rafts perpendicular to the direction of the applied 
stress. Rafting in the high temperature low stress creep regime leads to 
an increase of γ -channel widths w , a central microstructural parameter 
which is easily accessible in experiments. Other elements of the micro -
structure, like the dislocation densities in both phases, the build up of 
dislocation networks which enwrap the γ ′ -particles, the different types of 
planar faults which were reported in the literature are more difficult to 
quantify.

To understand this interplay between microstructure changes and 
creep, simulations have been published that define and use a complex 
γ / γ ′ -microstructure (e.g. Refs. [ 12 – 14 ]), or even simulate the micro -
structure evolution along with the occurring creep, for instance by using 
phase field simulations (e.g. Refs. [ 15 – 18 ]). Beside numerous extensive 
experimental works, these simulations have helped to identify the onset 
of the microstructure transition [ 17 ], and they were able to reproduce 
creep curves [ 16 , 18 ]. However, the latter models use empiric power 
laws for the creep rate dependence on stress, and they consider only a 
single temperature and load stress. Hence these simulations, beside 
being laborious and slow, have not been validated in a range of stresses 
and temperatures, so their prediction power is unknown. 
In the present study, a constitutive creep model is proposed which 
allows to rationalize the evolution of the creep rate ˙ε . It accounts for the 
effects of temperature T and stress σ . However, unlike in simple 
empirical approaches, it singles out a central recovery related, thermally 
activated process which accounts for a critical activation energy in an 
Arrhenius ansatz. Our approach allows to directly integrate the stress 
dependence as a mechanical aid to thermal activation, which eliminates 
the need to use an empirical power law. Most importantly, our model 
combines a dislocation-based view of high temperature plasticity with 
the increase of γ -channel width associated with rafting. The creep model 
is kept as simple as possible and hence, cannot account for some details 
known to be relevant for high temperature creep. Also, the transition 
towards normal plasticity at high stresses (dominated by slip regardless 
of temperature) is not covered by the present model. However, its cur -
rent success suggests that it can serve as a framework for future exten -
sions by additional mechanisms, like the shearing of γ ′ -particles (e.g. 
Ref. [ 4 ]). At present we show that our model can successfully rationalize 
an experimental creep data set, measured for an SX of type ERBO1 [ 19 ] 
subjected to uniaxial tensile creep at stresses ranging from 270 to 330 
MPa and temperatures between 920 and 980

3. Creep model 
Since their first introduction by Kocks and Mecking [ 22 , 23 ], statis -
tical work hardening and recovery models have found considerable in -
terest in materials science and engineering to predict the flow curves of 
metals and alloys. The original model was based on one state variable of 
the material, namely the dislocation density ρ , which varies in response 
to plastic deformation and at least in part, controls the yield stress 
needed for continued deformation. In these models, the variations of ρ 
are expressed in terms of increments (dislocation generation) and dec -
rements (recovery) occurring with increasing strain. Numerous re -
finements have been introduced to extend and improve the original 
model (e.g. Refs. [ 12 , 24 – 36 ]). Among them are the distinction between 
different dislocation classes ([ 12 , 25 , 27 , 29 , 31 – 33 ]), the introduction of 
other state variables ([ 30 , 34 , 36 ]), or the distinction between static and 
dynamic recovery ([ 35 ]). One common goal of all these models is to 
break down the most important aspects of plastic deformation in order 
to identify the basic underlying principles. Another goal is to derive a 
method to predict the flow behavior of materials with minimum effort in 
respect of computation time and computer memory. This allows to apply 
them embedded in larger scale models, for instance in the crystal plas -
ticity finite element method (CP-FEM, e.g. Ref. [ 37 ]) or in texture 
models like the grain interaction model GIA (see e.g. Ref. [ 38 ]), the 
self-consistent visco-plasticity (SCVP) model (e.g. Ref. [ 39 ]) or the 
Alamel model (e.g. Ref. [ 40 ]). On the other hand, the more refined 
models add more complexity, and hence more fitting parameters to the 
model.

6. Discussion 
The aim of the present approach was to develop a physics-based 
creep curve model that is as simple as possible, but that can still cover 
a range of temperatures and load stresses. Across larger ranges, it is to be 
expected that the underlying mechanisms can vary. However, as the 
model shows, a single recovery mechanism is sufficient to describe the 
creep curves throughout the stages I, II, and III in the range around T =
950 ± 30
◦
C , σ = 300 ± 30 MPa . In the present model, the assumed 
mechanism is recovery (dislocation annihilation, Eq. (11) ) after ther -
mally activated, mechanically assisted/impeded dislocation climb (Eq. 
(7) ). A specialty of the present model is that the strain rate itself is 
derived from the very same mechanism: a certain glide burst is assumed 
to occur after each dislocation dipole annihilation, which lead to Eq. 
(13) . The generation of dislocations depends on the geometrical re -
strictions for dislocation glide, which leads to Eqs. (4) and (5) . An 
important part of these restrictions is given by the spatial distribution of 
the γ and γ ′ phase. At the temperatures and stresses given, dislocations 
can only move in the γ -phase [ 20 ]. The corresponding geometrical re -
strictions can be expressed by a single parameter, the channel width w , 
which is measured between the cuboidal γ ′ -particles at first, and be -
tween the rafts later on. This microstructure parameter can be taken 
from microstructure simulations (e.g. Refs. [ 17 , 18 , 20 ]), or it can be 
measured if it is directly visible, i.e., if it lies in the plane of a 2D 
micrograph. But even in general, w as a hidden 3-D feature can be 
derived from 2D experimental micrographs using artificial intelligence 
methods [ 47 ]. Fortunately for the present model development, w and its 
evolution with strain is known [ 20 ], and it has been used as-is (Eq. (1) ) 
with parameters directly fitted from SEM images. With this, the model

7. Summary and conclusions 
In the present work a creep model for single crystal Ni-base super -
alloys was developed which rationalizes entire creep curves based on 
elementary processes which govern high temperature plasticity and 
microstructural evolution. The time dependent element of the model is 
related to recovery, the diffusion-controlled climb of dislocations in the 
presence of internal and external stresses. At present, a central necessary 
input of the model is the absolute width of the γ -channels between 
γ ′ -particles or rafts. This parameter retains its physical meaning 
throughout rafting and coarsening of the γ ′ phase, but its value varies 
strongly. The channel width is geometrically linked to the γ ′ volume 
fraction and the particle sizes, which in turn depend on the alloy 
composition and other phases, if present. So far, the model makes no 
assumptions about the causes for the microstructure evolution as it oc -
curs, but it uses the measured channel width instead. At present, the 
latter characterizes the microstructural evolution of single crystal su -
peralloys like ERBO/1 (CMSX4 type) under conditions of high temper -
ature and low stress creep. For these conditions, the model has been 
validated for this alloy based on a set of [001] tensile creep data 
performed around T = 950 
◦
C and σ = 300 MPa. Additional experiments 
are desirable to further develop and/or validate the model for other 
alloys and conditions. For now, despite its simplicity, the model has 
some remarkable features, which can be summarized as follows: 
(1) Entire creep curves are described well in the high temperature 
and low stress creep regime, with a maximum of only 5 fitting 
parameters, which all have a physical meaning. 
(2) The apparent activation energies of creep, as interpreted by the 
present model, are in reasonable agreement with the apparent 
activation energies of tracer diffusion of Ni in the γ -phase, as 
listed in the data base of ThermoCalc. 
(3) From fitting a single creep curve measured at one temperature 
and one stress, the model can predict creep curves in adjacent 
temperature and stress ranges. There is no need to use a specific 
activation energy as an input of the model. 
(4) In the present model, the coarsening of the microstructure is 
represented by the increase of the γ -channel widths as measured 
experimentally. In future work, this input can also be provided by 
phase field calculations. This would allow to derive creep curves 
from simulated microstructures. Good experimental or simulated 
input data are required to draw reliable conclusions regarding the 
roles of microstructure variations and alloy chemistry in creep. 
(5) The model can be refined by considering a larger range of input 
data which reflect the effects of larger temperature and stress 
intervals, alloy chemistry, microstructure, and mechanical 
anisotropy.
