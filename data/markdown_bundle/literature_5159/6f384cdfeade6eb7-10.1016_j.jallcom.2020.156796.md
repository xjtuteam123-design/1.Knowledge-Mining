abstract
An ultra- ﬁne grained (UFG) FeNiCoCr multi-principal element alloy (MPEA) with uniformly distributed
nanoscale Cr-rich precipitates was fabricated by mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering
(SPS). Compression tests show that the yield strength, compressive strength and failure strain of this as-
fabricated FeNiCoCr alloy are 1525 MPa, 1987 MPa and 24.4%, respectively. The irradiation response of
this alloy was studied following Fe
2þ ion irradiation with a peak dose of 58 dpa at temperatures of 300
and 500 /C14 C. Our study indicates that the current FeNiCoCr MPEA exhibits modest radiation induced grain
boundary (GB) segregation and possesses higher resistance to defects coarsening and void formation
compared to conventional Fe eNieCr stainless steels.

1. Introduction
MPEAs, alternatively termed high-entropy alloys (HEAs), consist
of multiple principal elements with concentrations between 5 and
35 at.%. MPEAs have recently attracted attention as structural ma-
terials due to their superior strength, ductility, wear resistance, and
corrosion resistance among other properties [ 1,2]. Recent studies
[3,4] have shown that MPEAs could also possess excellent irradia-
tion tolerance, making them potential candidates for high-
temperature ﬁssion or fusion structural applications. Kumar et al.
[4] showed that the newly developed FeNiMnCr MPEA exhibited
remarkably less irradiation degradation compared to conventional
austenitic FeNiMnCr alloy at mid-range doses (3 e10 dpa) up to
700
/C14 C, e.g. lower density of irradiation induced dislocation loops
and less segregation at GBs.
MPEAs may be produced by advanced fabrication techniques to
improve their performance through microstructural engineering.
The combination of MA and SPS is an innovative route to produce
UFG or even nanocrystalline bulk MPEAs [5,6]. Wu et al. [6] showed
that MPEAs fabricated by MA and SPS exhibited greatly improved
strength due to GB strengthening when compared to their coarse
grained counterparts, meanwhile maintaining good ductility. In
addition, the introduction of abundant GBs through MA and SPS is
expected to enhance the irradiation tolerance with GBs serving as
sinks or recombination centers for irradiation-induced defects
[7e9]. However, it has been reported that ﬁne grained materials
could be at risks of irradiation induced grain coarsening due to
thermal spike and increased defect concentration [ 8,10]. In the
present study, a FeNiCoCr MPEA was fabricated by MA and SPS to
obtain an UFG microstructure. The aim of this paper is to investi-
gate the strength of this UFG MPEA and study its ion irradiation
response from several aspects, including irradiation induced de-
fects, void and GB segregation, as well as grain thermal stability
under irradiation.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure
Observation was made on the non-irradiated region of the
300 /C14 C irradiated sample. TKD orientation map, Fig. 2a, shows that
the microstructure of FeNiCoCr consists of equiaxed grains with
dominantly high-angle GBs (HAGBs). Twin boundaries were also
observed, indicating recrystallization could have occurred during
SPS, which has also been observed during SPS in a similar
CoCrFeNiMn HEA [ 15]. The average grain size was estimated to be
~400 nm. No texture was found by the examination of the corre-
sponding pole ﬁgures, indicating that FeNiCoCr fabricated by MA
and SPS has a random orientation distribution. In addition, micro-
scale regions rich in Cr, which were un-indexed in the TKD orien-
tation map, were revealed by EDS mapping, Fig. 2b. Those regions,
with a total area fraction of 0.07, are believed to be the un-alloyed
Cr material. Scanning TEM (STEM) image and the corresponding
EDS maps, Fig. 3 , reveal Cr-rich secondary precipitates of globular
shape. Those secondary precipitates show a total area fraction of
0.04 and an average size of 60 nm, uniformly distributed in the
matrix. Similar nanoscale Cr-rich particles in long-range ordered
structure have been observed in cast FeCrCoMnNi HEA following
thermal processing at 500 e700 /C14 C[ 9]. It is believed that those Cr-
rich particles shown in Fig. 3 are precipitates formed during the
cooling stage of SPS. EDS line-scans under STEM indicate that the Cr
concentration in those precipitates varies from 27% to 35%. Since
some amount of the Cr was not alloyed into the matrix and some
Cr-rich particles precipitated out from the matrix, the measured Cr
concentration in the matrix of 13% was lower than the nominal Cr
concentration of the MPEA of 25%.

Defects In order to detect the irradiation induced defects in
FeNiCoCr, grains near the highest damage depth (i.e. ~1 mm from
the surface) were tilted close to <C110> zone axis and observed in
weak-beam dark ﬁeld (WBDF) mode. Fine dislocation loops were
detected in the irradiated regions, as revealed by the white dots in
the corresponding WBDF TEM micrographs, Fig. 6 . The loop sizes
were determined by the longest axis of the dots (marked by arrows
in Fig. 6 ). Only dots larger than 1.5 nm were counted. The sample
thickness was assumed to be 150 nm for the loop volumetric
density calculation.
The loop average size was 3.5 ± 1.2 nm for 300 /C14 C and
3.9 ± 1.5 nm for 500 /C14 C irradiated samples, respectively; the loop
number density was 2 :7 /C2 1022 m/C0 3 for 300 /C14 C and 3 :0/C2 1022m/C0 3
for 500 /C14 C irradiated samples, respectively. In addition, samples did
not show an evident difference in network dislocation density
between the irradiated and un-irradiated regions.
Voids Bright ﬁeld TEM images were taken in under-focus con-
ditions to examine the voids in both 300 and 500 /C14 C irradiated
FeNiCoCr. Normally, the voids are prominent in the middle damage
region (200 e300 nm away from the highest damage region), as
well as the near-surface regions due to the rapid exit of interstitials
to the surface sinks [ 8]. Therefore, these regions were examined,
and the under-focused TEM images from the middle damage re-
gions are shown in Fig. 7 . No irradiation induced voids were
detected in both irradiated samples. Note that the white features in
the under focus images are unlikely to be irradiation induced voids,
as these features were also detected in the regions beyond irradi-
ation damage depth. These features could be a result of defocusing
of secondary precipitates and unalloyed Cr or localized interparticle
regions that did not undergo complete welding during the MA
process. It is well known that MA involves welding, and unwelded
local areas can potentially remain in the microstructure [ 18].
Radiation induced segregation (RIS) EDS line-scans were carried
out across random GBs close to the highest irradiation damage
regions in the FeNiCoCr samples. The scanning step size was typi-
cally 1 e2 nm. Sharp GBs that tend to be close to the edge-on
condition were chosen. Two factors need to be considered for
detecting the RIS in the MA & SPS sample. First, composition may
vary from grain to grain, e.g. up to 5% difference in Fe concentration
between two neighboring grains were detected. Second, there is a
high number density of Cr enriched precipitates uniformly
distributed in the sample. Both factors can introduce inaccuracy in
determining the RIS. Therefore, RIS was only measured between
grains without composition variation and precipitates were avoi-
ded during measurements. For the 500 /C14 C irradiated sample, six out
of eight valid measurements showed RIS. Enrichment of Ni and Co
and depletion of Fe and Cr were detected in the 500 /C14 C irradiated
sample, as shown in Fig. 8 where representative concentration
proﬁles for each element are displayed; in contrast, no evident
elemental enrichment or depletion was detected in the 300 /C14 C
irradiated sample. As mentioned above, the measured Cr concen-
tration of 13% in the matrix, is lower than the nominal Cr concen-
tration. The estimated concentration change from GB to grain
interiors for Ni, Co, Fe, Cr after peak 58 dpa irradiation at 500
/C14 C
were 3.8%, 2.9%, /C0 3.6% and /C0 3.1%, respectively. As the micro-
structure is dominated by high-angle boundaries (HABs), the
measurements were mainly carried out on HABs, which tend to
exhibit greater solute segregation than low-angle boundaries
(LABs) [ 19]. However, it is still possible that some measurements
were taken from LABs across which RIS was too low to be detected,
and this could be the reason why RIS was not detected across every
GB in the 500 /C14 C irradiated sample.
4. Discussion
There are two unique aspects of the current FeNiCoCr MPEA
compared to conventional Fe eCreNi austenitic steel, i.e. composi-
tional complexity and UFG structure. The effects of these two fac-
tors on the irradiation response are brie ﬂy discussed as follows.
It is proposed that in MPEAs each atom has different bond
conﬁguration and lattice potential energy (LPE), and that a high
number of principal elements lead to large LPE ﬂuctuations be-
tween the lattice sites. Atoms are more dif ﬁcult to diffuse to new
positions where the LPE is higher. Therefore, low LPE sites in MPEAs
serving as atomic traps can hinder diffusion [ 20,21]. It is reported
that the normalized activation energy for diffusion Q =Tm
(Tm being the melting point) for each element in CoCrFeMnNi
MPEA is indeed higher than the value for the corresponding pure
metal [ 20]. The sluggish diffusion is expected to affect the

surface without encountering vacancies during irradiation, leaving

MPEAs, interstitial clusters and vacancies are more likely to travel
in short-range localized 3D manner, increasing the probability of
vacancy-interstitial recombination. These effects can suppress the
void formation and swelling in the MPEAs.

The irradiation response of the FeNiCoCr MPEA fabricated by
MA and SPS processes is compared to that of conventional
FeeCreNi steel. Our measured loop size and number density in
FeNiCoCr after 300 /C14 C irradiation are on the same order of magni-
tude as those reported in 300 e400 /C14 C irradiated conventional
FeeCreNi alloy [ 25]. Increasing the irradiation temperature from
300 to 500 /C14 C did not result in evident changes in loop density and
size in the current FeNiCoCr alloy, but can lead to prominent in-
crease in size and decrease in number density in conventional
FeeCreNi alloy. For example, T. Okita. et al. [ 26] reported that the
dislocation loop density decreased by ten times in an austenitic
alloy when temperature increased from 300 to 500 /C14 C with a dose
of ~1 7 dpa. T.M. Williams et al. [ 27] reported that loop size
increased from 3 to 10 nm in stainless steel as temperature
increased from 300 to 500 /C14 C with a dose of 1 dpa. It is believed that
the sluggish diffusion is the reason for the suppressed growth of
dislocation loops in the current FeNiCoCr MPEA. Meanwhile, in
conventional FeeCreNi steel, void swelling occurred when irradi-
ation dose is > 10 dap at 300 e700 /C14 C, which compromises their
application as fuel cladding material in advanced reactors [ 26,28].
The current results indicate that FeNiCoCr alloy possesses high
resistance to void formation at 300 e500 /C14 C. It is believed that the
unique interstitial motion due to the compositional complexity and
the abundance of GBs increase the probability of vacancy-
interstitial re-combination, and thus prevent void swelling. We
recognize here that the presence of Co in the MPEA composition is
unfavored for nuclear applications from the stand-point of long-
term activation. However, we believe that our study in principle
demonstrates that compositionally complex MPEAs, especially the
UFG ones, have the potential to provide enhanced tolerance to ra-
diation damage due to the fundamental mechanisms described
above, which would hold true should a replacement for Co can be
identiﬁed in the MPEA composition.
RIS has been explained by inverse Kirkendall mechanism in both
conventional Fe eCreNi austenitic steel and FeNiCr-containing
MPEAs [ 29,30]. Brie ﬂy, during irradiation, vacancy diffusion to-
wards GB occurs, and in exchange, an equal number of atoms
diffuse in the opposite direction, i.e., away from GBs. Irradiation
studies on the Fe e20Cre24Ni austenitic steel have shown enrich-
ment in Ni and depletion of Fe and Cr in the 300 e600
/C14 C temper-
ature range for doses from 0.1 to 3 dpa [ 31]. It is known that point
defect recombination at low temperatures and back diffusion at
high temperatures can reduce the RIS. The peak GB segregation for
the Fe eCreNi alloy (Ni concentration 1.6 times higher at GBs)
occurred at intermediate temperatures ~500 /C14 C. Another study
showed that in FeNiMnCr MPEA, the peak segregation temperature
was shifted to a higher temperature and RIS was mitigated at GBs at
peak segregation temperature after above 10 dpa [ 4].
M. Vaiday et al. [ 32] reported the diffusivity of the major ele-
ments in CoCrFeNi MPEA by using the radiotracer method. It was
found that the diffusivities follow the order DNi < DCo < DFe <DCr .
Generally, Fe and Cr tend to deplete while Ni and Co tend to
segregate along the GBs during irradiation. Our results indeed show
the enrichment in Ni and Co and depletion in Fe and Cr across GBs
in FeCoNiCr alloy after irradiation. Our study is in agreement with
previous studies on RIS in FeNiCr based MPEAs in regards to the
segregation/depletion behavior of the elemental constituents
[4,30].
The degree of RIS is signi ﬁcantly affected by the alloy compo-
sition. C.M. Barr et al. reported [ 30] high level of RIS in CoCrFeNiMn
MPEA after a dose of 2 e3 dpa at 500 /C14 C, e.g. up to 7% Ni and Co
enrichment and 14% Mn depletion. In comparison, the current
FeCoNiCr alloy showed a less degree of RIS. It is known that Mn has
a signiﬁcantly lower melting point than the other constituents, and
lower energy is needed to create Mn atom-vacancy pairs. Therefore,
a higher number of Mn atoms would diffuse away from GBs,
resulting in a higher level of RIS in CoCrFeNiMn. In light of this,
increasing the number of components does not guarantee a
decrease in RIS. The type of the elements and relative diffusivities
seem to play more important roles in the RIS behavior.
Irradiation can be conducted with either a raster or defocused
beam. Raster beam can achieve irradiation over a large area and
multiple samples; however, it is inherently pulsed in nature, with
irradiation taking place on a given area for only a fraction of the
time. Defocused beam, although limited to smaller area, allows for
continuous irradiation. The irradiation mode can affect the micro-
structure evolution during irradiation as demonstrated by studies
in recent years [ 33,34]. Jonathan et at [ 34]. Reported that raster
beam, compared to defocused beam, resulted in lower swelling
levels in pure iron at 450
/C14 C, and the swelling levels decreased at
lower frequency. Getto et al. [ 33] reported that in the same
100 appm helium pre-implanted ferritic-martensitic steel, the
resultant void swelling and dislocation density were higher after
defocused beam irradiation than after rastered beam irradiation.
The authors attributed the lower irradiation damage by rastered
beam to in-cascade annealing of defects between pulses, which
decreases vacancy supersaturation. The statistics of the irradiation
induced defects are 1.5 e2 factors lower in the rastered-beam
irradiated sample than in the defocused-beam irradiated sample
when the raster frequency, i.e. 1016 Hz in vertical direction and
255 Hz in horizontal direction, was in the middle range. It was
proposed that increasing the frequency to above 500 Hz can
eliminate the effect of in-cascade annealing in the materials stud-
ied. Note that in the current irradiation experiment, rastering of the
beam was conducted with a vertical and horizontal frequency of 64
and 517 Hz, respectively. Therefore, some degree of in-cascade
annealing was expected during irradiation. The effect of irradia-
tion method on the irradiation response of MPEAs requires further
investigation.
5. Conclusions
The FeNiCoCr MPEA fabricated by MA and SPS method produced
UFG microstructure with uniformly distributed nanoscale Cr-rich
precipitates. The yield strength, compressive strength and failure
strain of the FeNiCoCr alloy is 1525 MPa, 1987 MPa and 24.4%,
respectively. Fe þ2 ion irradiation study indicated that this alloy
exhibits modest radiation induced segregation and possesses good
resistance to defects coarsening and void formation, which can be
attributed to compositional complexity and abundance of GBs in
the alloy. Overall, the FeNiCoCr MPEA fabricated exhibits better
radiation resistance compared to conventional Fe eCreNi stainless
steels.
