ABSTRACT
The concept of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) introduces a promising approach to designing cryogenic alloys. 
However, assessment of their mechanical behavior is mostly limited to liquid nitrogen temperature. Additionally, 
tailoring discontinuous plastic flow (DPF) at near 0 K is essential for preventing inhomogeneous localized 
deformation and premature failure. Here, we show that precipitation strengthening via coherent nano -
precipitates introduces a synergetic effect in improving mechanical properties and governing DPF at liquid he -
lium temperature (4.2 K). A direct-aged Cr

synergy (2186 MPa – 16.2 %) at 4.2 K, with remarkably low stress-drop amplitude, despite its considerably high 
strength. Micro-plasticity mechanisms, facilitated by nanoprecipitates and their short-range interaction with 
dislocations, improve the strain-hardening rate and reduce the size of dislocation avalanches, leading to insig -
nificant stress drop. These findings demonstrate that employing coherent precipitates can be a promising design 
strategy for cryogenic alloys, even in extreme environments close to absolute zero.
1. Introduction
Various microstructural design strategies have been explored to 
develop structural materials with high strength and toughness for ser -
vice under cryogenic conditions. Recently, driven by the demands of 
carbon neutrality, liquid hydrogen at 20 K, offering a significantly 
higher volumetric capacity compared to high-pressure gaseous 
hydrogen, has attracted considerable attention. In parallel, the growth 
of frontier industries such as space exploration and superconducting 
technologies has further emphasized the need for metallic materials 
capable of reliable load-bearing performance under extremely low 
temperatures.
Among potential candidates for ultra-low temperature applications, 
austenitic stainless steels with face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal struc -
ture have been widely considered due to their high strength and ductility 
[ 1 – 6 ]. However, austenitic stainless steels exhibit limited FCC stability 
at cryogenic temperatures, undergoing deformation-induced martens -
itic transformation to α ʹ phase, which potentially causes a brittle fracture 
at extremely low temperatures. Moreover, they show pronounced 
discontinuous plastic flow (DPF) associated with drastic and significant 
stress drops and localized deformation [ 7 – 9 ], significantly increasing 
the risk of catastrophic failure. Kim et al. [ 10 ] demonstrate that large 
stress drops and localized deformation bands in SUS316L trigger a 
brittle-fracture mode. DPF at extremely low temperatures, also referred 
to as low-temperature serrated deformation, has been observed in 
various alloys [ 8 , 11 – 13 ], and its structural implications have motivated 
extensive research into the underlying mechanisms [ 8 – 10 ].
Meanwhile, the cryogenic mechanical properties of FCC-structured 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs), which are known for their excellent per -
formance at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K) [ 14 – 21 ], have also been 
investigated at extremely low temperatures, including the character -
ization of DPF [ 22 – 26 ]. While HEAs typically exhibit smaller stress

The improvement of tensile strength and uniform elongation is 
attributed to its variation in strain-hardening capability according to the 
deformation temperature. Fig. 4 b shows the strain-hardening rate curve 
of the DHEA at 4.2 K and 293 K. Both display a three-stage strain- 
hardening behavior, which is frequently observed in the precipitation- 
strengthened HEAs with low stacking fault energy (SFE) [ 19 , 21 ]. In 
the early plastic deformation regime (stage A), strain-hardening rates 
significantly decrease due to dynamic recovery and early dislocation 
rearrangement. Subsequently, a considerable increase in 
strain-hardening rates is observed during stage B, crucial for sustaining 
the strain-hardening capability. In the final stage C, the strain-hardening 
rates progressively decrease until reaching the plastic instability con -
dition. It is noted that the strain-hardening rate at 4.2 K maintains a 
consistently higher value throughout the entire plastic deformation, 
leading to increased tensile strength and ductility compared to that at 
293 K.

3.3. Deformation mechanisms
To understand the microstructural origins of the remarkable me -
chanical response of our DHEA, TEM and scanning TEM (STEM) were 
used to unravel the deformation structures of the tensile specimen 
deformed to fracture. Fig 7 a,b reveals that plastic strain is accommo -
dated with planar characteristics on multiple {111} slip planes at both 
293 K ( Fig. 7 a) and 4.2 K ( Fig. 7 b). The enlarged STEM images ( Fig. 7 c, 
d) reveal a complex deformation substructure at both temperatures, 
characterized by a high density of dislocations and sharp planar features 
as indicated by the red arrows. The DF image ( Fig. 7 e) also shows a high 
density of sharp planar features within the complex deformation sub -
structure at 4.2 K, which are identified as stacking faults (SFs), as 
confirmed in Fig. 8 . Notably, dislocation pairs, i.e., two perfect dislo -
cations as those marked by a white arrow, indicate the shearing of L1
2 
precipitates during plastic deformation, as they are energetically 
favorable to compensate APB in L1
2 
precipitates [ 46 ]. Such dislocation 
pairs induced by L1
2 
precipitates were also observed in grains with 
relatively low dislocation density (Fig. S5). At 4.2 K ( Fig. 7 d), the high 
density of dislocations makes the identification of individual dislocation 
pairs challenging. Nevertheless, the substantial difference in the 
required stress levels for precipitate shearing (632 MPa) and dislocation 
bowing (1680 MPa) suggests that the prevailing mechanism of 
precipitation strengthening is through particle shearing.
Fig. 8 presents high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) analysis of deformed 
DHEA. At 293 K ( Fig. 8 a), abundant SF intersections are observed, 
leading to the formation of various types of sessile stair-rod dislocations

3.4. DPF mechanism
The study of the deformation mechanisms at 4.2 K provides insights 
into the DPF behavior of our DHEA. Two representative models, the 
thermomechanical instability and the dislocation-based mechanical 
instability, have been suggested to elucidate the underlying mechanism 
of DPF [ 7 , 8 ]. The thermomechanical instability model, originally pro -
posed by Basinski [ 61 ], attributes DPF to quasi-adiabatic heating 
accompanied by localized softening and deformation. Near absolute 
zero, metals experience a drastic reduction in both heat capacity and 
thermal conductivity. Accordingly, the deformation-induced heat 
cannot dissipate efficiently, leading to localized temperature spikes that 
facilitate avalanche-like DPF. However, detectable localized 
temperature spikes during deformation at extremely low temperatures 
follow stress drop signals [ 25 ], implying that the thermomechanical 
instability alone cannot fully account for the origin of DPF.
In contrast, the dislocation-based mechanical instability model de -
scribes that the origin of DPF is associated with dislocation avalanche 
events [ 26 , 60 , 62 ]. At extremely low temperatures, as discussed in Sec -
tion 3.3 , cross-slip is substantially suppressed due to the limited mobility 
of jogged screw dislocations compared to edge dislocations, resulting in 
dislocation pile-up at sessile barriers. Consequently, below the critical 
temperature, where cross-slip rarely happens, plastic deformation oc -
curs through the avalanche slip of piled-up dislocations by breaking 
down the sessile barriers. These dislocation avalanches are character -
ized by a sharp drop in flow stress, followed by recovery to a critical 
stress level needed to initiate the next avalanche, thereby manifesting as 
DPF during plastic deformation.
Based on the pioneering work of Seeger [ 60 ], which proposed that 
edge dislocations are dominant at extremely low temperatures, several 
key studies have supported the mechanical instability mechanism. In 
austenitic stainless steels, Obst et al. [ 7 ] reported that DPF is absent 
when martensitic transformation, which involves screw dislocation ac -
tivity, is active, but appears when such transformation is suppressed, 
thereby supporting the essential role of edge dislocations in DPF. 
Furthermore, in the CrMnFeCoNi alloy, which does not undergo 
martensitic transformation during plastic deformation under ultra-low 
temperatures [ 22 ], Tirunilai et al. [ 26 , 63 ] demonstrated that the 
mobility of screw dislocations and ability to cross-slip fundamentally 
governs dislocation avalanche behavior and thus DPF behavior.
The barriers responsible for the dislocation avalanche were mainly 
considered to be sessile dislocations, including stair-rod dislocations 
such as LC locks [ 7 , 8 ]. As the plastic deformation progresses, the density 
of sessile barriers increases, and avalanche slip dominates plastic 
deformation rather than individual cross-slip because the mobility of 
screw dislocation decreases with increasing dislocation density [ 26 ]. In 
our DHEA, therefore, the high density of stair-rod dislocations arising
