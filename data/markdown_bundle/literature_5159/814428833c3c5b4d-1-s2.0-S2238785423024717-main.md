abstract
The common problem of low tensile yield strength (TYS) prevails in high ductility dilute-
alloying Mg-RE alloys prepared by traditional hot extrusion. The design strategy of heter-
ostructures in the microstructure of Mg alloys can effectively improve the strength-
ductility synergy in mechanical properties, which is expected to break the trade-off
dilemma between strength and ductility. In this work, we successfully prepared a series
of high ductility as-extruded Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr alloys with all elongations (ELs) greater than
26.0% at room temperature. By controlling the extrusion process and thus introducing
heterogeneous ﬁberous structure, we ﬁnally obtained a superior Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr
(wt.%) alloy with strength-ductility synergy, which exhibits a TYS of 246 MPa, ultimate
tensile strength (UTS) of 274 MPa and an EL of 29.0%. The microstructure examination for
the alloy with the heterostructure reveals that the structure consists of alternating ﬁla-
mentous deformed-grain and ﬁne-grain layers. The overall ﬁne grains in the microstruc-
ture, a large amount of nanoprecipitates and the relatively high density of residual
dislocations contribute to the high TYS of the alloy. The alloy maintaining high ductility is
attributed to the activation of multiple slip systems, especially the active and mobile
<cþa> dislocations, the inhibition of the P-type dislocation to B-type dislocation transition,
{10
1 2} tensile twinning generated early during tensile testing, and full intergranular slip
transfers caused by high geometric compatibility. The present work can further promote
the development of dilute-alloying Mg-RE alloys with high strength-ductility synergy.

1. Introduction
In the face of growing environmental problems and the energy
crisis, the development of lightweight materials is considered
a good choice [ 1,2]. As the lightest structural metal, Mg with a
density of ~1.74 g/cm
3 and its derivatives have been applied in
a wide range of applications with the intrinsic advantages of
abundance in Earth, recyclability and biocompatibility,
including the transportation industry, 3C electronics and
biomedical ﬁeld [ 1e4]. Pure Mg with a hexagonal close-packed
(hcp) crystal structure has low ductility and poor absolute
strength at room temperature and is usually rarely used
directly as a structural material [ 1,5,6]. Therefore, it is mostly
used in the form of Mg alloys. Nevertheless, the further
development of Mg alloys still faces considerable challenges
due to the prominent problem regarding the strength-ductility
trade-off dilemma [7,8]. For this difﬁculty, considerable efforts
have been expended for the development of Mg alloys with
strength-ductility synergy, including heterogeneous micro-
structural designs [ 2,7e10], grain reﬁnement techniques
[11e13], twin boundary pinning [ 14], introducing long-period
stacking-ordered phases [ 15,16], engineering high-density
low angle boundaries [ 17], boundary engineering and segre-
gation strategies [ 18,19], etc. Among these strategies, the
heterogeneous microstructure exhibits a greater potential in
balancing or breaking out the dilemma of the strength-
ductility trade-off. Its microstructure usually consists of both
ultraﬁne/ﬁne grains in the recrystallization zone and coarse
grains in the unrecrystallized zone, which can be easily ach-
ieved through controlled recrystallization in a simple hot
extrusion process [ 7,8]. Meanwhile, the difference between
soft domains and hard domains in microstructure with het-
erogeneous structure leads to generate noticeable strain gra-
dients during elastic ‒plastic deformation, resulting in the
generation of back stress in soft domains [ 7]. With further
deformation, the accumulation of the strain gradient requires
extra strain hardening to be accommodated, leading to the
generation of heter-deformation induced (HDI) stress
strengthening to simultaneously improve strength and
ductility [ 2,7]. In addition, compared with the homogeneous
microstructure, the coarse grains in the heterogeneous
microstructure can store more dislocations and conduct slip
transfer with adjacent ﬁne grains, thus releasing local strain
concentrations and activating multiple slip systems during
deformation, which leads to an enhancement in work-
hardening capacity and thus facilitates strength-ductility
synergy [ 7e9]. As a typical example, Fu et al. [ 8] reported
that the as-extruded ZK60 alloy with heterogeneous structure
exhibits a higher strength-ductility synergy with a tensile
yield strength (TYS) of ~270 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) of ~340 MPa and an elongation (EL) of ~23.5% than the
homogeneous structure of the ZK60 alloy with a TYS of
~100 MPa, a UTS of ~290 MPa and an EL of ~21%.
Based on the previously reported cross-slip energy barrier
theory [ 1,5], corresponding molecular dynamics simulations
show that high-ductility Mg alloys with multiple components
have low solute concentrations, which has been extensively
veriﬁed experimentally in some Mg-Zn-X-X’ alloys [ 20e22].
This implies that for composition-speciﬁc multicomponent
high-ductility Mg alloys, their strength is usually low and
difﬁcult to strengthen by heat treatment due to their low
alloying composition. Therefore, the introduction of the het-
erogeneous microstructure might allow these low-alloying Mg
alloys to maintain strength-ductility synergy.
In this work, based on the Mg-Zn-Gd/Zr alloy systems
predicted by the cross-slip energy barrier theory and some
existing experimental veriﬁcation results of Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr al-
loys [ 1,5,23,24], we designed a Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr (wt.%)
alloy with a lower alloying level. By controlling the extrusion
process and thus introducing the heterogeneous structure in
the microstructure of the alloy, we ﬁnally succeeded in
achieving high strength-ductility synergy in the alloy. There-
fore, the microstructure of the as-extruded alloys was char-
acterized systematically and the mechanism related to
strength-ductility synergy was explored in detail. The pre-
sent work can further promote the development of dilute-
alloying Mg-RE alloys with high strength-ductility synergy.
2. Experimental procedures
The Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr (actual chemical composition: Mg-
1.35Gd-0.53Zn-0.53Zr, wt.%) alloy ingot with dimensions of
100 mm in diameter and 1500 mm in length was prepared
from commercial pure Mg (99.99 wt%) and Zn (99.95 wt%), Mg-
30 wt% Gd and Mg-30 wt% Zr master alloys by semicontinuous
casting technology under argon protection. Prior to the
extrusion process, cylindrical billets with a size of F90
mm /C2 150 mm were machined from the cast ingot and then
homogenized at 510
/C14 C for 20 h followed by air cooling. These
billets were preheated at each given extrusion temperature
(ET, 360, 390, 420 and 450
/C14 C) for 2 h and forward extruded with
four extrusion processes at a ram speed of 0.5 mm/s and
extrusion ratio (ER) of 25:1 at all ETs and two extrusion pro-
cesses at the same ram speed and ER of 9:1 at 360 and 390
/C14 C.
The ﬁnished extrudates were cooled in air. For simplicity, the
as-extruded alloys were denoted as T360-R9, T360-R25, T390-
R9, T390-R25, T420-R25 and T450-R25.
The microstructure and mechanical properties were stud-
ied on the samples cut from the center of the extrusion bars.
Microstructural observations were analyzed using optical
microscopy (OM, Leica), scanning electron microscopy (SEM,
TESACN MIRA 3) equipped with energy dispersive and back-
scattered electron spectrometer (EDS and BSE) and electron
backscattered diffraction (EBSD, JEOL JSM-7900F). According to
the predicted grain size, the step size of the EBSD scan was set
as 0.5, 1 and 2 mm. All EBSD data were analyzed using the HKL-
Channel 5 software. 2 e15

3. Results
3.1. Mechanical properties after extrusion
Fig. 1 shows the tensile and compression stress ‒strain curves
of the as-extruded Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr alloy alloys. Table 1
summarizes the mechanical properties of these alloys. As

tensile mechanical properties of all remaining alloys satisfy

excellent strength-ductility synergy. On the other hand,
except for the alloys with an ER of 9, the CYS/TYS of the
remaining alloys are all greater than 0.88, exhibiting a low
yield asymmetry. The T360-R9 alloy has the lowest EL of 26.0%
and the highest yield asymmetry with a CYS/TYS ratio of 0.72.
Keeping the ER constant at 9 and increasing the ET to 390
/C14 C,
the strength of the alloy decreases while the EL slightly in-
creases. The UTS, TYS and EL of the T390-R9 alloy are 237 MPa,
175 MPa and 27.1%, respectively. However, keeping the ET
constant at 360
/C14 C and increasing the ER to 25, both the
strength and EL of the alloy increase. The T360-R25 alloy has
the highest UTS of 246 MPa, the highest TYS of 274 MPa and an
EL of 29.0%. Continuing to keep the ER at 25, as the ET in-
creases from 360
/C14 C to 450 /C14 C, the TYS gradually decreases to
128 MPa for the T450-R25 alloy. Meanwhile, the EL ﬁrst in-
creases to the highest value of 36.4% for the T420-R25 alloy
and then decreases to 32.6% for the T450-R25 alloy; the CYS/
TYS increases to the highest value of 0.94 for the T360-R25
alloy and then gradually decreases to 0.88 for the T450-R25
alloy.
Based on the premise that the mechanical properties all
satisfy UTS /C21 230 MPa, TYS /C21 150 MPa and EL /C21 25%, Fig. 2
compares the strength and EL of some Mg eGd series alloy
bars air-cooled via a single hot extrusion process, including
the present alloys with ER ¼ 25, binary Mg-7Gd alloy [ 25],
ternary Mg-6Gd-0.6Zr [ 26], Mg-1.5Gd-1Zn [ 27] and Mg-2Gd-
2Mn alloys [ 28], multiple Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr [ 23,24] and Mg-Gd-Y-
Zr-Nd/Zn alloys [ 9,21]. As shown in Fig. 2 , the RE content of
the studied alloy is signiﬁcantly lower than or equal to that of
these reported alloys. However, the strength-ductility synergy
of the studied alloys is comparable or even better than that of
these reported alloys, especially the T360-R25 and T390-R25
alloys. In addition, compared with the reported Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr
alloys [ 23,24] with the same elements, this performance and
cost advantage is even more visible. Although the UTS of the
previously reported Mg-2Gd-2Zn-0.5Zr alloy [ 24] has the
highest UTS among all compared alloys, the T360-R25 alloy
still has a comparable TYS and higher EL when the Gd and Zn
contents are reduced, implying that the composition of the
same series of alloys is optimized. This demonstrates that the
cost of the alloy in the study can be effectively reduced and the
competitiveness of industrial applications improved.

3.3. Microstructural analysis after extrusion
Fig. 5 shows OM images of the extruded alloys taken from the
mid-position of the longitudinal section parallel to the extru-
sion direction (ED). Compared with the microstructure of the
as-homogenized alloy, it is clear that the grain size of the as-
extruded alloys is greatly reﬁned after extrusion, indicating
that dynamic recrystallization (DRX) occurred during these
extrusion processes. However, not all extruded alloys exhibit a
uniform microstructure. The microstructure of the T360-R9
and T390-R9 alloys is composed of coarse elongated un-DRX
grains and ﬁne DRX grains ( Fig. 5 a and b), which belong to a
heterogeneous lamellar structure [ 2,7]. The microstructure of
the T360-R25 alloy shows alternating ﬁlamentous deformed-
grains and recrystallized ﬁne-grains ( Fig. 5 d), i.e. a heteroge-
neous ﬁberous structure, similar to the report by Fu et al. [8]. In
the T390-R25 alloy ( Fig. 5 e), the structure appears to decay.
The microstructure of these alloys shows a special heteroge-
neous structure. Although increasing the ET or the ER can
obtain a homogenous microstructure, it also leads to an in-
crease in the grain size of the alloy after extrusion, as the case
of the T420-R25 and T450-R25 alloys ( Fig. 5c and f).
To investigate the inﬂuence of extrusion on the second
phases, Fig. 6 shows SEM images and EDS results for the T360-
R9, T360-R25 and T450-R25 alloys. According to Fig. 6, there are
two levels of size of the second phases. These second phases
appear in the form of strings after extrusion, which are
aligned along the ED in these alloys. As the ET or the ER in-
creases, these second phases increase slightly in volume and

for points A, B and C, it can be inferred that these second
phases are W, Zr-rich and Zn
2Zr3 phases, respectively. Obvi-
ously, the types of these second phases do not change with

4. Discussion
In this work, the studied Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr alloy shows
high ductility after extrusion, and the as-extruded T360-R25
alloy exhibits high strength-ductility synergy with a hetero-
geneous ﬁberous structure in the microstructure. The mech-
anism strength-ductility synergy is discussed in detail below.
4.1. Plasticity mechanism
4.1.1. Active and mobile <cþa> dislocation
The activation and mobility of stable non-basal <cþa> dislo-
cations are important for the ductility of Mg alloys [ 1,5,35,36],
which can accommodate strain along the c-axis deformation.
In the present study, according to the cross-slip energy barrier
theory [ 1], the addition of Gd, Zn and Zr in appropriate con-
centrations is identiﬁed as important for facilitating the for-
mation of pyramidal <cþa> dislocations. Obviously, these
stable P-type <cþa> dislocations can be found in the T360-R25
alloy with the heterogeneous structured microstructure, which
are present in both DRXed and deformed grains ( Fig. 11 ).

Meanwhile, it can be reasonably inferred that P-type <cþa>
dislocations are also present in DRX grains in other alloys with
higher recrystallization fractions. However, their number and
density should be low, consistent with the alloys with high
recrystallization fractions possessing low KAM values in Fig. 8.
According to the vertical relationship between the basal plane
and the g ¼ 0002 direction under the two-beam condition, the
P-type dislocations can be considered as full <cþa> disloca-
tions due to the dislocation segments deviating from the basal
plane are located on the pyramidal plane [ 35,37]. In Mg alloys,
some previous reports [ 1,38,39] indicate that the full <cþa>
dislocations are inherently unstable and prone to transform or
dissociate to the sessile type as the prime source of poor room
temperature ductility. However, some reports [36,37]a l s op o i n t
out that <cþa> dislocations stably exist and glide on the py-
ramidal plane in pure Mg single crystals and polycrystalline Mg
bulk alloys via molecular dynamics simulations and TEM
corroboration observations, which is favorable for ductility.
Obviously, there is a high probability that these P-type <cþa>
dislocations in the present study have been activated and can
be considered as mobile <cþa> dislocations without or sup-
pressing the transformation from glissile dislocations to sessile
dislocations, which is an important factor for the as-extruded
Mg-Gd-Zn-Zr alloys with high ductility.
Moreover, there are also some B-type <cþa> dislocations in
the deformed grains of the T360-25 alloy ( Fig. 11 e and f).
Compared to mobile P-type dislocations, the mobility of B-type
dislocations may be lower or even sessile [40e43], which would
become barriers to new dislocations in subsequent straining.
Due to the obstacle function, the newly formed P-type dislo-
cations are thus easily transformed into B-type dislocations
during tensile deformation. Therefore, the higher density of B-
type dislocations is not favorable for the ductility. According to
Fig. 8a

are greater than that of T360-R25, which means a higher
dislocation density. Under this circumstance, the newly
generated P-type dislocations during tension have a higher
possibility of transforming into B-type dislocations due to the
higher moving resistance. Consequently, the lower EL values of
26.0% and 27.1% are acquired in the T360-R9 and T390-R9 al-

dislocation density in other alloys shows a monotonic down-

TEM images of Fig. 12, it is clear that some newly activated P-
type dislocations are observed in the T390-R25 alloy after
stretching to 2% strain at room temperature, resulting in a
higher EL in the remaining alloys (EL > 32%). This also implies
that ﬁlamentous deformed-grains with low dislocation density
are more favorable for ductility than lamellar deformed-grains
containing a high density of dislocations, which facilitates the
reactivation of P-type dislocations and inhibits the transition
from P-type dislocations to B-type dislocations. This is consis-
tent with the result that the as-extruded alloys with hetero-
geneous lamellar structure (T360-R9 and T390-R9) have higher
ductility compared to the as-extruded alloys with heteroge-
neous ﬁber structure (T360-R25 and T390-R25). In addition,
Fig. 8 shows that there are virtually no twins in the misorien-
tation maps of the as-extruded alloy alloys. However, Fig. 12
also shows the presence of some newly activated twins in the
alloy stretched to 2% strain, implying that the twinning defor-
mation should also be considered.

4.1.3. High geometric compatibility factor
Except for the T420-R25 sample with the RE texture, the IPFs of
the remaining samples show the basal ﬁber texture ( Fig. 9 ).
Therefore, the basal slip is more suppressed in the remaining
samples, due to the fact that the crystallographic c-axis of
most grains is perpendicular to the stretching direction. In the
T360-R25 alloy, these mobile <cþa> dislocations and {10
12 }
tensile twinning accommodate the c-axis deformation. As the
deformation proceeds, prismatic slip is also activated due to
its high Schmid factor (~0.41, Fig. 13 ), i.e., multi-slip system
activity. Therefore, except for the effect of the Schmid factor
on slip systems, the strain compatibility at GBs is considered
necessary for the multi-slips. This is attributed to dislocation
pile-ups at GBs generating local high stress [ 49]. It is well
known that GBs are typical locations where high local stress is
generated. Therefore, the intergranular strain compatibility
could be fulﬁlled by full slip transfer due to a large number of
<cþa> and <a> dislocations ( Fig. 11 ). The slip transfer
behavior can be studied using the geometric compatibility
factor m’, which is calculated as follows [ 8,49]:

5. Conclusions
In this work, we successfully prepared a Mg-1.5Gd-0.5Zn-0.5Zr
alloy with high ductility at room temperature via hot extru-
sion. On this basis, by further controlling the microstructure
of the alloy via the extrusion process, we also ﬁnally obtained
an as-extruded alloy with superior strength-ductility synergy.
The mechanisms of high ductility and strength-ductility
synergy have been investigated in detail. The conclusions
are as follows:
(1) After extrusion, both homogeneous and heterogeneous
structured alloys exhibit high ductility with the EL
greater than 26.0% at room temperature. Among them,
the homogeneous structured 420-R25 alloy with the RE
texture exhibits the highest EL of 36.4%, but a lower TYS
of 157 MPa and UTS of 230 MPa.
(2) Compared with the homogeneous structured T420-R25
and T450-R25 alloys, the T360-R25 alloy with the het-
erogeneous ﬁberous structure exhibits superior
strength-ductility synergy with the highest TYS of
246 MPa, UTS of 274 MPa and a higher EL of 29.0%. The
reason for the alloy to still maintain a high EL is mainly
attributed to the activation of multiple slip systems,
especially the active and mobile <cþa> dislocations, the
inhibition of the P-type dislocation to B-type dislocation
transition, full intergranular slip transfers caused by a
high geometric compatibility factor (~0.76) and the
generation of {10
1 2} tensile twinning during early
tensile testing.
(3) In the T360-R25 alloy, the high TYS can be achieved
mainly due to the overall heterogenous ﬁne-grained
structure, many nanoscale precipitates and a high
density of dislocations. The GB, Orowan and dislocation
