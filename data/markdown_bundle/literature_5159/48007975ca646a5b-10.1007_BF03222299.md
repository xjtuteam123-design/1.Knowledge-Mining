Overview 
Vanadium Alloys for Fusion 
Reactor Applications 
R.F. Mattas, B.A. Loomis, and D.L. Smith 
Fusion reactors will produce a severe op­
erating environment for structural materi­
als. The material should have good mechani­
cal strength and ductility to high tempera­
ture, be corrosion resistant to the local 
environment, have attractive thermophysical 
properties to accommodate high heat loads, 
and be resistant to neutron damage. Vana­
dium alloys are being developed for such 
applications, and they exhibit desirable 
properties in many areas. Recent progress in 
vanadium alloy development indicates good 
strength and ductility to 700°C, minimal 
degradation by neutron irradiation, and re­
duced radioactivity compared with other 
candidate alloy systems. 
INTRODUCTION 
The development of fusion reactors as 
a means for producing electricity is a 
long-term energy option for the United 
States. Fusion offers an essentially un­
limited fuel supply and a reduced po­
tential hazard to the environment. Most 
of the development effort to date has 
focused on magnetic confinement of the 
fusion plasma, and the leading magnetic 
confinement concept is the tokamak. The 
largest tokamak experiments in the 
United States are the Tokamak Fusion 
Test Reactor facility at Princeton Uni­
versity and the D-IIID facility at General 
Atomic in San Diego, California. Most 
reactor design development has empha­
sized the deuterium and tritium nuclear

The high-energy helium particle gives

that surrounds the plasma. The neutron 
serves a dual purpose for the fusion 
system. First, the energy deposited by 
the neutron heats the blanket and shield, 
and the heat is then removed by the 
coolant. The heated coolant is circulated 
and used to generate electricity in the 
normal fashion. Second, the neutron is 
used to generate more tritium fuel by a 
nuclear reaction with a lithium-bearing 
material. The production of tritium is 
necessary since it has a relatively short 
half-life and little exists in nature. 
An example of a fusion reactor design 
is shown in Figure 1 and on the cover of 
this issue.2 This is the proposed concep­
tual design of the International Thermo­
nuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) that 
is being developed jointly by the United 
States, the European Community/Japan, 
and Russia. The major radius of the reac­
tor (distance from the center to the 
midpoint of the plasma) is about 6 m, 
and the overall diameter is approxi­
mately 25 m. The plasma is confined by 
large D-shaped superconducting mag­
nets that surround the torus. The blanket 
and shield system that captures the fu­
sion neutrons is 1 m to 1.5 m thick and 
located inside the magnets. The reactor 
is expected to produce approximately 1 
GW of fusion power. 
Vanadium alloys are being investi­
gated as the structural material for the 
blanket and shield system. An example 
of the design of the blanket and shield is 
shown in Figure 2,3 The major constitu­
ents of the blanket are the structural 
material, a tritium breeding material, 
and coolant. The example shown in Fig­
ure 2 uses liquid lithium as both the 
breeder and the coolant. The other major 
concept for the blanket employs a lithium 
ceramic material as the breeder with 
either water or helium as the coolant. In 
most cases, beryllium is also added to 
the solid breeder blanket as a neutron 
multiplier in order to enhance the tri­
tium breeding rate.3

plasma is the first wall, and this is the 
area that experiences the harshest envi­

PROPERTY REQUIREMENTS 
The general property requirements 
for the fusion structure are substantial. 
The material should have good mechan­
ical strength and ductility to high tem­
perature, be corrosion resistant to the 
local environment, have attractive ther­
mophysical properties (e.g., low ther­
mal expansion coefficient and low elas­
tic modulus) to accommodate high heat 
loads, and be resistant to neutron dam­
age. In addition, the material should not 
become highly activated during opera­
tion. Reduced-activation materials en­
hance the attractiveness of fusion sys­
tems by reducing the safety risks as­
sociated with activation product release 
during a possible accident, reducing the 
concerns associated with waste disposal, 
and enhancing the potential for recycling 
following storage. 
Vanadium alloys offer significant ad­
vantages over iron-based alloys in sev­
eral areas. They exhibit good mechanical 
strength to above 700°C, good fatigue 
properties, good corrosion resistance to 
liquid lithium, and recent results indi­
cate good resistance to radiation damage. 
They also exhibit reduced activation 
compared with iron-based alloys.

applications were developed originally 
as part of the fast-breeder reactor pro­
gram during the 1960s.4 References 5-7

An important factor for fusion ap­
plications is the ductile-to-brittle transi­
tion temperature (DBTT). As a rule, it is 
believed that the DBTT should remain 
below room temperature during the

body-centered cubic metals, like vana­

centered cubic (fcc) metals. A number of 
factors, including interstitial content and 
radiation damage, can cause an increase 
in the DBTT. The DBTT of vanadium 
alloys is also sensitive to the concentra­
tion of alloy constituents, as shown in 
Figure 5.9 The DBTT as measured by

metals, like copper or austenitic stainless

the desired lifetime of a fusion alloy is 
100-200 dpa, such swelling rates are 
clearly unacceptable. Past fusion design 
studies have set the maximum allow­
able swelling at only 5-10%,3 meaning 
that the average swelling rate should be 
in the range of 0.02-0.1 % / dpa. Body­
centered cubic metals generally exhibit 
reduced swelling rates compared with 
fcc metals. In addition, alloying can re­
sult in an extended incubation period 
where no swelling is observed and in a 
reduced peak swelling rate once swell­
ing is initiated .

ACTIVATION 
One of the potentially attractive fea­
tures of fusion reactors is reduced levels 
of radioactivity and reduced concerns 
about radiation waste compared to fis­
sion reactor systems. In order to achieve 
the potential of reduced activation, fusion 
reactors need to utilize materials that 
exhibit reduced activation and that ulti­
mately could be recycled into fusion re­
actor manufacture. The vanadium al­
loys being considered offer this poten ­
tial. Figure 7 compares the activation 
levels of a candidate fusion alloy with 
several "reduced activation" iron-based 
alloysP The upper curve shows the ab­
solute level of radioactivity, in Curies/ 
em, and the other curves show the rela­
tive values of the other alloys compared 
with the vanadium alloy. For times less 
than one day after shutdown, the vana­
dium alloy has an activation level that is 
one-half to one-fifth that of the iron­
based alloys. For times from one year to 
100 years, the vanadium alloy has an 
activation level that is 0.1 to 0.001 that of 
the iron-based alloys. It is estimated that 
in the 10-100 year time frame the vana­
dium alloys could be recycled back into 
new fusion reactor fabrication, thus re­
ducing long-term storage requirements.

FABRICATION 
Developmentoffabrication techniques 
for vanadium alloys is ongoing. Recently 
the tensile properties of V-(0-20)Ti and 
V -(O-lS)Cr-STi alloys after butt joining 
by tungsten-inert gas (TIC) welding were 
determined at 2S°C.14 Tensile tests were 
conducted on both annealed and cold­
worked materials with a TIC weld zone. 
The TIC weld zones of these alloys had 
tensile properties comparable to those of 
recrystallized alloys without the weld 
zone. Least affected by the welding were 
the tensile properties of the V -STi and V­
STi-SCr alloys. Although the tensile 
properties of the V-STi and V-STi-SCr 
TIC welded alloys are acceptable, the 
weld properties can be improved by fur­
ther optimization for minimum grain 
size in the heat-affected zone. 
FUTURE WORK 
Development of vanadium alloys has 
resulted in a potentially attractive 
structural material for fusion reactor 
applications. Presently, the alloy of 
greatest interest is V-STi-SCr. To fully 
qualify vanadium alloys for fusion 
structural applications, additional work 
is needed on fabrication and on under­
standing the effects of the fusion neu­
tron spectrum on properties. However, 
the work to date provides promise that a
