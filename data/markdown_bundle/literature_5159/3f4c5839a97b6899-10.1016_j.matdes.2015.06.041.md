abstract
In the present study, new quaternary MgY 1.65Zn0.74Al0.53 and MgY 3.72Zn1.96Al0.45 alloys (wt.%) were syn-
thesized employing the Disintegrated Melt Deposition (DMD) casting technique followed by hot extru-
sion. Microstructural characterization revealed the presence of 14H long-period stacking ordered
structure (LPSO) and Mg
4Y2ZnAl3 phases aligned along the direction of extrusion in both alloys.
Reﬁned grains ( 65 lm) due to the effect of dynamic recrystallization (DRX) were also observed to
co-exist with larger worked grains ( P20 lm) in the extruded microstructures. Compared to monolithic
Mg, signiﬁcant increase in the microhardness ( /C2467–88%), tensile yield strength ( /C24245–290%) and
ultimate tensile strength ( /C24113–144%) were observed in the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys. Despite the signiﬁcant
increase in strength of materials, failure strains of both Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys were comparable to mono-
lithic Mg. Ignition temperatures of both Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys were found to outperform commercially
available AZ31, AZ80 and WE43 (high-temperature) Mg alloys, and the highest ignition temperature of
770 /C176C was achieved in the MgY
3.72Zn1.96Al0.45 alloy.

1. Introduction
Automakers of today are rapidly turning their attention toward
the use of light-weight and high-performance materials in vehicle
designs. The key stimulus to the phenomenon can attributed to the
recent legislation and strong environmental pressures placed on
the automotive industry to produce vehicles with better fuel econ-
omy and lower CO 2 emissions [1–3].
In recent years, Mg–rare-earth (RE) alloys have attracted much
attention due to their excellent mechanical strength and
high-temperature properties [4,5]. With the advent of such
high-temperature alloys, one could expect greater utilization of
Mg materials in the near future especially in powertrain systems
like the transmission casing, engine oil pan and cylinder block
for greater vehicle weight reduction. Such applications usually
require Mg materials to possess good strength at service conditions
ranging from 150–200 /C176C, which most commercial Mg alloys can-
not deliver [6,7].
In the past decade, much work has been devoted to the devel-
opment and understanding of high performance Mg–RE alloys
and in particular, the Mg–Y–Zn system because of its promising
mechanical performance [8–10]. It was demonstrated by
Kawamura et al. that an ultra-high room temperature tensile yield
strength of 610 MPa with 5% elongation, and a yield strength of
300 MPa at 473 K could be achieved in MgY 2Zn1 (at.%) alloy syn-
thesized via the rapidly solidiﬁed powder metallurgy (RS/PM)
technique [11].
To date, most of the studies conducted on the Mg–Y–Zn system
revolve around the compositions of Mg 100/C03xY2xZnx (at.%, x P 1),
and little work was done and reported on the effect of low Y
(62 at.%) and Zn ( 61 at.%) content on the microstructure and
mechanical properties of Mg–Y–Zn system [8,9,12–15]. No prior
attempt has also been made to synthesize and characterize Mg–
Y–Zn LPSO-containing alloys using the large volume production
capable Disintegrated Melt Deposition (DMD) casting technique,
reported in literature to be a suitable fabrication method for syn-
thesizing composites of Mg integrating various types of reinforce-
ments [16–19].
Since it was reported that the addition of 0.5 at.% Al to the Mg–
Y–Zn system could enhance corrosion resistance signiﬁcantly
[20,21], and the effect of Al on the microstructure and mechanical
properties of Mg–Y–Zn system has yet to be investigated in detail,
the purpose of this study is consequently to synthesize and

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Processing
In this study, two quaternary alloys were prepared by melting
pure Mg (99.95%), with Mg–30Y (wt.%) master alloy, pure Zn
(99.98%) and Al (99.95%) under argon gas atmosphere at 750 /C176C
using the DMD solidiﬁcation technique ( Fig. 1 ). The melt was dis-
integrated by two jets of argon gas oriented normal to the melt
stream prior to depositing into a 40 mm steel mold. The as-cast
ingots were machined down to 36 mm diameter and soaked at
400 /C176C for 60 min prior to extrusion at 350 /C176C at the ratio of 20:1
using a 150T hydraulic press.
2.2. Microstructural studies
Chemical composition analysis was performed using the
Inductively Coupled Plasma ICP–AES technique to determine the
weight percentages of Al, Y and Zn alloying elements in the
extruded materials. Microstructural characterization was done
using Olympus optical microscope (OM) and Zeiss ﬁeld emission
scanning electron microscope (FESEM) equipped with energy dis-
persive spectroscopy (EDS) to study the microstructural morpholo-
gies of the as-cast and extruded Mg–Y–Zn–Al samples. Etching was
performed on mechanically polished samples using acetic-picral
(5 mL acetic acid, 6 g picric acid, 10 mL H 2O and 100 mL
95%-ethanol) as the etchant. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) studies were
conducted on polished samples (transverse and longitudinal
(extrusion direction) surfaces) for phase detection as well as tex-
ture analysis. Automated Shimadzu LAB-X XRD-6000 diffractome-
ter using Cu K a radiation (k = 1.5406 Å) with scan speed of 2 /C176/min
was employed for the XRD studies.
2.3. Mechanical characterization
Vickers microhardness evaluation was carried out on
polished samples using Matsuzawa MMT-X7 automatic digital
microhardness tester. Test load of 100 gf and dwell time of 15 s
were selected as the test parameters. Round tension samples of
5 mm diameter and 25 mm gauge length were prepared from the
extruded rods. Tensile properties were evaluated based on the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis of Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys
Synthesis of the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys was successfully
accomplished using the DMD casting technique. The chemical
compositions (based on ICP–AES) and densities of the developed
Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys are shown in Table 1 . Visual inspection of the
as-cast materials indicated no macropores, shrinkage cavities and
any other macrostructural defects. In addition, near dense materi-
als can be obtained after extrusion. These ﬁndings are consistent
with previous observations made on Mg based materials synthe-
sized via the DMD and hot extrusion route [16,22–24].
3.2. Microstructure development
X-ray diffraction (XRD) studies suggested the presence of three
distinct phases ( a-Mg, Mg 12YZn and M 4Y2ZnAl3) in the extruded
Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys as indicated in Fig. 2 (a) and (b). The detection
of Mg 12YZn conﬁrmed the presence of 14H-LPSO phase (reported
by Zhu et.al as possessing the hexagonal structure with stacking
sequence of ABABCACACACBABA [25]), which suggests that DMD
is another suitable technique for the fabrication Mg–Y–Zn alloys
containing LPSO phase.
The microstructures of the as-cast and extruded Mg–Y–Zn–Al
alloys are shown in Fig. 3(a)–(f). A duplex microstructure consisting
of a-Mg and the dispersion of secondary LPSO phases (with lamellar
morphology) along grain boundaries was distinctly observed in the
optical micrographs of both MgY 1.65Zn0.74Al0.53 and
MgY3.72Zn1.96Al0.45 alloys. However, when viewed under SEM in
Fig. 3(e) and (f), a third phase (particles with brightest contrast) could
be observed to exist adjacent to the lamellar-structured secondary
phases. EDS analyses in Figs. 4 and 5 further conﬁrmed the presence
of this third phase, and the ratio of the elements in the three indepen-
dent phases (from EDS) were consistent with the chemical composi-
tions of the detected phases ( a-Mg, Mg 12YZn (14H-LPSO) and
Mg4Y2ZnAl3) in the XRD studies. Here, it is also worthy to note that
Mg4Y2ZnAl3 phase precipitated earlier in the as-cast Mg–Y–Zn–Al
alloys remained stable even after hot extrusion at 350 /C176C.
As observed from Fig. 3(a), (c), (e) and (f) the volume fraction of
LPSO phase increased with higher content of Y/Zn. Likewise, the
morphology of the LPSO phase changed from an isolated distribu-
tion to the continuous network-type with increasing Y/Zn
additions. Upon extrusion, both the LPSO and Mg 4Y2ZnAl3 phases
were broken down, with the LPSO phase regions evidently elon-
gated in the extrusion direction. In addition, reﬁned grains
(65 lm residing in the vicinity of the LPSO phase regions) and rel-
atively larger worked grains ( P20 lm) were also observed to
co-exist in the extruded microstructures of both Mg–Y–Zn–Al
alloys.
According to literature, the reﬁnement of grains was attributed
to the dynamic recrystallization (DRX) of a-Mg via the
particle-stimulated nucleation (PSN) mechanism [14,15,26].
During extrusion, the hard LPSO particles are capable of inducing
regions with high density of dislocations that were ideal nucleation
sites for recrystallization. Furthermore, the broken down LPSO
phases also served as pinning sites to prevent the growth of
DRXed grains which further contributed to the effect of grain
reﬁnement.
Hirano et al. reported that the DRXed grains (after extrusion)
have weaker basal texture [reduced (0 0 0 2)//E.D] which was
responsible for ductility enhancement in LPSO alloys. On the con-
trary, the unDRXed worked grains possess a more pronounced
basal texture which was consistent with the XRD diffraction pat-
terns obtained in this study [12]. From Fig. 2(a) and (b), the strong
basal texture (due to the worked grains after extrusion) can be
conﬁrmed by the sharp increase in (0 0 0 2) and (1 0 /C01 0) peak
intensities in the longitudinal (extrusion) and transverse surfaces
respectively. The alignment of basal planes in the worked grains
made slip unfavorable (low Schmid Factor) in the tensile direction
and contributes to the strengthening of LPSO alloys.
3.3. Mechanical characteristics
3.3.1. Microhardness
The hardness values of extruded Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys are listed
in Table 2. The signiﬁcant increase in hardness ( /C2467–88%) of devel-
oped Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys compared to monolithic Mg can be attrib-
uted primarily to the presence of much harder LPSO phase in the
materials. Tong et al. [14] measured the hardness of LPSO phase
to be 153.3 ± 7.6 HV0.2, which was evidently higher than the
a-Mg matrix. Similarly, the higher hardness observed in
MgY3.72Zn1.96Al0.45 alloy compared to MgY 1.65Zn0.74Al0.53 could be
explained by the presence of higher volume fraction of LPSO phase
apparent in Fig. 3 (a) and (c) for hardening.
3.3.2. Tensile strength
The room temperature tensile properties of the developed Mg–
Y–Zn–Al alloys are listed in Table 3 . The mechanical properties of
monolithic Mg, MgY 2Zn1 (at.%) alloy (identiﬁed in literature as
the LPSO-containing formulation with the best combination of
strength and ductility), and two other commercially available Al
alloys are also included for the purpose of benchmarking. The
mechanical strengths of the developed Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys were
observed to be comparable to both the Al alloys, and a relatively
high strength of 374 MPa with reasonable strain (comparable to
MgY
2Zn1) was achieved in the MgY 3.72Zn1.96Al0.45 alloy. Here it is
worthy to note that the good strengths of both alloys were
achieved despite the lower Y/Zn content.
The tensile stress–strain curves of both Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys are
illustrated in Fig. 6 . The signiﬁcant increase in TYS ( /C24245–290%)
and UTS ( /C24113–144%) in both the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys compared
to monolithic Mg can be attributed to: (a) the presence of
well-dispersed LPSO and Mg 4Y2ZnAl3 phases in the Mg matrix

ening of materials, (b) effective load transfer from the Mg matrix to
the harder LPSO phase (Young’s modulus of LPSO phase reported in
recent work to be 102.3 ± 5.5 GPa compared to 49.3 ± 1.2 GPa of
Mg [15]), (c) the presence of worked grains with pronounced basal

3.3.3. Failure strain
Despite the high strengths observed in the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys,
failure strains of both alloys were comparable to monolithic Mg.
The retention of strains of the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys can be attributed
to: (a) the homogeneous distribution of broken down LPSO and
Mg4Y2ZnAl3 phases which generates sufﬁcient DRXed grains with
random texture (lesser (0 0 0 2)//ED) favorable for slip (consistent
with studies reported in literature [14,28]), and the (b) absence
of coarse LPSO phases (due to the lower Y/Zn content) with higher
tendency of cracking (causing premature failure) during tensile
test.
On the contrary, failure strains of both Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys were
lower compared to the Al alloys, and this could be potentially
addressed by the incorporation of judiciously selected reinforce-
ments for texture modiﬁcation in the resulting Mg–Y–Zn–Al metal
matrix composites (MMCs) to enhance ductility. For example,
Al2O3 was widely reported to be an effective reinforcement for
enhancing the strength and/or ductility of Mg alloys [22,29–31].
In similar fashion, the addition of ceramic reinforcements to Mg–
Y–Zn–Al could possibly provide more nucleation sites in addition
to the LPSO/Mg4Y2ZnAl3 phases for DRX to occur. With greater area
fraction of DRXed grains (with more random texture favorable for
slips) in the resulting microstructure, ductility could be expected
to improve. Further work is continuing in this area.

4. Conclusions
In the present study, MgY 1.65Zn0.74Al0.53 and MgY3.72Zn1.96Al0.45
alloys (wt.%) with lower Y/Zn content were synthesized and char-
acterized. Accordingly, the following conclusions can be made:
(a) DMD followed by hot extrusion is a suitable technique for
the fabrication Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys containing LPSO phase.
(b) Compared to monolithic Mg, signiﬁcant increase in the hard-
ness ( /C2467–88%), TYS ( /C24245–290%) and UTS ( /C24113–144%)
were observed in both Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys, and can be
attributed to: (a) the presence of well-dispersed LPSO and
Mg
4Y2ZnAl3 phases in the matrix restricting the movement
of dislocations, (b) effective load transfer from the Mg matrix
to the harder LPSO phase, (c) the presence of worked grains
with pronounced basal texture unfavorable for slip, (d)
reﬁned DRXed grains which activated Hall–Petch strength-
ening, and (e) the effect of solid solution strengthening due
to the high solubility of Y and Al in Mg.
(c) The retention of strains in the Mg–Y–Zn–Al alloys despite
signiﬁcant gain in strength can be attributed to: (a) the
homogeneous distribution of broken down LPSO and
