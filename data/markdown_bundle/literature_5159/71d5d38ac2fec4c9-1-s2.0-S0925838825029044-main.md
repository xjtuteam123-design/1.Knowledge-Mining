ABSTRACT
This paper comprehensively reviews the microstructural characteristics, micro-cracking mechanisms, and 
various other methods for inhibiting crack formation in selective laser melting (SLM) manufactured nickel-based 
superalloys, emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of microstructural influences on their micro-crack 
formation. The appearance of high residual stress levels and unstable chemical and structural states in rapid 
heating and cooling processes of SLM can amplify crack susceptibility in nickel-based superalloys. Additionally, 
the superior properties of the superalloys are, in turn, dependent on their numerous trace alloying elements and 
dense precipitate phases (e.g., γ ′ , carbides). This inherent conflict between crack formation and high performance 
poses a continuing challenge. Current studies lack consensus on dominant cracking mechanisms (e.g., solidifi -
cation vs. liquation cracking) and fail to provide universally effective suppression strategies. Based on this and 
underscoring the importance of effective crack suppression strategies tailored to specific micro-cracking mech -
anisms, this paper summarizes the current developments in the research on crack formation mechanism during 
solidification and liquation and its suppression approaches, including composition optimization and heteroge -
neous particles introduction. Additionally, this paper also summarizes current research on simulation calcula -
tions in several additive manufacturing (AM) processes and then discusses in detail the applications of 
thermodynamic simulations, physical analysis models, first-principles calculations, and machine learning 
methods in studying micro-cracks in SLM. In conclusion, this review explores the relationship between micro -
structural characteristics, cracking mechanisms, and suppression strategies and compares the advantages and 
limitations of various crack inhibition methods, aiming to support further advancement in developing techniques 
to produce crack-free SLM components.
1. Introduction
Additive manufacturing (AM), commonly referred to as 3D printing, 
is a process that joins materials to create objects from three-dimensional 
model data, typically layer- by-layer, in contrast to traditional subtrac -
tive manufacturing methods. This process employs a Computer-Aided 
Design (CAD) model, which slices the design into layers to guide the 
sequential addition of material to construct the final physical object [1, 
2] . Compared to traditional fabrication techniques such as casting, 
forging, and welding, AM offers rapid shaping and improved production 
efficiency while enabling the creation of components with complex ge -
ometries, thereby reducing material waste [3,4] . Currently, AM is 
widely utilized in industries, such as aerospace, automotive, and 
biomedical, for production using materials including steel [5 – 7] , Ti al -
loys [8,9] , Al alloys [10 – 13] , superalloys [14 – 16] , composites [17,18] , 
and gradient materials [19 – 21] .
Based on its technical principles, additive manufacturing can be 
categorized into categories such as Powder Bed Fusion (PBF), Directed 
Energy Deposition (DED), Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), Stereo -
lithography (SLA), Material Jetting, and Bioprinting [22] . Among these, 
powder fusion-based AM processes — such as powder bed fusion using a 
laser beam (PBF-LB), powder bed fusion using an electron beam 
(PBF-EB), and directed energy deposition using a laser beam (DED-LB) — 
typically exhibit a more complex thermal history, resulting in

Nickel-based superalloys exhibit high mechanical performance at 
elevated temperatures and excellent corrosion and wear resistance. 
Therefore, they are commonly used to manufacture hot-end components 
of aerospace engines [28] . Nickel-based alloys are a class of superalloys 
where nickel is the primary element, combined with varying amounts of 
other elements like Cr (Chromium), Fe (Iron), Co (Cobalt), Mo (Mo -
lybdenum), W (Tungsten), Ti (Titanium), Al (Aluminum), etc. The su -
perior high-temperature properties of nickel-based alloys are primarily 
attributed to their high degree of alloying and the synergistic effects of 
multiple strengthening mechanisms, including solution strengthening, 
precipitation strengthening, and grain-boundary strengthening.
The microstructure of nickel-based alloys consists of a face-centered 
cubic (FCC) γ matrix and various precipitate phases [29] . For example,

improving the toughness of the alloy. However, excessive carbides may

mechanical properties of the alloy [32,33] .
Solution strengthening is achieved by increasing the atomic binding 
force, lattice distortion, and recrystallization temperature while 
decreasing atomic diffusivity in solution and stacking-fault energy. In 
nickel-based alloys, elements such as A1, Fe, Ti, W, Co, and Mo dissolve 
into the matrix or precipitate, enhancing solid solution strengthening 
[34,35] .
Specific grain boundaries and dendritic strengthening elements, such 
as B (Boron), Mg (Magnesium), and Ce (Cerium), can reinforce the grain 
boundaries and dendritic regions through interstitial atoms or second 
phases [36,37] .
In some cases, topologically close-packed (TCP) phases, such as μ , σ , 
and Laves phases, may form in nickel-based alloys. The TCP phase 
structures are highly complex, with coordination numbers reaching 
14 – 16, and are generally regarded as electronic compounds. The pres -
ence of refractory alloying elements, such as Mo, W, and Ti, promotes 
the formation of TCP phases, consuming significant amounts of solid 
solution-strengthening elements and reducing long-term creep resis -
tance of the alloy. Furthermore, microcracks are often initiated at TCP 
phases and the γ matrix interfaces. Therefore, reducing the tendency for 
TCP phase precipitation is critical in the optimal design of nickel-based 
superalloys [38,39] .
The precipitation and evolution of various phases in nickel-based 
superalloys are critical in determining their high-temperature perfor -
mance and thermal stability. For instance, in the Allvac 718Plus alloy, 
the γ ′ phase is the primary strengthening phase, whereas the η phase is 
the dominant grain boundary phase. These phases significantly influ -
ence the alloy ’ s high-temperature strength, fatigue resistance, and creep 
performance [40] .
2. Current status of research on nickel-based alloys fabricated 
by SLM
The principle of SLM is illustrated in Fig. 1 (a). Before printing, 3D 
computer-aided design models of the target component are prepared 
and sliced into 2D profiles that serve as objective templates. Next, a laser 
generator produces a high-energy laser beam modulated by a beam 
expander and focusing optics before passing through a galvanometer. 
The laser beam then selectively irradiates a bed of metallic powder ac -
cording to the current 2D layer profile (i.e., the current objective tem -
plate) under the control of a group of lenses. This melts the powders 
within the layer to form an integrated, strongly metallic-bonded sheet. 
As the current layer is completed, the build platform drives the building 
substrate and the printed part to reduce in thickness by a layer. The 
resulting powder layer is then fed by a re-coater that draws alimentative 
powders from the powder bed, and the above process is repeated for all 
remaining templates until the entire component is formed. The build 
chamber is filled with inert gases (e.g., nitrogen and argon) throughout 
the process to ensure the safe SLM process generates a pure product 
[41] .

3. Microstructural characteristics of nickel-based alloys 
fabricated by SLM
The microstructure of alloys produced through SLM exhibits distinct 
characteristics. In 3D printing equipment, the energy of laser beam 
typically follows a Gaussian distribution [62 – 64] ( Fig. 4 (a)). When the 
laser heat source interacts with the metallic powder, a melt pool that 
resembles a welding process occurs on a micrometer scale ( Fig. 4 (b)). 
The thermal distribution within the melt pool is spatially non-uniform. 
During the additive manufacturing process, the cooling conditions at 
various locations within the melt pool differ; heat transfer at the pe -
riphery of the melt pool predominantly occurs through conduction in the 
surrounding powder and solidified layers, whereas thermal dissipation 
at the surface primarily occurs via radiation, with conduction being 
significantly more effective than radiation [58] . Although thermal 
dissipation on the outer side of the melt pool also occurs through con -
duction in the solidified layer and powder, the thermal conductivity of 
the solid is higher than that of the powder for the same alloy composi -
tion. This results in a steeper temperature gradient at the bottom 
boundary of the melt pool compared to its sides. Consequently, this 
disparity leads to the Marangoni convection [65 – 67] , causing the 
molten material to flow from the center to the periphery during SLM 
( Fig. 4 (c)).
When process parameters change, Marangoni convection can influ -
ence the shape of the melt pool [45,59,60,69,70] . Increasing the laser 
power raises the peak temperature, deepening the Marangoni convec -
tion within the melt pool. Conversely, extending the laser dwell time has 
a minimal effect on the peak temperature; instead, it primarily raises the 
temperature of the surrounding powder, leading to a broader melt pool 
[67] . Thus, by adjusting the process parameters, the morphology of the 
melt pool can be effectively controlled [46] . For instance, the opera -
tional mode of SLM (conduction or keyhole) depends on the peak tem -
perature. As reported by Bayat et al. [59] , keyhole mode occurs when 
metal vaporization generates recoil pressure, producing deep melt pools 
(high depth/width ratio) but risking keyhole porosity. To accurately 
predict this transition, King et al. [45] proposed a dimensionless model 
correlating normalized melt pool depth with normalized enthalpy, 
which outperforms traditional VED criteria. Experimental validation by 
Sadowski et al. [69] further demonstrated that intermediate laser 
powers (100 – 150 W) optimize process stability, achieving uniform scan 
tracks with minimal defects and controlled melt depths.
Marangoni convection also causes inconsistent heat dissipation di -
rections at different locations within the melt pool. FEA of the temper -
ature field within the melt pool reveals that the temperature gradient is 
smaller, and the cooling and solidification rates are higher near the 
center. In contrast, the temperature gradient is more significant, and the 
cooling rate is lower near the bottom of the melt pool, as shown in Fig. 5
[71] .

Therefore, the solidified microstructure within the melt pool transitions 
from cellular crystal at the bottom to cellular dendrites higher up, with 
finer fibrous dendrites forming closer to the upper region of the melt

During the SLM process for nickel-based alloys, when the liquid 
phase begins to solidify, the rate of atomic restructuring at the crystal -
lization front far exceeds the rate of molecular diffusion. As a result, the 
matrix predominantly captures the strengthening solute atoms, leading 
to the formation of a supersaturated solid solution before any precipitate 
phase can form [74] , resulting in a phenomenon known as "solute 
trapping." Consequently, the microstructure of nickel-based alloys pro -
duced via SLM includes the Laves phase resulting from solute trapping, 
along with a small amount of γ’ phase distributed among the interden -
dritic regions, as depicted in Fig. 6 (b), (c).
Current research on the morphology and microstructure of the melt 
pool is based on single-track experiments [75 – 81] , which involve 
single-track laser printing on bulk materials, as shown in Fig. 7 [75] . 
Studying SLM single tracks can reduce the variables of concern and thus 
facilitate a better understanding of grain and texture development in the 
SLM process. For instance, Acharya et al. [76] integrated computational 
fluid dynamics (CFD) with single-track experiments, demonstrating the 
ability to predict temperature gradients, melt pool morphology, and 
mushy zone dimensions. Wang et al. [77] revealed that epitaxial grain 
growth dominates at melt pool boundaries in SLM 316 L single tracks, 
whereas low scanning speeds promote equiaxed grain formation near 
the surface layer. Yadroitsev et al. [78] measured the molten pool di -
mensions through single-track experiments, with the 
single-track-related defects investigated and discussed. Building on this 
theoretical framework, T. Guraya et al. [82] demonstrated the SLM of 
IN738LC in keyhole mode, as illustrated in Fig. 8 . Their findings, 
consistent with prior literature, indicate a low porosity level (0.14 %), 
and keyhole mode SLM has proven effective in promoting grain growth 
predominantly at high angles to the build direction.
4. Grain growth characteristics of nickel-based alloys produced 
by SLM
During the multi-layer printing process, as the heat source moves 
continuously, the melt pool bonds metallurgically with adjacent tracks 
and layers, forming a "scaly" melt pool structure as illustrated in Fig. 9
[83] . The overlapping melt pools repeatedly remelt the coarse cellular 
structures from the previous layer, replacing them with refined cellular 
grains at the base of the melt pool. This cyclical process reduces the 
primary spacing between dendrites, resulting in finer cellular spacing

material evaporation and gas expansion also affects the direction of heat 
flux [87] . The combined effect of the complex heat flux direction and the 
preferred growth direction encourages the formation of small irregular 
grains with arbitrary orientations. When certain grains proliferate in one 
direction, they can suppress the growth of adjacent, slower-growing 
grains, resulting in short-range cellular dendrites between columnar 
grains (grain competition growth), as shown in Fig. 12 .
These various grain growth mechanisms cause the microstructure of 
nickel-based alloys to feature numerous elongated and straight grain 
boundaries, along with a significant number of high-angle grain 
boundaries. Additionally, the dendrites of nickel-based alloys are mixed 
with a substantial amount of brittle Laves phase or γ’ phase, which 
makes the alloy prone to microcracking.

5. Mechanisms of microcrack formation in nickel-based alloys
Microcracks, referred to as thermal cracks, are common microscopic 
defects in SLM nickel-based alloys. These cracks mainly result from the 
micro-segregation of alloying elements and the high thermal stresses 
that occur during the printing process. When thermal stress accumulates 
in segregated areas or defects within the heat-affected zone (HAZ), 
cracks are likely to form, as shown in Fig. 15 . Owing to the epitaxial 
growth characteristics of the microstructure, these microcracks can 
swiftly propagate along grain boundaries, often extending across three 
to four melt pools before halting. The presence of deep cracks can 
significantly degrade the performance of the alloy.
Crack formation mechanisms can be categorized into solidification 
cracks, liquation cracks, oxidation cracks, ductility cracks, and strain- 
age cracking. Given that 3D printing involves a continuous metallur -
gical bonding process of micron-scale melt pools, certain cracking 
mechanisms, such as solidification and liquation cracks, are akin to 
those seen in welding.
As the temperature gradient shifts, cellular dendrites form at the 
interface between the molten and solid states, specifically at the solidus 
line. The rapid heating and cooling that the metal powder undergoes 
subject the molten metal to non-equilibrium solidification conditions, 
resulting in solute trapping phenomena that accumulate solute atoms at 
the solid-liquid interface. Numerous alloying elements in nickel-based 
alloys enrich various solute elements within the liquid film, encapsu -
lating the dendrites and expanding the solidification range. This creates 
a solid-liquid coexistence zone marked by solid-rich and liquid-poor 
conditions, leading to two significant phenomena. First, the liquid film 
around the primary dendrite arms weakens the inter-dendritic bonding

final stages of solidification.

7. Regulation from material aspects
7.1. Reducing or eliminating segregated elements
Modifying the physical and chemical properties of the alloy itself 
presents an effective route to reduce crack susceptibility. Strategies 
aimed at suppressing microcrack initiation can generally be categorized 
into three primary approaches. The first involves addressing elemental 
micro-segregation at grain boundaries by reducing or eliminating spe -
cific segregating elements. This method not only mitigates elemental 
segregation and narrows the solidification temperature range but also 
prevents the formation of low-melting-point eutectics. For instance, 
thermodynamic simulations indicate that CM247LC without Hf exhibits 
a narrower solidification range than its Hf-containing counterpart. 
Building on this insight, Griffiths et al. [139] fabricated CM247LC 
samples with and without Hf using SLM and compared their cracking 
behavior under different processing conditions. Results showed that the 
crack density in the non-Hf samples (CM247LC NHf) was reduced by 
36 % and 75 % compared to the Hf-containing samples under two 
respective processing conditions. Specifically, under Condition 1, the 
average crack densities for CM247LC and CM247LC NHf were 
1.27 mm/mm² and 0.81 mm/mm², respectively, whereas, under Con -
dition 2, the values were 0.12 mm/mm² and 0.03 mm/mm², as depicted 
in Fig. 25 .
Dacian Tomus et al. also noted the segregation of elements such as 
Mn, Si, and C at the grain boundaries of Hastelloy-X alloys [140] . 
Reducing the content of these elements improved the printability of the 
Hastelloy alloy. Another study observed that the segregated elements in 
Hastelloy-X included Mo [141] , suggesting that the segregated elements 
are not specific. In the case of IN738LC alloys, reducing the Zr content in 
the feedstock powder from 0.12 wt% to 0.024 wt% effectively lowered 
the crack density, with area ratios decreasing from 0.87 % to 0.15 % in 
the XOY plane and from 1.81 % to 0.21 % in the XOZ plane [142] .

7.1.1. Adding specific elements
Reflecting on the mechanisms of crack formation, if the liquid film 
surrounding the dendrites maintains sufficient fluidity and surface ten -

strengthening the bonds at the grain boundaries. Even with the precip -

broaden the solidification range, thus ensuring that the solid-liquid

7.1.2. Introducing heterogeneous particles
The research on compositional optimization aims to control or 
eliminate crack initiation points. However, eliminating crack initiation 
remains challenging owing to the complexity of the temperature gra -
dients during 3D printing and the multidirectional grain growth caused 
by Plateau-Rayleigh instability. Some studies focus on introducing nano- 
and micron-sized particles to address the potential or delayed cracking 
characteristics in nickel-based alloys during printing. The essence of this 
crack suppression method is to refine the grain structure and reduce the 
proportion of high-angle grain boundaries [152] . The introduced 
heterogeneous powders serve as grain refiners. When cracks rapidly 
propagate toward these particles, they reduce the boundary energy and 
lower the driving force for dislocation-driven crack propagation (DDC). 
Additionally, the inherent strength or toughness of the particles can slow 
down or prevent further crack propagation, as illustrated in Fig. 29 . This 
method involves incorporating nano- and sub-micron-sized heteroge -
neous powders directly into commercial alloy powders using ball milling 
techniques. When the mixed powders are melted during the 3D printing 
process, the nano- and sub-micron particles diffuse into the melt, acting 
as heterogeneous nucleation sites. The preparation method and experi -
mental process are relatively straightforward, and the suppression 
strategy is comparatively simple.
This method was initially applied in the 3D printing of aluminum 
alloys [146] ， and has gained rapid popularity. In aluminum alloys, 
grain refiners (i.e., effective nucleating particles) are often commercially

8. Simulation and modeling of AM nickel-based alloys
Additive manufacturing is a rapid solidification process. In SLM, in 
particular, the rapid cooling and complex thermal gradients involve 
multiple physical and chemical processes, such as gas expansion, solid- 
liquid-gas phase transformation, solid-state phase changes, and element 
segregation, as shown in Fig. 31 [161] . Experimental methods Similar 
principles apply can directly observe the microstructural changes in 
materials fabricated by SLM, however isolating or controlling these 
processes experimentally can be challenging. Therefore, simulation and 
computational methods play a critical role in exploring the micro -
structural evolution of materials during fabrication [162] .
Current simulation methods for analyzing SLM processes primarily 
include analytical models, numerical methods, experimental ap -
proaches, and machine learning techniques [42,43,163] . Studies have 
focused on modeling various aspects of the SLM process, such as tem -
perature fields [62,164 – 167] , stress fields [63,168] , powder flow [169, 
170] , microstructure [171 – 173] , and solid-liquid interfaces [174,175] .
For instance, Y.L. Li et al. [176] used finite element (FE) methods to 
simulate the temperature field of AlSi10Mg fabricated by SLM, revealing 
the effects of laser power and scanning speed on thermal behavior. The 
results indicated that higher laser power and lower scanning speeds 
increased the thermal gradient. Cooling rates remained relatively stable 
with increased laser power but significantly increased with higher 
scanning speeds, reaching a magnitude of 106 K/s [177] . Similar find -
ings were reported by P.P. Yuan [165] and B. AlMangour [178] in their 
studies of TiC/AlSi10Mg and SS316L stainless steel, showing strong 
agreement with experimental data.
Q. Chen et al. [68] used the level-set method to investigate powder 
flow behavior during SLM to create a three-dimensional FE model, 
simulating powder melting, droplet formation, and fluid dynamics 
within the melt pool. The model accurately described the impact of the 
Marangoni effect on ceramic tracks in SLM. H. Chen et al. [169]
developed a discrete element model to visualize the flow and velocity 
fields at the particle scale within the powder bed and the relationship 
between powder bed quality and layering parameters. Similarly, D.D. 
Gu et al. [170] combined discrete element and finite volume methods to 
establish a three-dimensional FE physical model with mesoscale 
coupling, analyzing the flow behavior and melt-solidification charac -
teristics of WC/Inconel718 composite powders. They found that flow -
ability improved with powder particle sizes below 25 μ m but 
deteriorated when particle size exceeded 25 μ m.
In microstructure simulations, phase-field (PF) modeling provides a 
unified framework integrating thermodynamics and kinetics, enabling 
comprehensive studies across material composition design, process 
optimization, microstructure control, and performance prediction 
[179] . For instance, Yuhong Zhao et al. [180] applied PF modeling to 
predict the precipitation, segregation, and crack susceptibility of NbC, 
which serves as a crack initiation site in austenitic stainless steel, 
thereby optimizing casting parameters.
In the context of SLM research, Liu et al. [181] utilized a multi -
physics model integrating the thermal lattice Boltzmann method with PF 
modeling to simulate microstructural evolution during alloy solidifica -
tion in SLM, elucidating the effects of latent heat, melt flow, and cooling 
rate on the solidification process. Similarly, Kubra Karayagizdengren 
et al. [182] developed a computational framework that weakly couples 
an FE thermal model to a non-equilibrium PF model to investigate the

The analytical models and numerical calculations used in SLM are 
continually evolving and advancing. Several reviews have comprehen -
sively summarized the current modeling and simulation approaches in 
metal additive manufacturing [184–186] . Numerical simulations offer a 
fast and reliable method for predicting AM processes; however, several 
challenges persist in practical applications, as listed below:
1. Unlike conventional alloy melting and solidification processes, AM 
involves transport phenomena across a broad range of time and length 
scales. In computational methodologies, spatial discretization scales 
vary significantly—from the laser spot size to the macroscopic distri -
bution in complex structural components. This requires thermal-solid 
coupling simulations to operate at microsecond-level time steps, 
imposing high computational demands.
2. The physical mechanisms within the melt pool are highly intricate, 
involving phenomena such as infiltration, surface tension, and Mar -
angoni convection, which simple models fail to fully predict [163,187] . 
For instance, FEM models often simplify the metal powder as a contin -
uum solid with equivalent thermomechanical properties and overlook 
the vigorous melt pool dynamics by assuming a steady state [188] . In 
CFD modeling, a major challenge is quantifying the influence of tem -
perature and chemical variations on surface tension, which affects 
Marangoni turbulence and ultimately alters the melt pool morphology 
[189] . Furthermore, the applicability of classical solidification theories, 
such as rapid solidification models, remains uncertain in AM. Using 
synchrotron X-ray imaging, Damien Tourret et al. [190] measured the 
solid-liquid interface velocities of Ni-Mo-Al alloy single crystals under 
AM conditions. Their measured growth velocities were significantly 
higher than the absolute stability threshold predicted by classical 
models, such as the Kurz-Giovanola-Trivedi (KGT) model [191] . 
Addressing these discrepancies requires further theoretical advance -
ments and precise evaluations of critical thermophysical parameters, 
including liquid diffusivities, solid-liquid interface excess free energies, 
and kinetic coefficients.
3. High-fidelity models effectively describe fusion zone characteris -
tics in SLM; however, a significant gap in the literature regarding re -
sidual stress and distortion behavior remains. For example, in AM of 
nickel-based alloys, a key challenge in SLM geometrical and mechani -
cal modeling arises from their high melting points, which complicate 
precise temperature control and necessitate careful thermal manage -
ment to mitigate cracking and warping. Achieving intricate geometries 
free of defects remains particularly challenging for IN625 and IN718, 
highlighting the critical need for improved design integrity in AM with 
these alloys [192] .
4. At the microscale, PF models can predict phase morphologies 
during solid-state phase transformations [193] . In contrast, cellular 
automata effectively simulate grain and sub-grain growth during solid -
ification with moderate accuracy and computational efficiency [194] . 
Monte Carlo (MC) simulations, which are based on the thermodynamic 
free energy of site interactions, offer a probabilistic approach to 
modeling grain growth with high computational efficiency [195] . 
However, these methods do not sufficiently capture solute trapping at 
the nanoscale or the effects of grain boundary diffusion. A key challenge 
is seamlessly integrating multiple length scales—macroscopic, meso -
scopic, microscopic, and nanoscopic—to bridge the gap between 
different modeling approaches. Enhancing multiscale integration would 
significantly improve the accuracy and applicability of SLM simulations.
5. Beyond computational advancements, integrating cutting-edge 
technologies such as real-time monitoring systems and in-situ sensing 
can offer deeper insights into the dynamic nature of the SLM process. 
Research in this direction is essential for improving process control, 
enabling early defect detection, and ensuring consistent part quality 
[184] .
As computational models continue to evolve, integrating advanced 
methodologies can further enhance their predictive capabilities. With 
the rapid advancement of machine learning in the manufacturing sector, 
researchers are increasingly leveraging data-driven approaches to 
analyze microstructural changes in metal additive manufacturing. A 
noteworthy trend in SLM characteristics modeling is the integration of 
machine learning algorithms, which are being explored to augment 
predictive accuracy, optimize build parameters, and minimize defects. 
By combining numerical simulations with experimental data, the syn -
ergy between artificial intelligence and physics-based models provides a 
more comprehensive understanding of the SLM process, ultimately 
improving the efficiency, reliability, and overall quality of fabricated 
components.
9. Thermodynamic calculations in microcracks
Thermodynamic analyses of microcrack formation primarily rely on 
the non-equilibrium Scheil model. The Scheil-Gulliver equation char -
acterizes solute redistribution during non-equilibrium solidification, 
assuming negligible diffusion in the solid phase and complete mixing in 
the liquid phase under a wide range of experimental conditions [196] . 
More specifically, Scheil-Gulliver simulations model a stepwise cooling 
process initiated from a fully liquid state. As the temperature drops 
below the liquidus, the equilibrium amounts and compositions of the 
solid and liquid phases are calculated. The solid phase is then removed 
from the system, and in the next step, at a slightly lower temperature, 
only the remaining liquid is considered for further solidification. This 
iterative procedure continues until solidification is complete and no 
liquid remains [197] .
According to the non-equilibrium Scheil model, alloying elements 
can effectively alter the solidification range of a material. The first 
approach utilizing thermodynamic modeling involves calculating, 
within the framework of the traditional Scheil model, the alloying ele -
ments capable of adjusting the solidification interval. This adjustment 
can help regulate local residual stress and thereby reduce crack sus -
ceptibility. For example, the addition of Hf in IN738LC [148] and Zr in 
Haynes 230 [150] , as discussed in previous crack suppression strategies, 
was shown to influence solidification behavior and mitigate microcrack 
formation.
The second approach refines the Scheil model using experimentally 
obtained data. As previously discussed, Zhongji Sun et al. [149] utilized 
APT to analyze the elemental partitioning between dendritic and 
interdendritic regions at the sub-nanometer scale. Because each APT 
data point shown in Fig. 27 can be regarded as representing an indi -
vidual solidification event, the solidification interval between these two 
regions can be determined by calculating their respective solidus tem -
peratures (see Fig. 32 a). To specifically evaluate the impact of Ti par -
titioning, only the Ti concentration profile measured in the 
interdendritic region ( Fig. 27 c) was used within the calculation domain 
(160 nm), whereas the concentrations of all other elements were 
maintained as in the dendritic region (position at 0 nm), with Ni used as 
the balancing element. This approach enabled a quantitative assessment 
of how the segregation of each element influences the local solidus 
temperature. The calculated shifts in solidus temperature for several key 
elements are shown in Fig. 32 (b). Such calculations can be viewed as a 
refined or experimentally enhanced Scheil model, offering a more ac -
curate representation of chemical segregation behavior and its effect on 
solidification characteristics. Using a similar strategy, Zhongji Sun [198]
further adjusted the Al content to drastically reduce crack density in the

Another approach involves the use of a physics-based computational 
alloy design strategy — known as the Alloys-by-Design (ABD)

SLM processing conditions.
By integrating thermodynamic calculations with microstructure- 
based criteria, the researchers were able to rationally explore the com -
plex, nonlinear relationships between alloy composition, processability, 
and mechanical performance — establishing a predictive and systematic 
pathway for the design of next-generation SLM superalloys.
The integration of thermodynamic, solidification kinetics, and 
physics-based models for the design of novel alloys has been receiving 
growing attention in recent years. For example, Wei Song et al. [200]
recently developed a novel crack-free Ni-based superalloy, ZGH451 – 1, 
using a similar design strategy, further demonstrating the potential of 
this approach in advancing AM-specific alloy development.

The model emphasizes the initial formation of cavities and in -

The RDG model determines the maximum deformation rate sus -
tained in the mushy zone before cavity formation at the root of the 
dendrites. In contrast, the SKK model compares the stress in the mushy 
zone with the size of shrinkage cavities to predict crack occurrence using 
fracture mechanics. However, these models require numerous inputs 
regarding material properties and processing conditions, which may not 
be well-established, particularly under the rapid cooling rates charac -
teristic of AM.
A novel approach to predicting hot crack susceptibility, based on 
vapor cavitation and building on a conceptual model similar to the 
Rappaz-Drezet-Gremaud criterion, was introduced by Constantin Zenz 
et al. [206] . This approach includes nonequilibrium solidification, 
weakly coupled thermo-mechanics, recoating with continuum powder, 
and the potential use of the amount of vapor that cavitates within a 
critical portion of the mushy zone as an indicator for crack initiation. 
The approach has been successfully validated and demonstrates the 
application of the hot cracking model across different scales in powder 
bed fusion (PBF) processes, ranging from single scan tracks to multiple 
printing layers.

dendritic morphology evolution, which are crucial for accurately pre -

the mushy zone occurring owing to pressure drop is unlikely. Instead, a 
porosity-based crack initiation model is probably the underlying 
mechanism. The latest hot cracking model proposed by Kou [209] , in -
cludes closely spaced columnar dendrites formed by solidification and 
growth, as well as the separation of adjacent grains caused by tensile 
deformation perpendicular to the growth direction, with liquid feeding 
occurring along the opposite growth direction. Unlike the RDG model, 
the Kou model does not address the entire mushy zone but focuses on 
events at the grain boundary. A void may form if the net separation rate 
due to deformation exceeds the sum of grain growth and liquid feeding.

12. Summary and outlook
Advancements in additive manufacturing (AM), particularly in metal 
SLM, have significantly transformed industrial practices for nickel-based 
superalloys. However, this transformation is not without challenges, as 
micro-cracking remains a critical issue that hampers the mechanical 
performance and reliability of components produced via SLM. A 
comprehensive understanding of microstructural features, micro- 
cracking mechanisms, and effective suppression strategies is essential 
for advancing the application of nickel-based superalloys in high-end 
industries such as aerospace.
(1) Current Understanding of Microstructural Features and Cracking 
Mechanisms
Recent studies have elucidated the microstructural characteristics of 
nickel-based superalloys produced by SLM, highlighting the significance 
of dendritic structures and their role in crack formation. The identified 
micro-cracking mechanisms include SC, LC, OC, DDC, and SAC, each 
influenced by residual stresses during the cooling process. The interplay 
between these mechanisms underscores the necessity for tailored sup -
pression strategies tailored to specific cracking causes. For example, 
DDC can be reduced by optimizing process parameters such as laser 
speed and power, whereas SC or LC may require adjustments in alloy 
composition or post-processing treatments. Therefore, effective crack 
suppression strategies are essential, with current approaches focusing 
primarily on process control methods (parameter optimization, sub -
strate preheating, and laser beam profile modification) and changes in 
material composition (introducing nanoparticles and modifying super -
alloy design). A thorough examination of these strategies reveals their 
interconnectivity and underscores the need for developing the most 
effective crack suppression methods tailored to each cracking 
mechanism.
(2) Systematic Approach to Micro-Crack Suppression for Specific 
Alloys
The research underscores the importance of adopting a systematic 
approach for studying micro-crack suppression strategies tailored to 
specific nickel-based superalloys. This approach should progress 
through foundational experiments focused on optimizing processing 
parameters, followed by alloy modifications, and culminating in post- 
processing treatments. By establishing a coherent framework, re -
searchers can derive a generalized mechanism that elucidates the rela -
tionship between micro-cracking phenomena and suppression 
strategies, facilitating the advancement of nickel-based superalloys 
within SLM processes.
This systematic investigation will enhance the understanding of the 
micro-cracking mechanisms and contribute to the formulation of uni -
versally applicable suppression strategies. As a result, this comprehen -
sive understanding will serve as a cornerstone for developing robust 
nickel-based alloys that meet the demanding requirements of AM 
applications.
(3) Critical Need for Evaluation of Innovative Nickel-Based Alloys in 
SLM
As demand for innovative nickel-based superalloys grows, expe -
diting the testing of these new materials for practical applications is 
imperative. The goal is to ensure that newly developed alloys exhibit 
properties suitable for SLM without sacrificing performance in real- 
world scenarios. A critical aspect of this testing is analyzing how crack 
suppression strategies influence the high-temperature performance of 
these alloys. This dual focus will help validate the feasibility of new 
alloys for SLM processes and their later applications in high-stress 
environments.
Research should emphasize evaluating the applicability of new 
nickel-based alloys by investigating their mechanical performance, 
thermal stability, and resistance to micro-cracking under operational 
conditions. Establishing this relationship will provide insights into the 
long-term performance and reliability of SLM-produced components.
(4) Importance and Advantages of Simulation Calculations in Micro- 
Crack Suppression
Solidification cracking has been studied for decades; however, no 
existing criteria can definitively state whether a crack will occur during 
solidification. However, they can provide powerful indications of crack 
susceptibility. Various modeling methods exist for crack susceptibility in 
AM, such as thermodynamic simulations, first-principles calculations, 
and physical models. However, an inability exists to fully capture the 
complex thermal and mechanical interactions during the AM process. 
Addressing these limitations is essential for advancing predictive 
capabilities.
However, simulation studies in AM have yielded similar results, but 
the collective research suffers from a lack of standardization and 
quantitative data. The lack of standardization and quantification of 
cracks similarly diminishes the ability to confidently compare results 
between studies, making the determination of the most effective crack 
mitigation or elimination methods challenging. A robust, standardized, 
and quantifiable measure of cracking must be developed to enable 
comparison of results from a given experiment with existing literature.
In conclusion, the path forward for nickel-based superalloys in ad -
ditive manufacturing necessitates a holistic approach that integrates a 
deep understanding of microstructural characteristics, systematic 
experimentation, and sophisticated computational modeling. By 
focusing on these areas, the field can make significant progress in 
developing high-performance materials that are resistant to micro- 
cracking, ultimately paving the way for their successful use in critical 
industries.
