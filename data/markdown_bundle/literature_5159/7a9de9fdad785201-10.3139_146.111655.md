Microstructural and mechanical properties
of novel b-type Ti–Nb–Ni alloys containing
a second phase
The microstructure and mechanical properties of novel b-
type Ti74-xNb26Nix (x = 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 at.%) alloys
with a low Young’s modulus and containing different
amounts of a second phase (Ti 2Ni and Ni 3Nb) were investi-
gated. The experimental results show that the relative vol-
ume fractions of Ti 2Ni phase in the Ti–Nb–Ni alloys in-
creased with increasing Ni content. The mechanical
properties of Ti–Nb–Ni alloys varied with the amount of
second phase. Ti 70Nb26Ni4,T i 66Nb26Ni8, and Ti 62Nb26Ni12
alloys containing a small amount of Ti 2Ni phase exhibited
a higher plastic strain; however, Ti 58Nb26Ni16,T i 54Nb26 ·
Ni20, and Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloys containing a large amount of
Ti2Ni phase and a small amount of Ni 3Nb phase exhibited
a small plastic strain. The increase in strength and decrease
in plasticity of alloys can be mainly attributed to the in-
crease in high-strength brittle Ti 2Ni phase and grain refin-
ing of the b-phase matrix. In addition, Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloy
with a low Young’s modulus has a high yield strength and
elastic energy; this has potential application as biomedical
and functional materials.
Keywords: Ti–Nb–Ni alloy; Mechanical properties; Sec-
ond phase; Microstructure
1. Introduction
Commercially pure titanium (CP-Ti) and Ti alloys (Ti-6Al-
4V and Ti-6Al-7Nb) are widely used in manufacturing
load-bearing orthopedic implants [1, 2]; they have a low
density, a high specific strength, superior corrosion resis-
tance, excellent biocompatibility, and a low Young’s mod-
ulus [3, 4]. Ti-6Al-4V as an implant material with a higher
strength, corrosion resistance, and hardness compared with
CP-Ti [5, 6]. However, the release of toxic aluminum (Al)
and vanadium (V) ions may induce long-term severe health
problems [6]. Therefore, Al- and V-free Ti alloys such as
Ti–Nb [7], Ti–Nb–Fe [8 – 10], Ti–Fe–Ta [11], Ti–Nb–Ag
[12], Ti–Mo–Nb–Zr [13], and Ti–Nb–Sn [14] have been de-
veloped in the biomaterial field. The elasticity moduli of
these Al- and V-free Ti alloys are still considerably higher
than that of human bone [15], leading to the stress-shielding
phenomenon [3] and affecting the health of patients. There-
fore, it is essential to develop new Al- and V-free Ti alloys
with a low Young’s modulus in the biomaterial field.
Recently, b-type Ti–Nb-based alloys have attracted
much interest as a promising biomaterial [7 – 12]. These al-
loys exhibit a martensitic transformation from a disordered
b phase (bcc) to an orthorhombic a@ martensite phase. This
mainly depends on the alloy composition and material pro-
cessing or occurs during the cooling or loading [7, 13, 16 –
18]. Addition of a small amount of suitable metal enhances
the mechanical and/or biomaterial properties of Ti–Nb-
based alloys [13, 16 – 20]. When the heat of mixing between
the additive metal such as Zr, Mo, Ta, and Sn and main
alloy metals is small, a homogeneous solid solution is
formed, enhancing the strength and maintaining a low
Young’s modulus and high ductility of Ti–Nb-based alloys
[13, 14, 18 – 23]. When the negative heat of mixing between
the additive metal such as Ni, Fe, and Cr and main alloy
metals is large, a second phase is formed, or a martensitic
transformation from a disordered b phase (bcc) to an orthor-
hombic a@ martensite phase occurs, enhancing the strength
and maintaining a low Young’s modulus and high ductility
of Ti–Nb-based alloys [8, 24, 25]. Haghighi et al. [8] re-
ported that the phases and properties of Ti–Nb–Fe alloys
depend on the minor addition of Fe. In addition, the re-
ported Ti-11Nb-0.5Fe alloy has a typical a + b biphasic
Widmanstätten microstructure [8]. Slokar et al. [24] re-
ported that Ti–Nb–Cr alloys have a low elastic modulus,
high strength, and high plasticity.
With the increase in biomedical applications of TiNi
shape-memory alloys, the high Ni content poses a threat to
patients [26]. Therefore, Ni is rarely added to Ti–Nb-based
alloys. However, a small amount of Ni in Ti-based alloys
has less effect on the body [26]. Therefore, when a small
amount of Ni is added to Ti–Nb alloy, the resulting material
can also be considered as a biocompatible material. As the
heat of mixing between Ni and Ti (Nb) is –35 kJ · mol –1
(–30 kJ · mol –1) [27], the additive metal Ni leads to the for-
mation of a second phase from the parent phase.
In binary as-cast Ti–Nb alloy, when the mass percentage
of Nb is 34 – 40, the elastic modulus of Ti–Nb alloy de-
creases with the increase in Nb content. The elastic modu-
lus of b-type Ti-40Nb alloy (mass%) is 55 GPa [28], 1 – 5
times that of human bone (10 – 30 GPa) [29]. The aim of
this study was to obtain new Ti 74Nb26 (at.%) or Ti-40.5Nb
(mass%) biocompatible materials with a low elastic modu-
lus and high strength by adding Ni and to further evaluate
the effect of second phase on the phase or microstructure
as well as mechanical proprieties, especially elastic modu-
lus, of Ti 74-xNb26Nix alloys by X-ray diffraction (XRD)

2. Experimental procedure
Various combinations (some 30 grams) of pure Ti, Nb, and
Ni (purity of 99.9 % or higher) were used to prepare the
ingots of six Ti–Nb–Ni alloys with nominal compositions
of Ti 70Nb26Ni4,T i 66Nb26Ni8,T i 62Nb26Ni12,T i 58Nb26Ni16,
Ti54Nb26Ni20, and Ti50Nb26Ni24 (at.%) under a high vacuum
(3 · 10–3 Pa) using a magnetically controlled tungsten arc-
melting furnace in an argon atmosphere. All the ingots were
remelted at least four times to achieve chemical homogene-
ity. For microstructural studies, the surfaces of the alloys
were prepared using standard metallographic procedures,
consisting of grinding up to 2 000 grit with a SiC paper, pol-
ishing with a colloidal silica suspension, and then etching in
a mixture of 10 vol.% HF, 45 vol.% HNO 3, and 45 vol.%
H2O. The microstructures of the alloys were examined
using SEM. Using an operating voltage of 30 kV, the phase
of the alloys was characterized by means of XRD analyses
using Cu-K a radiation. The cross-sections of cylindrical
samples, 3.0 mm in diameter, were examined.
One cylindrical sample with a diameter of 3.0 mm and
height of *5.0 mm was prepared from the as-cast rods
and subjected to uniaxial compression testing at room tem-
perature using a SANS 1500 electronic universal testing
machine at a strain rate of 2.5 · 10–4 s–1. Three measure-
ments were performed for each specimen, and the results
were averaged, as shown in Table 1. To ensure the accuracy
of the measurement of Young’s modulus, a clip-on extens-
ometer attached to the specimen was used to measure the
strain. The Young’s modulus of each sample was deter-
mined as the slope of the linear portion of the stress–strain
curve [30]. After compression, the surfaces were investi-
gated by SEM using a Hitachi SU 1510 machine. The vol-
ume fraction of second phase ( VV) was estimated using the
ratio of the area of second phase to the area of the entire mi-
crostructure in SEM micrographs. The relative areas ( AA)
of second phase were calculated using the weight method.
The cut areas of second phase and entire microstructure
were weighed using an electronic balance; the weights were
labeled as Gs and GT, respectively. Using the equations
AA = Gs/GT and VV = AA [31, 32] and SEM micrographs,
the volume fraction of second phase was estimated.

The separation of Ti 2Ni and Ni 3Nb phases from b parent
phase can be analyzed from the heat of mixing. As the heat
of mixing ( DH) between Ti and Nb is 2 kJ · mol –1 [27], a
continuous solid solution is formed in Ti–Nb alloy solution.
An enhancement of DH indicates the increase in inter-
actions among the main atomic pairs [33]. As the DH of
Ti–Ni atomic pair (–35 kJ · mol –1) is larger than that of
Ni–Nb atomic pair (–30 kJ · mol –1) [27], the atomic interac-
tion forces in the Ti–Ni alloy system are much larger those
in the Ni–Nb alloy system under the same atomic ratios.
When Ni is partially replaced with Ti, the interaction force
of Ti–Ni atomic pairs can be strengthened more than
Ni–Nb atomic pairs in Ti–Ni–Nb atomic clusters by adding
a higher amount of Ti than Nb. During the cooling of
Ti–Nb–Ni alloy solution, the stable intermetallic Ti 2Ni
will produce the first nucleation and precipitation. For
Ti58Nb26Ni16,T i 54Nb26Ni20, and Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloys, with
the increase in Ni concentration, the interaction forces of
partial Ni–Nb atomic pairs can be strengthened, resulting
in the nucleation and precipitation of stable intermetallic
Ni3Nb.

3.4. Fracture morphology of alloys
Figure 5a shows the morphology of as-cast Ti 70Nb26Ni4 al-
loy after compression. The main shear plane and larger
macroscopic cracks were not observed in Fig. 5a. Through
the local magnification of fracture surface, smaller cracks
are shown to be distributed in the sample surface (Fig. 5b).
As these small cracks form and expand during compression,
the plastic deformation resistance of the material decreases
during the plastic deformation, indicating that the work-
hardening ability became weak. This is consistent with the
result of the true stress–strain curve shown in Fig. 2b. In ad-
dition, for Ti 66Nb26Ni8 alloy, a macroscopic crack was ob-
served on the outer surface of the specimen, which is not
completely broken as shown in Fig. 5c. When the crack
grows slowly, the specimen stress decreases. The result is
consistent with the stress decrease in the true stress–strain
curve shown in Fig. 2b. Microcracks and dimples (the la-
beled black arrow in Fig. 5d) were observed in Fig. 5d, in-
dicating that the fracture mechanism of Ti 66Nb26Ni8 alloy
is ductile failure. The main and second shear planes were
observed in Ti 62Nb26Ni12 alloy shown in Fig. 5e. By obser-
ving the slip displacement of the upper part in the main
shear plane, the distribution of multiorder slip planes is
shown in Fig. 5f. The width of each slip plane has a range
of 50 – 60 lm (the labeled black arrow in Fig. 5f); the value
is approximately equal to the displacement of stress de-
crease in the stress–strain curve shown in Fig. 6. Thus, the
slip of the shear plane decreases the stress in the stress–
strain curve.
As the fracture mechanisms of Ti 58Nb26Ni16,T i 54Nb26 ·
Ni20, and Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloys are almost brittle fracture,
Ti50Nb26Ni24 alloy with a large r0.2, low E, and high We
can be considered as a biomedical material. Next, the frac-
ture morphology of Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloy is analyzed. The
large flaws are shown in Fig. 7a. Smaller regions of ductile
fracture were observed near the flaws, indicating a smaller
plastic strain in Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloy; however, the larger re-
gions of cleavage fracture were observed in Fig. 7a, indicat-
ing that the fracture mechanism of Ti 50Nb26Ni24 alloy is
brittle failure, consistent with the smaller plastic strain in
the stress–strain curve shown in Fig. 2. Figure 7b shows
the cleavage step of different high cleavage planes. In ad-
dition, the quasi-cleavage and intergranular fractures are
also shown in Fig. 7c. Thus, the fracture mechanism of
Ti50Nb26Ni24 alloy is mostly cleavage with quasi-cleavage
and intergranular fractures.
4. Conclusions
The microstructure and mechanical properties of novel b-
type Ti74-xNb26Nix (x = 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 at.%) alloys
containing second phases, Ti 2Ni and Ni 3Nb, were investi-
gated. The main results are summarized as follows:
1. Ti 72Nb26Ni4 alloy has one phase: b;T i 66Nb26Ni8 and
Ti62Nb26Ni12 alloys have two phases: b and Ti2Ni. How-
ever, the Ti 74-xNb26Nix (x = 16, 20, and 24 at.%) alloys
have three phases: b,T i2Ni, and Ni 3Nb.
2. With the increase in Ni content, the relative volume
fraction of Ti 2Ni phase increased, and the stability of b
phase became weak. The molybdenum equivalent
values cannot indicate the stability of b phase in
Ti74-xNb26Nix alloys.
