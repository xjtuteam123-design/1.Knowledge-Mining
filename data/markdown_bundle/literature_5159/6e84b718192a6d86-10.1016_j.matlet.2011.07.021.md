A new alumina-forming austenitic stainless steel with greatly improved high-temperature oxidation
resistance and strength was developed via alloying 3.0 wt.% Al in the Fe-25Ni-18Cr based alloy. Continuous,
stable and exclusive alumina scale was formed in either dry air or air with 10% water vapor mixed
environment at 800 °C. The long-term high-temperature oxidation performance is appreciably enhanced
which is associated with the high density of the B2-NiAl precipitation phase maintaining the Al
2O3 surface
layer. Moreover, when tested at 750 °C in dry air environment, the new steel showed high yield and fracture
tensile strength of 310 –335 and 480 –500 MPa, respectively.

1. Introduction
Commercial austenitic stainless steels are primarily utilized as
construction materials for high-temperature components because of
their good high-temperature creep strength and oxidation resistance
at a relatively low cost. When the temperature is higher than ~ 600 –
650 °C in harsh environments with water vapor or other aggressive
species, the Cr 2O3-based scales would lose their protectiveness and
eventually cause failure of the steels [1]. Recently, Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) [2–9] initiated a program to develop creep-
resistant alumina-forming austenitic (AFA) stainless steels capable of
service in harsh environments at 600 –900 °C. High-temperature
ultraﬁne precipitation strengthened (HTUPS) family of austenitic
stainless steel [10] was used as a starting point for the alloy
development. Nevertheless, AFA alloy oxidation is strongly dependent
on composition, temperature, and aggressive species present (e.g.
H2O). Above 750 –800 °C, depending on composition, some AFA
compositions transition to internal attack of Al (severe oxidation)
after a few hundred to a few thousand hours [4].
It was argued that intermetallic B2-NiAl precipitates acted as
reservoir and supply Al to the growing alumina scale [3]. To enhance
long-term oxidation durability of these austenitic steels, volume
fraction of B2-NiAl has to be increased so that the Al 2O3 layer can be
maintained adequately. As such, it is interesting to further tune Ni
contents in the AFA alloys to properly increase the volume fraction of
B2-NiAl precipitates. In addition, it is believed that the concept for the
HTUPS family could also be applied in other types of austenitic
stainless steel systems [3,4]. Therefore, it is worthwhile developing
new AFA steels with improved properties based on different
commercial stainless steels.
2. Experimental procedure
In our study, the commercial NF709 steel (Fe-20Cr-25Ni, wt. %)
was selected as our base alloy. Table 1 lists nominal compositions of
the representative alloys which were prepared by arc-melting a
mixture of commercially-pure elements. The ingots were remelted
and then cast into a 15 × 15 × 100 mm copper mold. The as-cast bars
were homogenized at 1200 –1250 °C for 2 h, cold rolled 20 –30%, then
recrystallized at 1200 –1250 °C for about 5 –10 min to control grain
size (about 20 –30 μm), and subsequently water-quenched.
Oxidation specimens with dimensions of 14 × 10 × 2 mm were
polished to a 2000 grit surface ﬁnish using emery papers. Cyclic
oxidation tests were conducted at 800 °C in both dry air and air with
10% water vapor, with cycles of 500 or 100 h, respectively. Gauge-
length dimensions of tensile specimens are 20 × 3.0 × 1.0 mm and the
tensile loading direction was parallel to the rolling direction. Scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and electron probe micro-analysis
(EPMA) were used to characterize the oxide morphology and tensile
fracture surfaces. Before the SEM and EPMA observation, all the
oxidized samples were electrodeposited with a Ni layer on the surface
to keep the oxide layer intact.

Under our testing conditions, the oxide layer of the HTUPS4 alloy
(about 157 μm in grain size) started spalling off after around 1500 h of
oxidation, consistent with the results reported previously by Brady et
al. [4].
Fig. 2 shows cross-section of the alloys oxidized at 800 °C in air and
air with 10% water vapor, respectively. A denuded zone of needlelike
NiAl underneath the oxide layer was clearly observed, conﬁrming that
the NiAl precipitates are actually the Al reservoir for maintaining the
Al
2O3 surface layer. On the contrary, the original NF709 alloy formed a
much thicker Cr-rich oxide scale ( Fig. 2a), along with a little internal
oxidation. However, in air with 10% water vapor, the scales formed on
the modiﬁed alloys are complicated. For example, the scale formed on
NF709-1 (1.5% Al) after oxidation was Cr-rich oxides with a small
amount of subscale internal alumina and a large amount of internal
Cr2O3 (Fig. 2c). The oxide layer of the NF709-2 (2.0% Al) and NF709-3
(2.5% Al) alloys contains two parts: the outer layer is continuous
chromium oxide and the inner layer is internal oxidation zone formed
by internal alumina. Moreover, with the increase of the Al content
from 1.5 to 2.5%, the inner layer composition was transformed from
the chromium oxide to alumina and the volume fraction of the
internal alumina also gradually increased. A continuous and stable
oxide layer was formed in the modi ﬁed NF709 alloy with 3.0% Al
(Fig. 2f). EPMA mapping results shown in Fig. 3 conﬁrm that this oxide
layer is comprised of dense and continuous Al 2O3 scale. However,
either excessive addition of Al (e.g., 3.5% in NF709-5) or reduction of
Cr (e.g., 2.5% Al and 16% Cr in NF709-6) results in formation of an
outer layer consisting of Al 2O3 and Cr 2O3, together with an inner
layer of internal alumina (see Fig. 2 g and h), which leads to the
deterioration in the oxidation resistance. Such oxide scale structure
suggests that these two alloys fall at borderline for protective alumina
scale formation.
As mentioned earlier, increasing the Al content up to 3 wt.% Al
resulted in a transition from internal oxidation of Al to protective
alumina scale formation. However, addition of 3.5% Al in the alloy
seemingly broke the critical balance and led to formation of a mixture
of alumina and chromium oxides. Wagner [13] has clearly pointed out
that oxidized form would change from internal oxidation to outer
oxidation only when the volume fraction of internal oxidation reaches
a critical value. In our case, the oxidation process is dictated by the
competition between iron, nickel and chromium oxides as well as
alumina. Additions of less than 3.0% Al in the alloy cannot precipitate
enough B2-NiAl phases for providing aluminum to maintain contin-
uous Al2O3 layer on the surface, and the Cr2O3 formation on the surface
is thus dominant. Oxygen would penetrate the continuous Cr 2O3 layer
and causes occurrence of the internal oxidation of Al. It seems that
additions of 3.0% Al reached such a critical value which is suitable for
forming a continuous, stable Al2O3 layer, instead of the iron, nickel and

4. Conclusions
In summary, protective Al 2O3 scale in the Fe-25Ni-(16 –18)Cr
austenitic stainless steel was formed via adjusting the relative content
of Cr and Al, and exclusive Al
2O3 layer, together with high density of
the B2-NiAl precipitates, gave rise to the excellent oxidation
resistance at 800 °C in either air or air with 10% water vapor.
Moreover, the new AFA steel showed a high yield and fracture stress
at 750 °C, enabling it to be promising for industrial applications.
