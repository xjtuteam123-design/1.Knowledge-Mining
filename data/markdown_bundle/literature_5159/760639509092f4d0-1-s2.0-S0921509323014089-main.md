ABSTRACT 
Recently, Fe – Cr – Ni – Al – Ti ferritic alloys strengthened by L2
1
-type Ni
2
TiAl precipitates have demonstrated su -
perior high-temperature strength compared to conventional ferritic steels. However, it still shows a lower high- 
temperature strength than that of Ni-based superalloys. Therefore, in the current study, we designed new ferritic 
alloys to improve the high-temperature strength by introducing L2
1
-Co
2
TiAl precipitates, known as having better 
high-temperature properties than the Ni
2
TiAl structure. Specifically, the Ni element has been replaced with the 
Co element. In addition, the Ti element has been adjusted because the structural evolutions of precipitates, such 
as composition, lattice misfit and size rely on the Ti content. For instance, the addition of 2 wt% Ti forms a semi- 
coherent Co
2
TiAl precipitate with a relatively low density of misfit dislocations. More Ti addition up to 6 wt % 
leads to an increase in the lattice parameter of the precipitate, while that of the Fe matrix remains unchanged, 
which results in an increased lattice misfit. The increased lattice misfit causes a higher amount of misfit dislo -
cations, which leads to a reduction of the elastic strain field around the precipitates. It was found that the semi- 
coherent interface with a high level of the elastic strain field (2 wt % Ti), as compared to the precipitates with a 
high amount of misfit dislocations (4 and 6 wt % Ti), plays a crucial role in enhancing the mechanical properties 
at 973 K. In addition, the designed ferritic alloys show superior strength, compared to the previously-reported 
ferritic alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Steam turbines require high temperatures and pressures to increase 
thermal efficiency, which is closely related to creep resistance. The in -
crease of thermal efficiency in steam turbines leads to a reduction in fuel 
consumption and carbon dioxide emission [ 1 – 4 ]. Accordingly, extensive 
studies have been conducted to develop creep-resistant materials, 
Ni-based superalloys, and austenitic/ferritic steels [ 5 – 8 ]. Ferritic steels 
are more advantageous than Ni-based superalloys, due to their high 
thermal conductivity, low thermal expansion, and low cost [ 1 , 2 , 7 , 8 ]. 
However, it is still challenging for ferritic steels to be used as an alter -
native for Ni-based superalloys due to their microstructural instabilities 
at high-temperatures, such as coarsening behavior and formation of 
unexpected phases during long-term exposure to high-temperatures 
[ 9 – 11 ]. 
Recently, many studies have been carried out to develop new stra -
tegies for development of ferritic alloys with improved mechanical 
properties at elevated temperatures. For example, it has been reported 
that Fe – Cr – Ni – Al – Ti ferritic alloys strengthened by coherent pre -
cipitates (B2 – NiAl and/or L2
1
-Ni
2
TiAl) exhibit excellent creep proper -
ties than conventional ferritic alloys [ 12 – 14 ]. It was emphasized that the 
coherent interface and lattice misfit between the precipitate and matrix 
play a crucial role in the enhanced creep resistance. Better creep resis -
tance of ferritic alloys was obtained reinforced by the introduction of

2. Experimental 
The alloy ingots with a nominal composition of Fe – 10Cr – 10Co- 
6.5Al-xTi-3.4Mo-0.25Zr-0.005B (x = 0, 2, 4 and 6 wt %) were prepared 
by arc melting with high purity elements (99.95 wt% elemental purity) 
under a Ti-gettered high purity argon atmosphere (99.999 %). Here -
after, with the Ti contents, the alloys are denoted as Co0Ti, Co2Ti, 
Co4Ti, and Co6Ti, respectively. The ingots were re-melted more than 10 
times for homogeneity and solidified on a water-cooled copper hearth. 
The as-cast rod samples were produced by suction casting into a cylin -
drical copper mold with 3 mm in diameter and 50 mm in length. The 
nominal composition of the fabricated specimens is listed in Table 1 . 
To remove the elemental segregation during the casting process, 
homogenization treatment was carried out at 1,473K for 0.5 h, and air 
cooling until room temperature. In addition, aging treatment for the 
precipitation was carried out at 1073 K for 5h and air-cooled until room 
temperature. To identify the microstructure, mechanical polishing for 
developed alloys was conducted. Specifically, the SiC papers up to 2000 
grit were used for grinding, and then the buffing process was carried out 
with 1 and 0.3 μ m alumina suspension. Finally, vibration polishing was 
performed for 24 h with 0.02 μ m colloidal silica suspension. The 
microstructure of the polished specimens was characterized via 
Scanning-Electron-Microscopy (SEM), using a TESCAN Mira LMH 
equipped with ZEISS YAG backscattered electron (BSE) detector and 
BRUKER X-Flash4010 energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) de -
tector. The crystal structures of the alloys at room temperature and 973 
K were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using BRUKER AXS D8 
ADVANCE equipped with the heating system under Cu-K α radiation ( λ 
= 1.54187 Å). Transmission-electron-microscopy (TEM) was done for 
detailed microstructure inspection and phase identification using TEC -
NAI F20 G2 TEM equipped with energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) under 200 kV accelerating voltage. TEM samples were prepared

4.3. Lattice misfit between matrix and L2

It is well known that the structural changes of precipitates, such as 
morphological and spatial features are largely influenced by the inter -
face and strain energies at the matrix-precipitate interface [ 35 , 36 ]. For 
example, in the low lattice misfit, interfacial energy is dominant, and its 
equilibrium morphology is spherical [ 37 , 38 ]. An increase in lattice 
misfit gives rise to a high level of misfit strain field around the pre -
cipitates, which leads to the distance-dependent elastic interaction be -
tween precipitates [ 39 , 40 ], thereby exhibiting the preferential 
alignment of precipitates (the stable shape is cuboidal type). When the 
misfit strain is excessive over the critical level, the misfit dislocations are

around precipitates could play a considerable role in the strengthening

between the precipitates and mobile dislocations [ 12 , 41 ], thereby 
reducing the climb rate of the dislocations and increasing creep prop -

formation of interfacial dislocations may allow pipe diffusion, leading to 
the insufficient strengthening effect [ 41 ]. Thus, once the level of misfit

Co4Ti and Co6Ti alloys have a higher volume fraction of L2
