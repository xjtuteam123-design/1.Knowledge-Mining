ABSTRACT 
Strain-age cracking is known to occur in welded and additively manufactured Ni-base superalloys with medium 
to high γ’ phase fractions. In this work, a new model is developed for predicting the risk of such cracking, based 
on thermodynamic calculations. The model is compared to existing criteria relying on bulk alloy composition and 
other more advanced approaches. Relevant shortcomings of existing criteria and models are revealed and dis -
cussed. It is shown that the present model is able to describe the cracking tendency for a wide range of Ni-base 
superalloys. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys can currently be considered the state-of-the art in 
demanding high-temperature applications, such as turbines for jet pro -
pulsion or power generation [1] . The remarkable creep-resistant prop -
erties of many Ni-base superalloys stem from coarsening-resistant 
strengthening precipitates of an ordered face-centered cubic (FCC) L12 
phase ( γ’ ) embedded in a disordered FCC matrix ( γ ). However, the same 
γ’ precipitates can cause issues regarding the manufacturability of 
components using welding or additive manufacturing (AM) with regard 
to solid state cracking phenomena, most prominently strain-age 
cracking (SAC). It has been shown that the risk for SAC correlates 
with the volume fraction of precipitated γ’ , which is usually summarized 
in a “ weldability ” chart of the type shown in Fig. 1 [2 – 4] , where Al and 
Ti are assumed to increase the γ’ volume fraction. 
Similarly, to Fig. 1 , Tang et al. [5] used a criterion based on the 
overall Al, Ti, Nb and Ta contents to estimate the sensitivity to SAC. 
Their criterion can be summarized as:

for the most demanding conditions (highest temperatures) are very 
difficult to print, owing to their very high respective γ’ volume fractions 
( > 0.5). 
In general, a “ weldability ” chart of the type shown in Fig. 1 or the 
criterion formulated in Eq. 1.1 does not discriminate between cracking 
mechanisms, of which there are plenty, as it only considers the base 
composition of the alloy. For example, solidification cracking [6] and 
liquation cracking [7] may occur irrespective of the sensitivity to SAC. It 
is therefore important to elucidate the key factors influencing each 
cracking mechanism. 
One such approach was presented by Yu et al. [8] , where the risk for 
SAC in a given alloy was attributed to a rapidly increasing γ’ volume 
fraction with decreasing temperature, combined with a narrow ho -
mogenization interval (sub-solidus temperature range without thermo -
dynamically stable γ’ precipitates). Their approach relies on performing 
thermodynamic calculations to quantify these homogenization and 
precipitation temperature ranges in terms of a derivative. The approach 
seems promising at first glance; however, several major issues limit its 
applicability. 
First and foremost, it has been shown by, e.g., Zhou et al. [9] that 
crack-free alloys with > 0.55 γ’ volume fraction can be designed by 
considering the coherency stresses, quantified in terms of the lattice 
misfit parameter:

Secondly, Yu et al. [8] claim to have verified their SAC risk model by 
performing experiments on quaternary Ni-6Al-2Ti-X alloys, X being the 
fourth element (Co, Cr, W, Mo or Re), by correlating their calculated risk 
factors with actual crack lengths. However, as shown by their own 
evaluation of the SAC risk by the same criterion used by Tang et al. [5] , 
the elements they selected to vary are not strongly related to the for -
mation of γ’ , which is evidenced by all of the alloys having the same 
value using that criterion ( Fig. 3 d in Ref. [8] ). In fact, since no direct 
evidence of SAC was presented, it is likely that their observed cracks 
(which were not shown in the paper) constitute solidification or liqua -
tion cracks, the tendency of which was also evaluated. 
It is therefore clear that a comprehensive criterion for evaluating the 
SAC risk cannot rely on alloy composition alone and further must 
include i) an evaluation of the γ’ volume fraction at relevant tempera -
tures for each alloy and ii) the composition and temperature dependent 
lattice misfit between the γ’ precipitates and the γ matrix. Quantifying 
such properties of an alloy is readily done using thermodynamic cal -
culations based on the Calphad method [10] , provided that one has 
access to a suitable software suite and thermodynamic database. 
2. Method 
In this work, all thermodynamic calculations are performed using the 
Thermo-Calc Software package [11] version 2024a in combination with 
the critically assessed TCNI12 database [12] . Using these, it is possible 
to calculate composition and temperature dependent thermodynamic 
properties of relevance to this work, such as molar volumes and volume 
fractions of individual phases. 
2.1. Constructing the SAC risk factor 
As described in the introduction, it is crucial to take into account the 
temperature and composition dependent properties of the γ and γ’ 
phases in the alloy, such as individual phase composition, volume 
fraction and resulting misfit in order to confidently describe the SAC 
risk. Thus, a proper formulation of a SAC risk criterion should contain 
these quantities, evaluated at relevant temperatures. With relevant 
temperatures, it is here meant that the thermal history of the alloy 
should somehow be considered.

increase with γ’ volume fraction and its associated misfit, the two can be 
added or multiplied to produce a factor able to describe the SAC ten -

2.2. Determining the temperature limits for integration 
Having established the relationship between relevant thermody -
namic quantities and the risk for SAC ( Eq. 2.2 ) it is necessary to consider 
the calculation of the low and high temperature limits. As the high limit 
corresponds to the γ’ solvus, it is possible to use the existing Solvus for 
Ordered Phase property model, available in Thermo-Calc as of version 
2023b [13] . This property model has been developed to quickly, accu -
rately and efficiently search for the solvus of an ordered configuration of 
a given phase instead of, e.g., manually searching for the solvus or 
evaluating the full phase diagram of the given alloy within a pre-defined 
range, which may or may not encompass the γ’ solvus. The model will 
find the solvus by numerically searching for the temperature at which 
the volume fraction of the ordered phase, in this case γ’ , reaches zero, 
truncated at the second decimal (0.01 K accuracy). 
The lower temperature limit requires calculation of the lattice misfit 
δ as a function of temperature. This can be done in a similar fashion as 
searching for the γ’ solvus, i.e., numerically searching for the sign 
change of δ , starting from the γ’ solvus temperature and by decreasing 
the temperature. However, this approach is only feasible if δ is actually 
negative in some temperature range, for the alloy in question, see Fig. 2 . 
Therefore, it is necessary to set a minimum allowed low temperature 
limit, i.e., a stop criterion for the search, which is somewhat arbitrary 
but should be selected based on the expected lowest temperature at 
which any diffusive process actually would occur and/or which the 
material will actually be exposed to for long enough time to be signifi -
cant. During the course of this work, it was discovered that the value of

2.4. Use of the proposed model for strong deviations from equilibrium 
In the case of AM and welding, it is indeed possible that the melting 
and remelting of the material could cause segregation. If strong segre -
gation is expected, it is the segregated composition profile that should be 
studied using the SAC criterion here, not the bulk alloy composition. 
This can be done using Scheil solidification simulations, where for AM of 
Ni-base superalloys it is particularly useful to also include the effect of 
solute trapping [14] .

It is interesting to note that CM247LC, which is known to be noto -

susceptible using the SAC risk factor developed in this work, in agree -

based-criteria predict these to be very difficult to weld. In fact, these two 
alloys were developed by Zhou et al. [9] with SAC resistance in mind, by 
keeping the lattice misfit as close to zero as possible, while still main -
taining a high γ’ volume fraction. A similar comparison can be made for 
the recently introduced GammaPrint 1100 alloy [15] , which is predicted 
to be rather susceptible to cracking using the chart-based criteria but 
where the R

SAC. This is consistent with the fact that the alloy was developed spe -
cifically to be resistant to crack formation when produced by 
powder-bed based AM. 
Regarding alloys ABD-850AM and ABD-900AM, the comparison 
shows similar crack risks using either criterion. This can be explained by 
the fact that Tang et al. imposed their crack risk criterion for designing 
the alloys, which, since it is based on overall composition, will naturally 
enforce lower γ’ volume fractions, as evidenced by Fig. 1 d in Ref. [5] , 
where a maximum mole fraction of ∼ 0 . 3 is allowed. Such a restriction 
has a detrimental effect on the mechanical properties compared with 
alloys with a much higher γ’ volume fraction. 
In order to enable a more visual comparison of the SAC risk factors, 
the values can be normalized to the highest value (in this case the value 
for CMSX4) while also making sure that low or no SAC risk is denoted by 
negative values. This can be done for the weldability index by sub -
tracting the zero-level value (in this case 30) from the current value for 
the given alloy and dividing by the value for CMSX4 (from which 30 is 
also subtracted). The same can be performed for the criterion by Tang 
et al., but subtracting the limiting value of 4 instead. The different 
criteria are thus rescaled to the same scale and can be plotted in for 
example a bar chart, as in Fig. 3 . 
It is thus clear that the here developed SAC risk factor is able to 
predict the overall sensitivity of an alloy to this specific cracking 
mechanism and thus constitutes a valuable alloy design tool for Ni-base 
superalloys. The strength of the here selected approach lies in its gen -
erality, simplicity and computational efficiency, without the need to 
account for time consuming precipitation kinetics simulations. The 
study presented here also clearly demonstrates the necessity of consid -
ering the specific cracking mechanisms and not just relying on the 
overall composition of an alloy. However, the accuracy of predictions is 
naturally and intimately tied with the accuracy and generality of the 
used thermodynamic database. Therefore, this study also demonstrates 
the excellent capabilities of the TCNI12 database in terms of thermo -
dynamic and volumetric phase properties. 
However, for a full picture of the cracking tendency of a given alloy, 
it is necessary to consider the remaining cracking phenomena as well, e. 
g., solidification or liquation cracking and possibly ductility-dip 
cracking, as improvement in one of the criteria may result in detri -
mental effects on other criteria. 
4. Conclusions 
• The overall alloy composition, expressed in a weldability chart, 
cannot be used as a criterion to directly distinguish between different 
cracking mechanisms or even to predict the tendency for SAC. 
• An efficient and generally applicable SAC risk factor for Ni-base al -
loys has been successfully developed in this work, based on consid -
ering the main factors for SAC from a thermodynamic perspective.
