ABSTRACT
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are highly valued for their excellent high-temperature properties, and 
the enhancement of their room-temperature strength-plasticity matching and oxidation resistance is crucial for 
their processing and applications. In this study, the focus was on investigating the changes in the room- 
temperature mechanical properties and oxidation resistance of RHEAs when Cu was added to Ti

dispersed granular (Cu, Zr)-rich precipitation phases in RHEAs. These dispersed precipitated phases increase the 
tensile yield strength from ~686 MPa to ~870 MPa through a precipitation strengthening mechanism, and their 
interaction with dislocations during plastic deformation increases the uniform elongation from ~1.9 % to ~8 %, 
which in turn maintains the elongation at fracture at ~16 %. In addition, the addition of Cu element also refines

which in turn reduces the area of the oxide layer, and significantly enhances the oxidation resistance of RHEAs at

room temperature and enhancing their oxidation resistance.
1. Introduction
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted consid -
erable attention from metallurgists due to their flexible compositional 
design strategies and excellent properties [1 – 3] . Among them, re -
fractory HEAs (RHEAs) comprising refractory metals (W, Mo, Nb, Ta, Ti, 
etc.) have attracted significant interest due to their excellent 
high-temperature properties and biocompatibility [4 – 7] and show po -
tential applications in aerospace, medical-biological and other fields. 
However, the limited plastic deformability of RHEAs at room tempera -
ture has seriously hindered their applications [8 – 10] . For instance, 
TiZrNbTaMo RHEAs have excellent corrosion resistance in physiological 
environments, with a compressive strain of only ~6 % at room tem -
perature [11] . Subsequently, researchers obtained RHEAs systems with 
room-temperature tensile ductility through strategies such as reducing 
the content of inherently brittle elements and lowering the valence 
electron concentration (VEC) of the alloys [12 – 14] , whereas the lower 
strength of this type of RHEAs at room temperature hinders their prac -
tical applications. For example, TiZrHfNb RHEAs have a 
room-temperature tensile ductility of 22.4 %, while their tensile 
strength is only 720 MPa [15] .
In addition, RHEAs usually have poor oxidation resistance and un -
dergo a lot of substantial scale spallation or even complete oxidation at 
elevated temperatures [16,17] , which significantly impacts the prepa -
ration of such alloys for the thermal processing of large-size compo -
nents. For example, Shikh et al. [18] found that ductile

Achieving a good match between room temperature mechanical prop -

Copper alloying has been playing an important role in the compo -
sition of HEAs with Co, Cr, Fe, Ni and other elements [24 – 26] . One 
reason for this is the large positive mixing enthalpy between Cu and 
other elements, which leads to Cu segregating into interdendritic (ID)

that do not result in a loss of ductility, and the precipitation strength -
ening effect also enhances the strength [28,29] . For example, Hu et al. 
[30] added Cu to CrFeCoNi HEA not only increased their yield strength 
from 145 MPa to 310 MPa, but also had a high elongation at break of 
34 %. Meanwhile, a study by Lin et al. [31] found that the addition of Cu 
to Fe-Ni-Cr-Al and Ni-Cr-Al based alloys can reduce oxidation mass gain, 
significantly reduce the thickness of the oxide layer and enhance the 
oxidation resistance of the alloys. However, the effect of Cu alloying on 
the mechanical properties and oxidation resistance of RHEAs remains 
unknown. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the impact of Cu 
content on the mechanical properties and oxidation resistance of RHEAs, 
with a view to informing their composition design and application.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructural investigation
To investigate the effect of Cu content on the phase constituent of 
RHEAs, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization of Cu0, Cu1 and 
Cu2 alloys were performed, and the results are shown in Fig. 1 . It can be 
seen that all three RHEAs exhibit a body-centered cubic structure (BCC) 
dominated phase structure, and (Cu, Zr)-rich diffraction peaks were also 
found at ~37
◦
and ~41
◦
for Cu2 alloys, indicating that the introduction 
of Cu into RHEAs changes the alloys from a single-phase BCC structure 
to a dual-phase structure with BCC and (Cu, Zr)-rich phases.
In order to analyze the effect of the Cu element on the microstructure 
of RHEAs, backscattered electron (BSE) characterization of Co0, Co1 
and Cu2 was conducted, and the results are presented in Fig. 2 . From the 
BSE maps of the three alloys, it is seen that the RHEAs with different Cu 
contents exhibit typical dendritic crystal microstructures, consisting of 
bright dendritic (DR) regions and dark interdendritic (ID) regions, which 
indicate that the DR regions are enriched with elements with higher 
atomic numbers. Meanwhile, observation of the ID regions of the three 
alloys reveals that no precipitated phase is found in the ID region of Cu0 
alloy, while the ID region of Cu1 alloy contains a dispersed granular 
second phase (shown by the dashed yellow box), and the ID region of 
Cu2 alloy contains a reticulately distributed second phase (shown by the 
red arrows), indicating that the introduction of the element Cu to the 
RHEAs leads to the formation of a second phase in agreement with the 
XRD results, and the content of the second phase increases significantly 
with the increase of Cu content.
From the elemental distributions in Fig. 2 , it can be seen that all three 
alloys are enriched with high melting point Ta and Nb elements in the 
DR region, and low melting point Zr and Ti elements in the ID region. 
This phenomenon is the result of elemental segregation in the non- 
equilibrium solidification process, in which the high melting point Ta 
and Nb elements are preferentially solidified to form the DR zone, 
prompting the low melting point Zr and Ti to aggregate at the solid- 
liquid interface front, and finally solidify to form the ID zone. It is also 
observed that the precipitation phases in Cu1 and Cu2 alloys are mainly 
enriched with Cu and Zr elements, which indicates that the addition of 
Cu contributes to the formation of (Cu, Zr)-rich precipitation phases. 
This is mainly due to the fact that the low melting point Cu during

3.2. Mechanical properties
In order to investigate the effect of Cu content on the mechanical 
properties of RHEAs, tensile tests were conducted on three RHEAs with 
different Cu contents at room temperature, and the results are shown in 
Fig. 4 (a). From the figure, it can be seen that the addition of Cu element 
to RHEAs can significantly increase the strength, but also reduce the 
fracture elongation of the alloy. Specifically, when the Cu content is 
increased from 0 to 1 at%, the yield strength displays a notable increase 
from ~686 MPa to ~870 MPa, which is an increase of 27 %, while the 
elongation at break experiences a decrease from ~24 % to ~16 %. 
Further elevating the Cu content to 2 at% results in a sustained increase 
in yield strength to ~893 MPa, but the elongation at break exhibits a 
substantial deterioration to ~3 %. It was found that RHEAs with 1 at% 
Cu additions had satisfactory elongation at break while maintaining a 
fairly high strength, resulting in an optimal balance between strength 
and ductility. The true stress-strain curves as well as the work-hardening 
curves of the Cu0 and Cu1 alloys were further calculated and the results 
are plotted in Fig. 4 (b), where the engineer strain corresponding to the 
intersection of the work-hardening rate curve and the true stress-strain 
curve indicates the uniform elongation of the alloy. From the data in 
Fig. 4 (b), it can be seen that the work-hardening ability of RHEAs is 
significantly enhanced by the addition of Cu, and the uniform elongation 
of the alloy is increased from 1.9 % (Cu0) to 8 % (Cu1), which indicates 
that the addition of Cu increases the uniform elongation of RHEAs by 3.2 
times.

The yield strength and uniform elongation of the Cu0 and Cu1 alloys 
were compared with those of other representative cast RHEAs (shown in 
Fig. 5 ) [15,39 – 47] . It was established that the yield strength and uni -

increased from 0 to 1 at%. Extensive studies have shown that the 
deformation mechanism of BCC HEAs is primarily based on dynamic slip 
bands, with the absence of twinning, stacking faults, and other mecha -
nisms [48] . In single-phase BCC HEAs, many planar slips bands form 
during plastic deformation, where dislocations glide within narrow 
deformation bands. Since dislocations within the same deformation 
band typically share the same slip system, they are difficult to tangle 
with each other, leading to severe localized deformation. This results in 
low work hardening and reduced uniform deformation capability [49, 
50] . However, when the alloy contains finely dispersed precipitates, the 
movement of dislocations within the deformation bands is hindered, 
causing dislocations to escape from the bands and distribute randomly 
around the precipitates. These randomly distributed dislocations 
interact and tangle around the precipitates, significantly enhancing 
work hardening and improving uniform deformation capability [51,52] .

In order to investigate the effect of Cu content on the fracture pattern 
of RHEAs, the tensile fracture morphology of the three alloys was 
characterized using SEM technique and the results are shown in Fig. 6 . It 
can be seen that the fracture surface of the Cu0 alloy is mainly covered 
by dimples and plastic tear ridges, performing a typical ductile fracture 
pattern. The Cu1 alloy contains a number of riverine patterns and voids 
in addition to ductile dimples, exhibiting a mixed ductile and brittle 
mixed fracture pattern. The voids are mainly formed in the (Cu, Zr)-rich 
areas where the stress is concentrated, indicating that the (Cu, Zr)-rich 
particles only cause the formation of voids but not crack extension, 
which results in the good elongation of Cu1 alloy. The fracture surface of 
the Cu2 alloy is mainly composed of riverine patterns and reticulated 
cracks, which shows a brittle fracture pattern, and the reticulated cracks 
indicate that the deformation process cracks are extended by the retic -

tion in fracture elongation shown by the tensile stress-strain curves.

In order to investigate the effect of Cu content on the thickness of the 
oxide layer of RHEAs, we observed the cross-sectional morphology of 
the oxidized samples using the BSE technique, and the results are shown 
in Fig. 9 . The oxide layers of all three RHEAs are sealed layer structure, 
and the surface oxide layer is characterized by relative density and fewer 
cracks, while the internal oxide layer exhibits more cracks. This in -
dicates that the surface oxide layer is unable to prevent the further 
diffusion of oxygen into the interior of the alloys. The thickness of the 
oxide layer is statistically obtained to be ~73 μ m, ~68 μ m, and ~63 μ m 
for Cu0, Cu1, and Cu2 alloys, respectively, indicating that the addition 
of Cu element to RHEAs can effectively reduce the thickness of the oxide 
layer. Meanwhile, it can also be observed that there are more cracks in 
the oxide layer of Cu0 alloy, while the number of cracks in the oxide 
layer of Cu1 and Cu2 alloys is significantly reduced, which is consistent 
with the observation in Fig. 7 , and further confirms that the increase of 
Cu element can enhance the densification of the oxide layer of RHEAs. In 
addition, we also characterized the elemental distribution of the oxide 
layer. Significant Cu element enrichment was observed in the surface 
oxide layers of Cu1 and Cu2 alloys compared to those of Cu0 alloys. This 
finding suggests that the addition of Cu elements contributed to the 
formation of a dense CuO oxide layer in the surface oxide layer of 
RHEAs, which is consistent with the XRD results. Observing the 
elemental distribution characteristics of RHEAs, we can find that the 
internal oxidation layer shows an obvious bias aggregation of O ele -
ments. The content is significantly higher in the region between the DR 
arms rich in Zr and Ti elements, and lower in the DR regions rich in Nb 
and Ta, indicating that oxygen preferentially reacts with Zr and Ti ele -
ments between the DR arms during the internal oxidation of RHEAs.
