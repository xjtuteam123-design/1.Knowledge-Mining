High temperature properties of Cu–Cr–Zr
alloys processed by conventional inverse
extrusion of atomised powders and
comparison with Conform consolidated
powders
C. A. Poblano-Salas *
Water atomised Cu–Cr–Zr alloy powders were consolidated by inverse warm extrusion and by the
commercial continuous rotary extrusion method, Conform. Those alloys consolidated by inverse
warm extrusion exhibited enhanced mechanical properties compared with their respective
Conform extruded counterparts, when tested at both room and elevated temperatures. The
processing parameters adopted in the inverse extrusion experiments resulted in products which
retained enough amounts of solutes in solid solution, which in turn, led to improved mechanical
properties after aging. Conversely, the excessive adiabatic heat generated in the Conform
machine eliminated the saturation effect produced by rapid solidification, negating any possible
further improvement on the mechanical properties by aging. The mechanical properties of an
inverse extruded Cu–2.8Cr–0.39Zr (at.-%) alloy at temperatures above 450 uC were higher than
those strengths reported for Cu–Be alloys and comparable to that of Cu–Ta and Cu–Nb
composites. Therefore, rapidly solidified Cu–Cr–Zr alloys can be possible candidates for
replacing such alloy systems for high temperature applications.
Keywords: Conform, Cu–Cr–Zr, High temperature, Inverse extrusion, Rapid solidification
Introduction
Different copper alloys have been employed since the
1950s in the fabrication of components requiring high
thermal and electrical conductivity. As pure copper does
not exhibit acceptable levels of strength, different alloying
elements such as Zn, Ag, Be, Co and Mg have been used
to improve its mechanical properties through solid solu-
tion strengthening. Unfortunately, additions of soluble
elements in copper have a deleterious effect on the
conductivity of the base metal due to the lattice distortion
and the charge scattering associated with the foreign
atoms within the copper structure.
1 For this reason,
elements with low solubility at room temperature, but
with the possibility of inducing high levels of strength are
attractive in this context; Cr and Zr fulﬁl these require-
ments. Both elements have a low solubility in copper at
room temperature, but ﬁnite solubility at the respective
eutectic temperature. Hence, they have been widely
exploited as alloying elements for high conductivity–high
strength applications, since the sloping solvus lines are
ideally suited for age hardening and their reduced
solubility at low temperature leads to improved electrical
conductivity. Moreover, through the use of rapid
solidiﬁcation (RS) and aging heat treatments, it is
possible to increase the density of ﬁnely distributed
precipitates that are responsible for the alloy strength.
2
Different RS processes such as chill block melt
spinning and atomisation have been employed to
produce Cu–Cr, Cu–Zr and Cu–Cr–Zr alloys. 2–4
Although the alloys produced by melt spinning show
higher solute supersaturations, and consequently result
in higher hardness and strength after aging than their
ﬂuid atomised counterparts, alloys processed by atomi-
sation processes are preferred for the following reasons:
(i) atomisation is cheaper for bulk production on an
industrial scale
(ii) no intermediate steps before consolidation, such
as chopping in the case of ribbons, are required
(iii) it is feasible to produce powders with morphol-
ogies resulting in products with higher compres-
sibility and ‘green’ density than those produced
from melt spun ribbons.
Sintering is traditionally employed after compaction in
conventional powder metallurgy processing. However,

when RS alloys are considered, sintering is not an
option, as the sintering temperature for a pressed
powder alloy is close to its melting temperature, which
inevitably eliminates the beneﬁts of rapid solidiﬁcation.
Hence, extrusion of powders provides an attractive
option, as a ﬁnal product with full densiﬁcation can be
produced at relatively moderate temperatures.
In RS copper alloys, a supersaturated solid solution is
required after extrusion in order to provide strengthen-
ing in the ﬁnal product on aging, and also to recover the
maximum possible electrical conductivity. Hence, good
control of the extrusion temperature is crucial when
processing RS alloys. Inverse extrusion, in this context,
is attractive as little friction between the container and
the preform exists, thus reducing the maximum heat
generated during this process as compared with conven-
tional forward methods. Both forward and inverse
extrusion methods have been previously employed for
the processing of RS Cu–Cr and Cu–Cr–Zr alloys.
5,6 In
Cu–Cr–Zr alloys of similar composition, the forward
extrusion process resulted in samples with slightly better
mechanical strength than inverse extruded samples.
However, both methods are discontinuous and the
maximum size of the extruded bar is limited by the
press size, the dimensions of the extrusion rig, and the
amount of powder processed in each batch. In this
context, the continuous rotary forming extrusion
process (Conform) represents an attractive alternative
for processing RS Cu–Cr–Zr alloys as it can produce
continuously extra long solid shapes of various geome-
tries from different feedstocks, including metallic
powders. The process has been employed for the
processing of RS aluminium alloy powders,
7 although
some investigations on granular dispersion hardened Cu
alloys and recycling of bulk Cu–P alloys have also been
performed.8 However, no processing of RS Cu–Cr–Zr
alloys employing this method has been reported.
The mechanical properties of RS Cu–Cr–Zr alloys
consolidated in previous investigations either by direct
or inverse extrusion were higher than those of con-
ventionally processed alloys.
5,6 However, no character-
isation of mechanical properties at elevated temperature
was performed. Although such alloys showed increased
mechanical properties as compared with Cu–Cr–Zr
alloys processed by conventional methods, the mechan-
ical properties at room temperature were inferior to
those of both Cu–Be alloys and Cu–Nb (Ta) composites,
which typically exhibit strengths in the range of 1300–
1400 MPa.
9,10

Production of powders
Five Cu–Cr–Zr alloy powders were fabricated from ﬁve
different master alloys, which in turn were produced by
vacuum induction melting. A 5 kg laboratory water
atomiser (Atomising Systems Ltd, RD 106) was
employed to produce the powders. The atomisation
conditions and procedure are described elsewhere.

Mechanical properties
The aging response of alloys 1, 3 and 4, consolidated by
inverse extrusion, at 450 uC for different times is shown
in Fig. 4; data from alloy T1 consolidated by Conform
are also included. The largest peak hardness measured in
the extruded alloys (295 VHN) was found in the sample
based on powder 3 aged for 1.5 h. This hardness was
slightly higher than the level reported by Correia et al.
12
for a Cu–2.48Cr–0.32Zr (at.-%) alloy processed by
vacuum canning direct extrusion (290 VHN) and also
higher than the peak value of 260 VHN obtained by
Hendrichs et al.
6 in a Cu–1.95Cr–0.13Zr (at.-%) alloy
processed by inverse extrusion. The maximum hardness
measured in samples based on powders 1 and 4 were 174
and 269 VHN respectively. As both alloys processed by
Conform showed similar response to aging, only results
form alloy T1 are included in Fig. 4. Powder T1 showed
inferior microhardness in the ‘as conformed’ condition

maximum hardness of 153 VHN was achieved on aging,
that value being similar to the hardness measured in the

The electrical conductivity measured on inverse

Microstructure
The microstructure of Cu–2.8Cr–0.39Zr (at.-%) alloy in
the as extruded condition, consolidated by inverse
extrusion, is shown in Fig. 5 a. The material had a ﬁne
equiaxed subgrain structure and no evidence of ﬁne
precipitation within the subgrains was found at this
stage. A subgrain size of 197 nm was measured by image
analysis from a total of 130 cells. The longitudinal
structure of the alloy did not show evidence of ﬁne
precipitation either, although in some regions ﬁne
particles were observed within the elongated grains
(Fig. 5 b). By employing the nanoprobe mode in the
TEM microscope EDS analyses were performed on
these particles, the results suggesting they were formed
predominantly by Cu and Zr.
The microstructure of Cu–2.8Cr–0.39Zr (at.-%) alloy
in the peak hardness condition, i.e. aged at 450 uC for
1.5 h, was also studied by TEM; a BF micrograph from
a transverse section is shown in Fig. 6. The cell size of
the alloy was 178 nm, being 18 nm smaller than that
reported in as extruded samples. Such a difference with
respect to the as extruded alloy could be attributed to
the presence of subgrains of smaller size in the structure
present as a result of the aging heat treatment (point
‘A’). Figure 6 also shows the presence of intense black
dot type precipitation resulting from the decomposition
of the supersaturated solid solution due to aging. An
approximate size distribution of these ﬁne precipitates
was calculated by measuring the strain ﬁeld they
produced in the material. This ﬁeld is represented by
the black contrast zone surrounding each precipitate
observed under a double beam condition. A mean size of
4.2 nm was calculated from a total of 200 particles
measured.

Discussion
Properties of powders
Table 1 indicated that substantial proportions of alloy-
ing elements were lost during preparation of the
powders by water atomisation. These losses could be
attributed to oxidation of both Cr and Zr, and
particularly the latter, during induction melting in air
and also by further oxidation by reaction with water in
the atomiser itself. Evidence of internal Cr
2O3 particles
was found in consolidated samples in the as extruded
condition, suggesting that they were incorporated into
the alloy during air melting. Sarin and Grant
15 found
ZrO2 decorating the grain boundaries in a gas atomised
Cu–0.32Cr–0.10Zr (wt-%) alloy. However, no evidence
of such a compound was found in the present study
possibly because it was preferentially incorporated into
the slag during melting.
As shown in Fig. 3, the maximum solute solubility
extension for the powders was observed for the ﬁnest
size fractions ( 245 mm). This is expected because the
solubility extension during atomisation is a function of
the solidiﬁcation front velocity in the melt, which, in
turn, is a function of undercooling, and in general of the
cooling rate. Maximum solubility extensions of 1.38 and
1.5 at.-%[Cr zZr] were calculated for powders 2 and 5
respectively, somewhat smaller than those reported for
Cu–Cr–Zr alloys produced by low pressure water
atomisation (LPWA) and close coupled gas atomisation
(CCGA) in previous investigations.
5,6
The morphology of all Cu–Cr–Zr powder batches was
characteristic of that commonly reported for water
atomised copper alloys.
16 However, the difference in
particle shapes observed in powder 4 compared with the
other batches, especially for the z45/263 and 245 mm
size fractions, resulted in different properties from those
of the other batches. Powder 4 had a larger median
particle size which, unexpectedly, led to a 4–7% lower
apparent density of the powder than the rest of the other
batches (Table 4). The decrease can be explained by a
greater roughness of the z45/263 and 245 mm particles
in this batch, which increased the frictional area and
resulted in a decrease in the packing efﬁciency of the
particles.
The microstructure of the Cu–Cr–Zr powder batches
was mainly characterised by equiaxed cells having mean
sizes in the range 2–5 mm, as has been reported for Cu–
Cr, Cu–Cr and Cu–Cr–Zr atomised alloys in previous
investigations.
3,17–19 An increased cell size was measured
as the powder fraction size increased, consistent with
lower cooling rates prevailing for coarser powder
fractions. A second phase was observed in the micro-
structure of particles of all the size fractions studied
(z63/275, z45/263 and 245 mm) except for powder 1.

Samples processed by Conform
The microstructure of alloy T1, studied by TEM,
showed highly poligonised subgrains and some clearly
recrystallised areas, both types of structures arising from
the high processing temperatures developed during
Conform processing. Such elevated extrusion tempera-
tures are the result of the high adiabatic heat produced
by particle shearing in the extrusion process, when the
rotating wheel forces the powder to move towards the
machine abutment. The response to heat treatment of
the alloy was poor for all the testing temperatures
employed, since almost no increase in hardness was
measured on aging. The reason for this was evidently the
complete removal of supersaturated Cr and Zr from
solid solution due to the excessively high temperature
developed in the Conform machine, which limited the
maximum achievable strengthening in the consolidated
product on aging. Likewise, the mechanical properties in
compression at room and elevated temperatures for
alloys T1 and T2 were inferior compared to those of the

Conclusions
The processing conditions employed in the consolida-
tion of Cu–Cr–Zr powder preforms by inverse warm
extrusion resulted in as extruded products retaining high
levels of solutes in solid solution, which, on aging,
yielded exceptional mechanical properties. The decom-
position of the supersaturated solid solution into ﬁne
precipitates was responsible for the improvement in
properties with respect to the as extruded condition.
Consolidation of water atomised Cu–Cr–Zr alloy
powders by inverse warm extrusion resulted in alloys
with excellent mechanical properties at room and
elevated temperatures, which otherwise have been
achievable in copper based alloys by expensive processes
such as mechanical milling of expensive composites or
by employing Be, which is a well known toxic and
environmental hazard. Alloys 1, 3 and 4 showed
remarkably good mechanical properties at room and
elevated temperatures. The mechanical properties at
room temperature of the Cu–2.8Cr–0.39Zr (at-%) alloy
represented a twofold increase relative to the values
reported for a commercial Cu–0.8Cr–0.16Zr–0.04Mg
(at.-%) alloy.
10 The proof strengths of the three alloys at
450 and 600 uC were higher than the values reported for
a commercial Cu–Be–Co (C17200) alloy. 25 The max-
imum calculated tensile strengths for the Cu–2.8Cr–
0.39Zr (at.-%) and Cu–2.5Cr–0.18Zr (at.-%) alloys at
600uC were comparable with the ultimate tensile
strength reported for a Cu–20Nb composite 9 at the
same temperature. Therefore, the alloys processed in the
present investigation by inverse extrusion can be
considered as good candidates for substituting conven-
tionally processed Cu–Cr–Zr alloys, Cu–Be–based
alloys, and also expensive Cu–Nb or Cu–Ta composites.
The excessive adiabatic heat generated during proces-
sing of Cu–Cr–Zr powder mixtures by the Conform
method removed most of the Cr and Zr from solid
solution, reducing the age hardening ability of alloys T1
and T2, which in turn resulted in poor mechanical
properties at room and at high temperature.
