1. Introduction
SLM is one of the primary technologies in additive manufacturing 
(AM). It utilizes a computer-controlled laser system to selectively melt 
successive powder layers to fabricate complex 3D components [1 – 3] . 
SLM offers distinct advantages such as low tooling costs, short 
design-to-production cycles, and unrestricted design freedom, particu -
larly for manufacturing complex-shaped parts [4] . Aluminum alloys, 
known for their lightweight, high strength-to-weight ratio, good 
machinability, and corrosion resistance, are widely used in automotive, 
aerospace, construction, and other industries [5,6] . In recent years, SLM 
technology has been further developed for fabricating medium- and 
high-strength aluminum alloys.
Among the developed aluminum alloys, Al-Mg alloys have been 
identified as high-performance aluminum alloys for automotive and 
aircraft structural materials due to their superior strength, high 
ductility, good weldability, and excellent corrosion resistance [4] . 
Extensive research has been conducted on Sc- and Zr-modified Al-Mg 
alloys and the results demonstrate the critical role of nanoscale Al ₃ (Sc, 
Zr) precipitates in grain refinement and precipitation strengthening 
[7 – 9] . Spierings [10 – 12] reported that SLMed Sc- and Zr-modified 
Al-Mg alloys achieved a room-temperature tensile strength of 
530 MPa after hot isostatic pressing (HIP) treatment, surpassing that of 
cast and wrought Al-Mg alloys. Additionally, Gao [13] investigated cast 
Al2.5Cu0.3Sc alloys and achieved a high concentration of Sc atom 
segregation at the particle-matrix interfaces of the Al ₂ Cu strengthening 
phase through three different heat treatment conditions. This inhibited 
the coarsening of the Al ₂ Cu phase at high temperatures, indicating that 
Sc enhances the high-temperature strengthening effect of the Al ₂ Cu 
phase. Zhang [14] added 2 wt% Zr in the SLM process, resulting in

However, most studies have focused on improving the

To enhance the high-temperature mechanical properties of Al alloys, 
the authors ’ group previously designed an AlCuMnMg-0.8Zr alloy, 
leveraging the high cooling rate advantage of SLM technology. Cu, Mg, 
and Mn were added as the solid solution strengthening components. Zr

solidification. The alloy was expected to possess high strength at high

strengthening and solid solution strengthening. The processing param -
eters and defect formation mechanisms of the designed alloy were 
investigated [15,16] . This study examines the microstructure and pre -
cipitate evolution of SLMed Al-Cu-Mn-Mg-Zr alloy under different heat 
treatment conditions, as well as the effects of heat treatment on the 
room-temperature and high-temperature tensile properties of the alloys. 
The findings provide data and theoretical support for the application of 
SLM technology in fabricating heat-resistant aluminum alloys.
2. Experimental procedures
2.1. SLM process
The alloy powder used in the experiment was prepared by the gas 
atomization method. The composition of the alloy powder was Al- 
1.05Cu-3.18Mn-0.96Mg-0.81Zr (mass percentage). Measurements and 
statistical analysis confirmed that the morphology and particle size of 
the alloy powder met the powder spreading requirements for SLM, with 
detailed data reported in previous studies [16] . The SLM equipment 
used was the EP-M150 selective laser melting system from E-plus-3D 
Technology Co., Ltd. Based on parameters identified in earlier research, 
the selected SLM parameters were a laser power of 300 W, a scanning 
speed of 1000 mm/s, and a hatch spacing of 0.15 mm. The printing 
process was conducted under argon gas protection, maintaining a 
chamber oxygen content below 0.1 %. The built sample dimensions were 
10 *10 * 10 mm³ .
2.2. Heat treatment
The prepared samples were subjected to two heat treatment 
methods: solution treatment and direct aging treatment. Before heat 
treatment, the phase transformation temperature of the samples was 
tested using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, 449F3) under an 
argon-protective atmosphere to determine the heat treatment temper -

2.3. Microstructure and mechanical characterization
All the samples were subjected to mechanical grinding and polishing, 
followed by etching with Keller ’ s reagent for 15 s. The microstructure 
morphology of the etched samples was observed using an optical mi -
croscope (MDS 400). The morphology and size of precipitates before and 
after heat treatment were analyzed using a field emission scanning 
electron microscope (FE-SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300) equipped with energy- 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), along with the element distribution in 
the samples. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was used to analyze 
the grain size, grain orientation, grain boundaries, and stress distribu -
tion of the samples, with the data processed using Channel 5 software. 
EBSD samples were prepared through mechanical polishing and electro- 
polishing. Electro-polishing was conducted at 273 K in an electrolyte 
(10 % perchloric acid + 90 % anhydrous ethanol) with a voltage of 30 V 
and a duration of 60 s. The element distribution and precipitates in the 
samples were further characterized using a transmission electron mi -
croscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200). TEM samples were prepared with a 
precision ion polishing system (Gatan 691). Diffraction spots and high- 
resolution images were calibrated using Digital Micrograph software.
The hardness test was conducted using a microhardness tester (HV- 
1000B) with a load of 300 gf and a dwell time of 15 s. Five random 
measurement points were selected for each sample, and the average 
value was used as the final measurement. The tensile properties of the 
samples were tested using an electronic universal testing machine 
(SHIMADZU) with a tensile strain rate of 1 mm/min. The ultimate ten -
sile strength, yield strength, and elongation were calculated based on the 
processed data. On the stress-strain curve, the stress value corresponding 
to 0.2 % residual deformation was taken as the yield strength. The 
tensile sample dimensions were consistent with those in our previous 
study [16] .

3.2. Direct aging treatment
To determine the optimal aging heat treatment conditions, the 
samples were heat-treated at temperatures between 300 
◦
C and 400 
◦

for 2 – 10 h, and the microhardness was tested, as shown in Fig. 4 . It can 
be seen that at an aging temperature of 300 
◦

sample initially decreases and then remains relatively stable as the aging 
time increases. When the aging temperature is 400 
◦
C, the trend shows 
the opposite, with the sample hardness reaching a peak value of 135 HV 
after 2 h of aging. At the aging temperature of 350 
◦
C, the sample

at 8 h. Compared to the hardness of 105 HV before heat treatment, the 
hardness value increased by 28.6 % after 2 h aging treatment at 400 
◦

Thus, the selected aging heat treatment condition was a temperature of 
400 
◦
C and a time of 2 h. The samples subjected to heat treatment under 
this condition were then characterized for microstructure and precipi -
tate evolution, and the tensile performance at room and high tempera -
tures was tested.
Fig. 5 shows the longitudinal section SEI of the SLMed Al-Cu-Mn-Mg 
alloy and the alloy after aging treatment. The precipitates are long strip- 
like white precipitates along the grain boundaries. After aging treat -
ment, the precipitates undergo a considerable reduction in size and a 
dramatic rise in quantity, changing from long strip-like shape to 
discontinuous short strip-like and dot-like shapes.
During the SLM forming process, the alloy powder undergoes 
repeated melting and solidification, and the high-temperature gradient 
and rapid cooling rate lead to the preferred orientation of grains. Fig. 6
shows the inverse pole figures (IPF) and pole figures (PF) of the alloy ’ s 
longitudinal section before and after heat treatment. The figures reveal 
that both the as-fabricated and heat-treated samples exhibit strong 
< 001 > texture, with weaker textures in other directions. This is mainly 
due to the aluminum alloy matrix having a face-centered cubic struc -
ture, where the growth rate along the < 001 > direction is the fastest, 
causing most columnar grains to preferentially grow along the forming 
direction. This phenomenon was also observed in other alloys [18] . 
Fig. 7 shows the IPF, recrystallization structure map, and Kernel average 
misorientation (KAM) map of the columnar grains of the SLMed and 
aging-treated samples. Both columnar grain regions display a mixed 
structure of blue recrystallized grains and yellow substructured grains, 
as shown in Fig. 7 (b) and Fig. 7 (e) . During the SLM forming process, 
inconsistent solidification shrinkage induces thermal stress within the 
sample. The KAM map is used to evaluate the dislocation density and 
strain distribution. As shown in Fig. 7 (c) , before heat treatment, strain is 
concentrated at grain boundaries. After heat treatment, the strain dis -
tribution becomes more uniform and increases significantly, as shown in 
Fig. 7 (f) . These indicate that the strain induced by thermal stress accu -
mulation during SLM forming causes dislocations to proliferate and 
concentrate near grain boundaries, while heat treatment reduces strain 
concentration at the grain boundaries. Fig. 8 (a) and Fig. 8 (d) clearly 
show that the fine equiaxed grains have no significant preferred orien -
tation and consist of substructured grains. This is due to the high cooling 
rate at the edges of the melt pool, which prevents the grains from 
recrystallizing, and the aging treatment temperature is insufficient to 
reach the recrystallization temperature. The strain distribution in the 
equiaxed grains before and after heat treatment is similar to that in the 
columnar grain regions. After aging treatment, the local misorientation 
changes from the grain boundaries to within the grains, with the strain 
distribution becoming more uniform, releasing some internal stress, and 
increasing distortion energy, causing the misorientation to shift from the 
grain boundaries to within the grains [19] .

strength of the alloy, it also limits the slip ability of the dislocations, 
resulting in a decrease in toughness. Moreover, when dislocations stack 
around the precipitates, the local stress increases significantly, leading 
to stress concentrations. These stress-concentrated regions may become 
microcrack initiation points, thus accelerating the fracture failure of the 
alloy. At 300
