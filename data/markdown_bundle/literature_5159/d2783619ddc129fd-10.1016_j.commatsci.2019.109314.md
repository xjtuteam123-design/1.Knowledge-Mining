1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal (SC) superalloys, which mainly consist of γ′
precipitates with L1 2 structure embedded coherently in fcc γ matrixes,
are crucial for the blade applications in the advanced aero engines
owing to their outstanding mechanical properties and oxidation re-
sistances at high temperature [1,2]. Their high-temperature strength
and creep resistance primarily arise from three aspects, i.e., the solid
solution strengthening of γ matrixes, the anomalous yield and pre-
cipitation strengthening of γ′ phases as well as the γ/γ′ interfacial
strengthening [3], and all of which strongly depend on the content of
refractory elements such as Re, Ru, Mo, W etc. For example, corre-
sponding to the second and third generation superalloys containing
about 3 wt% and 6 wt% Re, their temperature resistant capacity in-
crease by 30 °C and 60 °C, respectively [4]. This Re-addition induced
reinforcement of creep resistances is usually called as Re-e ﬀect [5].I n
which Re not only may reduce stacking fault energies of γ matrixes and
raises anti-phase boundary energies of γ′ precipitates [6,7], but also can
slow down coarsening kinetics of γ′ precipitates and dislocation climb
rates in γ matrixes due to its slow di ﬀusion coe ﬃcient [8], and even
facilitates to modify the mis ﬁt between γ matrix and γ′ precipitate and
further lead to the interfacial dislocation networks to be enriched [9].
Previous investigations showed that primary dislocations with
[110]1
2 Burgers vectors are con ﬁned to activate in γ channels at the early
stage of creep of Ni-based SC superalloys under high-temperature and
low-stress. They can be captured by γ/γ' interfaces through dislocation
reactions to form dislocation networks [10], which lead to the internal
stress caused by mis ﬁt between disordered γ matrix and ordered γ′
precipitate to be released [11]. As a result, the dislocation does not
penetrate the ordered γ′ precipitates, but climbs up or down in the front
of γ/γ' interfaces, and the interfacial dislocation network can e ﬀectively
prevent the sliding of dislocations and reduce the climb rate of dis-
locations around ordered γ′ precipitates. The upward and downward
dislocation climbs are associated with vacancy absorptions and re-
leases, respectively, which result in a vacancy ﬂow from the emission
port to the absorption port of dislocation cores [12]. Re tends to seg-
regate to the dislocation core [13]. In this case, a strong hindrance to
the di ﬀusion of vacancies has been observed [12].

As a typical strengthening element, Re is recognized to strongly
partition to γ phases [22–24]. For example, Miyazaki et al. found the
partitioning of Re into γ′ phases decreases with increasing Re content in
Ni-Al-Re ternary alloys [22], and an extra X-addition makes the ten-
dency of Re partitioning into γ phase stronger in Ni-Al-Re-X (X = Cr,
Mo, W, Ti, Ta, Nb, Co) quaternary alloys [23]. Later, with the help of
ﬁrst-principles calculations, Zhou et al. [24] further pointed out this
preferential partitioning of Re to γ phases in Ni-Al-Cr alloys can be
attributed to a smaller substitution energy of Re for host atoms in γ
phases than those in γ′ phases. However, a reversed partitioning be-
havior induced by other alloying elements were also reported [25,26].
For example, by atom-probe tomography (APT) and ﬁrst-principles
calculations, Amouyal et al. [25] found that W preferentially partitions
to γ′ phases in ternary Ni-Al-W alloys, but it is reversed to γ phases in
multi-component commercial alloys because of a weaker driving force
of W to γ′ phase than Ta. In addition, a transmission electron micro-
scopy (TEM) based energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis by
Carroll et al. [26] also exhibited that Re partitioning to the γ matrix in
Ru-addition superalloys is much less than in Ru-free superalloys. These
results imply a complicated correlation and synergetic e ﬀect between
alloying elements could exist in Ni-based SC superalloys [27].
As for the distribution of Re atoms in γ matrixes, clustering or not
has been a controversial issue. By atom probe ﬁeld ion microscopy (AP-
FIM), Blavette et al. [28] ﬁrstly reported some short range orders of Re-
crystals about 1.0 nm exist in Re-modi ﬁed versions of CMSX-2 and PWA
1480 superalloys. Later, Wanderka et al. [29] further observed a Re-
cluster of 5-atoms in γ matrixes of CMSX-4 superalloys. Using molecular
dynamics (MD) simulation, Zhu et al. [9] even found Re clusters to
emerge in γ′-Ni
3Al phases besides γ-Ni phases, and pointed out the
clustering tendency can be attributed to a decreasing total energy of γ
and γ′ phases. Re-clusters are thought to play a key role in the high
temperature creep of superalloys due to their more e ﬃcient obstacles
against dislocation motion compared to isolated solution atoms. How-
ever, an extended X-ray absorption ﬁne structure (EXAFS) analysis [30]
and a modern atomic probe tomography (APT) [31] showed only Ni
atoms are detected around Re in a Ni-Re binary alloy. And consequently
Mottura et al. [32] speculated Re-clusters are likely to be unstable be-
cause the interaction between the nearest neighboring Re atoms is a
strong repulsion in γ-Ni(Re) phases.

2. Calculation details
The ﬁrst-principles calculations are performed by the Vienna ab
initio simulation package (VASP) [42] based on the density functional
theory (DFT), in which a plane-wave basis set with the projector aug-
mented wave (PAW) [43] is used to characterize the ion-electron in-
teraction, and the exchange correlation term is described within a
generalized gradient approximation (GGA) parameterized by Perdew-
Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [44]. In our self-consistent ﬁeld (SCF)
calculation, the cuto ﬀ energy of plane wave functions is set at 350 eV,
and the sampling of irreducible wedges of Brillouin zones is carried out
with 4 × 4 × 4 regular Monkhorst –Pack grids of special k-points [45],
corresponding to 3 × 3 × 3 supercells. A conjugate gradient method
[46] has been used to optimize the geometry and all atomic positions in
the supercell are relaxed until the force on each atom is less than
0.09 eV/Å. The calculation of total energies and electronic structures is
followed by cell optimization with SCF tolerance of 1 × 10
−5 eV under
GGA-PBE potential.
