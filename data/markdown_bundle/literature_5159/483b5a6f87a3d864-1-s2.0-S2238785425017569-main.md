ABSTRACT
High-entropy alloys are analyzed under various loading conditions and with different metal element composi -
tions using a modified viscoelastic spring lattice model. A custom drop structure program has been developed to 
simulate the microstructure of high-entropy alloys. The results indicate that an increase in aluminium content 
positively influences the tensile strength of these alloys, although it negatively impacts ductility. Additionally, 
higher temperatures reduce the maximum tensile strength, likely due to phase transitions occurring within the 
alloy. The tensile strength also increases under high loading rate conditions, leading to brittleness in the material. 
At the same time, the influence of various variables on yield stress and yield strain is also studied. Finally, the 
model effectively simulates the crack development process of high-entropy alloys.
1. Introduction
Traditional alloys typically employ a primary element as the matrix 
(e.g., iron-based steels, aluminum-based aluminum alloys) and enhance 
their properties through the addition of other metallic elements. How -
ever, the potential for developing novel alloy systems is constrained by 
the limited number of available elements in the periodic table. To 
overcome this limitation, Professor Jian-Wei Ye proposed the seminal 
concept of equimolar multi-component alloys in 1996, aiming to inno -
vate alloy design [ 1 ]. Prof. Cantor also began to explore the direction of 
multi-major element alloys in the middle and late 20th century, and 
successfully formulated CoCrFeMnNi alloys with equal molar ratios and 
published a paper in 2004, which are widely studied as “ Cantor alloys ” 
[ 2 ]. This alloy possesses a Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) solid solution 
structure and demonstrates exceptional mechanical properties [ 3 ]. The 
same year, Prof. Jianwei Ye defined this multi-major element alloy as a 
high-entropy alloy (HEA) [ 4 ]. HEAs possess unique microstructures and 
properties that challenge traditional knowledge, which has drawn 
considerable interest from researchers since their emergence. Compared 
to conventional alloys, HEAs exhibit four characteristic effects: the 
high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect, sluggish diffusion effect, 
and cocktail effect [ 5 – 7 ]. Lattice distortion arises from significant 
atomic size mismatches among constituent elements, substantially 
enhancing material strength. Sluggish diffusion occurs due to the 
multi-element environment, slowing atomic mobility and promoting 
stable nanoscale precipitate formation. The high-entropy effect lever -
ages elevated configurational entropy to stabilize solid solution phases 
while suppressing complex intermetallic compounds. Lastly, the cocktail 
effect harnesses synergistic elemental interactions to yield unexpected 
properties such as superior mechanical performance, enhanced corro -
sion resistance, and high-temperature stability. As a result, various 
compositions of HEAs have been reported over time. Currently, the 
HEAs system encompasses a wide range of compositions [ 8 – 12 ].
In research, scientists commonly use scanning electron microscopy 
and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy to analyze the composition of 
HEAs. In contrast, X-ray diffractometry is used to examine their micro -
structure. However, these experimental methods can be time-consuming 
and have various limitations. With the rapid advancement of computer 
technology, integrating computational approaches with materials sci -
ence provides a more efficient and cost-effective way to study HEAs. 
Currently, molecular dynamics methods are the primary computational 
techniques employed in this area of research. Sharma [ 13 ] investigated 
the segregation-ordering behavior of Al

23 ]. In contrast, the finite element method can efficiently predict stress

machine learning heavily relies on large-scale and high-quality datasets, 
and the datasets for some scenes are difficult to obtain, expensive, and 
their “ black-box ” characteristics lead to poor physical interpretation of 
the models [ 25 , 26 ].

2.1. Structure simulations
To ensure compatibility of these complex attributes with VLSM while 
preserving model ’ s authenticity, the following simplifications are 
implemented. 
I. Omission of Metal Complexes: Metal complex formation is 
excluded to simplify the model.
II. Unified Solid Solution Strengthening: The strengthening effect 
of BCC or FCC solid solutions is represented by an increase in the 
corresponding elastic modulus.
III. Phase-Dependent Parameters: When multiple phases coexist 
within the same system, a single metal may be assigned different 
microscopic parameters depending on the phase.
Crucially, it must be emphasized that VLSM operates at the meso -
scopic scale. Consequently, a single lattice point does not represent an 
individual metal atom; rather, it represents a cluster of atoms possessing 
a specific lattice structure.

into the computational system of VLSM. Firstly, the metal elements in

modulus. In the traditional linear elastic phase, the modulus of elasticity 
is fixed and the rate of variation of modulus is zero. When the yield stress 
is reached, the material enters into the viscoelastic response phase, at 
which the instantaneous modulus of elasticity gradually decreases and 
the absolute value of the rate of variation of modulus gets maximum. 
Yet, in the viscoelastic lattice spring model, the modulus rate of change 
is not zero from the start, and according to Fig. 7 (a), there is a minimum 
value, i.e., a maximum value of the absolute value, and the displacement

derivative, the third-order derivative of stress is obtained as curves in

4.2. Effect of temperature
In practical applications, particularly in aerospace materials, tem -
perature is a crucial factor to consider when examining the mechanical 
properties of materials. This section introduces four different tempera -
ture models to analyze the effects of temperature on the mechanical 
properties of AlCrCoFeNi HEA within the temperature ranges of 223 K, 
293 K, 318 K, and 343 K.
In our previous study, the effect of temperature on the mechanical 
properties followed a certain power exponential formula, and since this 
study is only qualitative, the function and its parameters from the pre -
vious study were followed. The calculated stress-strain curves are shown 
in Fig. 9 (a), with Fig. 9 (b) as a comparison.
From a physical perspective, an increase in temperature negatively 
affects the stability of metal bonds. Consequently, the force between 
neighboring metal springs decreases, reflected in the macroscopic me -
chanical properties by a reduction in tensile strength. From a compu -
tational standpoint, according to relevant equations, the initial modulus 
of elasticity of the spring connecting neighboring lattice points decreases 
with rising temperature, leading to a decrease in calculated stress. 
Specific values are shown in Table 4 .
In some literature, the linear deformation region of HEAs is noted to 
be very short; however, it corresponds to very high stress levels, indi -
cating that this section is associated with a significantly high modulus of 
elasticity. Some calculations suggest that this linear deformation region 
is not significantly influenced by temperature and retains a nearly 
constant slope. Nonetheless, there is a clear pattern of variation of yield 
stress and yield strain with temperature. As the temperature decreases, 
the yield stress and yield strain increase synchronously, corresponding 
to the prolongation of the traditional linear change stage as shown in

are obtained by differentiating the stress several times, where the 
second-order derivatives of the stresses need to be nonlinearly fitted, 
and the normal distributions of the R2 and residuals of the fitted results 
are shown in Figs. 10 and 11 .
When the displacement exceeds the yield strain, the HEA enters a 
viscosity-dominated nonlinear deformation process, where higher tem -
peratures lead to reduced ductility and a decrease in maximum tensile 
strength. Recent experimental data in the literature align with the cal -
culations presented in this study [ 3 , 10 , 39 – 42 ].

5. Conclusion
In this study, we simulated the mechanical properties of HEAs for the 
first time using the VLSM. By conducting uniaxial tensile tests with 
varying particle contents, temperatures, and tensile rates, we obtained 
stress-strain curves and displacement profiles that align closely with 
published data. The key findings of the study are as follows. 
1. The maximum tensile strength of HEAs significantly increases with 
higher aluminum content while ductility decreases rapidly. This 
suggests that the small volume of metal atoms locally enhances the 
strength of the solid solution within the AlCrCoFeNi HEA. However, 
the overall alloy exhibits increased brittleness. With the increase of 
Al content, the yield strain does not present significant rule of 
change, but the yield stress gradually increases.
2. Temperature plays a critical role in the behavior of HEAs. Lowering 
the temperature initially strengthens force between lattice points, 
raising the fracture threshold to a certain extent and allowing for 
greater tensile strength and ductility. This indicates that the impact 
of temperature on the material properties is more complex than that 
of metal content. As the temperature decreases, the yield stress and 
yield strain increase synchronously.
3. At a constant temperature, we varied the tensile rate in our simula -
tions and found that a higher tensile rate increases maximum tensile 
strength, with no significant effect on ductility. The effect of strain 
rate on yield strain is limited. With the increase of strain rate, the 
yield strain increases within a small range, and the yield stress also 
increases.
4. When an initial defect occurs in the alloy, it randomly generates new 
cracks at locations with high load-bearing. These new cracks grad -
ually connect with the original defects, ultimately leading to 
fracture.
