Effect of solution treatment temperature on
microstructure and mechanical properties of hot
isostatically pressed superalloy Inconel* 718
G. Appa Rao , M. Srinivas and D. S. Sarma
Hot isostatically pressed superalloy Inconel 718 heat treated as per the standard schedule of aerospace material
speciﬁcation (AMS) 5662 (solution treatment at 980uC/1 h/water quenching followed by two step aging at 720 uC/8 h/
furnace cooling to 620 uC/8 h/air cooling to room temperature) has shown yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) comparable to that of wrought heat treated alloy, but it had poor ductility and inferior stress rupture
properties. To ﬁnd an appropriate heat treatment to improve these properties, the inﬂuence of solution treatment
temperature on the structure and properties of the alloy has been studied. The results show that although YS and UTS
decrease with increasing solution treatment temperature, considerable improvement occurs in the ductility of the unaged
alloy. For the aged alloy, an increase in solution treatment temperature resulted in only a marginal improvement in
strength, but the 650 uC ductility and stress rupture life were improved signiﬁcantly. This has been attributed to the
dissolution of MC carbides and disruption of prior particle boundaries (PPBs) at higher solution treatment temperatures
that had led to enhanced bonding between the particles. Fractography studies revealed a transgranular ductile mode of
fracture for the unaged condition for all solution treatment temperatures. In contrast, aged alloys subjected to lower
solution treatment temperatures have shown decohesion of particle boundaries leading to an intergranular mode of
fracture, while alloys solution treated at temperatures greater than 1250 uC revealed a transgranular ductile mode of
fracture suggestive of improved ductility. The improvement of high temperature properties obtained by higher solution
treatment temperature makes it possible to explore the near net shape capability of HIP technology to its maximum
potential, when adopted for producing alloy 718 components. MST/6124

Introduction
Ni – Fe base superalloy Inconel 718 is used extensively in
aircraft, aerospace, nuclear and petrochemical industries,
because of its good room temperature as well as elevated
temperature properties.
1,2 Alhough processing of alloy 718 is
generally carried out through an ingot metallurgy route, this
has inherent problems associated with microsegregation of
alloying elements.
3,4 For improved homogeneity and a ﬁne
grained microstructure, processing of alloy 718 is carried out
through a powder metallurgy (P/M) route. In this route, the
prealloyed powder produced by an inert gas atomisation
(IGA) technique is consolidated either by sintering under
atmospheric pressure (CAP) or by hot extrusion methods.
The resulting material is further isothermally forged to
required shapes that exhibit tensile and fatigue properties
equal to or better than their counterparts made through an
ingot metallurgy route.
5,6 Osprey forming and powder
injection moulding (PIM) are the two different advanced
techniques used for making near net shaped components from
alloy 718. 7,8 However, the residual porosity present in
components produced by these techniques has been found
to be a major drawback for critical applications. This
limitation can be overcome by use of hot isostatic pressing
(HIPing), which has the potential for producing near net
shaped components with near theoretical density, ﬁne
microstructure, and isotropic properties.
9
In the present study, the HIP technique has been used for
the consolidation of alloy 718 prealloyed powder with the
basic aim of producing near net shaped integral turbine rotors
for use in the reactive type turbine of a space vehicle’s engine,
which will have a short service life of the order of a few
minutes. Before making full size components, the as HIPed
material was characterised and the alloy has been found to be
highly homogeneous and of ﬁne grain size. The HIPed alloy
had prior particle boundaries (PPBs) decorated with the
brittle oxides (Al
2O3 and TiO2) as well as MC (Nb and Ti) C
carbides.10 The UTS and ductility values of the HIPed alloy
were comparable to those of the wrought heat treated alloy,
although the YS of this alloy was a little lower. In order to
balance strength and ductility, the HIPed alloy was heat
treated as per the standard AMS 5662 schedule.
11 The
extensive precipitation of c0 and c9 during aging treatment
resulted in a substantial improvement in YS, with a marginal
improvement in UTS, but the presence of PPBs led to lower
ductility and inferior stress rupture properties.
12 With the aim
of improving these properties, the HIPed alloy was given
solution treatments over a wide range of temperatures
( 8 5 0–1 2 7 0uC) followed by standard aging treatment. The
microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloy as a
function of solution treatment temperature with and without
subsequent aging treatment are presented here.

The stress rupture properties of the aged alloy as a function
of solution treatment temperature were evaluated at 650 uC
under 690 MPa stress, and the data are graphically

5596 speciﬁcation for wrought IN 718.
14 The corresponding
rupture ductility of HIPed alloy (about 2.1%) was, however,
much lower when compared with the minimum requirement

SEM FRACTOGRAPHY
The fractured surfaces of unaged and aged alloys were
examined to correlate fracture characteristics with structure
and properties. Typical fractured surfaces of selected
samples of alloys tensile tested at room temperature are
presented in Fig. 10. It was observed that alloys solution
treated at 850 uC and 955 uC (unaged condition) exhibited
almost the same features of ﬁne dimples and a few areas
with particle boundary decohesion, suggestive of ductile
fracture. On the other hand, alloys solution treated at
higher temperatures showed more areas of both coarse and
ﬁne dimples, conforming to a fully ductile transgranular
mode of fracture (Fig. 10 a). The features seen in this
fractograph further indicate signiﬁcant void coalescence
and considerable stretching of material, resulting in
increased values of ductility for the alloy solution treated
at 1270uC. In contrast to this, the fractured surfaces of aged
alloys subjected to solution treatment in the temperature
range 850 – 1150uC showed particle boundary facets with a
few areas of ﬁne dimples, suggesting that failure had taken
place due to the decohesion of PPBs, and thus reduced
values of ductility at room temperature (Fig. 10 b). The
fractography of alloys tested at 650 uC showed a fully
intergranular mode of fracture due to the increased
embrittlement of PPBs at elevated temperature.

the fracture surface of the aged alloy solution treated at
1270uC showed more areas of ﬁne dimples, suggestive of a

showed ﬁne dimples as well as some areas of PPB facets,

12 Inferior ductility and poor stress rupture life
observed in P/M (HIP) processed c – c9 strengthened Ni base
superalloys was attributed to the formation of PPBs, and to
mitigate this problem, thermomechanical working of HIPed
alloys has been proposed.15,16 This has been found to be the
most reliable method for improving the high temperature
properties, but the near net shape advantage of HIPing
technology is lost. Hence it is necessary to improve the
mechanical properties of HIPed components by other
means such as modiﬁed HIPing and heat treatment
procedures. Although thermal treatments are expected to
partially improve the microstructure, the high tensile
strength values combined with moderate ductility and
stress rupture life obtained by this approach can only be
considered for certain components, such as integral turbine
rotors of reactive type turbines in liquid propulsion engines
of satellite launching vehicles, which have a very short
service life of the order a few minutes. For wrought alloy
718, different processing techniques have been established
to obtain the desired combination of mechanical properties
to meet the speciﬁc requirement.
17 However, hardly any
information is available in the literature regarding the
suppression of PPBs in HIP processed alloy 718 to improve
its ductility and stress rupture properties. Since the solution
treatment temperature is expected to have a signiﬁcant
effect on the microstructure of the subsequently aged alloy,
the present investigation has been taken up to evaluate the
role of this important parameter in the microstructure and
mechanical properties of HIPed alloy 718.
Usually wrought products of alloy 718 are solution
treated in the temperature range 925 – 1010 uC.
18 The
primary objective of this treatment is to dissolve the
coarse c0 and c9 precipitates formed during slow cooling of
ingots, and to allow the d-Ni
3Nb phase to precipitate at the
grain boundaries in small quantities. Depending on the
application and property requirement, the solution treat-
ment temperature is suitably selected to alter the volume
fraction and morphology of d phase, which can control the
grain size and inﬂuence the content of c0 strengthening
phase in the aged alloy. To realise the ﬁne grain structure
essential for improved tensile and fatigue properties,
solution treatment is performed at d subsolvus tempera-
tures. On the other hand, to produce the coarse grain
structures required for low tensile strength combined with
enhanced creep resistance, solution treatment is carried out
well above the d solvus temperature of 1010 uC.
13 After
solution treatment, aging is carried out at 720 uC for 8 h,
followed by furnace cooling to 620 uC and holding at 620 uC
for 8 h, and then air cooling to room temperature. This two
step aging treatment is preferred for this alloy because the
strength values attained using this treatment are found to
be better than those obtained by aging at a single
temperature.
18
In the present investigation the alloy was solution treated
over a wide range of temperature from 850 uC to 1270 uC.
This was necessary to study the effect of solution treatment
temperature on the PPBs and formation of strengthening
phases as well as mechanical properties, so that an optimum
heat treatment schedule for HIPed alloy could be developed
to obtain the desired combination of properties. SEM and
TEM studies clearly indicated that the HIPed alloy
subjected to solution treatment at d subsolvus temperatures,
i.e. 850 uC and 955 uC, consists of grain boundary d
precipitates with different morphology and variable
volume fraction. At 850 uC the d phase had a platelike
morphology and the plates were found to be placed very
closely at the prior particle boundaries, while strengthening
phases such as c0 and c9 were coarsened at this temperature
(Fig. 6a). This indicates that solution treatment at 850 uCi s
only an over aging treatment. On the other hand, the
solution treatment at 955 uC led to complete dissolution of
coherent precipitates as this temperature is much higher
than the c0 solvus (about 915uC).
13 At this temperature, the
alloy contained d phase with an acicular morphology, with
reduced volume fraction at the grain boundaries. The d
phase has been reported to be an incoherent precipitate,
which retards recrystallisation and hence effectively con-
trols the grain size.
19 The existence of globular d as
individual particles at the grain boundaries acts as an
obstacle to grain boundary crack propagation, while the
presence of acicular d results in irregular/serrated grain
boundaries, resulting in better creep properties in wrought
alloys.
20 It has also been reported that alloys with grain
boundary d are susceptible to the formation of c0 and c9
denuded zones because of the high niobium concentration
in d-Ni
3Nb. These zones are found to be softer and to
possess higher plasticity than the matrix, with extensive
precipitation. As a result, they relax the stress concentration
at the grain boundaries during deformation and thereby
delay grain boundary cracking. Hence the existence of a
certain amount of d phase at grain boundaries is considered
to be essential to balance strength and ductility. However, a
higher volume fraction of intragranular d phase will affect
the alloy strength.
Solution treatment at d subsolvus temperatures did not
show any effect on the initial grain size of the HIPed alloy,
although these temperatures were enough to induce some
grain coarsening in wrought alloys.
21,22 The sluggish grain
growth in HIPed alloy is attributed to the presence of PPBs
decorated with stable oxides and MC carbide particles.
Solution treatment in the temperature range 1050 – 1150 uC
apparently brought no change in the grain size in spite of
the complete dissolution of grain boundaryd and the partial

dissolution of grain boundary carbides at these tempera-

MECHANICAL PROPERTIES
The YS and UTS of the HIPed alloy were drastically
reduced after solution treatment in the temperature range
850 – 1270uC (Fig. 7 a), while lower strength values led to a
considerable improvement in ductility (Fig. 7b). The drastic
drop in alloy strength after solution treatment at 850 uCi s
due to the coarsening of c0and c9 strengthening phases, and
precipitation of grain boundary d (Fig. 6a). The further
drop in strength observed for solution treatment at 955uCi s
attributed to complete dissolution of strengthening phases.
The lowest strength values of alloys solution treated at
temperatures between 1050 and 1270 uC is attributed to the
considerable grain growth (Fig. 5 f), and the dissolution of
strengthening phases and carbides (Fig. 6 c). The %EL and
%RA values of the alloy as a function of solution treatment
temperature presented in Fig. 7 b show that ductility is
strongly inﬂuenced by the size and volume fraction of
strengthening phases in the matrix. While the coarsening of
c0and c9 resulted in only a marginal improvement in
ductility, complete dissolution of the above strengthening
phases at 955 uC produced a signiﬁcant improvement in
ductility. The sharp rise in %EL and %RA values for alloy
solution treated at 1270 uC indicates that dissolution of
carbides and formation of liquid ﬁlm at the particle
boundaries is much more effective in improving the ductility
than the dissolution of strengthening phases. This is due to
the fact that liquid ﬁlm favours diffusion across the particle
boundaries, resulting in an enhanced bonding between the
particles, as discussed earlier. The tensile data of the alloy in
the solution treated condition suggest that PPBs alone are
not detrimental to alloy ductility. The ductility is found to
be closely related to the relative strength of the prior particle
boundaries and the matrix.
The room temperature tensile properties of aged alloy
presented in Fig. 7 show that YS and UTS values were
increased with increasing solution treatment temperature
from 850 uC to 1150 uC, and then decreased slightly at
temperatures greater than 1250 uC. These strength values
have a direct correlation with changes in the size and
volume fraction of strengthening phases as well as the
variation in grain size. The increase in YS and UTS values
of the aged alloy for solution treatment temperatures
between 1050 and 1150uC is attributed to the dissolution of
d phase, resulting in excess Nb content in the matrix
enabling the formation of a greater volume fraction of c0
and c9 strengthening phases during aging treatment when
compared to that of the alloy solution treated below the d
subsolvus temperature.
24 The strength values are indepen-
dent of grain size in the temperature range 850 – 1150 uC,
since the average grain size of the alloy remained unchanged
over this temperature range. The volume fraction of major
strengthening phase c0 in the aged alloys solution treated at
1150 and 1270uC remained the same, however, the strength of
the alloy was slightly lowered at 1270uC due to the fact that
considerable grain growth occurred at this temperature
(Fig. 5f). On the other hand, the ductility of the aged alloy
solution treated at between 850 and 1150 uC was almost the
same, since no major changes occurred in the PPBs at these
temperatures. Secondly, the ductility values of aged alloy
solution treated at d subsolvus temperatures (850 – 980uC)
remained unchanged although grain boundary d phase was
present. This indicates that the grain boundary d phase does
not have a signiﬁcant effect on the ductility of HIPed alloy
when compared to its profound effect on the ductility of
wrought alloy 718. The sharp rise in ductility of aged alloy
corresponding to solution treatments at temperatures greater
than 1250uC is due to the reduced PPB networks resulting in
the transgranular ductile mode of fracture (Fig. 10c).
The 650uC tensile properties of the aged alloy presented
in Fig. 8 show that the YS and UTS values follow a trend
very similar to that observed at room temperature, thus
meeting the AMS 5662 G strength speciﬁcation require-
ment. The 650 uC ductility of the alloy solution treated at
1270uC showed the highest values, at 8% EL and 13% RA
(Fig. 8b). Although these values are slightly less than the
minimum speciﬁcation requirements of 12% EL and 15%
RA for wrought IN 718, they are far superior to the 3% EL
and 7.7% RA obtained with the standard solution treatment
temperature of 980 uC. The improvement in ductility is
attributed to the reduced PPB networks as discussed earlier.
A comparison between the fracture mode of samples
solution treated at low and high temperatures clearly
showed a distinct difference in the mode of failure. Samples
solution treated between 850 and 1150 uC exhibited particle
boundary decohesion, which resulted in lower values of
ductility, while the sample solution treated at 1270 uC
consisted of ﬁne dimples, suggestive of adequate ductility.
The present investigation on the stress rupture properties
of aged alloy shows that rupture life and ductility can be
increased considerably by increasing the solution treatment
temperature. A very similar trend of stress rupture proper-
ties in HIPed alloy has also been observed recently by Habel
et al.
14 In the present alloy the longer rupture life realised by
solution treatment at higher temperatures is due to the
increased volume fraction of c0 and reduced PPB networks.
Considerable improvement in rupture ductility could be
realised when the alloy was solution treated well above thed
solvus temperature. This trend observed in HIPed alloy is in
contrast to the reported behaviour of wrought alloys, as
wrought IN 718 tends to exhibit better ductility when
solution treated at d subsolvus temperatures. This indicates
that the d phase could not enhance the creep resistance of
HIPed alloy due to the presence of prior particle boundary
networks.
The results of the present investigation suggest that
solution treatment of HIPed alloy at the standard
temperatures recommended for wrought material allows
the d phase to precipitate at the grain boundaries. Although
this phase is desirable for grain size control in wrought
alloy, it does not seem to be required for grain size control
in HIPed alloy. The grain size of HIPed alloy is controlled
by PPB precipitates, not by d phase. Therefore the HIPed
alloy needs to be solution treated at a temperature well
above the d solvus to minimise undesirable phases at the
PPBs, which ensures the occurrence of desirable changes in
the grain boundary morphology to improve the tensile
ductility and stress rupture properties. The required tensile
strength values combined with a moderate improvement in
the ductility and stress rupture properties accomplished by
the increased solution treatment temperature shows that
there is ample scope for exploring the near net shape
capability of HIP technology to its maximum potential to

Conclusions
1. Solution treatment of HIPed superalloy 718 in the
temperature range 850 – 1050 uC resulted in precipitation
and coarsening followed by dissolution of d, c0, and c9
phases, but it had no inﬂuence on the concentration of prior
particle boundary networks. However, at higher tempera-
tures between 1150 and 1270uC dissolution of MC carbides,
disruption of PPB networks, and considerable grain growth
occurred.
2. Solution treatment in the temperature range
850 – 1270uC resulted in lower YS and UTS values with
excellent ductility in the unaged material, illustrating that
PPBs alone are not detrimental to alloy ductility; it falls
drastically only when aging strengthens the matrix.
3. At room temperature and at 650 uC, the YS and UTS
of the aged alloy increases with increasing solution
treatment temperature up to about 1150 uC, beyond which
it decreases slightly. On the other hand, the ductility of the
aged alloy increases with increasing solution treatment
temperature and attains its highest value for a solution
treatment temperature greater than 1250 uC.
4. Both stress rupture life and rupture ductility of the
aged alloy were improved signiﬁcantly with increasing
solution treatment temperature. This has been attributed to
the enhanced bonding between particles and the increase in
grain size that occurred at high temperature.
