INTRODUCTION
Practical implementation of intermetallic alloys on
the basis of γ(TiAl) + α2(Ti3Al) phases (hereafter (γ + α2)
alloys) began in the United States, Japan, and Europe.
Note, first of all, the successes achieved in the General
Electric Co., whose 25-year effort has succeeded in
the development of an industrial technology for man-
ufacturing low-pressure GTE turbine blades for Boe-
ing-787 and 747-8, which got certification on the
engine with intermetallic blades [1, 2]. As other exam-
ples, one should note successes of Rolls Royce,
ACCESS, and Mitsubishi [3–5]. Meanwhile, the
achievements mentioned do not reduce the impor-
tance of further research efforts, since the low techno-
logical properties—the limited ductility below the
brittle–ductile transition, low technological plasticity ,
and difficult machinability—significantly restrict the
applicability of the (
γ + α2) alloys.
T o improve the technological properties of the
(γ + α2) alloys, various approaches were used in the
past two decades. These approaches were related to the
development of new alloys, microstructure optimiza-
tion, improvement of melting methods, thermo-
mechanical processing, use of powder-metallurgy
technologies, granule compaction, and directional
solidification. The major part of works was made in the
field of the alloy development and microstructure
optimization by means of various thermomechanical
treatments. The following significant results obtained
in the past two decades to improve the technological
properties of 
(γ + α2) alloys can be noted:
(1) the production of a duplex structure using a simple
heat treatment in the alloys similar to Ti–48Al–2Cr–
2Nb, in which the lamellar structure is unstable because
of a low content of the 
α2 phase [1, 6, 7]. This is an
effective way to create a structure with the grain/col-
ony sizes d ~ 100 μm and an elongation at room tem-
perature up to 1.5–2%. The disadvantage of these
alloys in the duplex state is the relatively low creep
resistance (at 
Т > 700°C), low strength, and low frac-
ture toughness;
(2) the production of a duplex structure through
hot extrusion in the (γ + α2) alloys with an enhanced
heat resistance, for example, in Ti–(45–46)Al–(8–
10)Nb–(0–0.3)C alloys. Owing to the high content of
niobium, which is well-soluble in both intermetallic
phases and significantly decelerates diffusion in them,
as well as of carbon, which forms carbides, the accept-

(3) the production of a refined lamellar structure by
hot deformation, usually at temperatures

improvement of the room-temperature ductility; for

However, the method proved to be effective only for
relatively low alloys and did not result in a significant
increase in the ductility of the alloys with high heat

Abstract⎯The microstructure and mechanical properties of the Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy in
the as-cast state (after gasostatistic processing) and of the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy after hot extrusion at
temperatures corresponding to the α + γ phase field followed by heat treatment have been studied. The extruded
heavy-alloyed alloy has demonstrated significantly higher plastic/mechanical properties at room temperature
with close values of the plasticity/tensile strength and long-term strength at elevated temperatures. A compari-
son of the results with literature data has shown the properties of the as-cast Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B
to be similar to or superior to those of the best-known casting γ (TiAl) + α2 (Ti3Al) alloys.
Keywords: titanium aluminides, microstructure, mechanical properties

When considering these approaches, it should be
noted that the achievement of a high heat resistance in
the 
(γ + α2) alloys due to heavy doping or to the for-
mation of a fully lamellar structure with a small inter-
lamellar distance always leads to a decrease in ductil-
ity . The most heat-resistant 
(γ + α2) alloys (both in the
as-cast state and after intermediate thermomechanical
treatment) demonstrate, as a rule, a room-tempera-
ture elongation much less than 1% [12–15, 22], which
presents significant obstacles to their practical imple-
mentation.
One aim of this work was to estimate the efficiency
of two approaches: duplex-microstructure formation
in one of the most heat-resistant 
(γ + α2) alloys; and
the formation of a refined lamellar microstructure in
the β-solidifying alloy with a low content of alloying
elements. The lowered doping supposedly will allow
avoiding the formation of very thin (<100 nm)
γ/α2 plates typical of high alloys [19, 20, 23, 24] and
the prevention of an adverse β(B2) ⇒ ω phase trans-
formation (which leads to the embrittlement of the
material at high temperatures [23, 25]), which will be
favorable for plasticity .
Another aim of this work was to study the relation-
ship between the most important mechanical proper-
ties, such as plasticity and strength at room tempera-
ture and long-term strength at temperatures of 
600–
750°С, for two alloys: the high-niobium Ti–45Al–
8Nb–0.2C alloy in the state after hot extrusion at tem-
peratures of the 
(α + γ) phase field followed by heat
treatment; and a relatively low-doped Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy in the as-cast state. The
resulting mechanical properties are compared with
each other and with the literary data.
EXPERIMENTAL
The Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy was produced by
vacuum arc melting at the GfE Metalle und Materi-
alien (Germany) in the form of extruded rods with
dimensions of 
∅48 × 90 mm. The cast material was
subjected to quasi-isothermal hot extrusion in a shell
at 
Т = 1250°C with the degree of deformation of 7 : 1,
annealing at Т = 1280°C for 2 h, cooling in a furnace
to 900°С at a rate of ≈1 K/s, aging at this temperature
for 3 h, and a further cooling in the furnace. The heat
treatment was carried out so that to form the equilib-
rium duplex structure and to dissolve the metastable
β(B2) phase. The Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B
alloy was also produced at the GfE Metalle und Mate-
rialien. The ingot was prepared by vacuum arc melt-
ing; further, then it was remelted and homogenized in
a cold crucible, further, gasostatically processed at 
Т =
1200°C and P = 2000 bar (4 h), and, finally , mechan-
ically treated. The size of the ingot after mechanical
treatment was 
∅60 × 200 mm. The density was mea-
sured using bulk alloy samples of a regular shape by
weighing on an analytical balance. The ratio of the densi-
ties of the Ti
⎯45Al–8Nb–0.2C and Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr ,Mo)–0.2B alloys was 4.24/4.03 g/cm3.
From the rods of the extruded and cast alloys, stan-
dard tensile test samples were prepared with the
dimensions of the gage part of ∅5 × 25 mm. The
extruded-material samples were cut parallel to the
extrusion direction. The tensile tests were performed
at 
Т = 20, 600, 700, and 750°C at the initial strain rate
dε/dτ = 3.3 × 10–4 s–1. Before the tests, the surfaces of
the samples were mechanically polished using an abra-
sive paper with a grit of 240–4000 units and pastes with
a grit of 5–0.2 
μm. For each alloy , two samples per
point were tested. When plotting the temperature
dependences of the ultimate tensile strength and elon-
gation, all of the experimental values were used. The
tests of the long-term (100-h) strength were carried
out using 2147 P-30/1000 creep testing machines
(Russia) (three-lever testing machines with a lever arm
of 7 : 1. Standard test samples with the dimensions of
the gage part of 
∅5 × 25 mm were used. The tests for
the long-term strength were performed under loads of
350–550 MPa and temperatures Т = 600, 700, and
750°С. The tests were carried out in air. The sample
elongation was measured after the completion of the
tests from the change in the total sample length
divided by the gage-part length.

Microstructural Studies
Figures 1a and 1b show macro- and microstruc-
tures of the as-cast Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B
alloy (after gasostatic processing). It can be seen that
the macrostructure is homogeneous and fine-grained.
The microstructure of the ingot is lamellar. It consists
of 
γ/α2 lamellas with typical filamentary borides having
a length of 150 μm, which are located both along the
boundaries and in the bulk of the colonies. Figure 1c
presents a histogram of the size distribution of the col-
onies; an average size of colonies was 
d ≈ 52 μm, the larg-
est colonies reached 200–280 μm. Figures 2a and 2b
show electron micrographs of the Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy . No β(В2) phase is

tent of 
β-stabilizing elements in the alloy . The X-ray

γ and α2 phases. The energy dispersive

α2 than in the
γ phase (Table 1).

Mechanical Tensile Properties
Figure 4 demonstrates the mechanical tensile
properties of the alloys as a function of temperature.
The room-temperature plasticity and strength of the
alloy subjected to extrusion were much higher than
those of the cast alloy , which is obviously due to the
presence of a duplex microstructure. However, the rela-
tive elongation 
δ ≈ 1.0% and the strength σu = 614–
632 MPa obtained in the cast Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy approximately corre-
spond to or even exceed the properties of promising
cast 
(γ + α2) alloys (Table 2) [15, 22–24, 28, 29].
The elongation of alloys increases slightly with
increasing temperature from 20 to 750°С. As was
shown for stoichiometric TiAl [30, 31], the growth of
plasticity with increasing temperature is caused by the
passage from brittle to ductile state due to the defor-
mation delocalization because of changes in the oper-
ative mechanisms of deformation. The deformation is
delocalized due to the absorption of lattice disloca-
tions by grain boundaries, grain-boundary sliding, the
enhanced nucleation of lattice and twinning disloca-
tions, and the activation of cross slip and dislocation
climbing. These processes occur more intensely in the
alloy with a duplex structure owing to the presence of
a fine globular structure. Therefore, the Ti–45Al–
8Nb–0.2C alloy showed a higher elongation at 
Т =
750°С than the Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B
alloy . For the extruded alloy at Т = 750°С, we obtained
a relatively high scatter of values of the elongation (δ =
5.5 and 9.1%), which is due to a fierce competition
between the strengthening and softening processes in

γ/α2 boundaries, in which the processes of
absorption of lattice dislocations is complicated. In
the alloy with a fine duplex structure, the reduction in
the ultimate tensile strength with increasing tempera-
ture indirectly evidences for the activation of deforma-
tion along intergrain and interphase boundaries and
for a gradual transformation of grain boundaries and
interphase boundaries from barriers into sinks for dis-
locations [30, 31]. As a result, the strength properties
of the extruded alloy were significantly higher at room
temperature, but in the range of operating tempera-
tures of 
600–750°С they proved to be similar for both
alloys.
The heat treatment of the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C
alloy was carried out in such a way that excludes the
appearance of the 
β(В2) phase and the β(В2) ⇒ В19
transformation. These phases have not indeed been
found in this study; however, the elongations obtained
at room temperature in the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C and
Ti–(40-44) Al–8.5Nb alloys after extrusion and heat
treatment were similar [19]. This means that the
room-temperature plasticity of the Ti–45Al–8Nb–
0.2C alloy was determined by the presence of a duplex
structure; i.e., the presence of lamellar phases such as
the orthorhombic 
В19 phase, which reduces the heat-
resistant properties of the material, is not necessary to
achieve elongations of 2–3%. The lower mechanical
properties obtained for the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy
as compared to the data of [19] are explained by the
low volume fraction of the lamellar component and
the lack of the pearlite-like structure formed by lamel-
las with a nanometer thickness.
Long-Term Strength Tests
Table 3 presents the results on the long-term
strength tests of the alloys under consideration. The
Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy has a slightly higher long-
term strength in comparison with Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B at 600–700°С and a close
strength at 750°С. Thus, the lamellar structure with a
colony sizes d ≈ 52 μm characteristic of the relatively
low-alloyed (γ + α2) alloy and the duplex structure
with grain/colony sizes d ≈ 10 μm of the heavy-alloyed
doped with niobium and containing carbon additives
have a close long-term strength in the temperature
range of 600–750°С.
On the whole, both combinations of mechanical
properties obtained are of significant interest. Mean-
while, from the practical point of view , the application
of the Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy seems
to be more favorable, taking into account that it is not
subjected to deformation, especially for the produc-
tion of large machine parts with a complex geometry ,
such as low-pressure-turbine blades for gas turbine
engines. A possible technological route may include
the manufacture of castings with a shape close to that
of the blades followed by machining (route employed
in the General Electric [1, 2]); or the use of the invest-
ment casting technology for the production of blades
that in fact do not require subsequent mechanical
treatment, e.g., by using centrifugal casting (the
approach that is successfully developed by ACCESS
and Rolls Royce [3, 4]).
Note that the high-temperature properties of the
cast Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy can be
improved markedly by refining the composition, for
example, by slightly increasing the niobium content. In
this case, as was noted, one should take into account the
possibility of a decrease in the low-temperature ductil-

CONCLUSIONS
The microstructure and mechanical properties of
the Ti–43.7Al–3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy in the as-
cast state (after gasostatic processing) and of the
Ti⎯ 45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy subjected to hot extrusion
and subsequent heat treatment have been studied. The
room-temperature plasticity and ultimate tensile
strength of the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2C alloy proved to be
significantly higher than those of the cast Ti–43.7Al–
3.2(Nb,Cr,Mo)–0.2B alloy; however, the plasticity ,
ultimate tensile strength, and long-term strength at
600–750°С turned out to be close. The deformed alloy
is more favorable for the manufacture of high-duty
parts where the demand for the low-temperature plas-
ticity is rigidly specified at a level of 2–3%. On the
other hand, the manufacture of parts using casting
technology is much more attractive from the practical
viewpoint, since the deformation process, in particu-
lar, the hot extrusion of intermetallic 
(γ + α2) alloys,
remains an extremely expensive operation. A further
refining of the alloy composition on the basis of
β-solidifying alloys and the improvement of heat
treatment regimes are necessary to achieve a more bal-
anced mechanical properties of as-cast alloys. As fol-
lows from the results obtained for the Ti–45Al–8Nb–
0.2C alloy , the key to the improvement of low-temper-
ature ductility of cast alloys with the retention or
improvement of high-temperature properties can be
the achievement of smaller sizes of colonies/grains at
higher degrees of alloying with elements that increase
the heat resistance of the
