Effect of Temperature on the Structure and Mechanical 
Properties of Zr–2.5% Nb Alloy Processed by Rotary Forging
S. O. Rogachev1,2  · V. A. Andreev2 · A. S. Kuznetsova1 · M. V. Gorshenkov1 ·

Abstract The influence of the temperature of the rotary 
forging process as well as the temperature of the post-defor-
mation annealing on the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of the Zr–2.5% Nb zirconium alloy was studied. 
The mechanical properties were measured at both 23 and 
300 °C. It was found that rotary forging of the alloy at both 
200 and 300 °C forms a highly inhomogeneous ultrafine-
grained structure with elongated crystallites and a high 
dislocation density. Some equiaxed crystallites with high-
angle boundaries were found in the structure. The amount 
of the regions with the small-angle misorientation in the 
alloy structure after rotary forging at 300 °C is higher than 
after rotary forging at 200 °C. The structure of the alloy after 
forging at 300 °C is more stable to heating than at 200 °C. 
The best balance of strength and ductility of the alloy is 
provided by rotary forging at 200 or 300 °C followed by 
annealing at 400 °C.
Keywords Zirconium alloy · Severe plastic deformation · 
Rotary forging · Microstructure · Mechanical properties
1 Introduction
Today Zr–Nb alloys, in addition to their main application in 
the nuclear power industry, are considered as biocompatible 
structural materials for the manufacture of dental implants 
[1–4]. One of the most common commercial high-strength 
zirconium alloys is Zr–2.5% Nb (E125) [5 ]. However, the 
mechanical properties of the Zr–2.5% Nb alloy as deliv -
ered do not meet the established requirements from medi-
cine (yield strength not less than 600 MPa, ultimate tensile 
strength not less than 800 MPa). To increase the strength, 
zirconium alloys are usually subjected to cold deformation 
using traditional deformation processing [6 –8]. The values 
of the yield strength of the cold-deformed Zr–2.5% Nb alloy 
usually reach 490–560 MPa and sometimes higher [1 , 9]. 
However, cold deformation degrades the ductility and cor -
rosion resistance of the alloy.
The highest strength of zirconium alloys as well as 
the improvement of some of their other properties can be 
achieved using severe plastic deformation methods, such as 
equal channel angular pressing (ECAP) [10–12]. Moreover, 
zirconium alloys after the ECAP processing have a gain in 
the corrosion resistance [13]. The disadvantage of the ECAP 
technique is the small length of the processed workpiece. 
In order to increase the length of the workpiece being pro-
cessed, the ECAP-Conform technique was developed [14], 
but its practical implementation is associated with a number 
of problems.
An alternative to the ECAP technique is rotary forging 
(RF), which is an efficient and technologically advanced 
method for producing long workpieces [15– 19]. However, 
considering that zirconium alloys have a hexagonal lattice, 
they are difficult-to-deform to high degrees of strain, and 
therefore their processing is usually carried out under warm 
deformation conditions. For example, during ECAP of the 
Zr–2.5% Nb alloy, the workpiece is heated to temperatures 
of 350–450 °C [12]. The RF process is possible at lower 
temperatures. In a recent study, we achieved the medically 
required properties of the Zr–2.5 Nb alloy through RF at 
a temperature of 200 °C: the yield strength—660 MPa, 
the ultimate tensile strength—805  MPa, and the

At the same time, the most optimal regimes of defor -

other industrial applications.
2 Materials and Research Methods
Rotary forging of the as recrystallized Zr–2.5% Nb alloy 
workpiece from an initial diameter of 12 mm to a final diam-
eter of 6.5 mm was carried out using a modernized two-die 
rotary forging machine V2129.01. The initial length of the 
workpiece was about 600 mm. The workpieces before forg-
ing and between passes were heated to two temperatures, 
namely (1) 200ºC and (2) 300ºC.
After RF, some samples were annealed in an electric 
furnace at temperatures from 150 to 500 °C with a step of 
50 °C, exposure for 1 h, and cooling in air.

tudinal section were carried out using thin foils and a JEM-
1400 (JEOL) transmission electron microscope (TEM).

tudinal plates 0.3 mm thick from forged samples were cut by 
wire electro spark machining; then this plates were mechani-
cally thinned to a thickness of 0.15 mm. At the next stage, 
3 mm diameter discs were cut out followed by electrolytic

3 Results and Discussion
3.1 The Microstructure and Mechanical Properties 
of the Samples After Rotary Forging
As a result of RF of the original workpieces with a true 
strain e = 1.22, their length increased to 2000 mm. No 
defects such as cracks were observed on the surface of the 
workpieces. According to XRD data all samples consisted 
mainly of hcp α-Zr phase (Fig.  1). A quantitative estimate 
of the dislocation density in the structure of the alloy under 
different conditions based on X-ray analysis data is given 
in Table 1.

The microstructure of the zirconium alloy after RF was 
characterized by TEM (Figs. 2 and 3). As a result of RF at a 
temperature of 200 °C, a strongly inhomogeneous ultrafine-
grained grain–subgrain structure with a high dislocation 
density was formed in the alloy (Fig.  2). Dislocation den-
sity was 1.2 × 1014 to 2.8 × 1014  m−2 (Table 1). Such a large 
scatter in the calculated dislocation density values is asso-
ciated with a large scatter in crystallite sizes. Most of the 
structural elements (subgrains) had a pronounced elongation 
along the axis of the workpiece. The size of the equiaxed 
crystallites with high-angle boundaries was 140–320 nm, 
and the transverse dimension of the elongated subgrains with 
low-angle boundaries was 70–480 nm. The misorientation 
angle of crystallites was determined qualitatively based on 
the analysis of several dark-field images as well as the shape 
of reflections on the ring SAED pattern. The structure con-
tains both clusters of smaller crystallites and larger ones, 
which indicates the presence of a bimodal structure. The 
results obtained correspond to earlier ones [20].
With an increase in the RF temperature to 300 °C, the 
elongation of the structural elements remains (Fig.  3). The 
dislocation density is slightly less compared with 200 °C-RF 
sample (Table 1). The size of the equiaxed crystallites was 
180–470 nm, and the transverse dimension of the elongated 
subgrains was 110–380 nm. However, the structure of the 
alloy after RF at a temperature of 300 °C contains a large 
number of regions 500–1000 nm in size with low-angle mis-
orientation; this is the main difference in the structure of 
the samples after RF at a temperature of 200 and 300 °C. 
The result obtained is quite unexpected, because usually, 
an increase in the deformation temperature (by the example 
of ECAP or torsion in a Bridgman chamber) of zirconium 
alloys lead to an increase in the amount of structural ele-
ments with high-angle misorientation [12, 21].

Zr–Nb alloys during severe plastic deformation processing 
was observed earlier [21, 22].
The formation of the ultrafine-grained structure in the 
Zr–2.5% Nb alloy led to a significant increase in its tensile 
strength characteristics at ambient temperature compared to 
the recrystallized alloy (Table  2). The stress–strain curves 
of the alloy before and after RF are shown in Fig.  4a. The

peratures is comparable (810–815 MPa) which is 1.6 times

lized alloy. At the same time, the 0.2%-yield strength of 
the alloy after RF at a temperature of 300 °C is slightly 
lower than its 0.2%-yield strength after RF at a temperature

which is 1.9 and 2.1 times higher compared with the recrys-
tallized alloy. This is due to the lower dislocation density in 
300 °C-RF sample. The relative elongation of the alloy after 
the 300 °C-RF is slightly higher than after the 200 °C-RF, 
about 19 and 16%, respectively.

3.2 The Microstructure and Mechanical Properties 
of the Samples After Rotary Forging and Annealing
Rotary forging led to an increase in the microhardness of 
the alloy by an average of 40%. Moreover, a weak anisot-
ropy of properties is observed: in the workpiece’ longitu-
dinal section, the microhardness is higher on average by 
6% than in the workpiece’ transverse direction. The val -
ues of the microhardness of the alloy hardened by RF are 
retained after annealing with 1 h holding at a temperature of 
400 °C, regardless of the RF temperature (Fig.  5). Increas-
ing the annealing temperature to 450 and to 500 °C leads to 
a decrease in the average microhardness values by 10 and 
12%, respectively, regardless of the RF temperature.
Despite the rotary forged samples have good strength and 
ductility, the uniform deformation, which most adequately 
characterizes the alloy’s ductility margin, is not very high. It 
was of interest to study the effect of annealing on the tensile 
mechanical properties of the alloy. Two annealing tempera-
tures were chosen: 400 °C is the maximum temperature at 
which the microhardness remains unchanged (compared to 
the microhardness of the alloy hardened by RF) and 450 °C 
is the temperature at which the microhardness starts to 
decrease. The results of a tensile test at ambient temperature 
showed that annealing at a temperature of 400 °C (hold for 
1 h) reduces the ultimate tensile strength of the alloy by 4% 
(in the case of the 200 °C-RF) or does not change the ulti-
mate tensile strength (in the case of the 300 °C-RF). Anneal-
ing at a temperature of 400 °C reduces the yield strength of 
the alloy by 10 and 6%, for the 200 °C-RF and 300 °C-RF, 
respectively (Table  3). In this case, regardless of the RF 
temperature, the total relative elongation of the alloy after 
annealing was about 19%, and the uniform elongation was 
5.5–7%. Thus, we see improvement in uniform deforma-
tion (1.7–1.9 times) with a little decrease in strength after 
annealing at 400 °C.
Increasing the annealing temperature to 450 °C, regard-
less of the RF temperature, causes a decrease in the ulti-
mate tensile strength of the alloy by 7%, as well as a further 
decrease in the yield strength by 5–6%, while the relative

Thus, the change in the 300 °C-rotary forged sample 
structure is less sensitive to heating compared with the 
200 °C-rotary forged sample structure. The changes in the 
mechanical properties of the alloy after annealing, namely a 
decrease in strength and an increase in ductility, can be asso-
ciated with a change in the microstructure. Namely, a little 
decrease in the crystal defect density and the growth of crys-
tallites after annealing at 400 °C, on the one hand, reduces 
slightly the stress flow, which leads to a little decrease in the 
yield strength. On the other hand, such a structure provides 
relaxation mechanisms, which causes an increase in ductility 
[24, 25]. A further increase in the annealing temperature to 
450 °C increases the fraction of recrystallized grains, but the 
structure still retains a high dislocation density. Therefore, 
the strength decreases slightly, but the ductility does not 
noticeably change. Despite the rather noticeable difference 
in the microstructure of the alloy after annealing at the same 
temperature (between 200 °C-RF samples and 300 °C-RF 
samples), the mechanical properties are almost the same. 
This indicates that the mechanical properties of the zirco-
nium alloy are more sensitive to the crystal defect density 
than to the size of crystallites, as noted in [20].
3.3 The High‑Temperature Mechanical Properties 
of the Samples After Rotary Forging
It was also of interest to study the effect of a test tempera-
ture on the tensile mechanical properties of the alloy. The 
temperature of 300 °C was chosen as the test temperature 
for comparison with literature data (300 °C is a typical 
operating temperature of the Zr–2.5% Nb alloy in a nuclear 
reactor [6 ]). Table  4 shows the values of the mechanical 
properties of the RF processed alloy at a test temperature 
of 300 °C; and Fig.  10 shows the correspond stress–strain 
curves. An increase in the test temperature to 300 °C of 
the 200 °C-RF processed sample led to a decrease in both 
the yield strength and the ultimate tensile strength of the 
alloy by 26–27%. For the 300 °C-RF processed sample, the 
decrease in strength characteristics at a test temperature 
of 300 °C was smaller: the yield strength and the ultimate 
tensile strength decreased by 6 and 17%, respectively. In
