High Temperature Low-Cycle 
Fatigue of Friction Welded Joints-
Type 304-304 Stainless Steel and 
Alloy 718-718 Nickel Base 
Superalloy 
This paper assesses the high-temperature low-cycle fatigue of the Type 304 stainless 
steel and Alloy 718 superalloy friction-welded joints. Strain controlled low-cycle 
fatigue tests for 304-304 and 718-718 friction-welded specimens were carried out at 
923K in air to obtain the fatigue strength of the joints. These materials were selected 
as the cyclic hardening and softening materials, respectively. The 304-304 welded 
specimens showed inferior fatigue strength in comparison with the base metal while 
the 718-718 specimens exhibited fatigue strength equivalent to that of the base metal. 
The difference in the fatigue strength between the two materials is discussed from 
the viewpoint of the cyclic deformation behavior and strain reduction at weld in­
terface. 
1 Introduction 
Friction welding is the method of joining two materials uti­
lizing a temperature raise by friction [1, 2], This method has 
the advantages of high efficiency, low cost, and high produc­
tivity. However, fatigue characteristics of friction welded joints 
have not been well understood [3], especially at elevated tem­
peratures [4], and the application of friction welding has been 
limited to special cases, such as automobile parts, in spite of 
many advantages. 
The aim of this paper is to describe a study of the high-
temperature, low-cycle fatigue strength of friction-welded 
joints. For the global understanding of fatigue strength, two 
materials were tested; Type 304 stainless steel and Alloy 718 
nickel-base superalloy. The former is a cyclic hardening ma­
terial and the latter a cyclic softening material. In comparing 
the test results obtained from these materials, differences in

Two types of strain-controlled tests were carried out. One

used to carry out the experiments. Specimens were heated to 
temperature by an electric resistance furnace for Type 304 
specimens and by an induction heater for Alloy 718 specimens. 
Temperature variation along the gage length during tests was 
within ±5 K. All of the tests were conducted at 923K in air 
using a fully reversed triangular strain wave at 0.1 percent/s. 
The number of cycles to failure was defined as the cycle at 
which the tensile stress amplitude decreases to 75 percent from 
saturation for Type 304 stainless steel and from the stress

At welding the temperature was raised up to 1500 K, so there
is a possibility of microstructural change. To confirm the mi­
crostructural change, grain size was examined by optical mi­
croscopy and elemental distribution by Electron Probe
Microanalyser (EPMA). The elements examined were Ni, Cr,
and C. These elements were selected for the reason that M23C6
precipitation at grain boundaries was anticipated.
Figure 4 shows the optical micrographs of the base metal
and welded specimen near the interface. The averaged grain
size was about 100 micrometer while the grain size was reduced
to 10 micrometer by welding. Figure 5 also shows the line
analysis of Ni, Cr, and C elements (a) for the base metal and
(b) for the welded specimen. If M23C6 type carbides precipitate
at grain boundaries, the count of Cr or C should increase in
a period of grain size, i.e., 10 micrometer period. However,
such an increase was not found and the count level of all the
elements of the weld specimen does not differ from that of
the base metal. Thus, we can conclude that the increase in the
hardness caused by welding results from the decrease in the
grain size but not from the precipitation of the carbides at
grain boundaries and within grain.
Figure 6 compares fatigue lives of the base metal, welded
smooth and hourglass specimens. Only the hourglass specimens
used the diametral extenso meter and the diametral strain was
converted into the axial strain using the equation stated in the
experimental procedure. The base metal has the maximum
fatigue strength, the welded smooth specimen has the inter­
mediate strength and the welded hourglass specimen exhibits
the minimum fatigue strength. All the hourglass specimens
fractured at the weld interface; thus the fatigue strength ob­
tained from the hourglass specimens is that of the weld inter­
face. The fatigue life of the weld interface is 20-30 percent of
the base metal, and does not appear to depend on strain range.
The fatigue life of the welded smooth specimen is about a half
of the base metal at large strain range, but it approaches that
of the base metal as strain range decreases. At the total strain
range of 0.5 percent, there was no difference in fatigue life
between the base metal and welded smooth specimen.

For the welded smooth specimen, since the hardness near
the weld interface is raised by the finer grain size, nonuniform
strain distribution may occur along the gage length which has
an effect on the low-cycle fatigue life. In order to examine the
strain concentration or reduction that occurred in the welded
smooth specimen, the strain distribution was measured using
the diametral extenso meter . While the welded smooth specimen
was fatigued in stroke control, the diametral strain distribution
was measured, moving the diametral extenso meter in a 1 mm
step. The diametral strain measured was converted into the
axial strain. Since the diametral strain is the strain in the section
at which the extenso meter was attached, the axial strain con­
verted is regarded as the strain with zero gage length in the
axial direction. This type of test was carried out after the stress
response was fully stabilized, so the conversion from the dia­
metral strain into the axial strain has the sufficient accuracy.
Figure 8 shows the strain distribution along the gage length
of the welded smooth specimen. Tests were carried out using
two strain amplitudes to help explain the cause of two different
fracture patterns-fracture at the base metal in low strain range
and fracture at the weld interface in large strain range. The
ordinate represents the axial strain converted from the dia­
metral strain and the null point in the abscissa denotes the
weld interface. In the figure, ~Et indicates the averaged axial
strain along the gage length. The figure shows that there exists
significant strain reduction near the weld interface. In the test
at 1.05 percent averaged strain range, the minimum strain range
occurred at the weld interface and was 0.80 percent. The max­
imum strain was at the base metal away from the interface
and was 1.25 percent. In the test at 0.60 percent averaged strain
range, the minimum and maximum strain ranges were 0.44
percent.and 0.74 percent, respectively.
In both the tests, the strain at the weld interface had the
minimum amplitude while the strain amplitude increased as
the distance from the interface increased. This strain distri­
bution corresponded well with the grain size distribution. Near
the interface the grain size was small, so the flow stress becomes
large and then the strain amplitude becomes small following
the Petch's relation [6].

Figure 10 shows the variation of stress range with cycles for 
the three types of specimens at the strain range of 0.7 percent. 
The three kinds of specimens all exhibited cyclic hardening 
behavior, and there was no large difference in stress range after 
100 cycles. However, in the initial stage of cycling, the cyclic 
stress of the welded hourglass specimen has the largest stress 
range, welded smooth specimen the intermediate stress range 
and the base metal the minimum stress range. The larger stress 
range of the welded smooth specimen in comparison with the 
base metal is understood from the strain reduction near the 
weld interface. For the hourglass specimen, a slightly larger 
strain was applied in a initial few cycles, because the stress-
strain relation under stabilized condition was used for the 
conversion from diametral strain into axial strain. 
3.2 Alloy 718-718 Nickel Base Superalloy. Figure 11 shows 
the fatigue lives of Alloy 718 base metal and friction welded 
specimens. In this case, in contrast to Type 304 stainless steel, 
the welded specimens showed no reduction in fatigue life from 
the base-metal ones. The cause of no reduction is discussed 
from the viewpoint of stress amplitude below. The failure 
locations of the Alloy 718 welded specimens were the weld 
interface, except for the test at 1.0 percent total strain range. 
At that strain range, the fracture part was 1.3 mm apart from 
the interface. 
Figure 12 illustrates the cyclic stress range with cycles for 
the base metal and welded specimen. In contrast to Type 304 
stainless steel, the cyclic stress range of Alloy 718 decreased 
with cycles and that of the both types specimens closely agreed 
with each other. 
Figures 13 (a) and (b) show the microvickers hardness 
before and after testing. Before testing, the hardness near the 
weld interface is drastically reduced by the thermal/mechanical 
effect at welding. The softened region extends to 5 mm from 
the interface. At the location about 5 mm from the interface, 
there is no change in the hardness. After testing, there was a 
significant reduction in hardness away from the interface, 
whereas the hardness near the interface was unchanged. Thus, 
the cyclic softening occurred only at the part where no reduc­
tion in hardness was yielded by the thermal/mechanical effect. 
Figures 14 (a) and (b) show the hysteresis loops of the base 
metal and welded specimen at 1st cycle at Ae, = 1 percent. There 
was no difference in the shape of hysteresis loop between the 
base metal and welded specimen. Deformation behavior of 
both the specimens was essentially elastic. At the total strain 
range of 1.5 percent, the plastic strain range was only 15 percent 
of the total strain range. As the cycling proceeds, the percentage 
of plastic strain range increased.

Figures 15 (a) and (b) represent the hysteresis loops at half 
life at Ae, = 1.0 percent. The plastic strain range is significantly 
increased in comparison with that at 1st cycle. Considering the 
above deformation behavior, fatigue lives of the welded spec­
imen was explained as follows. 
The hardness near the interface was significantly softened 
by the thermal/mechanical history in the welding procedure, 
so the strain concentration is expected to occur at the interface 
because the hardness corresponds to the amplitude of flow 
stress in general. However, at 923 K, the yield stress of Alloy 
718 superalloy is sufficiently large in the initial stage of cycling 
even at the interface. In the initial stage, the deformation 
behavior of the whole part within the gage length is considered 
as elastic. This is understood from the fact that the hysteresis 
loop of the welded specimen exhibits elastic behavior and the 
stress range of the welded specimen does not differ from the 
base metal specimen. 
As the strain cycle proceeds, the material softens. Softening 
is considered to occur especially in the regions away from the 
interface. At the interface, the material is sufficiently softened 
by welding, so presumably no additional softening occurs with 
strain cycling. This is understood from Fig. 13, where the weld 
interface shows no reduction in hardness with cycles. Then, 
after a certain number of cycles, the material reaches the state 
that all parts have the same yield stress and exhibit the same 
plastic strain range, which means that the fatigue life of all 
the parts has almost the same fatigue resistance. For this reason 
the welded specimen yields no reduction in fatigue strength in 
comparison with the base metal. 
4 Conclusions 
(1) Type 304-304 welded specimens have inferior fatigue 
strength in comparison with the base metal. The fracture part 
of the welded specimen was the weld interface at the large 
strain range while it was base metal 3-5 mm apart from the 
interface at the small strain range. This transition of the frac­
tured part was well explained from the axial strain distribution 
of the welded specimen in combination with fatigue lives of 
the base metal and weld interface. A simple life prediction 
method of the welded smooth specimen was proposed. 
(2) Low cycle fatigue lives of Alloy 718-Alloy 718 welded 
specimens exhibit fatigue strength equivalent to those of the 
base metal. The hardness of the interface was significantly 
reduced by the welding procedure but this softening had no 
effect on fatigue life. The cyclic softening and large yield stress 
were the main cause of the large fatigue life of the welded 
specimen.
