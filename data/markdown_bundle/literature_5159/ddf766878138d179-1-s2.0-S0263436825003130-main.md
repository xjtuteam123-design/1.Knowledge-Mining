ABSTRACT
A 4 kg nitrogen-doped (1 at.%) TiVNb lightweight medium-entropy alloy (MEA) was fabricated by vacuum 
magnetic suspension melting for high-temperature applications. The microstructural homogeneity, phase 
composition, temperature-dependent mechanical properties, and strengthening-ductility mechanisms were sys -
tematically investigated. The as-cast alloy comprises a coarse-grained BCC solid-solution matrix and FCC-Ti(N) 
precipitates, which exhibit two distinct distribution patterns, a near-continuous distribution along grain 
boundaries and a dendritic distribution within grains. Mechanical testing demonstrates that the alloy achieves 
compressive and tensile yield strengths of 962 MPa and 918 MPa at room temperature, respectively, while 
retaining high-temperature strengths of 545 MPa and 486 MPa at 800 
◦
C. Notably, it exhibits elongations 
exceeding 5 % at both 600 
◦
C and 800 
◦
C, with tensile failure characterized by a mixed fracture mode. This 
behavior effectively mitigates the intermediate-temperature embrittlement typically observed in similar alloys. 
Microstructural characterization further reveals that the FCC precipitates maintain structural stability following 
both room-temperature and high-temperature compression. Significant changes in their phase distribution and 
orientation difference demonstrate their deformation resistance within the TiVNb matrix. After heat treatment at 
600 
◦
C for 8 h, the alloy exhibits significantly improved microstructural homogeneity, with the nitrogen content 
in the FCC-Ti(N) phase increasing by over 20 at.%. This leads to remarkable enhancements in both strength and 
ductility. The room-temperature tensile yield strength rises to 1154 MPa (a 25 % improvement compared to the 
as-cast state), while the 800 
◦
C tensile yield strength reaches 526 MPa (an 8 % increase), accompanied by an 
increased elongation of 7.5 %. This study provides important theoretical and experimental insights for the design 
and development of high-temperature lightweight medium-entropy alloys.
1. Introduction
The growing demand for high-performance mechanical components 
in aerospace, aviation, and transportation industries has driven the 
development of novel lightweight alloys with superior stability and 
durability. Lightweight multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) have 
emerged as promising candidates due to their innovative compositional 
design and exceptional comprehensive properties. Specially, Ti- 
containing BCC-structured MPEAs demonstrate particular potential for 
hot-end applications, benefiting from their low density and high 
strength [ 1 ]. However, several critical challenges persist in these alloys, 
including room-temperature (RT) brittleness [ 2 ], poor machinability 
[ 8 ], inadequate high-temperature oxidation resistance [ 3 ], and most 
notably intermediate-temperature embrittlement (400 – 800 
◦
C) [ 4 , 5 ]. 
For example, the low-density BCC TiVNbAl alloy ( ρ = 5.58 g/cm
3

Al content optimization to improve mechanical performance [ 3 ]. Simi -

strength at RT, its yield strength at 800 
◦
C remains relatively low (~150 
MPa) [ 4 ]. Even after Zr content optimization ( ≤ 5 at.%) improved the

forged Al

numerous other alloy systems [ 7 – 20 ]. The underlying mechanisms are 
primarily attributed to oxidation, and stress concentration induced by 
hard brittle precipitates at intermediate temperatures. Considering that

To address this challenge, our research group successfully fabricated 
nitrogen-doped TiVNb medium-entropy alloys at the hundred-gram 
scale using vacuum arc melting. The incorporation of trace nitrogen 
was found to simultaneously induce FCC Ti-rich phase precipitation and 
enhance solid-solution strengthening in the BCC matrix [ 21 ]. This syn -
ergistic effect led to significant mechanical property enhancements, 
rendering the alloy with both high strength and ductility at elevated 
temperatures, while effectively suppressing intermediate-temperature 
embrittlement. These findings suggest good potential for scale-up pro -
duction. However, scaling up production from gram to kilogram-scale 
presents several fabrication challenges. The primary obstacles stem 
from mechanical property degradation due to composition segregation 
and structural defects, as material performance is highly sensitive to 
precipitate characteristics [ 22 – 25 ]. Many efforts focus on computational 
modeling of macro-segregation formation mechanisms, and develop -
ment of integrated processing strategies that combine optimized casting 
with thermomechanical treatments [ 26 , 27 ], aiming to replicate the 
excellent mechanical properties achieved in small-scale laboratory 
specimens to large-scale ingots. A particularly promising alternative 
involves developing high-performance alloys through optimized prep -
aration and heat treatment processes, which could potentially eliminate 
the need for subsequent forging/rolling operations. This approach 
would offer substantial engineering advantages, particularly in energy 
conservation and material utilization efficiency.
The preparation of high-entropy alloy (HEA) ingots remains pri -
marily based on vacuum melting technologies. Compared to emerging 
technologies such as additive manufacturing, this conventional method 
offers distinct advantages including lower energy consumption and 
greater technological maturity. Among vacuum-based techniques, vac -
uum suspension melting stands out for producing alloys with superior 
purity, compositional uniformity, reduced elemental segregation, and 
minimal shrinkage porosity. These characteristics greatly lower the 
microstructural defects that typically cause mechanical property 
degradation, making vacuum suspension melting a more appropriate 
method for alloy scale-up research [ 28 ].
Based on the above research background, the present study utilizes 
vacuum suspension melting technology to fabricate kilogram-scale ni -
trogen-doped TiVNb-N lightweight refractory medium-entropy alloy. 
Through heat treatment optimization, we aim to develop alloys that are 
lightweight, resistant to embrittlement at intermediate-temperatures, 
and superior in strength at elevated temperatures. This work estab -
lishes theoretical and experimental basis to facilitate the industrial 
adoption of these advanced structural materials.
2. Experimental methods
The TiVNb-N alloy was synthesized from high-purity (99.99 %) Ti, V, 
and Nb pellets in an equiatomic ratio of 1:1:1. Nitrogen was introduced 
through NbN to achieve a nominal concentration of 1.0 at.%. A 4 kg 
alloy ingot was produced via cold crucible induction levitation melting 
(SMTX-XF-20) with electromagnetic stirring maintained for 10 – 15 min 
to ensure compositional homogeneity and microstructural uniformity.
Microstructural characterization was performed using optical mi -
croscopy (Carl Zeiss Axioscope 5, Germany), scanning electron micro -
scopy (SEM; ZEISS EVO 18, Germany) and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM; FEI Titan Cube 80 – 300) equipped with energy- 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Phase evolution and deformation 
microstructures were further characterized using electron backscat -
tering diffraction (EBSD; Oxford Instruments Nordlys Max3) to investi -
gate orientation relationships and phase distribution.
Vickers hardness testing (HV-1000 STA) was conducted with a 0.2 kg 
load and 15 s dwell time, giving average value from multiple in -
dentations. Tensile tests were conducted using a uniaxial tensile ma -
chine (INSTRON 5967, USA) at RT, 600 
◦
C, and 800 
◦
C, with a strain rate 
of 0.3 mm/min. Dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens were machined 
with a gauge length of 5 ± 0.1 mm and cross-sectional dimensions of 
(1.2 ± 0.2) × (1.5 ± 0.2) mm
2
. Compression testing included RT ex -
periments conducted on a Shimadzu AGS-X 100 kN universal machine 
and high-temperature (800 
◦

◦

Gleeble-3500 simulator. The compression specimens, with dimensions 
of (6 ± 0.2) × (6 ± 0.2) × (9 ± 0.2) mm
3
, were tested at a strain rate of 
0.3 mm/min up to a true strain of 60 %. All specimens were polished to a 
smooth surface using SiC abrasive paper and were tested in ambient 
atmosphere.

Hardness measurements along the X-axis demonstrated excellent 
consistency, yielding an average value of 350.9 HV, nearly identical to 
the gram-scale counterpart (348.0 HV) reported in our previous study 
[ 21 ]. Metallographic analysis along the X-axis direction revealed a dual- 
phase microstructure consisting of coarse equiaxed grains with intra -
granular precipitates. No apparently distinct grain size was observed 
from the ingot core to edge regions ( Fig. 1 a1 and a2). The intragranular 
precipitates predominantly displayed a lamellar morphology with multi- 
directional orientation distribution ( Fig. 1 a3), mirroring the charac -
teristic microstructure observed in laboratory-scale vacuum arc-melted 
samples. This remarkable consistency in precipitate morphology be -
tween kilogram-scale and gram-scale alloys suggests excellent scalabil -
ity of the microstructure-property relationships.
Hardness measurements along the Y-axis direction demonstrated a 
slight increase from 349.4 HV at the top region to 365.3 HV at the 
bottom ( Fig. 1 b), representing an about 4.5 % hardness enhancement. 
Optical microscopy revealed that the microstructure maintained its 
fundamental characteristics of equiaxed grains with intragranular pre -
cipitates, consistent with observations along the X-axis. However, 
distinct microstructural variations were evident in the bottom region, 
where the formation of numerous sub-grains and a marked reduction in 
intragranular precipitates were observed ( Fig. 1 b3). SEM elemental 
mapping ( Figs. 2 a-c) confirmed excellent compositional uniformity for 
all principal elements (Ti, V, Nb) throughout the ingot from top to 
bottom. The observed hardness increase in the bottom region correlates 
strongly with the near-absence of precipitates, suggesting that solid 
solution strengthening becomes the dominant hardening mechanism in 
this zone. This microstructural evolution is likely attributable to the 
combined effects of thermal gradients and solidification conditions 
specific to the bottom region of the ingot during the casting process. It 
also reflects that rapid cooling will inhibit the formation of precipitates.
To further characterize the phase structure and composition of pre -
cipitates in the alloy, TEM analysis was performed on samples from 
location b1 ( Fig. 3 ). The precipitates were observed to distribute both 
along GBs and within grains. GB precipitates exhibited a near- 
continuous single-chain morphology ( Fig. 3 a), while intragranular pre -
cipitates displayed cross-oriented lamellar structures with chain-like 
distribution ( Fig. 3 b). Phase analysis revealed an FCC structure (a =
448 pm) for the precipitates, contrasting with the BCC solid solution 
matrix (a = 336.2 pm). Compositional analysis showed nitrogen con -
tents of 19.0 % in GB precipitates and 17.4 % in intragranular pre -
cipitates, with titanium constituting the remaining majority. These 
results conclusively identify the precipitates as FCC-Ti(N) phases. Their 
formation mechanism is attributed to titanium ’ s higher vacancy binding 
energy compared to vanadium and niobium [ 21 ]. During cooling, tita -
nium enrichment coupled with nitrogen-induced precipitation ulti -
mately formed a dual-phase microstructure, consistent with gram-scale 
TiVNb-N alloys prepared via vacuum arc melting.
The microstructural differences at location b3 can be explained by 
classical solidification theory [ 29 ]. The ingot bottom, being in direct 
contact with the cold crucible, experiences the smallest geometric 
constraint and highest cooling rate. This rapid heat dissipation sup -
presses precipitation kinetics, resulting in uniform elemental distribu -
tion within the solid solution matrix and corresponding to a 4.5 % 
hardness increase in this region. Such cooling rate variations produce 
distinct microstructural characteristics along the ingot ’ s vertical axis, a 
phenomenon analogous to that reported in Ni-Co-based superalloys 
[ 30 ].

3.2.1. Mechanical properties and failure modes
The compressive and tensile mechanical properties of the TiVNb-N 
specimens from Fig. 1 b1 section were evaluated across multiple tem -
perature regimes, as illustrated in Fig. 4 a and b. Experimental results 
indicate an outstanding strength-ductility balance under compressive 
and tensile loading conditions. At RT, the alloy exhibits compressive and 
tensile yield strengths of 962 MPa and 918 MPa, respectively; these 
values remain at 545 MPa (compressive) and 486 MPa (tensile) at 
800 
◦
C. Compared with gram-scale alloys (1057 MPa at RT and 586 MPa 
at 800 
◦
C), the compressive strength of 4 kg ingots differs by less than 10 
%. Remarkably, the compressive yield strength persists at 228 MPa even 
at 1000 
◦
C. Further analysis of tensile curves reveals that the TiVNb-N 
alloy possesses superior strength-ductility balance across the RT to 
800 
◦
C temperature range. The elongation of the alloy exceeds 5 % when 
tensile in air at 600 
◦
C and 800 
◦

temperature embrittlement problem commonly found in MPEAs such 
as TiVNb [ 5 ], TiVNbZr [ 4 ], and Ni-Co-Fe-Cr-Al-Ti [ 11 ]. This embrittle -
ment resistance within the 400 – 800 
◦
C range establishes a fundamental 
performance basis for medium- and high-temperature applications.
Analysis of the 800 
◦
C tensile fracture surface ( Fig. 5 ) revealed a 
mixed-mode failure mechanism combining dimple rupture and trans -
granular fracture. The central region exhibited predominantly dimpled 
morphology, whereas the transgranular fracture zone displayed char -
acteristic terrace-like topography with river patterns and increased 
surface roughness. The fracture characteristics of this ingot closely 
matched those of gram-scale TiVNb-N alloys of identical composition 
[ 21 ]. This ductile-dominated fracture behavior markedly contrasts with 
the intergranular fracture mode typically observed in low-strain alloys 
at intermediate temperatures. The observed fracture morphology, 
coupled with the extended dimple region, provides evidence for the 
alloy ’ s crack propagation resistance across the 400 – 800 
◦
C temperature 
range.

3.3. Strengthening of kilogram-scale TiVNb-N alloy by heat treatment
3.3.1. Evolution of microstructure morphology and composition after heat 
treatment
As previously discussed, the rapid cooling rates at the ingot bottom 
during solidification suppress precipitate formation, resulting in non- 
equilibrium microstructures. It may promote heterogeneous precipita -
tion during elevated temperature service, potentially causing localized 
performance variations. Therefore, aging treatment is essential to 
regulate uniform precipitate distribution throughout the alloy.
For most alloys, the optimal aging temperature is 600 
◦
C, as this 
regime balances precipitate nucleation and diffusion kinetics while 
avoiding excessive coarsening or dissolution at higher temperatures. 
Supporting evidence comes from studies on V-4Cr-4Ti alloys aged at 
500 – 700 
◦
C for 10 h, where interstitial C and N atoms combine with Ti to 
form Ti (CN) precipitates, significantly enhancing mechanical perfor -
mance [ 31 ]. Herein, in this study an aging treatment of 600 
◦
C for 8 h 
was employed to thermally activate precipitate formation, thereby 
improving microstructural homogeneity and optimizing the ingot ’ s 
overall mechanical properties.
Specimens sectioned along the ingot ’ s Y-axis were subjected to heat 
treatment and subsequent characterization. Fig. 7 presents the post- 
treatment morphology. Comparative metallographic analysis between 
as-cast and heat-treated conditions shows the matrix grain size increased 
only marginally from 119 μ m to 123 μ m in the upper region ( Fig. 7 a). 
Precipitates exhibited an increase in amounts and dispersion ( Fig. 7 a1), 
attributable to increased atomic diffusion under 600 
◦
C. The bottom 
region maintained its characteristic coarse-grained structure (~300 μ m) 
with subgrains (~40 μ m) ( Fig. 7 b), though now containing lamellar 
precipitates with dimensions similar to those in the upper region ( Fig. 7
b1). TEM analysis of aged specimens revealed lamellar precipitates a 
discontinuous distribution pattern along GBs while also being dispersed 
within grain interiors. It suggests the 600 
◦
C thermal treatment provided 
insufficient thermodynamic driving force to overcome the energy bar -

Mechanical property testing results demonstrate that the aging 
treatment enhances the alloy ’ s hardness and strength. The hardness of 
the bottom region increased from 365.3 HV to 415.7 ± 3.6 HV, while 
other regions improved from 348.3 ± 1.2 HV to 401.0 ± 6.2 HV. Fig. 8 a

compression, suggesting effective strengthening through precipitate 
pinning. The remarkable morphological stability ( Figs. 9 a2-c2), evi -
denced by the absence of precipitate torsion, correlates with the alloy ’ s 
exceptional strength retention across the entire temperature range.

4. Conclusion
A 4 kg TiVNb-N medium-entropy alloy ingot was successfully 
fabricated using levitation melting technology. Through a systematic 
investigation of microstructure, deformation behavior and heat treat -
ment effects, we revealed the critical role of FCC-Ti(N) precipitates in 
achieving strength-ductility synergy. The key findings are:
(1) The alloy ingot exhibits a dual-phase structure consisting of a 
BCC matrix and FCC-Ti(N) precipitates. The matrix has coarse equiaxed 
grains, while FCC precipitates display lamellar morphology with multi- 
directional chain-like distribution within the grain and near-continuous
