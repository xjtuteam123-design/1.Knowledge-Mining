ABSTRACT 
During laser repairing of Ni-based single crystal (SX) superalloys, stray grain (SGs) formation leads to a dete -
rioration of high-temperature properties and hot cracking. The main goal is to prevent SGs formation from 
forming, which controls the local solidification front thermal gradient ( G ) and solidification velocity ( V ). 
Meanwhile, the melt pool geometry, which is influenced by melt convection, has a significant impact on G and V . 
Here, experiments and theoretical calculations were used to study the evolution of the melt pool geometry during 
melt convection and its effects on the formation of SGs. Melt convection, i.e., Marangoni convection, out -
performed thermal conduction in the SX superalloys melt pool during directed energy deposition, as evidenced 
by the estimated Peclet number and Prandtl number. The integrative impact of laser power, scan velocity, and 
beam diameters exacerbated Marangoni convection as the laser energy density distribution increased. Marangoni 
convection caused the melt pool shape to change from a near-semicircle to an undulating morphology, promoting 
the oriented-to-misoriented crystal growth transition and resulting in high stray grain sensitivity. The heat and 
mass transport in front of the dendrite tips was altered by Marangoni convection, which aggravated the deviation 
of the epitaxial dendrite orientation with respect to the substrate. This work provides an in-depth insight into 
controlling SG formation during additive manufacturing of SX superalloys. 
1. Introduction 
Single-crystal (SX) Ni-based superalloys have been critical materials 
used in turbine blades and vanes due to their excellent strength, corro -
sion resistance, and especially their creep and fatigue resistance at 
elevated temperatures [1 – 5] . SX blades have often been scrapped after 
long service in a high temperature and pressure environment due to the 
damage resulting from abrasion and cracks [6,7] . The demand for the 
repair of broken SX blades is considerably increased due to extremely 
expensive replacement costs [8,9] . 
Due to its large temperature gradients, high cooling rate, and pre -
cision, directed energy deposition (DED), a metal-based 3D-printing 
technology, has been utilized to repair SX components [7,10 – 15] . The 
primary challenge to successful SX repairing is the preservation of the SX 
microstructure in the repair zone [16] . In Ni-based SX superalloys, 
however, stray grains (SGs), defined as equiaxed grains or misoriented 
columnar grains, were highly likely to develop [17] . SGs introduce grain 
boundaries (GBs) to deteriorate the high-temperature properties of SX 
[18] . Furthermore, SGs enhance the initiation and propagation of hot 
cracks, which leads to the failure of single crystal components [19,20] . 
As a result, suppressing the production of SGs in the repaired zone is 
critical for successful SX repair. 
Based on the location of formation of SGs, they could be classified as 
surface equiaxed grains, top equiaxed grains, bottom equiaxed grains, and 
fusion interface misoriented columnar grains. The unmelted powder 
particles sintered to the surface of the deposit, leading to the formation of 
surface equiaxed grains by heterogeneous nucleation [12] . Top equiaxed 
grains commonly arise from the columnar-to-equiaxed transition (CET) 
ahead of the solid-liquid interface. The discovery of the underlying pro -
cesses of CET has sparked a lot of research. Hunt [21] proposed an 
analytical heat flow model of CET during casting as a criterion for whole 
equiaxed grains or columnar grains. By combining Hunt ’ s CET model 
with the KGT model, Gaumann et al. [12] presented a CET model 
appropriate for fast solidification, such as laser process [22] . The forma -
tion capacity of SGs (the volume fraction of SGs, ϕ ) can be derived from 
the modified CET model with the expressions as follows [12,23] :

numbers were introduced to elucidate the competition of influencing

convection were then uncovered and linked to the formation of SGs. 
Further, the deviation of the columnar dendrites ’ growth direction was 
unveiled with the unique heat and mass transfers caused by the Mar -

deeper understanding of SX superalloys ’ solidification and microstruc -
ture evolution to reduce SGs under additive manufacturing conditions. 
2. Experimental details 
2.1. Materials and methods 
A third-generation SX superalloy powder (CMSX ® -10) with a parti -
cle size of 45 – 100 µ m was chosen as the feedstock material. The selected 
substrate was a second-generation SX superalloy. Table 1 lists the 
chemical compositions of the substrate and the powder. All samples 
were processed into a cylinder (diameter = 16 mm, thickness = 5 mm), 
while the surface for laser processing was parallel to the (001) crystal

The laser-based DED system contained a YLS-5000 system with a 
continuous-wave ytterbium fiber laser (IPG Photonics), a KUKA robot 
for carrying out the processing path, a powder feeding system (GTV MF-

customized processing chamber with an inert argon atmosphere.

of the melt pool geometry via altering processing parameters. The 
samples were divided into eleven groups by the variation of the laser 
power, beam scan velocity, and beam diameter, as listed in Table 2 . 
Beam diameter variation was performed by changing the amount of 
defocusing. The DED experiments were performed to reveal the effects

4.2. Mechanisms of the melt pool geometry transformation 
As DED belongs to the laser conduction mode, the thermal convec -
tion within the melt pool was Marangoni convection [35,37,40] . 
Therefore, the undulating melt pool geometry was dependent on the 
Marangoni convection. 
4.2.1. Influencing factors of Marangoni convection 
Three factors determined the Marangoni convection, including the 
material ’ s physical properties, the surface-active agent, and the power

4.4. Epitaxy inhomogeneity and deviation with Marangoni convection 
Microstructure inhomogeneity and epitaxy deviation were also 
caused by Marangoni convection. The non-uniform distribution of cells 
and columnar dendrites generated the inhomogeneous microstructure. 
Columnar dendrites were detected at the melt pool ’ s centerline in the 
first six layers of the same height, with cells produced close to the melt 
pool borders ( Fig. 8 a). This event occurred as a result of the Marangoni 
convection altering the constitutional supercooling. 
Due to non-equilibrium solidification during laser surface remelting 
and DED, solute pile-ups developed in front of the dendritic tips. The 
liquidus temperature dropped as the solute concentration increased 
when the solute partition coefficient, k , was less than one, causing 
constitutional supercooling and influencing dendrites development. As 
the constitutional supercooling increased, the dendrite transformed 
from a cellular crystal to a columnar dendrite [26] . The accumulated 
solute produced at the upstream (edge) dendrite tips was transported to 
the downstream (center) solidification front via fluid circulation. The 
elemental distribution of Ta, Al, and Re around the center obtained by 
EPMA supported the increase in solute concentration near the center 
( Fig. 17 ). Al and Ta were found to be segregated at the fluid interface by 
Marangoni convection, whereas Re ( k > 1) was scarce in the same lo -
cations. Thus, the larger solute concentration formed in the downstream 
dendrite tips induced C

Consequently, the center had a higher constitutional supercooling as 
opposed to the edges ( Fig. 18 b and c). Finally, the columnar dendrites

The epitaxy deviation was ascribed to the epitaxial growth direction 
varying from the substrate ’ s original dendrite orientation by Marangoni 
convection during DED. Cells occupied the initially several layers, with 
the heat flux direction (i.e., the direction of G ) controlling cell devel -
opment [11] . The direction of G was normal to the solid-liquid interface 
i.e., the melt pool geometry. There were two slopes, symmetrically 
distributed around the melt pool ’ s center, as shown in Fig. 8 a. Therefore, 
the cells tilted to the normal of the slops, causing the orientation devi -
ation from the substrate ’ s original orientation. This situation, however, 
solely evaluated the melt pool without convection. As the Marangoni 
convection took the hot fluid from the surface to the center, there was an 
extra temperature gradient along the inverse direction of the fluid flow. 
Consequently, the real direction of G was a mix of slope normal and the 
inverse direction of fluid flow, causing the cells to diverge even further 
from their initial orientation. In terms of aforementioned composition

5. Conclusions 
Single-crystal Ni-based superalloys were processed via laser remelt -
ing and DED under different parameters combined with theoretical 
calculations to uncover the changes in the melt pool geometry and ef -
fects on the epitaxy. The major conclusions were extracted as followed: 
(1) As laser power increased or the laser beam diameter and scan 
velocity decreased, the melt pool geometry changed from a near 
semicircular shape to an unusual, undulating one. The competi -
tion between heat convection and heat conduction was credited 
for the melt pool geometry change. 
(2) The deposit presented a “ ω -type ” melt pool morphology resulted 
from the Marangoni convection in the melt pool during DED 
processing. In the first several layers, the dendrites deviated from 
the substrate crystal orientation but retained the crystal growth 
direction in the following layers until almost the end of the de -
posit. The max orientation deviation of the deposit reached 14.7
◦
. 
(3) The SX superalloys have a higher Prandtl number ( Pr = 0.6) and 
a high Pe ( Pe = 9.97 >> 1), which demonstrated that heat con -
vection highly dominated the heat transport. The melt pool was 
prone to form an unusual, undulating morphology for the SX 
substrate. 
(4) Increasing laser power or reducing laser beam diameter 
dramatically enhanced the Marangoni convection, resulting in an 
undulating melt pool formation. The Marangoni convection was 
decreased marginally by increasing the laser scan velocity. The 
melt pool geometry changed to a nearly semicircular form as a 
result of the increased solidification velocity, shortening the 
duration of Marangoni convection. The increase in solidification 
velocity shortened the duration of Marangoni convection, pro -
moting the melt pool geometry to change to a nearly semicircular 
shape. 
(5) The relationship between the melt pool geometry and the crystal 
growth direction transitions was established at any arbitrary 
location. The unusual, undulating melt pool promoted crystal 
growth “ oriented-to-misoriented transitions ” (OMTs), intro -
ducing intersection points with high sensitivity of strain grains. 
(6) Marangoni convection influenced the temperature and solute 
fields on the solidification front, resulting in an inhomogeneous 
dendritic morphology with a non-uniform distribution of cells 
and columnar dendrites. The Marangoni convection also shifted 
the direction of cell development and brought about the non- 
uniform composition distribution that caused epitaxy deviation.
