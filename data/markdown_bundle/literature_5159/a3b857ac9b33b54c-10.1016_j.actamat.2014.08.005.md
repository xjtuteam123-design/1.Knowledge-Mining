Abstract
In this work, the remarkable high-temperature strength properties of monocrystalline c0-hardened Ni-base superalloys are reviewed
with the goal of understanding better the important role played by the c/c0 lattice misﬁt d with respect to microstructural stability and
high-temperature creep and fatigue resistance. In particular, attention is focussed on the phenomenon of microstructural degradation by
directional coarsening in the form of c/c
0 rafting which also occurs in turbine blades in service. The role played by the lattice misﬁt (which
is negative for all currently used Ni-base superalloys) is elucidated with respect to its sign, magnitude and temperature dependence. Based
on a detailed discussion of high-temperature deformation involving c/c0 rafting, it is argued that superalloys with a positive c/c0 lattice
misﬁt should exhibit better high-temperature strength properties than otherwise similar superalloys with a negative misﬁt. In this context,
the new class of c
0-hardened Co-base superalloys is of special interest. These alloys have promising high-temperature strength properties
and possess a positive lattice mismatch. It is suggested that this positive mismatch is one of the beneﬁcial factors that contribute to the
promising properties of these alloys and that this aspect should be explored further.

1. Introduction
The microstructural deformation mechanisms responsi-
ble for the remarkable high-temperature strength proper-
ties of monocrystalline c
0-hardened Ni-base superalloys
have been studied in considerable detail for some decades.
In the Ni-base superalloys studied in the past (e.g.
SRR99, SC16, CMSX-4), the c
0 precipitate phase is the
major phase, and can occupy a volume fraction of up to
more than 70%. To date, these alloys possess the best
combination of high-temperature creep and fatigue
strength and microstructural stability. These are essential
requirements for use in turbine blades in aircrafts and in
stationary gas turbines, which must oﬀer the best possible
resistance against severe high-temperature creep and ther-
momechanical fatigue loading in service. For recent sur-
veys of the current state of knowledge, reference is made
to some reviews [1–3], the monograph by Reed [4] and
the Proceedings of the 12th International Symposium on
Superalloys [5].
An important phenomenon occurring in Ni-base
superalloys subjected to creep and thermomechanical fati-
gue (during start-up and shut-down) at high temperatures
is directional coarsening, so-called rafting, which occurs
in unidirectional creep loading [1–4,6–12] and also under
shear creep loading [13]. Except at high temperatures
and rather low stresses, rafting is accompanied by a

2. Characteristics of c/c0 rafting at high temperatures
2.1. General features of rafting in high-temperature creep
Most studies of rafting in monocrystalline Ni-base
superalloys dealt with rafting during tensile high-tempera-
ture creep (e.g. [1–3,6–10]). In this case, the rafts develop
perpendicular to the stress axis, in agreement with the
model that was ﬁrst advanced by Pineau [32]. For positive
stresses r and negative misﬁt d, i.e. r /C2 d < 0, this model
predicts so-called N-type rafts perpendicular to the stress
axis, whereas, for r /C2 d > 0, so-called P-type rafts [33]
would develop parallel to the stress axis.
Initially, after small creep strains, the rafts of the major
c0 phase are embedded in the minor c-phase. Then, in the
absence of cutting of the c0 rafts, dislocation glide is con-
ﬁned to the softer c channels, and the rate-controlling
deformation mechanism consists of the glide–climb motion
of dislocations along the c/c0 interface [7,8], as ﬁrst
envisaged by Carry and Strudel [34]. At larger strains,
the situation is reversed. Now the minor c phase is embed-
ded in the major c0 phase. Fredholm and Strudel character-
ised this morphological change in terms of a stereological
parameter which deﬁnes the so-called connectivity [35].
Plastic deformation proceeds by dislocation glide in the
softer c phase until large enough internal forward stresses
are built up in the harder c
0 phase, permitting cutting of
the c0 phase [8,36], accompanied by progressive softening,
as observed at higher creep strains [11,37–39].
2.2. General features of rafting in thermomechanical fatigue
Blades subjected to service conditions experience not
only creep in the direction of the centrifugal forces but also
fatigue, in particular thermomechanical fatigue (TMF) in
the form of severe simultaneous changes in temperature
and stress that occur at the leading edges of turbine blades

engine. In the laboratory, TMF loading is simulated by dif-

in all cases they are of the sigmoidal type with an inﬂection

mary, secondary and tertiary creep [41]. When viewing

details of the three creep curves become apparent which
are completely missed otherwise. It follows that ﬁne details
of these more complex creep curves, as shown in Fig. 1 b,
cannot be described satisfactorily by the conventional
terms “primary, secondary and tertiary creep ”. When it
comes to the discussion of speciﬁc mechanisms and stages
of plastic deformation, attention must be paid to these fre-
quently overlooked details. In particular, referring to the
creep curves in the regime of rafting at temperatures of
950 and 1100 /C176C (see Fig. 1 b), it is important to note that
two distinct cases of high-temperature creep can be
distinguished, namely below and above about 1000 /C176C.
At 950 /C176C, the creep rate decreases at ﬁrst, indicative of
hardening, and then passes through a minimum. Rafting
commences only after the minimum creep rate has been
passed, accompanied by softening (see [2–4,7,8]). In con-
trast, at a substantially higher temperature of 1100 /C176C,
the creep curve of the alloy CMSX-4 has not only one
but two minima. Similar creep curves with two minima
of the creep rate have also been observed for the superalloy
SRR99 at 1050 /C176C [42,43], as well as for CMSX-4 creep-
deformed at 1150 /C176C [44], and more recently on two
Ru-containing superalloys, creep-deformed at 1180 /C176C
[45]. In the early work on CMSX-4, creep-deformed at
1100 /C176C [37,39], it was shown by detailed TEM and SEM
studies that rafting commences soon as the ﬁrst minimum
of the creep rate has been passed, as the creep rate
increases, i.e. during softening, and continues progressively
subsequently as the strain rate decreases, i.e. during strain
hardening, until the absolute minimum creep rate is
attained. The described behaviour, characterised by two
minimum creep rates and the onset and progressing of raf-
ting, both during softening and subsequent hardening, well
before the absolute minimum of the creep rate, contrasts
markedly with that observed at 950 /C176C. Since the process
of rafting depends on plastic deformation by dislocation
glide and climb and involves in particular time-dependent
mass transfer by diﬀusion [1–13], it is not surprising that,
at “very” high temperatures such as 1000 /C176C or more and
at a low stress, rafting occurs earlier than at 900 or
950 /C176C. The corresponding changes in the shape of the
creep curve reﬂect the complex superposition of the
described hardening and softening stages. The cited exper-
imental data on SRR99, CMSX-4 and some Ru-containing
superalloys suggest that the transition between the two
types of creep and rafting behaviour at approximately
1000 /C176C (depending on stress) is a general phenomenon.
In Section 5, attention will be drawn to a related behaviour
in the case of a c
0-hardened Co-base superalloy.

2.5. The eﬀects of rafting perpendicular and parallel to the
stress axis on the high-temperature creep and fatigue
behaviour
It is of interest to explore how rafting during tensile
creep, perpendicular to the stress axis in the case of nega-
tive misﬁt and parallel to the stress axis for positive misﬁt,
would aﬀect the high-temperature creep and fatigue behav-
iour. Due to the lack of promising Ni-base superalloys with
a positive lattice misﬁt, this problem can be investigated in
an indirect way on Ni-base superalloys with negative misﬁt
that have been pre-rafted in either tension or compression
and then contain rafts lying perpendicular or parallel to the
stress axis, respectively [3,14,22].
In such studies, it was found, as shown in Fig. 2, that the
initial tensile creep deformation at temperatures of 1000
and 1050 /C176C of specimens containing rafts lying parallel
to the stress axis is smaller than that of specimens in which
rafts perpendicular to the stress axis have developed [3,22].
After some per cent of strain, the “unnatural” rafts parallel
to the stress axis become unstable and transform into an
untidy, less-resistant c/c
0 microstructure. Only then does
the strain increase more rapidly. These results were veriﬁed
subsequently by Shui et al. [47]. In an alloy with positive
misﬁt, the rafts parallel to the stress axis are expected to
remain stable, and the beneﬁcial enhanced creep resistance
could then be exploited advantageously. In the assessment
of details of these data, some caution is required, because
interfacial dislocation networks are introduced during
pre-rafting which modify the internal stress state. Hence,
the rafts in pre-rafted material diﬀer from those that would
form in an alloy with positive misﬁt, and the subsequent
deformation will vary accordingly. Parts of this aspect have
been studied by TEM [22,47–49]. However, more detailed
work is necessary to fully clarify this complex situation.
Aside from that, and as discussed in Section 3, several
reasons can be stated why specimens containing rafts par-
allel to the stress axis are expected to exhibit a reduced
creep rate. Here, attention is drawn to the fact that, in
[0 0 1]-orientated crystals, the rate-determining glide–climb
motion of interfacial dislocations along vertical c/c
0 inter-
faces is expected to be slower than along horizontal c/c0
interfaces [22,49]. This behaviour depends on the magni-
tude of the component of the Peach–Koehler force that
acts on the edge components of the interfacial dislocations
which are responsible for the climb motion. One ﬁnds, for
purely geometric reasons, that the interfacial dislocations
moving on a vertical c/c
0 interface experience a smaller

3. The eﬀect of the lattice misﬁt on the high-temperature
deformation resistance, i.e. the material strength
The proposed advantages of a positive lattice misﬁt rest
partly on the claim that a c/c0 microstructure with a posi-
tive lattice mismatch exhibits a larger deformation resis-
tance than one with a negative lattice mismatch. So far,
the eﬀect of a positive lattice misﬁt has not been modelled
satisfactorily. In an earlier study, Mu ¨ ller et al. [50] dis-
cussed the creep deformation behaviour for positive and
negative lattice mismatch, based on ﬁnite-element compu-
tations of the stress states in the c
0 and c phase, the appli-
cation of an equivalent stress concept and a general
Norton-type creep law, without explicit consideration of
the dislocation mechanisms discussed here. Only minor,
and not very conclusive, diﬀerences were found between
the results obtained for positive and negative mismatch.
Given the lack of a rigorous model, some qualitative con-
siderations of the coherency stress states in the deforming
c channels are helpful to understand the yielding under a
tensile stress which is of interest here. For negative lattice
misﬁt, biaxial compressive coherency stresses prevail in
the c channels and will aid and promote tensile deforma-
tion in the horizontal c channels which lie perpendicular
to the stress axis. On the other hand, for a positive lattice
misﬁt, biaxial tensile coherency stresses will act in the c
channels and will reinforce the external tensile stress in
the vertical channels which lie parallel to the stress axis.
Hence, deformation is expected to commence in the two
sets of orthogonal vertical channels. This implies that the
deforming volume will be twice as large as in the case of
the deforming horizontal c channels for negative misﬁt
alloys which might facilitate the deformation. On the other
hand, the deformation will be hindered somewhat by the
transverse tensile coherency stresses. Recent preliminary
results [51], based on the earlier model of Svoboda and
Luka´sˇ [46], indicate that, in an alloy with positive misﬁt,
the creep rate is reduced. In another recent work, Gao
et al. studied the yielding of a Co-based superalloy mono-
crystal by 3-D discrete dislocation dynamics, taking into
account details of dislocation glide behaviour in c/c
0 micro-
structures [52]. The results of these authors indicate that
the deformation resistance at room temperature is larger
for a positive misﬁt alloy. Taking all these facts together,
there is increasing evidence that a positive misﬁt enhances
the deformation resistance. Nonetheless, the detailed
behaviour cannot be predicted reliably without a more rig-
orous micromechanical model becoming available.
Such a model needs to consider not only the dislocation
mechanisms on the microscale. The following simple qual-
itative arguments suggest that, in addition, the eﬀects of the
constraints on a larger scale should be taken into account.
For an alloy with the cuboidal c
0 microstructure and nega-
tive lattice misﬁt, the deforming horizontal c channels are
arranged in series with the almost non-deforming slabs of
material containing the cuboidal c0 precipitates surrounded

4. Summary of possible advantages of a positive c0/c lattice
misﬁt
The preceding discussion can be summarised as follows.
Aside from all other properties, a superalloy with positive
lattice misﬁt oﬀers the following possible beneﬁcial eﬀects:
/C15 The magnitude | d| of the misﬁt parameter would be
larger at low temperatures and smaller at the higher
temperatures, as demanded in the work of Svoboda
and Luka´sˇ for better overall creep resistance [46].
/C15 If rafting occurs (e.g. in a turbine blade), rafts would
form parallel to the applied tensile stress with the follow-
ing advantages.
/C15 Because of the slower glide–climb motion of dislocations
along the c/c
0 interfaces of these rafts parallel to the
stress axis and because of the constraints exerted by
the non-deforming material, the creep strain rate would
be reduced.
/C15 At the same time, the c
0-rafts lying parallel to the stress
axis would hinder eﬀectively the propagation of fatigue
cracks perpendicular to the stress axis and would thus
enhance the fatigue resistance.
5. The importance of positive c/c0 lattice misﬁt in the new c0-
hardened Co-base superalloys
In view of the nominal advantages of a positive c/c0 lat-
tice misﬁt, it is concluded that superalloys with a positive
lattice misﬁt could be ideal superalloys, provided that, in
addition, they possess all other properties that are essential
for good high-temperature strength such as high solidus,
liquidus and c
0 solvus temperatures, good solid-solution
hardening of the c phase, good microstructural stability,
etc. Hence, the task now is to search for alloys which satisfy
these demands. At present, none of the currently available
Ni-base superalloys oﬀer this combination of properties.
On the other hand, the new c0-hardened Co-base super-
alloys appear to be promising candidates to meet these
requirements. These alloys, which have only been the sub-
ject of research for a few years since their discovery by Sato
et al. in 2006 [23], possess a number of attractive properties
[24–30]. Like Ni-base superalloys, they exhibit the

6. Concluding remarks
Based on available experimental data and results of
some preliminary modelling, the role of the lattice misﬁt
parameter in c0-hardened Ni-base superalloys has been
reviewed with respect to the microstructural stability and
the creep and the fatigue resistance at high temperatures.
It is proposed that, for a number of diﬀerent reasons,
superalloys with a positive instead of a negative misﬁt
parameter could provide better high-temperature strength
than otherwise similar alloys with a negative mismatch. It
is desirable to check some aspects of the proposed beneﬁ-
cial eﬀects of a positive lattice misﬁt by detailed microme-
chanical modelling and speciﬁcally designed experiments.
Concerning the new Co-base c
0-hardened superalloys, it is
suggested that the positive lattice misﬁt of these alloys is
probably one important factor among others that make
these materials attractive candidates for future high-tem-
perature applications.
