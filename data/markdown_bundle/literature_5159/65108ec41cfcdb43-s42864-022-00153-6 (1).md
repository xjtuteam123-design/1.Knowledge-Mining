Abstract
Tungsten (W) materials are gaining more and more attention due to the extended applications of metallic systems in the 
extreme environments. Given W’s unique characteristics like room-temperature brittleness, additive manufacturing (AM) 
techniques could give them a higher design flexibility and manufacturability. With the growing focus and thriving develop-
ment of W-faced AM techniques, since the mechanical performance of additively manufactured W parts is still unsatisfactory, 
a critical review to further explore the possibilities of combining W and AM processes is urgently needed. In this review, 
we systematically explain the fundamentals of AM processes for W materials. Following the traditional classification, we 
further discuss the widely used AM processes including wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM), electron beam melting 
(EBM), laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), laser direct energy deposition (laser DED), and other modified yet emergent AM 
techniques. Accordingly, since additively manufacturing W materials is processing parameter-sensitive, we illustrated the 
effects of various important processing parameters on the AM process control and final parts’ quality. With this detailed 
understanding, various categories of AM-compatible W materials (i.e., pure W, W alloys, and W composites) were pre-
sented, and their general mechanical performance, distinct role (particularly the role of different alloying elements and added 
secondary-phase particles in W), and application-oriented benefits have been summarized. After clarifying the current status, 
main challenges, and triumphant successes for additively manufacturing W materials, we further provide a concise prospect 
into the development of additively manufactured (AMed) W materials by integrating potential fabrication, measurement, 
alloy design, and application’s considerations. In summary, this critical review investigates the fundamental and practical 
problems crucially limiting the applications of AMed W materials, and the comprehensive discussion concentrates the history 
of the development and combination between AM techniques and W design. All the understanding is of great importance 
to achieving foreseeable successful future applications of AMed W materials.

1 Introduction
Tungsten (W) and its alloys are unique, due to their high 
melting point (~ 3370 ◦C ), supreme mechanical properties 
(> 900 MPa expected ultimate tensile strength and > 400 
GPa Young’s modulus), low thermal expansion coeffi -
cient (< 4 µm⋅ (m⋅K)−1, reasonable thermal conductivity 
(> 150 W ⋅(m⋅K)−1) [1], and high chemical stability (e.g., 
high arc erosion resistance [2 ]). It has been ideally used as 
the materials for high-temperature and radiation-sustaining 
environments [3 ] (e.g., as collimators, heat sink materials 
[2], high-power integrated circuits [3 , 4], electrical switch 
contacts [5], and rocket nozzles [6]).
These applications inevitably require a flexible geometry 
design and a better interfacial joining and adhesion from 
“integrated molding”. Therefore, compared with the tradi-
tional powder metallurgy for W materials, current additive 
manufacturing (AM) techniques show a more significant 
potential to achieve the goal of fabricating W parts in a flex-
ible and integrated way, since these processing routes require 
no pre-set molds.
However, even with all these benefits, manufacturing 
and processing W and W-related materials in an additive 
way is still challenging, and few successful cases have been 
reported with high densification ratio and eliminated defects 
(e.g., microcracks) [7].
The primary reasons for these bottlenecks are more evi-
dent, with the recent research progress: In the liquid state, 
W has a high viscosity (8 × 10−3 Pa⋅s), high surface tension 
(2.361 N⋅m−1) [8], poor flowability, reasonable but highly 
dynamic laser absorptivity [9 ], and rapid solidification rate 
due to its high thermal conductivity [10], which could intro-
duce easy balling [11] and cavities during AM, as compared 
with other metals in Fig. 1 [1, 11].
In the solid-state, due to the body center cubic (BCC) 
structures of W, most of the thermal processes would require 
higher activation energy [3 ], because the slip mechanism 
changes with temperature [12, 13]. Dislocation slip in BCC 
metals is generally enabled by the thermally activated kink 
pairs’ nucleation and migration for screw dislocations. At 
low temperature, slip occurs on a slip system with high 
Peierls stress because only this system has a low enough 
activation energy barrier to generate the kink pairs to make 
dislocations mobile. The dislocation mobility is generally 
very low, contributing to limited plasticity. Due to the super 
high resistance to dislocation motion, fracture occurs before 
yielding. At higher temperatures, the mobility of screw dis-
locations gradually becomes comparable to that of edge 
dislocations, the phonon-drag mechanism start to control 
the dislocation motion, and plastic deformation can occur 
before fracture [12, 13]. With these characteristics, W is 
born with a high-ductility-to-brittle transition temperature 
(DBTT) [14], which lacks the room-temperature ductility 
and makes the fabrication hard to achieve. Impurities such as 
oxygen, nitrogen, or carbon can further increase the DBTT. 
In addition, the advanced-manufactured W parts are sus-
ceptible to intragranular cracking (IGC) [15] (part of which 
is oxygen-induced) and may be hard to be joined to other 
parts [16], which adds to the cost and difficulty of additively 
manufacturing W materials[10].
In this review, we will first go through the fundamentals 
for additively manufactured (AMed) W materials, and this 
discussion will consider additive manufacturing techniques’ 
basic melting, flowing, and solidification (particularly grain 
growth) processes in and near the molten pool. The guidance 
for physical and numerical phenomenon understanding and 
the comparison with traditional processing (e.g., sintering 
dynamics) will be provided.
With this fundamental knowledge, we will look into the 
W materials’ main AM techniques by classifying energy 
source and power input setup. Categories of wire arc addi-
tive manufacturing (WAAM, by arc melting), electron beam 
melting (EBM), laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), and laser-
directed energy deposition (laser DED) would be our main 
focus, and the state-of-art data regarding W materials’ qual-
ity will be presented.

Of course, more detailed molten pool dynamics is 
constantly extracted based on theories, experiments, and 
modeling advances [28]. The important message here is 
that with these descriptions, together with the W materi-
als thermophysical properties (like melting point, thermal 
conductivity, etc.), the molten pool boundaries and details 
with the inputs from the flow dynamics could be set up 
[10, 29] (e.g., the Diablo thermal model, etc. [10]), as 
shown in Fig.  2a.
In solid state, for the traditional processing techniques 
like sintering, what happens in W is governed by the pow -
der densification process and the subsequent grain growth, 
as shown in Fig.  2b [30, 31]. As illustrated by Fig.  2b, the 
powder densification process is usually dominant from 
1200 to 1700 °C and could already guarantee a relative 
density of > 90%, whereas the grain growth process would 
significantly speed up from 1700 to 2000 °C [31]. More 
specifically, the densification rate during the powder sin-
tering process could have a maximum value at ~ 1450 °C 
[31], which implies densification and grain growth have 
totally different kinetics and dynamics. The Arrhenius 
relationship-determined densification activation energy 
for powder sintering process will greatly determine the 
final shrinkage and change the sintering effectiveness [31]. 
Previous studies have confirmed that smaller W powder 
size could significantly lower the densification activation 
energy: 484 kJ⋅ mol−1 for micro-W powder [30] is reduced 
to 362 kJ⋅mol−1. This activation energy range also partially 
confirms the critical role of the grain boundary (GB) diffu-
sion in the sintering process [30].
However, in the AM process, the significant difference 
is that after the molten pool is set up and the localized 
materials have been added and shaped, (at least part of) the 
W materials will melt first, nucleate to solidify, and finally 
grow the grains. As shown in Fig.  2b, the thermal history 
during AM is reversely different from that of the traditional 
processing techniques with W powders. Therefore, the den-
sification range is directly bypassed, as the temperature is 
lowered from above a specific melting point. Besides, the 
melting and solidification process in AM could even occur 
back and forth (e.g., multiple times of remelting [12]), 
compared with the traditional near-equilibrium process-
ing like sintering.
With these differences, we could see that AM only shares 
the similarities in grain growth with the traditional process-
ing techniques like sintering. Since the melting is briefly 
discussed previously and the solidification nucleation pro-
cess is materials-specific (the understanding of which could 
be referred to Ref. [32]), we would focus only on the grain 
growth description process after nucleation in additive man-
ufacturing for W materials.

3.1 Arc melting
In 1955, Klopp et al. [38, 39] produced W–Re alloys by arc 
melting. At that time, the traditional arc melting is for bulk 
materials and joining parts’ processing. With the develop-
ment of the wire-shape materials and tailored processing 
procedures, arc melting idea has been evolving to yield many 
subsequent AM techniques like wire arc additive manufac-
turing (WAAM).
During WAAM, it requires an electric arc as the heat 
source and metallic wire as the feedstock, as shown in 
Fig. 3a [40]. Compared with electron and laser beam, arc-
provided volumetric energy density is still tricky to get 
W materials melt quickly, so the research into this area is 
limited [40, 41]. Besides, compared with WAAM for other 
metals like Al alloys [42], WAAM for W still requires strict 
oxygen control [40].
Up until now, with the reported results of tungsten 
inert gas welding (TIG) as the power source, WAAM 
manufacturing could already create W materials

3.2 Electron beam melting (EBM)
EBM techniques evolved from almost the same time as arc 
melting for W materials [38, 39]. EBM uses an electron 
beam as the heat source and controls with electromagnetic 
lens, as shown in Fig. 3b [43]. Generally speaking, compared 
with the laser-based AM techniques, the electron beam could 
offer higher beam power (up to 3 KW, while LPBF usually 
has < 1 KW [44]) [12]. One major drawback of EBM is the 
complexity of electron focus and control systems, as dem-
onstrated by Fig.  3b. Moreover, since the EBM needs the 
electron travelling, the whole system is naturally equipped 
with a high vacuum level after He/N2 purge [12, 44].
Since the electrons are measured by currents when trave-
ling, the processing window of EBM processes is usually 
depicted by using scanning speed and current (or current 
density) [44], as shown in Fig.  4a. With the suitable selec-
tion of EBM processes and parameters, columnar crys-
tal structure after epitaxial growth could be more easily 
achieved in EBMed W materials [44].
Currently, EBM-manufactured W materials could achieve 
a relative density of ~ 99.5% [44]. More importantly, with 
the EBM technology, facilitated by the suitable substrate 
preheating, the cracking could be solved in AM for W and 
W-related materials [44]. Many exciting results have been 
reported with EBM-fabricated W [44, 45]. Knowing all 
these benefits, EBM has also been developed as a supple -
mentary technique for W surface treatment for a better qual-
ity, given its controlled beam size and thermally influenced 
zones of the manufactured parts [46].
3.3 Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)
Compared with WAAM and EBM processes, the majority of 
research efforts and applications have been studied in LPBF, 
whether AM for pure W or related metallic systems [3, 17]. 
LPBF could be further divided into selective laser melting 
(SLM) and selective laser sintering (SLS) [17]. However, 
for W materials specifically, SLM and SLS could both hap-
pen simultaneously under the framework of LPBF, because 
of the high melting point of W and the relative low melting 
point of other alloying elements [3].
In general, LPBF uses high-energy laser beams to melt 
powder layer by layer to achieve the 3D printed parts of W, 
as shown in Fig.  5a, c. This high energy input differs from 
EBM, because it has fewer conductivity requirements or sen-
sitivity with coherent light as the power source. For pure W 
and its alloys, LPBF could introduce a spatial temperature 
gradient of 102–104 K⋅mm−1 and a cooling rate higher than 
 104 K⋅s−1 [14].
The main challenge of using LPBF methods for W mate-
rials is that virtually all LPBF-manufactured W parts show 
some degree of cracking (e.g., along grain boundaries) [12]. 
It has been suggested that this is due to the thermal stresses 
during rapid cooling [47], high von Mises stresses below 
DBTT [10], and entrapped oxygen in the pores when the 
pores coalesce [10]. Some pessimistic voice has already 
popped out that cracking issue may not be solved in LPBF-
fabricated W materials [48, 49]. Moreover, due to the wet-
tability, melting temperature difference [1], etc., the powders 
of W with other elements need pre-mixing to meet large 
direct area binding requirements between W and other ele-
ments [3].
Up until now, W materials by SLM could already achieve 
a higher density than 95% [38, 50] (as high as ~ 98.71% 
relative density in SLMed W [ 51]), and a general process -
ing window has been determined for the successful SLM

3.4 Laser directed energy deposition (laser DED)
The laser DED process directly inputs energy to the intended 
part during the processing, and the materials’ availability 
is limited to near-nozzle areas [1 ]. Though not comparable 
with the WAAM process, laser DED’s capacity to fabricate 
large parts is far beyond the EBM and LPBF processes [1].
Generally speaking, due to this configuration limita-
tion, during the DED processes, the relationship between 
the sizes of the molten pool and the powder flow is critical 
(see Fig. 5b, d)[24]. With the increasing laser energy input, 
the molten pool becomes wider and could introduce more 
W powders to the molten pool; however, if the size of the 
molten pool is larger than that of powder flow, the percent-
age of entering W will decline [1 ]. Because of this limit, 
the current DED process depends more on laser, instead of 
electron beams.
However, many problems and bottlenecks need to be 
solved, before laser DED could release its potential for W 
processing: First for laser DED processing, it could not con-
trol the surface roughness [54] and heat affected zone range 
[1] of the final printed parts. For example, high-speed and 
high-mass injection of powder introduces wavy surfaces, 
which is hard to balance with the manufacturing outputs 
[54]. Moreover, the high energy input into a focused area 
during the laser DED process could lead to the intermetallic 
formation by various equilibrium and non-equilibrium phase 
evolution like peritectic reaction [55, 56]. All these factors 
are limiting the applications of DED for W materials.
Currently, this method could produce (pure) W with a 
purity of > 99 wt.%, even on the heterogeneous substrate 
(like stainless steel) [1]. Laser DED for W alloys is attracting 
more attention than pure W. Researchers found that the rela-
tive density of W alloys fabricated by laser DED was higher 
than that fabricated by LPBF [56] because fewer tracks and 
layers are needed for laser DED to fabricate the same size 
sample since laser DED has larger laser spot size [4]. Given 
the potential huge melting temperature difference between 
W and other elements, laser DED could provide a “partially 
melt mixture” where a low melting point element will be 
the liquid phase to carry and disperse W powder particles

4 Key influencing factors
As mentioned in the last section, though these AM tech-
niques have different configurations and setups, the fun-
damental controlling factors and the presented printing 
issues (e.g., oxidation) are similar for all of them. Given 
this similarity, it is urgent for us to investigate and analyze 
these key manufacturing parameters to better control of the 
AM process and a higher quality of fabricated W parts. So, 
in this section, we will review the critical manufacturing 
parameters of oxygen contents, volumetric energy density, 
pattern design, preheating preparation, melting behavior, and 
interfacial characteristics in sequence, and their potential 
interplays will be revealed.
4.1 Oxygen contents
Oxygen level (i.e., vacuum level) is vital for successful 
W fabrication, because it controls the DBTT transition in 
most W materials [7 , 65, 66] and molten W is sensitive 
to oxygen [8 , 67]. Oxygen could origin from the oxidized 
powder surface and the residual oxygen in the chamber 
[68]. For example, in contrary to our common sense that 
W is very stable and oxidation resistant, W exposed to 
oxygen even at room temperature develops a layer of oxides 
with ~ 1 nm thickness within one hour [69], which is gen-
erally a non-trivial amount of oxygen for the fine-sized 
powder precursors [70]. Some study also indicates the gas 
entrapment due to the W’s complex shapes (e.g., polygonal 
and dendritic) [71].
During AM, the oxygen will react with W at a higher 
temperature and segregate to the GBs to cause intergranular 
cracking upon cooling [12, 48, 72]. Currently, study and 
comparison about different oxygen levels, e.g., comparing 
6.25 × 10−3 and 2.5 × 10−2 mol⋅L−1 oxygen, during the AM 
suggests the benefits of controlling oxygen for a better part 
with fewer cracks [68].
However, even at a low oxygen level with partial pressure 
of 0.01 Pa, W oxides could still be stable up to 2000 ◦C , 
based on their free energy of formation [7, 73], as shown in 
Fig. 6a. So, during the rapid solidification process associated 
with the AM, these oxides will be trapped in the system and 
act as the crack nucleation and initiation center [7], as illus-
trated by Fig.  6a3, a4. Therefore, a larger beam power and 
a larger volumetric energy density for a higher temperature 
could help reduce the oxygen content and suppress cracking 
behavior [12].
Some study also presents an interesting observation 
regarding the oxidation process during AM: Oxidation 
during AM for W materials may not directly happen 
with the built W parts, but could be introduced on the 
substrate, due to the high thermal energy inputs drained 
to the periphery [14], as shown in Fig.  6b. Therefore, it 
could be highly possible that the oxidation and the associ-
ated quality issues in AMed W materials could be solved, 
with the substrate as effective oxygen drainage. This find-
ing also implies that oxygen content plays an important 
role in preheating control, etc., because high-temperature 
preheating is only possible in the oxygen-controlled envi-
ronment [44].
Knowing all these adverse effects from oxygen and 
the potential causes for oxygen-induced cracking issues 
in W materials by AM, various techniques have been 
used to mitigate the influence from the oxygen contents 
during the processing. To control the oxygen content 
of W for AM, pre-processing including radio frequency 
induction plasma treatment proves to be effective [36]. 
Additionally, rational materials design could also reduce 
the oxygen susceptibility. For example, adding Ta has 
proven effective in reducing W’s oxygen sensitivity by 
mitigating the oxygen-induced nanopores for crack ini-
tiation [48]. Even more fundamental study of W oxida-
tion process has been conducted to quantify its oxida-
tion kinetics and dynamics (as shown in Fig.  6c), and 
the oxidation mechanism map for W materials could be 
helpful for AM purpose [67].

79]. Furthermore, even though higher volumetric energy 
density is wanted, excessive fusion could be introduced, if 
the volumetric energy density is too high [44]. The main dis-
advantage of extremely excessive power input is that it will 
disturb the melt pool and cause excessive powder particles 
to attach to the edge of the melt pool [44]. In addition, high 
energy input will also lead to the vaporization of volatile ele-
ments [80], given the high melting point of W. All of these 
factors may lead to a low-quality printing part.
As shown in Eq. (9), the volumetric energy density would 
also be related to the pattern design, which will be discussed 
in the following Sect. 4.3 in detail.
4.3 Scanning pattern
Fundamentally, the pattern design determines the thermal 
history of the processed W materials. Indeed, the pattern 
design in AM of W gives the needed flexibility for various 
applications and distinguishes AM techniques from the tra-
ditional ones [17, 28]. Specifically, due to the focused energy 
input (e.g., electron beam with a spot size of ~ 100–1000 µm 
and laser beam with a spot size of ~ 10–500 µm [81]), the 
pattern design could have local differences, which the tradi-
tional manufacturing techniques could not achieve.
When it comes to the local difference, together with 
the Rosenthal model [25] and these pattern parameters, 
how each scanned point behaves could be determined. 
For example, it could be revealed that the scanning of the 
EBM process may introduce multiple times (even > 10 
times) of melting and re-melting at a single scanning 
point [12].
Except for these general effects from different design, var-
ious specific patterns have been applied to additively manu-
facture W materials. For example, some researchers have 
used 90° alternating bidirectional scanning pattern in SLM 
processing, with a hatch spacing of 80 µm and a scanning 
layer thickness of 40 µm [14]. Others would also recommend 
using 67° alternating bidirectional pattern [75] with a hatch 
spacing of 90 µm and a scanning layer thickness of 30 µm for 
SLM process (as shown in Fig. 8a), and they claimed that a 
relative density of 94.5–97.0% with various laser scanning 
speed has been achieved [37]. Similarly, EBM could use the 
same flexible bidirectional scanning strategy to improve the 
final printing quality of W materials [44].
To obtain a more generic view of how the pattern design 
would influence the AMed W materials, some researchers 
have already studied the pattern-related parameters and 
quantified their contribution [82]. For example, the hatch 
spacing is observed to contribute to only ~ 7% variation in 
the final W densification. In comparison, the scanning speed 
would contribute to ~ 75% of variation in W densification by 
SLM [82] (as manifested by the statistical analysis from the 
data in Fig.  8b). Particularly, changing not only the hatch 
distance [82] but also the subtle parameter like point dis-
tance will greatly affect the AM relative density for W mate-
rials, as shown in Fig. 8c [50].

4.4 Preheating
Preheating is effective to mitigate the thermal residual stress 
[74], reduce the melt pool cooling rate and thermal gradi-
ent during solidification, delay or avoid the potential DBTT 
[84], and introduce the annealing process in W materials 
[74]. Preheating is necessary[65, 84], and the substrate 
would be sometimes heated up to 1000 °C, to help increase 
the consolidated density and reduce the microcracks [14]. 
Study has reported that preheating of < 400 °C is not enough 
for microcrack mitigation [6 ]. Generally speaking, higher 
substrate preheating will mitigate the high DBTT effects 
and increase the relative mass density of the AMed W [14], 
as shown in Fig. 9a.
However, if the preheating temperature is too high, a 
recrystallization process could be introduced [12]. Given 
that the larger recrystallized grains may be more suscepti-
ble to intergranular cracking and serve as the straight crack 
paths at or near the GBs [65], too high a preheating tempera-
ture is not favorable [12].

5.1 Pure W
It has to be admitted that it remains challenging to fabricate 
even pure W with AM techniques due to its reasonable but 
highly dynamic laser absorptivity (< 70% for SLM) (see 
Fig. 1b) [9], high melting point, high thermal conductivity 
(see Fig.  1a), high viscosity, high affinity for oxygen, and 
high DBTT. Problems such as porosities, cracks, balling, 
and distortion were observed, resulting in low mechanical 
properties.
Research has been mainly focused on optimizing the 
processing parameters and investigating their effects on the 
results, e.g., microstructure, defects, densification, solidi-
fication behavior, grain structures, surface roughness, and 
properties. For SLM process specifically, it was reported that 
W powders with spherical shapes and smaller sizes exhibit 
higher laser absorptivity [50, 94, 95], with the calculated 
absorptivity of the W powder layer reaching a maximum of 
0.6 at a particle diameter of 5 μm [94]. Figure 12a–c shows 
the compressive stress–strain curves of AMed pure W under 
different energy densities and scanning speeds. Power and 
volumetric energy density (as discussed in Sect.  4.2) were 
found to be the key parameters affecting W densification 
[18, 51, 96, 97].

eters, cracking has been widely reported in AMed pure W 
even under optimal conditions, due to the large thermal 
stress, hard-to-control crystallization, and embrittlement of 
pure W. Oxygen in the form of tungsten oxide segregated at 
grain boundaries during processing, inducing hot cracking 
[68] (see Sect.  4.1). It was reported that cracks emerged in 
every molten track and propagated along columnar grain 
boundaries [18, 68, 98]. Many strategies to mitigate crack -

cracking driving force and increasing resistance to cracking, 
which only reached minor success. For example, tuning the 
laser scanning speed and scanning strategy to reduce colum-

a positive impact yet did not solve the cracking problem. The

under debate, the effects of alloying elements on AMed

Alloying effect on densification Alloying can increase 
densification of AM tungsten parts. The mechanism can 
be attributed to lower melting point of alloying phases and 
modified laser absorption/melt viscosity/fluidity. Adding 
alloying elements can form phases that act as a binder dur -
ing laser AM. Su et al. [4]studied the densification of W–Cu 
alloy 4. Cu is immiscible with W and has a lower melt-
ing point. The sufficient wetting of W particles by molten 
Cu and the surface smoothing of W particles contribute to 
the densification of W–Cu during laser AM [4 ]. Impor -
tantly, since melting of Cu is used to serve as the molten 
pool for the solid W particles, so no huge energy input is 
needed for W melting [4 , 24]. For SLM processing of the 
W–Ni–Fe–Co, the melting of Ni, Fe and Co enhanced disso-
lution of W particles [21]. Besides, liquid films at the grain 
boundaries extended the time of grain shape accommoda-
tion [21]. Therefore, higher relative density can be achieved 
[21]. Zhang et al. [109] investigated SLM of W–Ni alloy 
and found that the addition of Ni can effectively reduce the 
melt viscosity and improve the melt thermal fluidity, which 
is beneficial for densification.
Alloying effect on grains Alloying can refine and modify 
the grain structures of tungsten materials processed by AM. 
The underlying mechanism is that alloying can either form 
precipitates restricting growth of W grains or change solidi-
fication grain structures due to melt composition change. 
Bose et al. [108, 110] reported that the adding alloying ele-
ments such as Mo, Ta, and Re can lead to finer grain struc-
tures of W. Cr decorated the grain boundaries of W while 
also precipitating as a Cr-rich phase [108]. The grain size 
of W-Cr alloy was reduced compared to conventionally liq-
uid phase sintered W heavy alloys [108]. Chookajorn et al. 
[111] developed a theoretical framework to design stable 
nanostructured alloys and identified W-Ti alloy as a candi-
date which showed heterogeneous chemical distribution to 
prevent nanostructures from coarsening. Ta element addition 
could also toughen the grains by the final cellular structure 
[48, 112]. For W–Ni alloy, Ni additions decreased the cool-
ing rate of the melt pool, which promoted the transformation 
from columnar grains to dendrites in the matrix [2 ].

5.3 W‑matrix composites
While many researchers have been confused with the con-
cept of “composites”, particularly when W is insoluble or 
immiscible with many other alloying elements, we believe 
that a better definition of “W composites” should be given to 
the systems where W is mixed with a heterogeneous phase 
and these secondary phases should not be elemental (like Cu 
element) or chemically transformable (like AlW intermetal-
lics in W–Al alloys) [117]. Since W requires a high process-
ing temperature during AM, ceramics secondary phases like 
oxides, carbides, nitrides, and borides are more suitable for 
additively fabricating W composites.
Indeed, W composites have been already focused for 
decades, even with the traditional manufacturing methods 
[118, 119]. For instance, these traditionally processed W 
composites with La2O3 particles would raise the DBTT 
temperature, which is not favorable for W’s applications as 
structural material under fusion-specific neutron irradiation 
[118]. On the contrary, understanding the importance of 
oxygen (as discussed in Sect.  4.1), the triumphant success 
of using ZrC particles to lower the DBTT of W has also been 
reported [119].
Given the knowledge, with the development of AM tech-
niques, to guarantee a better mechanical property of W com-
posites is even more urgently needed. Based on the previ -
ous results [119, 120], a right selection of the incorporated 
phases (e.g., ZrC) in W composites is the key to achieving 
better low-temperature mechanical performance, and how 
the W composites could be compatible with AM awaits more 
study.
Currently, in the scenarios of AM, W has been reported 
to be mixed with TaC [121], TiC [122, 123], ZrC [7], Y2O3 
[124, 125], La2O3 [38], etc. These secondary-phase particles 
have their unique features and advantages.
For example, rare earth oxides have already been reported 
to be uniformly distributed at the grain boundaries and inside 
the grains after the processing for W [38, 125]. Some study 
finds that micro-size oxides like Y2O3 could agglomerate in 
AMed W, while the nano-size particles will not [124], which 
is consistent with other composite and nanocomposite study 
[90, 126]. Besides, oxide incorporation (part of which will 
also be transformed into solid solution form like W–Y–O 
complex for W–Y 2O3 composites during AM) is found 
to introduce more low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) 
in AMed W composites [124], as shown in Fig.  15. This 
microstructural evolution is believed to be the reason for the 
mitigated cracking behavior in W-oxide composites, because 
of the preferential stress concentration near high-angle grain 
boundaries (HAGBs) [124].

sity is reduced by > 85% in W-ZrC composites (see Fig. 16a, 
b) [7 ]. The main reason is that the finer grain by ZrC

increase the densification and cracking resistance in AMed 
W materials. This section will prospect and discuss these 
fabrication improvements before, during, and after AM of 
W.
Before the fabrication starts, given the importance of pat-
tern design, power selection, etc., fabrication plans for W 
AM can be improved with the analysis of the previous suc-
cessful cases. Therefore, artificial intelligence and machine

Amid the fabrication process, controlling and optimizing

to melt deposited layers into a substrate is critical for achiev-

significant approaches of the process parameters optimization 
is the design of experiments (DOE). The different techniques 
of DOE such as Taguchi [133, 134], central composite design

roughness, stent dimensions, etc.

cracking via thermal stress reduction has been proposed 
to fabricate energy input-required hard-to-melt W materi-

ultrasonic vibration could lead to better densification and 
microstructures. The ultrasonic vibration at micron level 
in X–Y motion of the table enhances the necking between 
the powder particles and the layers of the powders, which 
directly increase the local hardness and other properties 
[138–140]. The dilution rate (indicating the degree of mix-
ing between the deposited layers and the substrate) could 
be raised and the bonding could be considerably reinforced 
with ultrasonic vibration assistance for additively manu-
facturing W.
Besides, thanks to the high stability of W itself, many 
W compounds are not stable, as their enthalpy of forma-
tion and Gibbs free energy would be both high. So, if W’s 
direct melting and good bonding is complex even with their 
alloys and composites, in situ routes combined with AM 
could open up new opportunities for high-quality W materi-
als. Indeed, W-related in situ processing routes (e.g., molten 
salt-assisted reduction [141] and optimized freeze-drying 
[125]) have been being developed recently [125, 141], and 
successful cases have been reported. Together with the ben-
efits of (nano-)composites in general for various functional 
applications [117, 142, 143] (in Sect.  5.2), this method 
could also significantly extend the application scenarios of 
W materials. Other methods like plasma-assisted processing 
[144], arc stability enhancement (mainly for thin wall struc-
ture) [145], and robust heat input tuning [146] also prove 
certain success in different materials already, which could 
be a potential solution to W part AM.

6.2 Measurement techniques
To clearly understand what happens during the W AM and 
how W materials evolve, more accurate monitoring and 
measurement techniques are urgently needed for AM pro-
cedures of W. Particularly, the high melting temperature of 
W and high environmental temperature, as discussed above, 
impose a great challenge for this purpose and cause a still 
unclear nature of detailed AM process of W materials.
To solve these issues and assist the next-generation W 
AM design, people have been equipping the AM systems 
with various in situ monitoring and observation techniques 
for W printing process investigation [149].
Pyrometry could directly monitor the temperature evolu-
tion during the AM of W materials, as the thermal history is 
important for its densification and cracking mitigation dur -
ing the AM processing [12]. For instance, some researchers 
have already used this technique to reveal the precise role of 
preheating in mitigating the crack initiation in the building 
direction of AMed W materials, though the pyrometry reso-
lution needs improvement [12]. Vrancken et al. [10] inves-
tigated the formation of microcrack in single laser-melted 
tracks through thermomechanical analysis and in situ high-
speed video. It is found that the formation of microcrack 
happens within the temperature regime of 450–650 K.
In addition, other excitation wavelength-based techniques 
are involved for W AM to tackle with the high-temperature 
environments (where various excitations are possible). For 
example, after correcting the temporally dependent emissiv-
ity, near-infrared (NIR) imaging has been applied to moni-
tor the porosity situation during AM for W, as shown in 
Fig. 18a [12]. This could effectively mitigate the inconsist-
ency of the porosity results by different methods (as shown 
in Fig. 18b) Other visualization and monitoring methods like 
imaging algorithm-enhanced monitoring [150] could further 
depict the dynamic processes of W AM and microstructural 
evolution.

6.3 Microstructural and alloying design
According to our discussion in Sect.  5, effective micro-
structural and alloying design would be beneficial for the 
successful AM processes and satisfactory performance of 
manufactured W materials.
From the point view of microstructures, the brittleness 
and the poor ductility of W materials are generally originated 
from the very high Peierls stress of BCC crystal structure 
and the weak cohesion in grain boundaries. Considering 
these two important microstructure factors, several strate-
gies have been applied to improve the properties of the bulk 
W. Alloying with Re is a practical approach to strengthen 
the GB cohesion and increase the 1/2 < 111 > screw dislo-
cation mobility [153, 154]. The effect of the Re addition 
is obvious. It has been reported that the DBTT of W alloy 
with 26 wt% Re is − 101 °C, and the rolled W-1.9Re and the 
swaged W–25Re both show good ductility at 25 °C [153, 
155]. Besides Re, iridium (Ir), titanium (Ti), and halfnium 
(Hf) have been proposed as the alloying elements to enhance 
the ductility of the bulk W material [152, 156, 157] (also 
see Sect. 5.2).

endures repeated thermomechanical loading, which is analo-

shows the typical planar slip characteristics, where the cross-

the wavy slip mode has been discussed for decades in fatigue 
[158], especially for face center cubic (FCC) metals (see 
Fig. 19a as a general guidance). Even though W is a BCC 
crystal, the wavy-slip idea may also be feasible. Consider -

except for the abovementioned experimental design, theo-
retical and numerical studies are another effective way. 
Numerous efforts have been carried out to investigate the 
evolution of temperature, residual stress, the solidification 
process, and grain growth during AM based on finite ele-
ment method (FEM) [10, 29], phase-field method [180], 
cellular automata [181], and kinetic Monte Carlo method 
[182]. On the other hand, multiscale simulation method 
would also be a powerful tool. For example, Discrete Dislo-
cation Dynamic (DDD) is a mesoscopic simulation method, 
which can be used to understand the collective behaviors 
of dislocations, the evolution of plastic deformation, and 
their effects on the fabricated part quality and mechanical 
performance. Cui et al. [183] coupled DDD and FEM to 
systematically clarify the cyclical mechanic and thermal 
loading effect as well as the Ta addition effect on the dislo-
cation evolution and mechanisms during AM of W. It has 
concluded that both improving the dislocation mobility and 
adding nanoparticles can improve the plastic deformation 
ability, and the Ta-nanoparticle addition is more efficient. 
Clearly, these simulation methods could be a powerful and 
generic potential design strategy.

6.4 Extended applications
Combining W materials with AM could also lead to new 
applications of these novel material systems. These applica-
tions will not be limited to merely structural applications, so 
this section will focus on the extended functional applica-
tions of AMed W materials.
First, for structural purpose in aerospace applications, 
W-related materials are chosen as a plasma facing material 
for stable and sustainable functioning, e.g., in the divertor 
and magnetic-confinement fusion reactors. The divertor, as 
a critical module of a thermonuclear facility, need remove 
helium ash from plasma reactions in a hostile environment, 
which requires stable materials like W. However, in the pre-
sent nuclear fusion system, the tungsten tiles could not fill 
the geometry conformally, thus necessitating machining. 
This kind of processing usually leads to high production 
costs and waste. AM of W on divertor may not only cut 
production costs but also simplify component construction 
[1]. As an extended need from the plasma-facing compo-
nents, thermal properties like thermal conductivity would 
also be important, when selecting the suitable materials. 
W materials by AM have been reported to have advanta-
geous and anisotropic thermal properties [114], as shown 
in Fig.  21a. Furthermore, the AMed W structures can be 
further tailored for the AM process with the aid of direct 
topology design techniques. For example, for plasma-facing 
component applications, the maximum thermal stresses due 
to high heat fluxes are reduced by tailoring a heterogene-
ous W/Cu material distribution with homogenization‐based 
topology optimization [184]. With these benefits, the AMed 
W materials could aggressively expand its niche as structural 
materials in extreme environments, regarding its stability, 
conductivity, and geometry flexibility
For surface and interface engineering, AM method like 
laser-assisted process could provide a different surface 
response in W materials. For example, a spectrally selective 
absorber surface made of W using an anisotropic Gaussian 
rough surface for high-temperature applications has already 
been achieved, based on the interplay of W’s unique optical 
properties (see Fig.  21b) and surface texturing [185, 186]. 
Similarly, due to the surface benefits from hard W materials 
(e.g., W–TiC nanocomposites) and AM methods, surface 
anti-wear design has been efficiently realized [117, 123, 
186]. Moreover, due to these surface and interface design, 
W-contained materials (e.g., Mo–Nb–Ta–W high-entropy 
alloys [187]) are made as an ideal catalyst [142, 187]. Given

7 Conclusion
W materials, including W alloys and W composites, are 
emerging as an important and irreplaceable class of novel 
materials for various applications. As the AM techniques 
are pushing the traditional boundary of metallic systems, 
combining novel AM techniques with W materials are 
also leading to more exciting discovery, higher system 
design flexibility, and unprecedented properties. To fur -
ther help with the development of AMed W materials, 
this critical review serves as an important guidance for 
the solutions to the challenging issues in additively manu-
facturing W systems.
In this review, we first systematically clarified the fun-
damentals of AM processes for W materials. The molten 
pool dynamics, solidification process, and grain growth dur-
ing these AM processes are treated in a (semi-) quantitative 
sense. General governing equations are provided, and the 
generic observations and analyses are summarized to better 
understand the non-equilibrium nature of additively manu-
facturing W materials.
Except for the analysis of the similarities among these 
suitable AM techniques for W systems, we have also classi-
fied the W-related mainstream AM processes (e.g., WAAM, 
EBM, LPBF like SLM, and laser DED) and discussed their 
differences in detail. The differences in power source, pro-
cessing window, and final printing quality are our main 
focus. Of course, the emerging techniques have also been 
briefly touched for a comprehensive review.
When additively manufactu ring W materials, the final 
microstructures and performance of these W parts are ultra-
sensitive to the related processing parameters. This review 
surveys through the effects from oxygen contents, volu-
metric energy density, pattern design, preheating, melting 
behavior, and interfacial characteristics thoroughly. More 
importantly, this review includes the fundamental mecha-
nisms of how these factors control the final performance and 
properties of the printed W parts. For example, important 
questions like crack mitigation are answered from different 
aspects, including oxygen evolution route, volumetric energy 
density interplay, thermal effects, molten pool history, and 
interfacial endurance.

ous categories of AM-compatible W materials (i.e., pure 
W, W alloys, and W composites) have been summarized.
