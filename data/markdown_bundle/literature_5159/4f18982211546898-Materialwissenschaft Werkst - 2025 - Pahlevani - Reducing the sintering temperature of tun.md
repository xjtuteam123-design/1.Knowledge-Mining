Abstract
The high sintering temperature of tungsten heavy alloys is one of the problems
in the production of this alloy. In this research, with the aim of reducing the
sintering temperature, W-(10-x) Ni-x Si alloy with different amounts of silicon
(x = 0.8 wt.%, 1.2 wt.% and 1.6 wt.%) were designed. These alloys were pressed at
1050 °C, 1100 °C and 1150 °C under a pressure of 30 MPa. The microstructure,
composition, density and compressive properties of the samples were examined.
The highest relative density was about 99.52% for W-8.8Ni-1.2Si alloys at
1150 °C. By increasing the silicon, the density and tungsten particle size initially
increased and then decreased. Intermetallic and unwanted compounds were
observed in the microstructure of the W-8.4Ni-1.6Si alloy. At 1100 °C, the com-
pressive yield strength decreased with an increase in silicon, but at 1150 °C,
it did not change significantly. In both temperatures, the compressive strain
decreased with increasing silicon. At 1100 °C, the compressive yield
strength of alloys with 0.8 wt. %, 1.2 wt. % and 1.6 wt. % silicon were about
1303 MPa, 1231 MPa, and 670 MPa, respectively. The results showed that
W-Ni-Si alloys had higher density and compressive yield strength than
common W-Ni-Fe alloys, although the sintering temperature was lower.

1 | INTRODUCTION
Alloys with about 88 wt. % – 97 wt. % tungsten that have
a density above 16 g ·cm−3 – 18 g ·cm−3 are known as
tungsten heavy alloys [ 1– 3]. In the structure of this
alloy, spherical tungsten particles are usually surrounded
by a metal matrix phase. The metal matrix phase of
tungsten heavy alloys usually consists of elements such
as nickel, iron, cobalt, copper, and dissolved tungsten
[4, 5 ]. Because of their good mechanical properties, high
density and corrosion resistance, these alloys are used in a
wide range of applications such as balance weights,
radiation shields, high-energy projectiles, and thermal
nozzles [ 6– 9].
The most common method of producing tungsten
heavy alloy is conventional powder metallurgy. However,
these alloys are produced by various other fabrication pro-
cesses such as hot pressing, hot oscillatory pressing, spark
plasma sintering, infiltration, powder extrusion , and pow-
der injection molding [ 6, 10– 14]. Yttria has an appropriate
effect on wear properties of W-Ni-Cu alloys that is pro-
duced by spark plasma sintering [ 11]. The 90W-7Ni-3Fe
alloy produced by the laser melting deposition method
has higher tensile strength and less elongation than
that produced by the liquid phase sintering method [ 7].
Hot pressing with microwaves causes a uniform structure
in the W-Cu alloy [ 15]. The relative density of hot pressed
90W-7Ni-3Fe alloy at 1200 °C for 90 min reached more
than 96 % [ 13]. Hot pressing is a process called pressure-
assisted sintering. In this method, the temperature can be
reduced to almost half of the melting point [ 16]. This is a
production process of powder metallurgy parts in which
temperature and pressure are applied to the sample con-
currently [ 17, 18 ]. The pressure used in this process is
about 20 MPa – 80 MPa [ 29, 30]. Because of the reactivity
and oxidation of powders, a controlled environment or
vacuum is used [ 19].
By now, many efforts have been made to reduce the
sintering temperature of tungsten heavy alloys [ 20, 21 ].
Researchers have sought this solution by changing the
matrix phase and adding elements with a low melting
temperature [ 20, 22 ]. In most cases, one of the elements
was nickel, and cobalt was one of the matrix elements.
Some elements that have been added to the matrix phase
are tin and manganese [ 20, 23 ]. Herein, we report our
recent success in designing and manufacturing a tungsten-
nickel-antimony alloy, which has higher compressive
yield strength and a lower sintering temperature than
tungsten-nickel-iron alloys [ 21].
Nickel and cobalt are the main components of the
matrix phase because of the proper wetting of tungsten
particles. Research has shown that the formation of the
eutectic phase between additive elements and the
nickel element can lead to a reduction in the melting
temperature as well as liquid phase sintering at lower
temperatures [20, 21]. In this work, silicon was introduced
into the tungsten-nickel alloy system with the objective of
lowering sintering temperatures, improving densification,
and achieving a refined microstructure. According to the
nickel-silicon binary phase diagram, a eutectic reaction is
observed at approximately 12 wt.% silicon and near
1143 °C [ 24]. Therefore, nickel-silicon alloy can be used
as a matrix phase in tungsten heavy alloys. The effect
of silicon addition on tungsten carbide (WC) structure
is investigated and reported that rapid coarsening occurred
at high temperatures owing to the formation of a liquid
phase [ 25]. In steel alloys, the addition of silicon to these
alloys has been reported to improve density, wear
behavior, and tensile strength [ 26– 28]. Research on the
tungsten-nickel-silicon-yttria alloy which fabricated by
uniaxial press and conventional pressure-less sintering
method showed that the addition of silicon increased
the density and compressive strength [ 29].
For the first time ever, this research investigated the
production of a tungsten-nickel-silicon alloy using the
hot pressing method and then evaluated the microstruc-
ture, density, tungsten particle size, and compressive yield
strength of the tungsten-nickel-silicon alloy.

2.2 | Compaction and sintering
The mixed powders were poured into a graphite mold
with a hole diameter of 30 mm and a length of 180 mm.
A graphite sheet was used to reduce the reaction between
the mold and the powders. Then the powders were
sintered by a hot press machine (KPF300, Khala
Poushan Felez, Iran) at temperatures of 1050 °C, 1100 °C
and 1150 °C for 15 min under 10 −3 Pa vacuum, Figure 3.
Temperature control was performed using thermocouples
directly in contact with the graphite mold, which has a
precision of ± 5 °C. The samples were heated under
vacuum at a rate of 20 °C/min until they reached the
desired temperatures. After 15 min of heating, the samples
were cooled down to ambient temperature. Initially, the
pressure on the powders was applied at a rate of
2 MPa/min, and the final pressure on the samples was
30 MPa. For comparison the tungsten-nickel-iron alloy
was also hot pressed with the same compaction conditions
and final temperature of 1400 °C. As a result, the amount
of powder loaded, approximately 45 g, leads to a disc
of 28 mm in diameter.

3 | RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS
3.1 | Microstructural characterization
Porosities were present in all samples at 1050 °C,
Figure 4. At this temperature, tungsten particles had an
improper distribution and were agglomerated. Moreover,
the matrix phase was not properly formed in the structure
for surrounding the tungsten particles. It could be ascribed
to an insufficient sintering temperature at which the
matrix metal could not flow in the whole structure, con-
sequently leading to high porosities and low density [ 30].
For all alloys, the size and number of porosities decreased
with increasing temperature. For 90W-9.2Ni-0.8Si and
90W-8.8Ni-1.2Si alloys, the number of pores decreased

90W-8.4Ni-1.6Si, there were porosities at all temperatures.
In the structure of this alloy, there were polygonal tung-
sten particles at temperatures of 1050 °C and 1100 °C.
These particles were very similar to raw tungsten powder.
The presence of high porosity and polygonal tungsten
particles in the structure indicated the lack of proper
sintering and the production of consolidated alloy.

research, the probability of intermetallic phase formation
increased with increasing amounts of silicon.

tungsten particles and reducing the final particle size [ 36].
Accordingly, sintering tungsten samples with a nickel-
silicon matrix can achieve a fine-grained microstructure.

For the W-8.4Ni-1.6Si alloy, the final density did not reach

For W-Ni-Fe alloy, at sintering temperatures of 1050 °C,
1100 °C, and 1150 °C, the relative density was about 82 %,
92 %, and 97 %, respectively [ 6]. At all temperatures, the
density of the W-Ni-Si alloy was relatively higher than that
of the W-Ni-Fe alloy. By adding low-melting-point ele-
ments to tungsten alloys, the sintering temperature
decreased [ 20– 22]. By substituting silicon for iron, the
melting temperature decreased, and therefore sintering
started at lower temperatures. Densification occurs by
bulk transport mechanisms such as interface or grain
boundary diffusion [ 39]. The diffusion and mass transfer
mechanism is accelerated by the presence of the liquid
phase during the sintering stage, leading to an increase
in the sintering process [ 40]. By adding silicon, the liquid
phase is created at lower temperatures. Therefore, the
mechanisms of sintering, mass transfer and diffusion start
earlier. Due to this process, it is expected that the presence
of silicon in tungsten alloys will improve the densification
at lower temperatures.
For the W-8.4Ni-1.6 Si alloy, unfavorable and interme-
tallic components were observed in the structure, Figure 6.
Undesirable and intermetallic components were also
observed in other tungsten alloys [ 21, 41 ]. The presence
of intermetallic components causes a drop in density
and mechanical properties [ 42]. The presence of interme-
tallic components in the W-8.4Ni-1.6Si alloy caused the
density to be low compared to the other two alloys.

3.3 | Compressive yield strength
The samples containing 1.2 wt. % and 1.6 wt. % silicon failed

4 | CONCLUSIONS
In this research, tungsten-nickel-silicon alloys produced
by hot pressing were investigated. For this purpose,
three samples with silicon contents of 0.8 wt. %, 1.2 wt. %,
and 1.6 wt % were studied. The density, microstructure,
and mechanical properties of these alloys were also
evaluated, and the following results were obtained:
1. The highest relative density (99.52 %) was obtained for
the W-8.8Ni-1.2Si alloy sintered at 1150 °C. Because of
the lower melting temperature of the 8.8Ni-1.2Si alloy,
sintering started at a lower temperature than the two
other alloys. In the W-8.8Ni-1.2Si and W-9.2Ni-0.8Si
samples at temperatures of 1100 °C and 1150 °C, the
relative density reached over 98 %. For the alloy con-
taining 1.6 % by weight of silicon, the relative density
did not reach more than 96.5 % at all temperatures,
which was explained by the presence of unwanted
and intermetallic compounds.
2. The microstructures of the W-9.2Ni-0.8Si and W-8.8Ni-
1.2Si alloys at temperatures of 1100 °C and 1150 °C
included spherical tungsten particles in the nickel-silicon
matrix, which was similar to the structure of common
tungsten alloys. The tungsten particle size of the W-
9.2Ni-0.8Si and W-8.8Ni-1.2Si alloys at temperatures of
1100 °C were 8.8 μma n d1 5 . 0 6 μm, respectively.
