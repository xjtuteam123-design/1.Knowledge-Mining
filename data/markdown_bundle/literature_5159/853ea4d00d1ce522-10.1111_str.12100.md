Dynamic High-temperature Testing of an Iridium Alloy
in Compression at High-strain Rates
B. Song *, K. Nelson †, R. Lipinski *, J. Bignell *, G. Ulrich ‡ and E. P. George ‡
*Sandia National Laboratories, Albuquerque, NM 87185, USA
†Sandia National Laboratories, Livermore, CA 94550, USA
‡Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN 37831, USA
ABSTRACT: Iridium alloys have superior strength and ductility at elevated temperatures, making them useful as structural materials for
certain high-temperature applications. However, experimental data on their high-strain -rate performance are needed for understanding
high-speed impacts in severe environments. Kolsky bars (also called split Hopkinson bars) have been extensively employed for high-strain -rate
characterization of materials at room temperature, but it has been challenging to adapt them for the measurement of dynamic properties at high
temperatures. In this study, we analyzed the dif ﬁculties encountered in high-temperature Kolsky bar testing of thin iridium alloy specimens in
compression. Appropriate modiﬁcations were then made to the current high-temperature Kolsky bar technique to obtain reliable compressive
stress–strain response of an iridium alloy at high-strain rates (300 –10 000 s
/C0 1) and temperatures (750 and 1030 °C). The compressive stress –
strain response of the iridium alloy showed signi ﬁcant sensitivity to both strain rate and temperature.
KEY WORDS: high-strain rate, high temperature, iridium alloy, Kolsky bar (split Hopkinson bar), stress –strain response
Introduction
Iridium alloys have been utilized in certain high-temperature
applications, due to their unique combinations of high-
melting temperature, high-temperature strength and ductility,
and excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance [1]. When
iridium alloys are used as cladding material for containment
of heat sources, safety is paramount. Typically, these materials
must have excellent impact resistance at high temperatures.
Experimental data are required to assess their performance at
the pertinent high strain rates and temperatures.
The mechanical properties of iridium alloys have been
investigated before at various strain rates and temperatures
[2– 11]. However, the strain rates in the literature were either
not suf ﬁciently high (below 50 s
/C0 1) for all potential
applications or the stress – strain curves of the iridium alloys
were not obtained. It has been especially challenging to
dynamically characterize materials at high temperatures,
even though Kolsky bar (also called split Hopkinson bar,
SHPB) testing has been extensively employed for high-strain
rate characterization of materials at room temperature [12].
In Kolsky bar experiments, the pressure bars serve as not
only loading actuators but also as mechanical sensors. The
stress– strain response of the material under investigation is
typically obtained from the strains measured on the pressure
bars. In order to make such measurements reliable, the
Young’s moduli of the pressure bars should be known and
remain constant during the experiment. It is not desirable
to heat the Kolsky bar system because the Young ’s moduli
of metals decrease signi ﬁcantly with temperature [13]. Even
if the pressure bars are heated only locally near the specimen
interfaces (as a result of coming into contact with the hot
specimen), a signi ﬁcant temperature gradient is produced
in both incident and transmission pressure bars. Such
temperature gradients generate Young ’s modulus gradients
and an associated mechanical impedance in both incident
and transmission bars, which modi ﬁes the stress wave
propagation and in ﬂuences the strain-gage measurements
on the bar surfaces and consequently stress and strain
calculations in the specimen. Special care is therefore
needed to perform high-temperature Kolsky bar tests.
As a remedy, some researchers have heated the specimen
separately [14 – 17]. The idea is to bring the hot specimen
in contact with the ‘cold’ (room temperature) pressure bars
right before the dynamic load is applied. This introduces
the concept of cold contact time (CCT), which is de ﬁned
as the time during which the hot specimen is in contact
with the cold pressure bars before it is dynamically loaded.
It is highly desirable to minimize the CCT so as to reduce
the temperature drop in the high-temperature specimen
and the production of a temperature gradient in the pressure
bars before dynamic loading due to heat transfer from the
‘hot’ specimen to the ‘cold’ pressure bars. The CCT has been
shortened in a few different ways. An electric screw driven
system was developed by Frantz et al . [14] to bring the
pressure bars into the furnace after the specimen had
already been heated to the test temperature and then
sandwich the specimen in between right before the striker
was launched. Lennon and Ramesh [15] used an
electropneumatic actuation system to quickly drive the

Modiﬁed high-temperature Kolsky bar for iridium alloy
testing
Due to certain applications of interest, it was desired that an
iridium alloy be characterize d at elevated strain rates and
temperatures in relatively thin sheet form. Before the automated
high-temperature Kolsky bar system described earlier could be
used to characterize the relatively thin (0.65 mm) iridium alloy
specimens investigated in this study, additional issues had to
be addressed. In order to ensure that the specimen is compressed
under an approximately 1D stressstate, the specimen diameter
cannot be too large. In this s tudy, a specimen diameter of
3 mm was selected. When such a small hot specimen makes
contact with the ‘cold’ (room temperature) pressure bars, its
temperature will drop much quicker than that of a larger
specimen. Thermal transfer analysis showed that the temperature
of the iridium specimen can drop by 500 °C from the original
temperature of 1030 °C within 1 ms. This presents a signiﬁcant
challenge to the current autom ated high-temperature Kolsky
bar system shown in Figure 1 because it is difﬁcult to reduce the
CCT to less than 1 ms.
An alternative method is to create a sandwich structure
consisting of the test specimen placed between two large
insulating platens and heat the whole composite in the
furnace [18]. In this case, the initial heat transfer is mainly
from the platens instead of the test specimen which stays
at a constant temperature for a relatively longer duration.
For the present study, analyses were conducted on a setup
with the thin iridium specimen sandwiched between a pair
of two different platens. The platens were made of zirconia
and maraging steel. Each platen had the same diameter as
the pressure bars and had thicknesses of 0.43 mm (zirconia)
and 2.93 mm (maraging steel). Analyses showed that the
temperature of an iridium specimen sandwiched between the
above platens remained unchanged for 20– 30 ms after being in
contact with the room temperature pressure bars. This provides
more than enough time for the current automated high-

iridium alloy specimen is interfacial friction between the
specimen and the platens at elevated temperatures. High-

stress state. This makes it critical to select an appropriate high-
temperature lubricant. In this study, a super enhanced graphite

Dynamic high-temperature experiments
Disc-shaped specimens were cut from a prime 52-mm-
diameter, 0.65-mm-thick DOP-26 iridium alloy blank using
electrical discharge machining with molybdenum wire and
then ground with a diamond abrasive to a ﬁnal diameter of
3.000 ± 0.025 mm to remove the residual electrical discharge
machining layer. The specimens were acid cleaned in a
solution of three parts HCl and one part HNO
3 by volume,
plus 15 vol.% HF for 30 ± 10 min, followed by rinsing in
distilled water and ethanol. The disc specimens were then
re-weighed, heat treated at 1375 ± 25 °C for 1 h ± 10 min in
vacuum (≤1×1 0
/C0 4 Torr) in a furnace dedicated for the iridium
alloy and re-weighed again to verify identity. The parallelism
of specimen surfaces was controlled within 0.0025 mm.
The setup shown in Figure 1 was used to characterize the
iridium alloy at high temperatures. The pressure bars, which
are made of C350 maraging steel, had a diameter of
19.05 mm. The incident and transmission bars were 3657
and 2134 mm long, respectively. The striker material and
length can be varied for mechanical testing at different strain
rates. The specimen holder was attached to a pneumatic slider
to hold and move the specimen into and out of the furnace,
typically within half second. A pair of clamps was installed
on the transmission bar, enabling the transmission bar to
move forward or backward under control of the transmission
bar slider. Another similar clamp was attached to the incident
bar as a bar stopper to ensure that the incident bar stayed in
place while the transmission bar was pushing the specimen
forward into contact with the incident bar. The whole process
was precisely controlled with a computer software program.

Therefore, we consider an extreme case to estimate the
upper boundary of uncertainty due to the introduction of
the alumina platens. We treat the whole sandwich structure
(two alumina platens plus iridium specimen) as a single
unit. The re ﬂected pulse contains information about the
total deformation of the whole sandwich structure, that is,
the deformations of both alumina platens and iridium alloy
specimen. As shown in Figure 7, the iridium alloy specimen
was subjected to a stress of 2400 MPa at a strain of 30%. Due to
the force equilibration in the whole bar system, the alumina
platens were subjected to the same force as the iridium alloy
specimen, which was calculated from the earlier stress to be
17 000 N. This force generated a stress of approximately
60 MPa in the alumina platen, which corresponds to a strain
of 0.00018 at 1030 °C. Because each alumina platen had a
thickness of 19.05 mm, the total displacement in the two
alumina platens is δ = 0.00018 × 19.05 × 2 = 0.0069 mm.
This displacement corresponds to approximately 1% strain
in the 0.65-mm-thick iridium alloy specimen. Therefore,
within the total calculated strain of 30% in the iridium alloy
specimen, there might be a maximum strain of 1%
attributable to the deformation of the two alumina platens.
Thus, the maximum uncertainty is approximately 3.3% in
the strain values of the iridium specimen.
Another uncertainty may include possible elastic
punching by the small-diameter iridium sample into the
relatively large-diameter alumina platens. Safa and Gary
[23] provided a guideline to correct the displacement for
such a punching at a dynamically loaded bar end. Here, we
used their analysis to estimate the uncertainty for the
iridium testing. At the strain rate of 11 550 s
/C0 1 and 750 °C,
the relative error was estimated as 12.7% at the measured
20% engineering strain. The relative error is reduced to 9%
at 40% engineering strain at the same condition. It is noted
that relative errors due to elastic punching are larger than
that of introduction of alumina platens but are smaller than
the range of data scattering at the same condition, which is
approximately 15 – 25% as shown in Figure 11. The relative
errors are smaller at lower strain rates and higher temperature
due to lower ﬂow stresses. The above uncertainty analyses are
only used for informational purpose, and corrections were not
included in this study.

Conclusions
The current high-temperature Kolsky compression bar
technique was modi ﬁed to enable high-strain rate
characterization of small iridium alloy specimens at high
temperatures. The modiﬁcations include the new design of a
sandwich structure consisting of two alumina platens with
the iridium alloy specimen in between. This modi ﬁcation
maintained a constant temperature of the iridium alloy
specimen during testing and minimized the temperature
gradient in the pressure bars during dynamic loading. An
uncertainty analysis was conducted to determine the
maximum strain error introduced by the insertion of the
alumina platens, and it was concluded that the effect of
alumina platens on the calculated stress – strain response of
the iridium alloy specimen was negligible. All these
modiﬁcations and analyses enabled acquisition of reliable
high-rate high-temperature compressive stress – strain curves
for the iridium alloy. The iridium alloy was dynamically
characterized in compression at four different strain rates
ranging from 300 to 10 000 s
/C0 1 at temperatures of 750 and
1030 °C. The compressive stress– strain response of the iridium
alloy in this study showed signiﬁcant sensitivity to both strain
rate and temperature. At a given strain, the ﬂow stress
increased with increasing strain rate at a ﬁxed temperature
and decreased with increasing temperature at a ﬁxed strain
rate. The experimental method developed in this study can
be applicable to any other thin samples as long as the
investigated materials have mechanical strengths below the
yield stress of the applied platens at high temperatures.
