High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted great research interest owing to their good combination of
high
 strength and ductility at both room and cryogenic temperatures. However, expensive raw mate-
rials are always added to overcome the strength-ductility trade-off at low temperatures, leading to an
increased
 production cost for the cryogenically used alloys. In this work, a series of nitrogen-doped
FeMnCoCr HEAs have been processed by homogenization annealing, cold rolling and recrystallization
annealing
 followed by water quenching. The microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of the
alloys are studied systematically. The Fe49Mn30Co10Cr10N1 alloy shows excellent mechanical properties
at
 both 293 K and 77 K. Particularly, the yield and ultimate tensile strength of 1078 and 1630 MPa are
achieved at the cryogenic temperature, respectively, while a satisfactory uniform elongation of 33.5 % is
maintained.
 The ultrahigh yield strength results from the microstructure reﬁnement caused by the acti-
vation
 of athermal martensitic transformation and mechanical twinning that occur in the elastic regime
together
 with the increased lattice friction due to the cryogenic environment. In the plastic regime, the
dynamic
 Hall-Petch effect caused by twinning, martensitic transformation, and reverse transformation
together
 with the high barrier to dislocation motion jointly contribute to the ultrahigh tensile strength.
Simultaneously,
 the transformation induced plasticity (TRIP) and the twinning induced plasticity (TWIP)
effects
 jointly contribute to the ductility. The design strategy for attaining superior mechanical properties
at
 low temperatures, i.e. by adjusting stacking fault energy in the interstitial metastable HEAs, guides the
development
 of high-performance and low-cost alloys for cryogenic applications.

1. Introduction
Metallic materials have been playing an indispensable role in
human life. Since the appearance of copper-tin alloys, various
metallic materials, such as iron-carbon alloys, aluminum alloys,
copper alloys and titanium alloys, have been developed. However,
due to the strength-ductility trade-off of those alloys that generally
incorporate only one or two principal elements, the ever-growing
demands for high-performance structural materials cannot be ful-
ﬁlled. Since the equiatomic FeMnCoCrNi high-entropy alloy (HEA)
with a single-phase face-centered cubic (fcc) structure, i.e. the Can-
tor alloy, was proposed [1–4], HEAs with the body-faced cubic (bcc)
structure [5–7], the hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure [8–10]

ments and thus tuning the stacking fault energy (SFE). The excellent
mechanical properties of the alloy are ascribed to the cooperative
contributions of the transformation induced plasticity (TRIP), the
twinning induced plasticity (TWIP) together with the bidirectional
transformation induced plasticity (B-TRIP) effects [15–17].
Recently, a large number of studies have shown that HEAs have
great potential for cryogenic applications. The fcc [18–21], bcc
[22] and fcc + bcc [23] structured HEAs with excellent cryogenic
mechanical properties have been developed. Taking the Cantor
alloy which is a typical representative of the fcc HEAs for exam-

It is noteworthy that the aforementioned HEAs for cryogenic
applications usually contain high content of expensive metal ele-
ments, e.g. Ni and V. To break through the limitation of this alloy
design strategy, we previously studied the mechanical behavior of
the Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 HEA in which Ni has been reduced com-
pared with the Cantor alloy at different temperatures [27]. Yield
and ultimate tensile strength of the alloy at 77 K (481 and 1003
MPa) are signiﬁcantly higher than that at 293 K (i.e. 273 and 567
MPa), while the uniform elongation is increased from 47 % at 293
K to 65 % at 77 K. The desirable cryogenic mechanical proper-
ties are mainly attributed to the signiﬁcant reduction of SFE at
the lower temperature, leading to the cooperative activation of
twinning and athermal transformation and the enhanced strain
hardening under deformation. In addition, the dislocation motion
energy barrier caused by the cryogenic environment also leads to
an increased ﬂow stress of the bulk material. Although compre-
hensive mechanical properties of the alloy have been improved by
adjusting the SFE, the cryogenic mechanical properties of the Fe-
Mn based HEAs in which austenite is the dominating phase are
still lower than that of the alloys enriched with Ni or V [24–26].
Especially, yield strength of the Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 HEA at room
temperature is still too low (≤ 280 MPa) to meet the demands
of practical engineering applications. In order to further improve
mechanical properties of the FeMnCoCr HEAs at both room and
cryogenic temperatures, the B-doped alloys have been prepared
by Seol et al. [28], showing that the yield strength of the HEA
at 77 K can be effectively enhanced from 481 to 1098 MPa with
the introduction of interstitial boron, while the uniform elonga-
tion has been signiﬁcantly reduced from 65 % to 22 %. At the
cryogenic temperature, the ultrahigh yield strength is attributed
to the strengthening induced by grain reﬁnement and solid solu-
tion, while the high ultimate tensile strength is attributed to the
TRIP and TWIP effects combined with the deformation-induced
short range ordering (SRO) [29]. Meanwhile, the high density SRO
structure formed during cryogenic deformation deteriorates the
ductility.
The inﬂuence of interstitial atom C on the mechanical prop-
erties of HEAs has also been studied extensively [11,30–33]. For
instance, compared with the C-free alloy, both yield and ultimate
tensile strength of the Fe49.5Mn30Co10Cr10C0.5 HEA have been ele-
vated around 100 %. However, the SFE of the alloy is increased due
to the solution of carbon, resulting in the suppressed TRIP and TWIP
effects and thus the dramatically decreased ductility. On the other
hand, the studies on austenitic stainless steels have shown that the
interstitial N atoms provide more signiﬁcant increase of strength
than C, while a good ductility can still be maintained [34,35]. The
investigation on the N-doped Cantor alloy has also shown that both
yield and ultimate tensile strength in the (FeMnCoCrNi)99N1 (atom
percent, at.%) HEA are higher than that without N alloying, which
is attributed to the joint contribution of solid solution and grain
reﬁnement [36]. However, although the TWIP effect is suppressed
due to the increased SFE in the N-doped HEA, the ductility of the
alloy is not reduced because more dislocations are activated. In the
present work, to attain excellent cryogenic mechanical properties,
interstitial N atoms are introduced into a metastable FeMnCoCr
HEA initially composed of fcc and hcp dual phases. The addition
of N can lead to the strengthening from both solid solution and
grain reﬁnement as well as the changed SFE. In addition, when
deformed in the cryogenic environment, more signiﬁcant defor-
mation twinning and/or athermal transformation are expected to
be the dominant strengthening and toughening mechanisms com-
pared with that at room temperature. By exploring the relationship
between deformation temperatures, mechanical properties and

2. Experimental methods
The nominal chemical compositions of the studied HEAs are
Fe50-xMn30Co10Cr10Nx (x = 0, 1.0, 1.8 and 2.6, at.%). The alloys
were melted and cast in a vacuum induction furnace using a
mixture of pure metals (purity > 99.9 wt.%) and MnN in Ar
atmosphere. After hot-forging at 1200 ◦C, the alloys were hot-
rolled at 1000 ◦C via several passes to reach a total thickness
reduction of 50 %. Subsequently, the alloys were homogenized
at 1200 ◦C for 2 h to eliminate the element segregation. Finally,
the homogenized alloys were further cold-rolled to a thickness
reduction of 60 % with several passes and then annealed at 900
◦C in a salt bath furnace for 3 min followed by water quenching.
Those ﬁne-grained materials were used for mechanical testing and
microstructural characterization. Here, the alloys with four differ-
ent N contents are denoted as N0, N1.0, N1.8 and N2.6 materials,
respectively.
The dog-bone shaped tensile specimens were prepared with the
gauge length and thickness of 15 mm and 2 mm, respectively. The
oxide layers and macro-defects of the specimens were removed by
mechanical polishing. The tensile tests at a strain rate of 10−3 s-1
were conducted using an Instron 5982 testing machine equipped
with a cold chamber controlled by liquid-nitrogen. The crystallo-
graphic structures of the alloys before and after tensile tests were
characterized by using a Rigaku Smartlab X-Ray diffractometer,
and then the dislocation densities were calculated based on the
X-ray diffraction (XRD) data. An anode with Cu-K/H92511 (/NAK=1.54056 Å)
radiation was used to step-scan the samples with the strain layers
removed at a 2/DC2 angle of 40◦−100◦. The step size was 0.05◦ and the
testing duration at each step was 4 s.
Orientation distributions and phase compositions of the alloys
before and after tensile testing were characterized by using a JEOL
JSM-7001 F scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with
the Oxford HKL electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) detector.
The operating voltage was 20 kV and the step size was 80 nm.
Subsequently, the data were post-processed using the Channel 5
software. The proportions of the constituent phases are the aver-
age values obtained from more than ﬁve regions. The specimens
for EBSD mapping were ﬁrst mechanically polished to achieve a
mirror-like surface and then electrochemically polished to remove
the strain layer. The deformation microstructures of the alloys were
further characterized by using a JEOL JEM-2100 F transmission elec-
tron microscope (TEM). The TEM samples were ﬁrst mechanically
polished to a thickness of 50 /H9262m and then thinned by using a
twin-jet electropolisher under 25 V and at -25 ◦C. The electrolyte
mentioned above was a solution of HClO4 (10 vol.%) and C2H5OH
(90 vol.%).
In the present study, the sample coordinates were deﬁned as
three orthonormal directions of the rolled plate, i.e., the rolling
direction (RD), transverse direction (TD) and normal direction (ND).
The EBSD samples were taken from the longitudinal section (RD-
ND plane), and the TEM samples were taken from the rolled section
(RD-TD plane). The cross-sectional area of the samples prior to
tensile testing was 2 mm × 3 mm (thickness × width), and the
loading direction (LD) is parallel to RD. The positions with the cross-
sectional area of 1.89 mm × 2.83 mm and 1.63 mm × 2.45 mm
were selected as the regions corresponding to the cross-section
area reductions of 11 % and 33.5 % for the deformed samples, respec-
tively. The average thickness and spacing of deformation twins and
/H9255-martensite laths are obtained by measuring at least ﬁve regions
under TEM observations using the same zone axis.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructures and mechanical properties
The EBSD results of the N0, N1.0, N1.8 and N2.6 alloys before
tensile testing are shown in Fig. 1. All the alloys are at a fully recrys-
tallized state with the average grain size of 6.2, 5.8, 5.3 and 4.5 /H9262m,
respectively. The phase distribution maps (Fig. 1(a1-d1)) show that
the N0 alloy is composed of ∼63 % /H9253-austenite (area fraction) and
∼37 % /H9255-martensite, while the N1.0, N1.8 and N2.6 alloys are with
the single-phase austenitic structure. The orientation distribution
maps (Fig. 1(a2-d2)) show that no preferential orientations exist in
the alloys.
Fig. 2(a–c) shows the engineering stress-strain, true stress-
strain and work hardening curves of the N0, N1.0, N1.8 and N2.6 alloys
obtained from uniaxial tensile testing at different temperatures,
respectively. The average grain sizes and mechanical properties of
the alloys are also listed in Table 2. It is found that at both room
and cryogenic temperatures, yield and ultimate tensile strength
increase signiﬁcantly with the increase of N. At 293 K, the ductility
of the alloys decreases slightly with N doping and necking occurs
before fracture. Namely, the uniform elongation decreases from 68
% in the N0 alloy to 47 % in the N2.6 alloy. However, when deformed
at 77 K, ductility of the alloys with the N content exceeding 1.0
at.% decreases dramatically and no necking occurs. Compared with
293 K, the yield and ultimate tensile strength of all the studied
alloys have been improved signiﬁcantly at the cryogenic temper-
ature. One may note that the yield strength of the N0 alloy is 398
MPa at 77 K, which is 110 MPa higher than that at 293 K. For the N1.0
alloy, the yield strength has been signiﬁcantly improved from 463
MPa to 1078 MPa when the temperature is lowered from 293 K to
77 K. Especially, an ultrahigh ultimate tensile strength of 1630 MPa
and a good uniform elongation of 33.5 % are also obtained in the N1.0
alloy at 77 K. Besides, with the decreasing temperature the elastic
modulus is elevated from 191 to 219 GPa. One can also ﬁnd that
the work hardening abilities of all the alloys at 77 K are higher than
that at 293 K (Fig. 2(c)), which is attributed to the enhanced ﬂow
stress at the cryogenic temperature [27]. Furthermore, an engineer-
ing strain of 11 % (∼0.1 true strain) can be regarded as the critical
point of the changed strain-hardening behavior at 77 K (as indicated
by the dashed grey line). That is, before this point the strain harden-
ing rate of the studied alloys decreases rapidly with the increased
strain. However, when the engineering strain exceeds 11 %, the
strain hardening rate of the N0 alloy starts to increase with defor-
mation, showing the hump-like strain hardening curve [37], while
that of the N1.0 alloy decreases slowly with the increasing load-
ing. The cryogenically deformed N1.0 specimen with the uniformly
elongated gauge part is shown in the inset of Fig. 2(c), suggesting
the satisfactory strain hardening ability of the alloy.
The XRD analysis of the alloys before and after deformation at
different temperatures is shown in Fig. 2(d). For the N0 alloy, the
athermal transformation from /H9253-austenite to /H9255-martensite occurs
at both 293 K and 77 K and the transformation is more signiﬁcant at
the lower temperature. For the N1.0 alloy, the martensitic transfor-
mation is only identiﬁed in the cryogenically deformed specimen
but not in under after room-temperature loading. With increasing
N doping, the N1.8 and N2.6 alloys do not show the TRIP effect at
either 293 K or 77 K.

4.2. Origin of the ultrahigh cryogenic yield strength in the
N-doped alloy
As shown in Fig. 2(a), with the increased N doping the yield
strength increases from 288 MPa in the N0 alloy to 596 MPa in
the N2.6 alloy at 293 K. Namely, the elevation of yield strength
induced by per at.% of N is ∼118 MPa. That is related to not only the
monotonically decreased average grain size but also the increased
dislocation density and lattice distortion with the increase of N.
At 77 K, the yield strength signiﬁcantly increases from 398 MPa
in the N0 alloy to 1206 MPa in the N2.6 alloy. It is noted that if
the effect of N solute atoms is considered, the elevation of yield
strength caused by doping per at.% of N is ∼448 MPa. Accordingly,
the yield strength of the N1.0 alloy is estimated to be around 847
MPa. However, the yield strength of this sample is as high as 1078
MPa, while a uniform elongation of 33.5 % can still be maintained.
Compared with other cryogenic alloys in which Ni and V are gen-
erally incorporated (Table 1) [18–28,50–56], the current N1.0 alloy
shows the more superior strength-ductility combination at both
room and cryogenic temperatures.
As the above statements, to possess the ultrahigh yield strength
of the N1.0 alloy, some unexpected strengthening mechanisms must
be activated in the elastic regime of the N-doped materials. The
microstructural evolution of the alloys deformed at 293 K has
shown that the athermal martensitic transformation is trivial in the
N1.0 alloy (Fig. S1). However, when deformed at the cryogenic tem-
perature signiﬁcant phase transformation as well as deformation
twinning has already been activated at an early stage of deforma-
tion (/H9255 ≤ 11 %, Figs. 3 and 5). Based on the variations of the SFE in
the Cantor alloy, Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 and Al0.6CoCrFeNi HEAs with
temperatures [23,27,44], i.e. the SFE decreases by 0.06∼0.09 mJ/m2
for each Kelvin decrease of the environmental temperature, the SFE
of the current N1.0 alloy at 77 K is estimated to be -3 to −1 mJ/m2.
This value is far below 20 mJ/m2, indicating that the TRIP effect can
be activated in the cryogenically deformed alloy.

4.3. Micromechanism of the N-doped alloy during plastic
deformation at 77 K
A good combination of the ultimate tensile strength (1630 MPa)
and uniform elongation (33.5 %) has been achieved in the cur-
rent N1.0 alloy at 77 K. The EBSD analysis shows that abundant
/H9255-martensite laths have been formed at an engineering strain of
11 %, i.e. the area fraction of /H9255-martensite increases from 0 to 22 %
during tensile loading (Fig. 3). As deformation continues, the frac-
tion of the hcp martensite slightly increases to 25 % at a fracture
strain of 33.5 %. This suggests that the TRIP effect becomes less
signiﬁcant than that at the early stage of deformation. The TEM
observation has shown that deformation twinning is also activated
in the cryogenically deformed N1.0 alloy when the strain is less than
11 %, and the twins and martensite laths can be distinguished by
morphologies (Fig. 5). At 33.5 % strain, a large number of lamel-
lar deformation twins and secondary twins are observed, while the
/H9255-martensite laths always coexist with twins (Fig. 6). The average
thickness and spacing of the deformation twins are reduced from
15 ± 0.9 and 80 ± 2.2 nm at 11 % strain to 6.5 ± 0.5 and 12 ± 1 nm
at 33.5 % strain, respectively, suggesting that the TWIP effect is the
dominant mechanism in this deformation stage. Simultaneously,
the average spacing of the /H9255-martensite laths also decreases sharply
from 103 ± 2.7 nm to 14 ± 1.1 nm with straining and the average
thickness of the laths changes from 14 ± 0.6 nm to 7 ± 0.7 nm. That
means the density of martensite laths increases locally under defor-
mation, even though the overall area fraction of /H9255-martensite does
not evolve dramatically. This is because in addition to athermal
martensitic transformation and mechanical twinning, the reverse
transformation from martensite to austenite, i.e. the B-TRIP effect
[17], also occurs in the N-doped alloy. The inﬂuence of the B-TRIP
effect becomes remarkable with increasing deformation, resulting
in the dynamically reﬁned grains. During deformation the signif-
icant reﬁnement of the microstructure then acts a strong barrier
to dislocation motion and enhances the ﬂow stress. In addition, the
multiple transformation behaviors release internal stress and delay
stress concentration, contributing to a retained ductility.
On the other hand, dislocation densities in the N1.0 alloy after
deformation are calculated with the method described in Section
4.1. When deformed at room temperature, the dislocation densities
are 7.9 × 1014 and 1.17 × 1015 /m2 in the 11 % and 33.5 % strained
samples, respectively. At 77 K, the corresponding dislocation den-
sities are 1.11 × 1015 and 4.15 × 1015 /m2, respectively. Namely, at
each constant strain the dislocation density at the cryogenic tem-
perature is much higher than that at 293 K. The dense dislocations
accumulated in the cryogenically deformed alloy also contribute to
its higher ﬂow stress compared with that in the room temperature
case.
The strengthening contributions of the N1.0 alloy deformed to
11 % and 33.5 % strains at 77 K are then quantitatively analyzed.
The corresponding ﬂow stresses at those strains are 1590 and 2188
MPa, respectively. The results show that except for the solid solu-
tion strengthening (∼299 MPa), grain boundary strengthening (∼72
MPa) and lattice friction strengthening (∼144 MPa), contributions
from the deformation induced /H9255-martensite are only 18 and 20
MPa in the 11 % and 33.5 % deformed alloy, respectively. Extraor-
dinarily, at a strain of 11 % the strengthening from dislocations
and dynamic Hall-Petch effect can reach 360 MPa and 697 MPa,
respectively. With further deformation up to 33.5 % strain, these
two contributions are 695 MPa and 958 MPa, respectively. That
is, for the cryogenically deformed alloy, the continuously reﬁned
microstructure together with the dense dislocations make a great
contribution to the enhancement of the ﬂow stress (Fig. 7(a)). As
the dynamic Hall-Petch effect originates from the hindrance of dis-
location slip by both twin boundaries and /H9255-martensite/austenite
interfaces, the total length of the two types of boundaries in the
N1.0 alloy is counted to quantify their individual contributions. The
results show that at an engineering strain of 11 %, twin boundaries
and /H9255-martensite/austenite interfaces provide 232 and 464 MPa to
the ﬂow stress, respectively. At 33.5 % strain, the corresponding
contributions are 440 and 518 MPa, respectively. Namely, at the
early stage of straining (/H9255 ≤ 11 %), the TRIP effect is the dominant
deformation mode. When the strain is higher than 11 %, the more
rapid strengthening from twin boundaries suggests that the TWIP
effect dominates the deformation. Schematic sketches illustrating
the micromechanisms of the alloys with and without N doping
during cryogenic deformation are shown in Fig. 7(b).

5. Conclusions
The mechanical properties of a series of non-equiatomic FeMn-
CoCrN HEAs with the varying N contents have been obtained at both
293 K and 77 K. The temperature-dependent microstructural evo-
lution at different strains was studied to explore the deformation
mechanisms. The conclusions are as follows:
(1) At 293 K, the yield and ultimate tensile strength increase from
288 and 697 MPa in the N0 alloy to 596 and 977 MPa in the
N2.6 alloy, respectively. Correspondingly, with the increase of
N content, the uniform elongation decreases from 68 % to 47 %.
At 77 K, the yield and ultimate tensile strength increase signiﬁ-
cantly from 398 and 1323 MPa in the N0 alloy to 1206 and 1620
MPa in the N2.6 alloy, respectively. The corresponding uniform
elongation has been reduced from 53 % to 11 %.
(2) The N1.0 alloy exhibits a high yield strength of 1078 MPa and
an excellent strain hardening ability at 77 K. Speciﬁcally, an
ultimate tensile strength of 1630 MPa and a good uniform elon-
gation of 33.5 % are obtained.
(3) The ultrahigh yield strength of the N1.0 alloy at the cryo-
genic temperature mainly originates from the reﬁnement of
microstructures induced by the activation of athermal trans-

formation and deformation twinning. In addition, the increased
lattice friction at low temperatures also contributes to the
strength elevation. In the plastic regime, the TRIP and the
TWIP effects dominate deformation prior to and after the
11 % engineering strain, respectively. The dynamic Hall-Petch
effect induced by the increased twin boundaries and /H9255-
martensite/austenite interfaces leads to an ultrahigh ultimate
strength. The excellent ductility of the alloy originates from the
joint contribution of dislocation slip, phase transformation and
deformation twinning.
(4) The SFE of the FeMnCoCr HEA has been increased with the intro-
duction of the interstitial N, leading to the mechanically stable
fcc structure in the N-doped alloys. When deformed at cryo-
genic temperatures, the N1.0 alloy becomes metastable again,
i.e. the TRIP, TWIP and B-TRIP effects are activated. The signif-
icant reﬁnement of microstructures induced by these effects
together with the solid solution strengthening caused by the
interstitial N atoms jointly contribute to the enhanced strain
hardening ability of the alloy. The material design concept pro-
posed in this work has the advantages of developing more
advanced cryogenic alloys with outstanding mechanical prop-
erties and low costs.
