abstract
A commercial Niobium eHafniumeTitanium (Nb-Hf-Ti) alloy (C-103), produced by vacuum arc remelting
(VAR), showed clear signatures of internal oxidation viz. presence of monoclinic-HfO 2 at all the grain
boundaries. Minor cold rolling, to ~10% reduction in thickness, initiated cold cracking. This was observed
at some, but not all, of the high angle Nb eNb grain boundaries containing coarse HfO 2 2nd phase oxide
precipitates. Closer microscopic examination revealed cold cracking at selective Nb-HfO 2 interfaces.
There appeared no clear mesoscopic experimental rationale behind this selective interfacial cold
cracking. In order to understand this, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed on the
metal-oxide interface models. They showed signi ﬁcant separation or decohesion energy difference
(~76%) with different atomistic bonding at the terminating layer of the Nb-HfO 2 interface. In particular,
the interface with one atomic layer of Nb bonded with oxygen of HfO 2 had very low work of separation,
which was about ~8% lower than the Nb eNb interface of the bulk Nb. In brief, the DFT simulations
brought out the de ﬁning role of metal-oxide interface ’s terminating layer on the interface decohesion.
This is in agreement with the past theoretical research performed on several metal||non-metal interfaces.
The present study extends this hypothesis to a practical example of selective cold cracking of a com-
mercial Nb-Hf-Ti alloy.

1. Introduction
Niobium (Nb) alloys retain reasonable strength, beyond the
capabilities of Ni-base superalloys [ 1], at very high temperatures.
Naturally, they ﬁnd niche applications [ 2] in aerospace. Often the
commercial Nb-alloys contain ~10 wt % hafnium (Hf) which pro-
vides excellent solid solution strengthening [ 3]: strengthening
extending to temperatures as high as ~1400 /C14 C[ 4,5]. Hf also acts as
a sacriﬁcial element for any internal oxidation [ 6,7]. Small quantity
of Ti with other elements like Zr (in very small amount), on the
other hand, improves the overall oxidation resistance [ 8,9]. This
study involved internal oxidation and associated cold cracking at
selective metal-oxide interfaces in a commercial Nb-Hf-Ti alloy (C-
103). The cold-cracking was observed in the form of room
temperature cleavage fracture without signi ﬁcant plastic
deformation.
One of the critical issues in a commercial Nb-alloy is its oxygen
content [1]. Enormous care is taken during the processing to avoid
internal oxidation [ 10]. The latter may appear as grain boundary
(GB) HfO 2 2nd phase oxide precipitates, and for higher oxygen
content even as grain interior Widmanst €atten oxides [ 11]. Past
studies [ 12,13] have shown that oxides affect both strength and
ductility. In complex forming environment, they appear to initiate
cold cracking [14]. However, very little has been documented on the
exact nature of the oxides and on the metal-oxide interface:
especially on the possible role of such interfaces on cold-cracking in
Nb, which is precisely the motivation behind the present study.
The starting material, a commercial C-103 alloy, had oxides
(HfO2) at all of its grain boundaries. Cold-cracking was observed in
some (but not all) of the metal-oxide interfaces. Extensive micro-
textural characterization failed to provide an apparent rationale
behind the mechanism of selective cold cracking. In order to look

The nature and decohesion of the metals||non-metals (oxides/
carbides/nitrides) (here ‘||’ means interface) were determined by
the atomic bonding of the terminating layer at the interface [ 15,16].
It has been noted that the electronic interactions, applicable at
metal-oxide interface, involve strongly hybridized metallic, ionic or
covalent bonding: all of which can be accounted for by the DFT
calculations. In the past, ab-initio simulations have been used to
understand the phase separation/decohesion between Nb||Al 2O3
[17], Al||WC [ 18], Al||VC [ 19], Al||VN [ 19], Al||TiN [ 20], Fe||WC [ 21],
g-Ni(Al)||a-Al2O3 [22], SiC||W [ 23] (see Table S1 ) and many other
similar interfaces. Using the ﬁrst-principles calculations, it was thus
possible to calculate the work of separation (Wsep), representing the
amount of reversible work per unit area of interface required to
separate the two crystals by mechanical relaxation of atomic po-
sitions after separation and not allowing any diffusion. The Wsep of
one particular interface viz. The … NbNb||NbOHfO … was found to
be signiﬁcantly lower than the … NbNb||NbNb …, …NbNbOHf||OHf
… and … NbNb||OHfO … interfaces, which may provide a possible
explanation for the experimentally observed selective cold
cracking.
2. Experimental methods
A commercial C-103 alloy, chemical composition listed in
Table 1, was produced by vacuum arc remelting (VAR) followed by
light hot forging. A batch of production ended up with higher ox-
ygen content: ~360 ppm (parts per million by weight) oxygen (O)
when the maximum allowed oxygen concentration was only
100 ppm. Other impurities (example: carbon (C), nitrogen (N) and
hydrogen (H)) were measured and found to be less than 0.01 wt%
each. These trace level impurities are not expected to affect the
mechanical properties [ 10]. Further, all the Nb eNb grain bound-
aries showed signature of internal oxidation.
Hot forged alloy was cold rolled to various thickness reductions.
This study, however, reported (for brevity) data on ~10% cold rolled
specimen, where observations on cold-cracking and grain bound-
ary HfO 2 were made with appropriate ﬁeld emission gun (FEG)
scanning electron microscope (SEM). Metallographic sample
preparations were performed by polishing with sub-micron
colloidal silica. These were then subjected to microscopic charac-
terization involving a FEI™ Quanta 3 d-FEG (Q3d-FEG: for analytical
microscopy) and a FEI ™ Nova-Nano-SEM (NNS: for imaging). In
particular, the Q3d-FEG was used for energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) and electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) e
EDAX™ systems. The NNS, on the other hand, provided imaging,
especially imaging with CBS ™.
The HfO
2 precipitates at the grain boundaries were extracted
using dissolution of the test specimen in an HF and HNO 3 (1:1)
solution. The residue was separated in a high-speed centrifuge,
followed by ﬁltration and then X-ray diffraction: a Bruker ™ D8-
Discover with micro-focus X-ray plus Vantec ™ detector. Area de-
tector signals from multiple 2nd phase clusters were obtained and
collated, for further details the reader may refer to Refs. [ 32]. The
phase identi ﬁcation and quanti ﬁcation were performed through a
commercial software Highscore-plus ™.
3. Computational details
DFT calculations were performed using the projector
augmented wave (PAW) method [ 24,25] as implemented in the
QUANTUM ESPRESSO package [ 26], which is a plane wave based
code. For the exchange-correlation functional the Perdew-Burke-
Ernzerhof (PBE) [ 27] variant of generalized gradient approxima-
tions (GGA) was employed. Total energy of the systems was
calculated by complete relaxation (geometric and atomic). The
relaxation was performed using force convergence to less than
1 /C2 10/C0 4 Ry/a. u. for all the structures. Monkhorst-Pack scheme
with converged k-point mesh of 3 /C2 3 /C2 1 was used in the entire
supercell. Converged cut-off energy of 50 Ry was used for conver-
gence of the total energy of the system. Davidson type diagonal-
ization method was implemented with the convergence energy
threshold of 1 /C2 10/C0 9 Ry.
Since a signiﬁcant proportion of the major alloying element (Hf)
was internally oxidized and the region near the GB became de ﬁ-
cient in Hf (as shown latter with analytical microscopy), the DFT
calculations were limitedly performed on interfaces containing Nb
and HfO 2, where also the cold cracking was observed. The super-
cells in the DFT simulations incorporated experimental Orientation
Relationship (OR) between Nb and HfO 2 (2nd phase oxide pre-
cipitates). The experimental OR was obtained from the EBSD (also
shown latter) as: (001) Nb//(001)Oxide and <001>Nb//<103>Oxide
(here ‘//’ means parallel to). The relaxed lattice parameter of the
bulk Nb (Im-3m) and HfO2 (P21/c), as listed in Table S2, were used as
an initial conﬁguration. In the plane of the interface, a 2 /C2 2 unit cell
of HfO2 (a ¼ 5.1034 Å,b ¼ 5.1508 Å) was placed on top of a 3/C2 3 unit
cell of Nb (a ¼ 3.304 Å) in order to maintain the lattice mismatch to
less than 3% (i.e. for Nb, 3 /C2 3.304 Å ¼ ~9.9 Å and for HfO 2,
2 /C2 5.103¼ ~10.0 Å) (see Fig. S1). The lattice parameters were found
to be in agreement with the literature [ 35e38] (see Table S2).
Finally, Wsep, which represents the work that is needed for the
breaking of the interfacial bonds and creation of two free surfaces,
was calculated. Of course, this calculation does not account for the
interplay of complex phenomena such as diffusion or plastic

4. Results and discussions
Fig. 1 represents a composite SEM micrograph of the 10% cold
rolled C-103 sheet. Superimposed on the same micrograph, of
visible cold cracking, are the Nb eNb grain boundaries. It is to be
noted that cracks were observed in ~20%, but not all, of such grain
boundaries. It may be further noted that the cold cracking remained
selective even at higher rolling reductions: a maximum of ~30% of
the grain boundaries showing visible cold cracking even towards
the latter stages (see Fig. S4 ). All the grain boundaries contained
coarse precipitates, identi ﬁed as hafnium oxide (HfO
2). However, a
few (and not all) such precipitate-containing grain boundaries
showed visible cold cracking. One such grain boundary containing
HfO2 precipitates, which was fractured along the grain boundary
surface, is shown in Fig. 2 . Cold cracking was observed only in the
rolled specimens. In particular, this was restricted only to selective
metal-oxide interfaces (see Fig. 3 a). In particular, only ~20 e30%
(and not all) of the metal-oxide interfaces showed this cold-
cracking (see Fig. 3b).
EBSD measurements were made on the hot forged structures,
before and after the cold rolling. They showed presence of grain
interior deformation banding (or narrow bands of strain localiza-
tions, see Fig. 4a). This, clearly an integral part of the cold deformed
microstructures, was observed in grain clusters with and without
the cold cracking. Near-boundary regions without visible cracks
showed (see Fig. 4b) misorientation developments or signatures of
plasticity. Boundaries with cold-cracking, on the other hand, had
insigniﬁcant misorientation evolution and hence limited plasticity.
There was no clear distinction in the nature (axis-angle values) of
grain boundaries with and without cold-cracking. Arguably, grain
boundaries with cracks appeared to have a higher probability of
possessing coarse 2nd phase (see Fig. 5 a). However, many of the
boundaries with coarse 2nd phase also did not show cold cracking.
Fig. 5 b shows that the GB misorientation angles are higher for
boundaries with cracks than without cracks. In general, cold
cracking was observed on some (and not all) of the Nb eNb high
angle grain boundaries containing coarse 2nd phase (HfO
2).
Non-aggressive dissolution of the matrix phase (as described in
the experimental method) was used to extract the 2nd phase
(Fig. 6 ). XRD data were obtained from many of such extracted
particles, using micro-focus XRD and an area detector. Analysis of
the XRD showed presence of majority (~90% by volume) mono-
clinic-HfO2, and minority Nb plus hafnium-niobium oxide
(Hf6Nb2O17) phases. Analytical microscopy (see Fig. 7), by EDS, also
conﬁrmed the presence of monoclinic-HfO 2 at all the GBs. Finally,
EBSD provided (Fig. 8a) crystallography of the GB HfO 2. It appeared
that the oxide phase nucleated at the GB and then had grown. Clear
growth twins decorated the oxide phase (also shown in Fig. 8a with
point-to-origin misorientation distribution over the twinning re-
gion of the precipitate). This nucleation causes Hf de ﬁcient regions
adjacent to the GBs (shown in Fig. 7). The oxide phase typically had
an OR with the matrix Nb. This is shown in Fig. 8 ba s( 0 0 1 )Nb//
(001)Oxide and <001>Nb//<103>Oxide. The experimental crystal
structures and OR were used for the subsequent DFT calculations.

Though experimental mesoscopic EBSD data (from the present
study) were extensive, they failed to explain the mechanism of
crack initiation. Analytical microscopy, with EDS, also did not
indicate any difference in the interface chemistry. It was hence
inferred that along with the precipitate (ppt) size, and misorien-
tation relationship, difference may exist in the atomistic nature of
the interfacial chemistry and bonding. Such an aspect would be
extremely dif ﬁcult to establish experimentally, and ab-initio
atomistic simulations were used for gathering further insights into
the experimental observations.
The Nb-HfO2 interface, with appropriate OR: (001)Nb//(001)Oxide
and <001>Nb//<103>Oxide (Fig. 8 b), was modelled for the DFT cal-
culations. Fig. 9 shows the schematic of the Nb-HfO 2 interface. The
difference in the nature, and hence energy, of such an interface
would depend on the bonding of the terminating layer [17e23]. The
Wsep was calculated for ﬁve different terminating layers and for
bulk Nb (see Table 2) using eqn. (1). The interface … NbNbOHf||OHf
… was found to show the highest Wsep value (9.63 J/m 2). This
implied that a cleavage in the HfO2 crystals was hard to occur due to
strong covalent bonding between Hf and O atoms. Whereas, in case
of the termination of … NbNb||NbOHfO …, the Wsep was found to be
the lowest. The difference in the Wsep values between these two
interfaces was found to be ~76%. The interface preference of Nb
surface formation after the cleavage of … NbNb||NbOHfO …, can be
rationalized from this difference in the Wsep. The difference be-
tween the Wsep of … NbNb||NbOHfO … (4.33 J/m2) and that of bulk
… NbNb||NbNb … (4.7 J/m2) is not signiﬁcant, but was still decisive
for the cleavage. In case of NbeNb, the bonding is metallic whereas,
in case of Nb eO, it is ionic because of transfer of electrons from Nb
to O, which justi ﬁes the higher Wsep in the latter case. Thus, the
grain boundaries where HfO 2 is formed with O interface with the
Nb metal, was found to be more susceptible to separation or cold
cracking on deformation. However, all the other interfaces were
found to show signi ﬁcantly high (at least greater than ~8%) Wsep as
compared to … NbNb||NbOHfO … interface. Thus, they were not
prone to separation. Although experimentally, it appeared that the
separation had taken place at the interface between Nb and O
terminated HfO 2 (… NbNb||OHfO …), the DFT computed Wsep
values revealed the termination to have taken place at the bulk Nb
at Nb terminated HfO 2 interface (… NbNb||NbOHfO …).
In order to understand the interaction at Nb-HfO 2 interface in
more details, Bader charge analysis was employed [ 30,31]. The net
average Bader charge on the atoms of different layers is compared
in Fig. 10. The net average positive charge on Nb1 atoms (bonded to
the O1 atoms of the interface) was found to be much higher than
that of Nb atoms in the subsequent layers away from the interface
(i.e. Nb2, Nb3). Thus, the Bader analysis con ﬁrmed that a net charge
transfer has taken place from the Nb1 to O1 (whose net average
negative charge content is higher than the O2 and O3 atoms). This
conﬁrms the bonding between Nb1 and O1. Thus, it implies that the
most relaxed cleavage out of all possible termination was one with
Nb saturated surface i.e. the separation had taken place by leaving
one atomic layer of Nb attached to HfO 2 (… NbNb||NbOHfO …)a t
the interface.

The present study thus acts as an interesting practical extension
to the exhaustive past research [ 17e23] on decohesion of metal||
non-metal (oxide/carbide/nitride) interfaces. The presence of
HfO2 precipitates de ﬁnitely played an important role in the local-
ized plastic deformation. The selective (10 e20% of the total) in-
terfaces of Nb-HfO 2 were subjected to decohesion without any
plastic deformation. Arguably, these were coarse 2nd phase parti-
cles existing at high angle Nb eNb grain boundaries. However,
decohesion was not observed in many such interfaces. The analysis
of interface separation energies based on Wsep, as shown in Table 2,
thus provided a plausible explanation for the interesting experi-
mental observation on selective cold cracking.
It is generally believed that the presence of coarse oxide/car-
bide/nitride particles is detrimental to the ductility of metallic
materials [ 32e34]. This has been attributed to the formation of
particle deformed zone. Prakash et al., for example, showed that
prolonged heat-treatment, which enabled partial dissolution of the
2nd phase, improved the ductility signi ﬁcantly, without sacri ﬁcing
the strength [ 32]. This was attributed to a ‘scaling’ of dislocation
mobility and a reduced severity of a particle deformed zone.
Another possibility, emerging out of the present study, which re-
mains scienti ﬁc and not technological at this stage, is to tailor the
interface chemistry. Ashby [ 34] in his classical 1966 paper, while
proposing a model for dispersion-hardened single crystals, recog-
nized the role of interface for relative plasticity and failure or
decohesion. The present study, which is a continuation of past
exhaustive research [ 17e23] on theoretical simulations, brought
out the possibility of extending DFT simulations in exploring en-
gineering failures.

5. Conclusions
A commercial NiobiumeHafniumeTitanium (Nb-Hf-Ti) alloy (C-
103) was produced by VAR. The alloy had 360 ppm oxygen, and
showed internal oxidation at all GBs. The GB oxide phase was
identiﬁed as monoclinic-HfO 2, and an OR (001) Nb//(001)Oxide and
<001>Nb//<103>Oxide was experimentally established. Subsequent
cold rolling led to cold-cracking, where ~20% (but not all) of the
metal-oxide interfaces showed visible cracking or decohesion
without any plasticity. The only difference found in the GB with and
without cracks were the ppt size and the misorientation between
the adjacent grains. Although this gives us the partial under-
standing of selective cold cracking, they failed to explain the
mechanism by which it occurs. DFT calculations were employed to
