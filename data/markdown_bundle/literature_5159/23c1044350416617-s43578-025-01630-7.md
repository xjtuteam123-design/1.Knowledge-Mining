Refractory high‑entropy alloys (RHEAs) comprising multiple high‑melting point elements have been 
proposed as replacements for Ni‑based superalloys. Here, we critically assess the research performed 
on RHEAs, provide perspectives on their salient features, and suggest directions for future research to 
fill gaps in current knowledge. We conclude that single‑phase RHEAs are either too weak in creep at 
high temperatures or too brittle at low homologous temperatures to make them viable replacements 
for Ni‑based superalloys. Precipitates can potentially enhance creep strength but only if their solvus 
temperatures are higher than the operating temperatures of superalloys, which rules out those reliant 
on Al additions. Ru‑containing precipitates have high solvus temperatures but there is essentially no 
information about their mechanical properties, so further studies are needed to evaluate their potential. 
Research is also needed to understand the strength–ductility/toughness tradeoff in the presence of 
precipitates, and the constraints imposed by the poor oxidation resistance of RHEAs. In the two main 
classes of single‑phase RHEAs, improving the room‑temperature ductility of V‑Nb‑Ta‑Mo‑W alloys should 
be a priority but attempts at further improving the room‑temperature ductility of Ti‑Zr ‑Hf‑Nb‑Ta alloys 
seems misguided as they currently have adequate ductility.
Introduction
High-temperature materials have been a persistent challenge 
for materials scientists and engineers. The goal for such materi-
als is simple to define but difficult to achieve: survive higher 
temperatures, bear higher loads, and often, while operating 
in reactive environments. To withstand temperatures of about 
1000–1100 °C, Ni-based superalloys are the current state-of-
the-art for high-pressure turbine blades in jet engines because 
of their relevant properties, including strength and fabricability, 
oxidation resistance, and density [1]. Gas inlet temperatures in 
jet engines can actually be a few hundred degrees higher than 
the above metal temperatures, because cooling channels within 
turbine blades and thermal barrier coatings on surfaces provide 
protection from the hot gases [2].
For applications at temperatures higher than the solvus 
temperatures of the intermetallic precipitates responsible for 
the superior creep strength of superalloys, alternatives become 
necessary but the pickings are slim. Examples include Pt and Ir 
that belong to the precious metals group and have relatively high 
melting temperatures and decent oxidation resistance. However, 
their high cost makes them viable only for certain niche applica-
tions such as crucibles for the melting/pulling of oxide glasses 
and fibers [3 –6] and containment vessels for the plutonia fuel 
in radioisotope thermoelectric generators used to power inter -
planetary spacecraft [7–11].
Other possibilities include refractory metals belonging to 
groups IV to VI of the periodic table such as W , Ta, Mo, and Nb 
with very high melting temperatures. However, some of them 
are brittle at room temperature (e.g., W , Mo) [12, 13], while oth-
ers can become embrittled by interstitial contaminants (e.g., Ta, 
Nb) [14, 15] and they tend to be heavy and have poor oxidation 
resistance [16, 17], which limits their widespread use. Silicide 
intermetallics based on refractory metals have been investigated 
to overcome some of these drawbacks (e.g., [18, 19]) but they, 
too, have not made significant inroads, except as molydisilicide 
heating elements in high-temperature furnaces. Much of what 
we know about the properties of refractory metals dates back 
to the decades of the 1950s–1980s when there was sustained

The early 2000s saw the emergence of a new material class, 
the so-called high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [ 24], followed by the 
refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) consisting of refrac -
tory elements [ 25]. HEAs generated considerable excitement 
in the materials science community because of the large com-
positional space available for the discovery of new alloys with 
potentially useful properties. Initially, configurational entropy 
was used as a simple criterion to subdivide alloys of this class 
into low-, medium-, and high-entropy alloys [26]. Here, for the 
sake of simplicity, we will use the term HEAs to refer to all alloys 
that consist of at least three elements in concentrated amounts, 
whether or not they are entropy-stabilized single-phase solid 
solutions. Other names, such as multiple principal element 
alloys (MPEAs) and complex concentrated alloys (CCAs), are 
also used in the literature to describe these alloys but it is not 
our intention here to discuss the relative merits of the different 
taxonomies. For purposes of this article, RHEAs are subsets of 
HEAs whose constituent elements are metallic elements primar-
ily from groups IV , V , and VI of the periodic table [27].
Much of the initial scientific interest in certain HEAs 
stemmed from their single-phase microstructure which was 
later found to be due to slow kinetics rather than inherent ther-
modynamic stability as secondary phases emerged in FCC HEAs 
after prolonged heat treatments [28]. Analogously, while the first 
reported RHEAs were single-phase alloys [25, 29], intermediate-
temperature anneals have since shown that they also decompose 
into multiphase microstructures [30–32]. Therefore, within the 
broader class of RHEAs, we include here alloys with secondary 
phases [27].
Overviews of RHEAs can be found in [ 27, 33, 34] and 
papers focusing on mechanical behavior include [35–38]. Here, 
our goal is narrower, namely, to point out challenges faced by 
RHEAs that need to be overcome before they can become viable 
high-temperature structural materials. One such challenge, for 
example, is that, as single-phase alloys, they either lack high-
temperature strength or low-temperature ductility. Additional 
research is needed to develop suitable multiphase RHEAs to 
confront these challenges. In this article, we examine some of the 
available data relevant for high-temperature service and efforts 
to produce multiphase RHEAs.
High‑temperature mechanical properties 
of “single‑phase” RHEAs
As mentioned above, certain RHEAs can be single-phase solid 
solutions at elevated temperatures, but many tend to decompose 
into multiphase microstructures at lower temperatures [30–32]. 
By decomposition, we simply mean that the initial microstruc-
tures disintegrate or separate into new ones; no spinodal-type 
reaction is necessarily implied. The high-temperature solid solu-
tion state can be kinetically prevented from forming multiphase 
structures relatively easily, that is, without resorting to heroic 
quench rates: uncontrolled furnace cooling in a vacuum furnace 
[39], air cooling in evacuated quartz capsules [40], as well as 
water quenching [41], all result in apparently single-phase BCC 
microstructures at room temperature. Of course, what appears 
to be a single phase at one resolution and with one characteriza-
tion technique might contain incipient secondary phases when 
examined at higher resolutions or with different techniques. 
Further studies are thus warranted for clarification. Meanwhile, 
the mechanical properties of nominally single-phase RHEAs 
have been investigated, as discussed in the following sections. 
That said, their tendency to phase separate will remain a con-
cern, possibly even a limiting constraint, in applications where 
a part is subjected to repeated thermal cycles across vulnerable 
temperature ranges.
Uniaxial mechanical properties under quasi‑static 
strain rates
VNbTaMoW and NbTaMoW were the first RHEAs synthesized 
[25] and among the few tested at temperatures to 1600 °C [ 42] 
which is above the γ’ solvus temperature of Ni-based superalloys 
[43]. The alloys had high yield strengths between 600 and 1600 
°C at 10–3  s−1: 862 to 477 MPa for VNbTaMoW and 561 MPa to 
405 MPa for NbTaMoW [42], as shown by the purple and red 
data points in Fig. 1. Subsequently, a ductile RHEA was discov-
ered, equiatomic TiZrHfNbTa, which could be rolled extensively 
at room temperature, recrystallized, and tensile tested [ 30, 40]. 
Despite this malleability, only Mills et al. [39] have investigated 
its high-temperature mechanical properties in tension above 
800 °C. In compression, on the other hand, there have been two

disadvantages, when compared to existing high-temperature 
wrought alloys.
Despite the almost exclusive focus in the RHEA literature on 
quasi-static tests, it is worth noting that they provide only yield 
strengths which are imperfect proxies for how materials might 
perform in applications such as jet engines where creep loading 
and cyclic loading determine component lifetimes. While data 
such as those shown in Fig.  1 can serve as initial screens, more 
relevant mechanical properties are indeed needed. That said, 
for applications where lifetimes are short, such as rockets and 
missiles [52], results of quasi-static tests are useful.
The higher compressive yield strengths of VNbTaMoW and 
NbTaMoW come with serious tradeoffs, including, most notably, 
severe brittleness in tension. Consider, for example, NbTaMoW , 
which has some ductility in compression, Fig. 2, red squares, but 
essentially no tensile ductility, even at elevated temperatures, red 
circles in Fig. 2, where the true strains range from approximately 
0.01 to 0.035% at temperatures of 800–1200 °C [53]. Further -
more, fracture toughness values up to 1200 °C were all below 11 
MPa m0.5, and they were dependent on microstructure and crack 
path, results that were partially attributed to a nanoscale oxide 
layer on the grain boundaries and to the differences between the 
columnar and equiaxed grain structure between specimens [53]. 
Clearly, additional work is needed to characterize the tensile 
mechanical properties of the V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W family of alloys 
and then find ways to improve their room-temperature ductility 
and fracture toughness.
Even the ductile TiZrHfNbTa alloy can be embrittled as a 
result of thermal instability as discussed below. Senkov et al. 
[44] could compress specimens up to 50% from room tempera-
ture to 1200 °C, Fig.  2, blue squares (with the caveat that their 
compression specimens were 3.8 mm in diameter and 5.7 mm 
in height for an aspect ratio of 1.5; therefore, strains in excess 
of about 34% are not truly meaningful as the specimen aspect 
ratio then becomes less than 1, preventing unconstrained 45° 
slip from occurring). Mills et al. [39] found that TiZrHfNbTa, 
which was initially ductile at room temperature became embrit-
tled at 800 °C at quasi-static tensile strain rates of 10–2  s−1 and 
 10–1  s−1, blue circles in Fig.  2, due to a Nb–Ta-rich BCC phase 
that formed on the grain boundaries during preheating which 
took approximately six minutes [39]. Tensile ductility returned 
at 1200 °C where the alloy becomes single-phase again. It is 
important to note that, for TiZrHfNbTa, the elongation given 
in [39], and more generally in the literature [37], is total elonga-
tion to failure, which includes both uniform and non-uniform 
deformation, more on this in "Room-temperature mechanical 
properties of RHEAs" section. Nevertheless, in RHEAs suscepti-
ble to phase decomposition at intermediate temperatures, there 
will likely be detrimental effects even in parts used mostly at 
higher temperatures (above ~ 1000 °C), because they have to 
cycle through these intermediate temperatures during start-up 
and shut-down of engines.

A related off-equiatomic RHEA Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 is one of 
the few RHEAs whose high-temperature fracture toughness has 
been measured yielding values ranging from 52 MPa m0.5 at 950 
°C to 88 MPa m0.5 at 1200 °C [54]. These are comparable to (if 
somewhat lower than) those of René N5, a second-generation 
single-crystal Ni-based superalloy [55]. The yield strength of 
 Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 is only 596 MPa at room temperature and 140 
MPa at 1200 °C [54], compared to CMSX-4 [46] whose yield 
strengths are 942 MPa and 146 MPa at room temperature and 
1204 °C, respectively.
To recapitulate, alloys belonging to the V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W 
system have high yield strengths but very low ductility. Poor 
ductility is a hard nut to crack because fracture is a “weakest 
link” phenomenon and there are multiple possible reasons 
for brittleness. After surveying the published literature, there 
appear to be no obvious pathways for improving the ductility 
of V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W alloys. In contrast, alloys based on the 
Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta system have good ductility (absent envi-
ronmental effects or phase decomposition) but low strengths. 
Strength is easier to solve for, with precipitation hardening 
being one of the likely paths forward (as discussed in a later 
section). However, given the ubiquitous strength–ductility 
tradeoff, it remains to be seen whether alloys belonging to the 
Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta system can be substantially strengthened 
without sacrificing ductility and toughness.
Uniaxial mechanical properties at creep strain rates
In the single-phase state, TiZrHfNbTa is significantly weaker 
in creep than a leading nickel-based superalloy CSMX-4, Fig. 3 
[56]. For example, at ~ 1100 °C, the stress to attain a minimum 
creep rate of 10–7  s−1 is a factor of 70 lower for TiZrHfNbTa 
than for CMSX-4; at 980 °C, it is a factor of 25 lower. Of course, 
CMSX-4 is a precipitation-strengthened alloy, so this weakness 
of a single-phase solid solution is not surprising. What is disap-
pointing though is that TiZrHfNbTa is also weaker than the 
single-phase solid solution matrix of CMSX-4 at 980 °C, purple 
line in Fig. 3 [56]. Most likely, this weakness is due to the more 
open BCC crystal structure of TiZrHfNbTa in which diffusion 
is expected to be faster than the close-packed FCC matrix of 
CMSX-4.
The creep exponent of TiZrHfNbTa at 980 °C is 2 at stresses 
below about 50 MPa and 4.2 at higher stresses, whereas at 1100 
°C, it remains at 2 over the entire range of applied stresses [56], 
red line in Fig. 3. Similar creep exponents (2.5–2.8) were meas-
ured in a different study [57] between 1100 and 1250 °C sug-
gesting that the creep mechanisms are the same between 980 
and 1250 °C. In radioactive tracer diffusion measurements of 
Zr-89 in TiZrHfNbTa between 850 and 1150 °C, Zr diffusivities 
in TiZrHfNbTa were relatively fast compared to a geometric 
mean of Zr diffusion in the pure metals suggesting that diffusion 
is enhanced [58]. If fast diffusion of other elements occurs in a 
similar fashion, it could be a hindrance to improving the creep 
life of TiZrHfNbTa.
An additional concern is the introduction of interstitial 
elements during creep. Monfared et al. [59] performed creep 
studies on Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 and found that its creep behavior 
is sensitive to the testing environment with the specimens tested 
in Ar having shorter creep life. Additionally, the stress exponent 
at 900 °C was 4.1 in vacuum and 1.2 or 5.7, depending on stress, 
in Ar atmosphere attributed to air ingress based on post-test 
inert gas fusion analysis. Lower creep exponents (around 1) are 
indicative of atomic diffusion creep mechanisms, such as Coble 
and Nabarro–Herring creep, while higher values (~ 3–5) imply 
diffusion-assisted dislocation climb [60]. These effects of inter-
stitial contamination on creep behavior do not bode well for the 
use of RHEAs at elevated temperatures in air.
Oxidation behavior of RHEAs
Oxidation is a critical issue for many high-temperature applica-
tions and detailed overviews of RHEA oxidation can be found in 
[61, 62]. Here, a few key aspects are briefly summarized. RHEAs 
have to contend with many of the problems that conventional 
refractory metals have to deal with such as volatile oxides [ 63] 
and pesting [64]. Oxidation resistance can be improved by add-
ing Al, Si, and Cr in sufficient quantities to form a scale [17, 
65], but such additions promote secondary phases in RHEAs 
[66–68]. Aluminum added to TiZrHfNbTa reduces mass gain in 
oxidation tests by scale formation below 1100 °C [69], but it can 
embrittle the alloy [70]. More recent efforts focused on reducing 
the Al content needed to passivate, and pesting was found to be 
prevented by the addition of as little as 4.8-at.% Al [71]. That 
paper also suggested a new design approach based on reducing 
Zr and Hf to prevent their oxides forming at the scale–metal 
interface. In the absence of strong scale formers, many RHEAs 
have poor oxidation resistance, a result of their thick and porous

Intermediate‑temperature phase stability 
of “single‑phase” RHEAs
As mentioned earlier, the first RHEAs to be reported had 
single-phase BCC microstructures (e.g., [ 25, 29]). However, 
as is the case in FCC HEAs [ 79], RHEAs are often metasta -
ble and decompose into multiphase structures at intermedi-
ate temperatures [30– 32]. Significantly more work has been 
done on microstructural evolution at different temperatures in 
Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta alloys than in V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W alloys and 
the former will thus be the main focus of this section.
The multiphase region of TiZrHfNbTa occurs, roughly, 
between 500 and 1000 °C. Cold rolling followed by annealing 
at 800 °C for 2 h [30] and up to 256 h [40] both resulted in 
a microstructure consisting of two phases, BCC1 and BCC2. 
Schuh et al. [31] annealed TiZrHfNbTa after high-pressure tor-
sion at 500 °C for 100 h and found three phases: BCC1, BCC2, 
and HCP . Annealing for 1 h above 1000 °C resulted in a single-
phase BCC microstructure [31]. After creep testing at 980 °C for 
61 h, Gadelmeier et al. [56] found secondary phases at the grain 
boundaries of TiZrHfNbTa but only in the gauge section, pre-
sumably driven by the applied stress. The embrittlement noted 
by Mills et al. [39] was attributed by them to a secondary BCC 
grain formed at 800 °C during preheating; further annealing at 
100 h confirmed its presence as a thermodynamically favored 
phase.
There are relatively few studies on how compositional modi-
fications affect thermal stability of the Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta system. 
Coury et al. [68] investigated effects of Cr, Mo, Al, Hf, and W in 
NbTaTi. The alloys were arc melted and homogenized at 1400 
°C for 35 h. Alloys with Cr (except CrMoNbTi) tended to form 
BCC plus C14 and C15 Laves phases, while Al-containing alloys 
formed A15 phases. Maiti and Struer [80] found that Zr and Hf 
clustered together and formed secondary HCP phases in ZrH -
fNbTa annealed at 1800 °C for 6 to 192 h, a temperature where 
the material should be single-phase BCC based on the papers 
mentioned earlier. Their anomalous result could have been the 
result of interstitial contamination as discussed below. Poulain 
et al. [81] examined the effects of O on the thermal stability of 
TiZrHfNbTa after 1000-h anneals at 900 °C in argon and vac-
uum. After annealing in argon, the microstructure consisted of 
a single BCC phase within the center of the sample, while the 
vacuum-annealed sample exhibited two BCC phases located 
mostly at grain boundaries, Fig. 4(a), (b). Presumably the argon 
environment was ‘cleaner, ’ i.e., had a lower partial pressure of O

Precipitate‑strengthened RHEAs
Al‑containing precipitates
To strengthen RHEAs, researchers have investigated the addi-
tion of alloying elements such as Al to induce the precipitation of 
intermetallics [82]. Senkov et al. [83] achieved a superalloy like 
microstructure in AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr with a B2 matrix contain-
ing BCC precipitates, Fig.  5. Because a continuous B2 matrix 
would likely be brittle, researchers subsequently attempted to 
topologically invert this microstructure, that is, make the BCC 
phase the matrix and the precipitates B2. This was achieved in 
a different alloy, Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr, by annealing it first 
at 1200 °C for 24 h, then at 1400 °C for 20 min followed by a 
water quench, and finally annealing at 600 °C for 120 h [84]. 
The inversion was later determined to be driven by elastic strain 
[85]. The initial B2 and BCC had a low lattice misfit of ~ 0.9%. 
Upon aging at 600 °C, the B2 phase becomes enriched in Al and 
Zr, causing an increase in its lattice parameter. Meanwhile, the 
BCC phase is depleted in Al and enriched in Ta, but its lattice 
parameter remains roughly the same. Consequently, the elastic 
misfit energy increases, along with the surface energy, driving 
the B2 phase to pinch itself off and become discontinuous [85]. 
This process is shown in Fig.  6(a)–(f), where the initial solu-
tionized microstructure (a) gives way to a B2 lattice (b), which 
slowly breaks down over the course of annealing into BCC and 
B2 (c–f).
While the above microstructures are reminiscent of 
Ni-based superalloys and represent a promising start, their 
thermal stability needs to be further characterized because 
mechanical properties can deteriorate if the microstructures 
are unstable. Consider, for example, the Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr 
alloy which has a B2 matrix containing BCC precipitates. It 
shows a rapid drop in yield strength above 600 °C, from 1676 
MPa at 600 °C to 45 MPa at 1200 °C [86], orange data points in 
Fig. 7; the latter values are similar to those of the single-phase 
(i.e., precipitate-free) TiZrHfNbTa with the BCC structure at 
such temperatures [44 ], blue data points in Fig.  7. Despite

Room‑temperature mechanical properties 
of RHEAs
The mechanical properties of RHEAs have mostly been meas-
ured at room temperature. On the whole, the reported tensile 
properties are not remarkable when compared to those of much 
cheaper alternatives, such as quenched and partitioned steels 
[94]. They will thus not be viable alternatives for current room-
temperature materials. For high-temperature applications, their 
room-temperature mechanical properties are of engineering 
interest only in so far as they are deemed to be “adequate” which, 
judging by the values of Ni-base superalloys, may be around 
18% tensile ductility in the case of CMSX-4 [ 46] and 100-MPa 
 m0.5 fracture toughness for René N5 [55]. For engine assembly, 
it was estimated in previous work on refractory silicides that 
the room-temperature fracture toughness need only be 20 MPa 
 m0.5 [95]. Alloys belonging to the Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta system 
(including lower-order and off-equiatomic subsets) appear to 
clear the above thresholds (e.g., [54, 96–98]). However, alloys 
belonging to the V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W system appear not to have 
adequate ductility or fracture toughness, at least based on 
information available to date. As for precipitate-strengthened 
Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta alloys, only Al0.5Nb1.25Ta1.25TiZr has been 
tested in tension [99], and it exhibited brittle tensile fracture 
with a fracture toughness of less than 5 MPa m0.5 in the two-
phase condition, even up to 1200 °C, akin to the NbTaMoW 
alloy [53] discussed earlier. This precipitate-induced embrittle-
ment only worsens the standing of Al-containing alloys whose 
solvus temperatures, as discussed before, are too low for high-
temperature applications. Therefore, it is imperative that tensile 
tests be performed on the Ru-containing alloys whose solvus 
temperatures are higher.
RHEAs as an alloy class, follow the typical strength–ductility 
tradeoff [37], with some exceptions noted later. Recent work by 
Kumar et al. [53], previously mentioned in the high-temperature 
section, provides additional information on the room tempera-
ture properties of NbTaMoW . They performed compression, 
tension, and fracture toughness tests on NbTaMoW at room 
temperature and found that, while factors such as grain structure 
and oxides do change the mechanical behavior, the alloy is brit-
tle and functionally useless in any sort of tensile loading. In stark 
contrast, TiZrHfNbTa has decent strains to fracture (10 to 15% 
[30, 100]) and yield strengths ranging from 940 MPa [100] to 
1145 MPa [30]. However, as mentioned earlier, these total duc-
tilities should be approached with caution as little distinction is 
made between uniform and non-uniform deformation and only 
total elongation is usually presented [37]. Furthermore, much of 
the tensile data are presented as true stress–strain curves with-
out showing the Considère criterion [30, 39, 40], so uniform 
strains cannot be estimated. Thus it is unclear from the litera-
ture the degree to which sample geometry influences ductility. 
Many Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta alloys exhibit little engineering work-
hardening [37], often below 100 MPa in an annealed condition, 
which can be a serious limitation in load-bearing applications.

While the initial results of the O- and Al-containing alloys are 
scientifically interesting, there is little to no mechanical proper-

present to evaluate their viability beyond Ni-based alloys. Fur -
ther, as previously mentioned, the addition of O [81] and Al 
[88] to RHEAs results in deleterious microstructures at inter -
mediate temperatures, which will need to be supressed if the 
application requires multiple cycles through these intermediate 
temperatures. Oxygen could also be a double-edged sword for 
low-temperature ductility. In a study on O additions to TiZrNb, 
Wang et al. [105] increased both ductility and strength at room 
temperature with O additions; however, tensile tests at liquid 
N temperatures showed that only the TiZrNb with no O addi -
tions was plastically deformable, implying O additions raised the 
ductile to brittle transition temperature. Furthermore, elevated 
temperature compression tests [105] show that the strengthen-
ing from O diminished with increasing temperature, with prac-
tically no strengthening at 1200 °C, not a particularly promising 
result if the goal is to supersede current superalloys. For both 
the O [102, 103, 105] and Al [104] additions, engineering work-
hardening is not substantially improved—and even hampered—
by alloying. The Al phase decomposition approach [104] results 
in an engineering work-hardening increment of 26 MPa but a 
uniform elongation of 20%. In contrast, O additions result in a 
yield-drop phenomenon [102, 103] and a magnitude of work-
hardening that remains unclear due to a dearth of engineering 
stress–strain data. The few that are available [105] show practi-
cally no work-hardening. In a variable loading situation, such 
alloys will undergo catastrophic plastic deformation at loads 
that barely exceed the yield point due to the small work-hard-
ening increment. If the room-temperature properties are to be 
of practical utility, engineering work-hardening will need to be 
improved, not just the strength and total elongation.
From a scientific standpoint, interest remains in under -
standing why, for example, the V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W RHEAs tend 
to be brittle [42] whereas the Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta alloys tend to 
be ductile [30]. A recent investigation into the intrinsic fac-
tors responsible for their different ductilities concluded that, 
in VNbMoTaW , which only has BCC elements, the dislocation 
core energy is relatively high and the dislocation structure is 
compact, whereas the HCP elements in TiZrHfNbTa cause 
the dislocation cores to spread, making them easier to move 
which in principle imparts ductility [ 106]. Despite such quali -
tative explanations, quantitative models of RHEA ductility are 
currently lacking. This is urgent for V-Nb-Mo-Ta-W alloys that 
have essentially no ductility at room temperature but relatively 
unimportant for Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta alloys that seem to have ade-
quate room-temperature ductility in their current states.
Theoretical treatments of strengthening in RHEAs have 
been successful in predicting general trends, if not absolute 
magnitudes. Contrary to dilute BCC metals, where plasticity 
is controlled by screw dislocations [107], some RHEAs can be 
predominately edge dislocation controlled [108, 109], especially 
alloys with large normalized volume misfits [110 ]. Analytical 
models have been developed for screw [111, 112] and edge dis-
locations [113, 114], both of which predict reasonably well the 
trends observed in experimental yield strength data, including 
recent analysis of high-temperature compression tests in TiZ-
rHfNb [109]. It should be noted that these analytical theories 
require elastic constants, which are rarely reported in RHEAs. 
The few exceptions include Al10Nb20Ta15Ti30V5Zr20 [87], TiZrH-
fNbTa [115], TiZrHfNb [116], and NbTaTiV [117]. Use of rule-
of-mixtures moduli is questionable since group IV elements go 
through a HCP to BCC transformation at high temperature [82], 
thus the room-temperature elastic moduli used in the weighted 
averages are for the HCP rather than the BCC structure.
Concluding remarks and future work
Inlet gas temperatures in jet engines can significantly exceed the 
solvus temperatures and even the melting points of currently 
used Ni-based superalloys because internal cooling channels in 
turbine blades and thermal barrier coatings on their surfaces 
keep the metal temperatures a few hundred degrees lower [2 ]. 
But there is an inefficiency associated with this cooling [118]. 
Hence the need for new materials that can go beyond Ni-based 
superalloys. Much of the current research on RHEAs is driven 
by the quest to find these new materials.
Unfortunately, from the creep data generated so far [56, 
57, 59], it is clear that single-phase RHEAs based on the 
Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta system cannot be realistically expected to 
replace leading nickel-based superalloys such as CMSX-4 at 
their current operating temperatures. They are also unlikely 
replacements for older superalloys that operate at lower tem-
peratures because, below about 800 °C, the Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta 
alloys undergo phase decomposition, associated embrittlement 
[39]. With respect to oxidation, RHEAs without passivating ele-
ments readily oxidize [69, 72, 73], but additions such as Al and 
Cr can passivate RHEAs [61, 69, 71, 74].
Precipitation strengthening has been proposed as a way to 
enhance high-temperature strength and some of the investigated 
alloys even have BCC-B2 microstructures [83, 84] that call to 
mind the FCC-L12 microstructures of Ni-base superalloys. How-
ever, the most extensively investigated of these contain Al-based 
precipitates whose solvus temperatures [ 32] are comparable to

or lower than the operating temperatures of Ni-base superal-

where Ni-base superalloys currently operate, making them unvi-
able. Additionally, both the matrix (BCC) and precipitates (B2) 
have more open crystal structures than the corresponding γ–γ′ 
microstructures, allowing for faster diffusion and creep than in 
currently used alloys.
Recently, certain precipitates have been identified with much 
higher solvus temperatures [90–93]. However, they require the 
addition of expensive precious metals, such as Ru. Consequently, 
little is known about these alloys beyond their microstructures, 
which appear promising [ 92]. Further investigations of their 
properties, especially tensile and creep behavior, are needed 
before viability can be assessed. Additionally, depending on the 
operating conditions (environment, time, temperature) their 
susceptibility to environmental degradation (especially that 
of the BCC matrix) needs to be evaluated along with possible 
approaches (alloying and/or coatings) to improve lifetimes.
RHEAs based on TiZrHfNbTa are malleable at room tem-
perature with relatively high yield strengths of around 1 GPa 
[37]. That said, for room-temperature use, they are not prac -
tical. For the sake of comparison, both β-Ti alloys [119] and 
quenched and partitioned steels [94] can achieve similar tensile 
properties. For potential high-temperature applications, further 
efforts to improve room-temperature mechanical properties of 
single-phase Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta alloys are of marginal utility 
since they already have enough ductility and fracture tough -
ness. In contrast, the tensile properties and fracture toughness 
of precipitation-hardened RHEAs, especially those containing 
Ru-rich precipitates are a different matter. They need to be first 
measured and then possibly improved in case they are found to 
be inadequate. The same holds for the V–Nb–Ta–Mo–W system 
for which there is a complete lack of tensile data. Both these 
latter systems have the potential for high creep strength at tem-
peratures above the solvus temperatures of Ni-base superalloys 
and are therefore worth investigating.
Fracture toughness was mentioned only in passing in this 
review due to the paucity of reported data, which in turn is due 
to the difficulty of making large enough specimens for valid frac-
ture toughness measurements. There is a similar lack of informa-
tion about the fatigue behavior of RHEAs. Both these properties 
present opportunities for future work but the focus, in our view, 
should be on high-temperature properties, especially of those 
RHEAs that have good high-temperature strengths, in order 
to test their viability as the next-generation alloys that can go 
beyond Ni-based superalloys.
Finally, it is worth investigating how RHEAs perform in 
creep relative to conventional (commercially available) refrac-
tory alloys. In previous systematic studies of solid solution FCC 
HEAs, it has been shown that simply increasing the chemical 
complexity (configurational entropy) does not increase the yield 
strength [120]. That is, the type and nature of the alloying ele-
ments (size and modulus misfit) rather than how many there 
are in a given alloy determines their contribution to strength. It 
may turn out that simpler refractory metals have creep proper-
ties that are comparable to RHEAs. At the very least, this is a 
hypothesis worth testing. In the same vein, it is worth investigat-
ing how RHEAs stack up against conventional refractory metals 
for short-life applications [52] where creep and environmental 
resistance are less of a concern.
