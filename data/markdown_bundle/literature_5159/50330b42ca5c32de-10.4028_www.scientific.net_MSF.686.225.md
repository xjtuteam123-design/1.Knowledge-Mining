Temperatures and Strain Rates

Keywords: Magnesium alloy AZ61, temperature, strain rate, strength, plasticity. 
Abstract. A series of tensile tests of AZ61 magnesium alloy were conducted using Gleeble-1500 
thermal-mechanical material testing system to learn the effect of the test temperatures and strain rates 
on the mechanical properties of the alloy. It is indicated that the higher the temperature, the lower the 
ultimate strength and fracture stress, and the larger the plasticity. It is also revealed that the larger the 
strain rate is, the higher the ultimate strength of the specimens will be, and the larger the plasticity of 
the specimens will be. The failure mechanism of the material under high temperature was also 
analyzed based on the fracture observation. It shows that the high temperatures will induce 
microvoids or microflaws in the material. 
Introduction 
It is known that the high temperature will bring a remarkable change of the microstructures of alloy 
materials. It is also revealed that the high heating or strain rates may also result in a considerable 
change of the microstructures. It has also been testified that the microstructure change may markedly 
change the mechanical properties of alloy [1-3]. The investigation on the relationship between the 
microstructures and the mechanical properties of alloy materials at high temperature as well high 
heating and strain rates may help to avoid alloy material failure at these particular environments or 
conditions. The investigation can also provide the available information for making use of 
high-temperature as well as high heating or strain rate technologies [4-6]. 
The failure of alloy materials caused by high temperature as well as high heating or strain rates is 
receiving increasing attention [7-10]. Liu et al [7] investigated the nonlinear softening of aluminum 
alloy subjected to high tempreture and fast heating. It was found that the differences in heating rate 
history may cause differences in both the grain size and the macroscopic mechanical properties of the 
material, which was accounted for the dynamics of recrystallization. Wang et al[8] conducted a set of 
tensile test of low-alloy steel 30CrMnSi subjected to high tempreture and fast heating histories with 
different heating rates. The test results showed distinct differences in both the rupture strength and the 
metallograph of the material. Yang et al [9] and Peng et al [10,11] investigated the effects of the high 
tempreature and the heating rate on the mechanical properties of aluminum alloy LY12 and indicated 
that the material experiencing higher heating rate histories possesses lower rupture strength and that 
the pre-stressed material fails at a lower tested temperature, which was attributed to the increase of 
microdefects due to the strong local thermal inconsistency at high heating rate [11]. In this paper, a 
systematic experiment was conducted with a Gleeble-1500 thermal-mechanical material testing 
system to investigate the effect of the temperature and strain rates on the ultimate strength and 
plasticity of magnesium alloy AZ61 specimens. The metallographs of the tested specimens were also 
observed and the failed mechanisms of the specimens were analyzed. This work is significant for 
understanding the mechanical behaviors and failure mechanisms of magnesium alloy materials under 
high temperature and high strain rate.

Experimental 
Firstly, magnesium alloy AZ61 was machined to cylindrical tensile specimens. The geometric 
dimensions of the specimens are showed in Fig. 1. Then the tensile tests of the specimens were 
performed under different temperatures and strain rates with Gleeble-1500 thermal-mechanical 
material testing system [8-11]. In the experiment, the specimens were heated by applying a direct 
electrical voltage drop to the two ends of these specimens [9-11]. The temperature within the testing 
section of the specimens was measured with a chromel-alumel thermocouple welded directly on the 
surface of the testing section and controlled with a computer, and all of the data in the experiment 
were recorded synchronously with the data acquisition element of the testing system. The procedure 
of the experiment is: firstly, the specimens are heated with heating rate 10 ℃/s to prescribed testing 
temperatures 100 ℃ and 200 ℃. Then keeping the testing temperatures 1s for ensuring the temperature 
to be equal in all parts of the specimens. At last, the specimens were stretched with three defferent 
strain rates 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0 s 
-1 until they fracture.

Results and discussions 
Fig. 2(a)-(c) show the obtained stress-strain relationship of AZ61 magnesium alloy specimens which 
are first heated to two prescribed testing temperatures 100 and 200℃ with heating-rate 10 ℃/s, and 
then stretched respectively with three different strain rates SR=0.01/s, 0.1/s and 1/s to fracture. It can 
be seen from Fig. 2(a)-(c) that all of the specimens pass three different regions during their tensile 
processes, namely, elastic, strain hardening and necking regions. In the elastic region, the stress is 
proportional to the strain and the curve of the relationship between the stress and strain is actually a 
straight line. The slope of the straight line represents the modulus of the elasticity of the specimens. It 
is found from Fig. (2) that there is very small effect of the temperature on the elastic modulus of the 
material. Further, in the strain hardening region, when the further load is applied to the specimens, the 
curve will rise continuously but become flatter until it reaches a maximum stress referred to the 
ultimate stress

the temperature markedly affect the ultimate strength of the specimens. It can also be observed from 
Fig. (2) that the higher the temperature is, the lower the ultimate strength of the specimens will be. 
Furthermore, it is also observed that while the specimen is elongating, its cross-sectional area will 
decrease. This decrease in the area is fairly uniform over the specimen’s entire gauge length, even up 
to the strain corresponding to the ultimate stress. At last, in the necking region, it can be seen that at 
the ultimate stress, the cross-sectional area begins to decrease in a localized region of the specimens, 
instead of over their entire length. As a result, a constriction or “neck” gradually tends to form in this 
region as the specimens elongate further. Since the cross-sectional area within the region is 
continuously decreasing, the smaller area can only carry an ever decreasing load. Hence the 
stress-strain diagram tends to curve downward until the specimen break at the fracture stress fσ . It 
can be found from Fig. (2) that the fracture stress increases with the increase of the temperature. 
Further, it is also observed that there a large deformation before the specimen ruptures and that the 
most of the deformation is reserved. The reserved deformation is the plastic deformation of the 
material. It can be seen from Fig. (2) that the plastic deformation increases with the increase of the 
temperature. Fig. 3(a) shows the measured stress-strain relationship of the specimens which are 
subjected a relative lower temperature 100 ℃, and then stretched with the three different strain rates, 
0.01, 0.1 and 1.0s -1. It can be seen from Fig. 3(a) that there is a very smaller effect of the strain rate on 
the ultimate strength, fracture stress and the plastic deformation of the specimens. However, even 
though the effect is very smaller, it can be seen that the larger the strain rate is, the higher the ultimate 
strength and the fracture stress will be, and the larger the plastic deformation of the specimens will be. 
Fig. 3(b) shows the measured strain-stress relationship of the specimens which are subjected a relative 
high temperature 200 ℃, and then stretched with the three different strain rates, 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0s -1. It 
can be seen from Fig. 3(b) that there is a larger effect of the strain rate on the ultimate strength, 
fracture stress as well as the plasticity of the specimens. It can be observed that the larger the strain 
rate is, the higher the ultimate strength will be. 
Fig. 4 shows the metallography near the fracture sections of the specimens subjected a different 
temperatures and strain rates. It can be seen from Fig. 4 that there are many microvoids or microflaws 
in the fractured specimens. Figs. 4 (a) and (b) show the metallography of the specimens subjected a 
identical strain rate 0.01s-1 and different temperatures 100 and 200 ℃. It can be seen from Figs. 4 (a) 
and (b) that there are relative larger microvoids or microflaws in the specimens subjected to relative 
high temperature, which makes the specimens possess relative lower ultimate strength. Figs. 4 (c) and 
(d) show the metallography of the specimens suffered a identical strain rate 1.0s-1 and different 
temperatures 100 and 200 ℃. It can also be observed from Fig. 4 (c) and (d) that there are relative 
larger microvoids or microflaws in the specimens subjected to relative high temperature, which also 
makes the specimens possess relative lower ultimate strength.

Conclusions 
The tensile tests of AZ61 magnesium alloy subjected different temperatures and strain rates were 
conducted for investigating the effect of the temperatures and strain rates on the ultimate strength and 
plasticity of the alloy material. It is revealed that the higher the temperature is, the lower the ultimate 
strength and fracture stress of the specimens will be, and the larger the plasticity of the specimens will 
be. It is also indicated that the larger the strain rate is, the higher the ultimate strength and the fracture 
stress of the specimens will be, and the larger the plasticity of the specimens will be. The 
microstructures of the fracture sections of the specimens were also experimentally observed. It is 
revealed that the high temperature will induce microvoids or microflaws in the specimens, which will 
reduce the strength of the specimens.
