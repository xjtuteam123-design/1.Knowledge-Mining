ABSTRACT 
Refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA) VNbMoTaW exhibits remarkably high strength in a much wide temper -
ature range, whereas the brittle fracture behavior limits the applications. Here we show a significant size effect 
on the strength, plasticity and fracture behaviour of the VNbMoTaW RHEA. As sample size goes from millimeter 
to sub-micrometer scale, the brittle cracking behavior of the RHEA is greatly suppressed, along with a simul -
taneous enhancement of strength and plasticity. The improved mechanical properties can be attributed to the 
disappearance of crack initiation sites, the size-dependent dislocation plasticity at small length scale and the size- 
dependent competition in thermodynamics driving-force for cleavage cracking and plastic yielding. The results 
imply a promising potential of the RHEA for making microscale devices with high temperature environment. 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a class of new materials with equal or 
near equal atomic molar fraction compositions, which exhibit high 
configurational entropy and favour to form simple phase structure 
[ 1 – 3 ]. HEAs formed with elements of high melting points are usually 
termed as refractory HEAs (RHEAs) [ 4 ]. Originated from the strong 
solution strengthening and severe lattice distortion, RHEAs usually 
display superior strengths, especially very high resistance to deforma -
tion at elevated temperatures [ 5 – 7 ]. For instance, VNbMoTaW RHEA 
shows strength higher than 400 MPa at extremely high temperatures 
~1600 
◦
C, much stronger than the conventional superalloys like Inconel 
[ 8 ], and resembling to ceramics. This alloy also has plastic deformation 
ability under compression [ 9 ], showing higher damage tolerance than 
engineering ceramics. Besides the high strength at various temperatures, 
some RHEAs also exhibit high resistances for radiation, friction and 
corrosion, etc. [ 6 ] These advantages endow RHEAs great potential for 
applications as structural materials serving under extreme conditions. 
Most of RHEAs suffer from brittle fracture, however, especially under 
opening stress states such as uniaxial tension, except for some ductile 
RHEAs (e.g., see Refs. [ 10 – 18 ]). A recent review by Senkov et al. [ 6 ] 
suggested that among ~200 compositions of RHEAs only tens of alloys 
show tensile ductility. The brittle fracture behaviour can also be seen 
during fracture testing. For instance, Zou et al. [ 19 ] performed fracture 
tests using microcantilever specimens and found a very low value of 
fracture toughness, namely 2 – 5 MPa m
1/2 
for NaMoTaW RHEA. 
Although there may be specimen size effect in this fracture experiment 
[ 20 ], the results of the low toughness values that are close to the typical 
values for ceramics [ 21 ], suggesting a brittle fracture behaviour for this 
RHEA. Also, obvious splitting cracking, which is usually found in brittle 
materials under compression, has also been observed in VNbMoTaW and 
NbMoTaW alloys [ 8 ]. These facts naturally raise some fundamental 
questions: what causes the brittle fracture behaviour in such RHEAs? 
On the other hand, it has been frequently reported that sample size 
affects the deformation behaviour of materials [ 22 – 26 ]. The phenomena 
that materials in smaller size could be stronger and more plastically 
deformable has been observed in various materials including metals and 
alloys, ceramics, and metallic glasses etc. [ 22 , 27 – 29 ], except for some 
metal/ceramic composites [ 26 ]. Size effect on mechanical properties has 
been also studied in various HEAs with different structures such as the 
single-phase face-centered cubic (FCC) or body-centered cubic (BCC) 
alloys, and the alloys with complex phases [ 21 , 30 – 40 ]. In these studies,

plasticity. 
2. Experimental 
The RHEA material with the composition of equiatomic VNbMoTaW 
was prepared by arc-melting in vacuum condition. To achieve a homo -
geneous distribution of elements in the alloy ingot, the alloy was re- 
melted four times with a total time of > 1 h in the liquid state. The 
prepared alloy is in the button form with a thickness of 6 mm and 
diameter of 30 mm. The microstructure of the alloy was examined by a 
standard x-ray diffractometer and a Leo Supra 35 scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) equipped with detectors for back-scattered electron 
imaging, electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD) and energy- 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The results of microstructure character -
ization suggest that the prepared VNbMoTaW RHEA has a single BCC 
phase, and a chemically homogeneous microstructure. The grains are 
equiaxed and coarse, with the average grain size of ~168 μ m. More 
details about the microstructure can be found in Ref. [ 9 ].

3. Results 
Fig. 2 shows the typical compressive stress-strain curve of the bulk 
samples with width of ~2 mm. The alloy exhibits a yield strength of 1.54 
± 0.15 GPa and an ultimate compressive strength of 1.66 ± 0.04 GPa, 
along with a nominal compressive plasticity (i.e., the plastic strain 
corresponding to the 5% stress-drop from the peak stress) of 6.34% ±
0.54%. These properties are much higher than the previously reported 
values for the same composition of RHEA by Senkov et al. [ 8 ] (1270 MPa 
and 0.5% for strength and plasticity, respectively), demonstrating that 
the casting of the studied alloy should be satisfied. The typical true 
stress-true strain curve was presented in the inset of Fig. 2 , which shows 
a little work-hardening behaviour after yielding.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Size effect on strength of the RHEA 
Fig. 7 displays the size effect on strength of the VNbTaMoW RHEA. 
Apparently, the strength increases significantly as the sample size goes 
into the sub-micrometer scale. A power function relation between 
strength and sample size can be obviously found, which is consistent 
with the previous studies in FCC or BCC single crystals [ 22 , 24 ]. The 
relation can be expressed as: 
σ ∝ d
 n
(1) 
where σ is the strength, d the sample size and n the exponent. For the 
strengths of the VNbTaMoW RHEA characterized either by σ
0.5 
or by σ
5
, 
the values of exponent n are close, i.e., n = 0.34 for σ
0.5 
and n = 0.33 for 
σ

. The value of n is similar to other pure BCC metals (e.g., 0.21, 0.38, 
0.41 and 0.48 for W, Mo, Ta and Nb, respectively [ 46 ]) or BCC HEA 
(0.33 for NbMoTaW [ 47 ]), but well lower than those for FCC metals (e. 
g., 0.61 for Au [ 25 ]), suggesting a relatively weaker size dependency. 
The power function relation suggests that the size effect on strength 
should be dominated by the dislocation mechanism. Volkert and Lil -
leodden [ 25 ] proposed that dislocations could easily leave from sample 
surface and thus dislocation density is lower in smaller pillar. Close to 
the consideration of Hall-Petch model, the lower dislocation density in 
smaller pillar could lead to lower internal stresses, and thus a higher 
applied stress is required for activating dislocation source for the plastic 
deformation [ 25 ]. Quantitatively, a truncated single-arm dislocation 
source model was also proposed by Parthasarathy et al. [ 48 ], which 
considers the statistical variation in the source length of dislocations 
imposed by the finite dimension of sample. This model has been applied 
to explain the size-dependent strength of micropillars in various metals 
and alloys [ 48 , 49 ], and also in HEAs [ 33 , 47 ]. Comparing the strength 
with the bulk sample, as shown in Fig. 7 , it is found that as increasing the 
pillar diameter, the strength decreases steeply in the range with size less 
than 2 μ m, while it becomes independent on sample size in larger 
samples. This implies that the power function relation may be valid only 
in a range of sample size, where the size-dependent dislocation source 
model is rational. It should be noted that the critical size of 2 μ m is only 
obtained here for the present alloy based on the data in this work, while 
for various other materials (or different crystallographic orientation) 
different critical size may be observed. 
4.2. Crack initiation mechanism and size effect on fracture 
The present experimental observations of the VNbTaMoW RHEA 
bulk samples reveal many cracks on the sample surfaces during 
compression. Based on the observations, the crack initiation mecha -
nisms of the RHEA under compression can be summarized into two types 
(as illustrated in Fig. 8 ): 
1) Crack initiation at triple junction of GBs. At a triple junction, plastic 
deformation across the GB is not continuous, thus slip steps and high 
tensile stress concentration appear, which leads to the crack initia -
tion. There are two cases for this kind of crack initiation. Near the 
triple junction, there are three neighbouring grains with different 
orientations. Let us denote the grain that is easiest to slip as G1 and 
the grain which is hardest to slip as G2. Firstly, when G1 and G2 are 
arranged horizontally, as sketched in Fig. 8 (d), the crack initiation 
happens because the more shrinkage of G1 than G2 in the 
compressive loading direction. Secondly, when G1 takes the position

2) Crack initiation around casting defects. In the as-cast alloys, casting 
defects like inclusions and pores are unavoidable. Solid mechanics 
proves that there is tensile stress concentration at the north pole and 
south pole of a pore in a plate when it is under compressive loading 
[ 43 ] (see Fig. 8 (g)). Similar tensile stress concentration could happen 
in all the casting defects, although the magnitude of the stress de -
pends on the type and shape of the defects. The tensile stress con -
centration drives the crack initiation in brittle materials, as can be 
seen in ceramics [ 43 ] and brittle metallic glass [ 29 ]. In brittle RHEA 
after compressive deformation, we also see the crack initiation from 
the casting defects ( Fig. 3 (b)), suggesting the similar mechanism akin 
to other brittle materials. The maximum tensile stress usually has a 
direction in the horizontal direction, which is vertical to the 
compressive loading direction; hence the initiated crack tends to 
grow along the vertical plane, consistent with the splitting fracture 
observed in experiments. 
After crack initiation, it will grow or propagate along the path that 
requires the minimum input energy (driving force) [ 50 ]. According to 
the present experimental results, there are two paths for crack propa -
gation: GB and cleavage planes . These two crack paths lead to GB 
cracking and cleavage cracking, respectively, as observed in the 
macroscopic compressive testing ( Fig. 3 ). The results suggest that in this 
RHEA, the GB may be not strong enough, which is consistent with the 
previous study [ 19 ] on the fracture of a bicrystal RHEA. Zou et al. [ 19 ] 
performed microcantilever fracture tests using single crystal and 
bicrystal samples cutting from the same RHEA film, and found that the 
fracture toughness values in the bicrystal samples are much lower than 
that of the single crystals, suggesting that less energy is required for 
crack propagating along GB. Further investigation by 3-dimensional 
atom probe (3DAP) pointed out the weakness of GB can be attributed 
to the segregation of covalent bonded elements along the GB [ 19 ]. 
Although GB cracking is one of main failure mechanisms, trans -
granular fracture of cleavage cracking also occurs, as shown in Fig. 3 . In 
some cases, crack deflection can be observed, as illustrated in Fig. 8 (c). 
The crack firstly grows along the GB and then deflects and propagates in 
the direction nearly parallel to the loading direction, leading to splitting 
cracks dominated by cleavage cracking. Crack deflection can be 
frequently observed in brittle materials like ceramics under compression 
[ 43 ]. The deflection is caused by the tensile stress concentration at the 
tip of the inclined GB crack, as sketched in Fig. 8 (f), where the maximum 
stress increases as increasing the size of the GB crack, determined by 
continuum mechanics. When the GB crack grows to a critical size which 
makes the local tensile stress field reaches the intensity enough to 
activate the cleavage cracking in the crystal interior, crack deflection 
happens. The competition of crack growth path depends on not only the 
intrinsic resistance to GB cracking and cleavage cracking, but also the 
external driving force, which is affected by crystal orientation, GB di -
rection and applied stress, as well as other external factors such as 
testing temperature and strain rate. More details concerning these 
mechanisms should be carefully studied in the future work. 
Based on the above analysis, it can be rational to expect that if 
removing the casting defects and the GB triple junctions, crack initiation 
may not happen. This is well consistent with the present results of 
micropillar compression. In all the micropillars, there is no grain 
boundary because they are single crystals with the same crystallographic 
orientation, thus no triple junctions to initiate GB cracks. Furthermore, 
the casting defects like pores and inclusions can be avoided because of 
the very small volume of samples, thus cleavage cracking in all the 
micropillar samples is impeded because of no tensile stress.

5. Conclusions 
In this work, the deformation and fracture behaviors of the as-cast 
VNbTaMoW RHEA were investigated using samples with sizes varying 
in a wide range of length scale. Significant size effect was observed for 
strength, plasticity and failure behavior. When the compressive sample 
has a millimeter length scale of dimension, the RHEA fails with a high 
strength of ~1.7 GPa, but a low compressive plasticity of ~6%, 
accompanying with severe cracking and damages. However, as sample 
size decreasing to the micrometer scale, both the strength and 
compressive plasticity improved significantly. For instance, the smallest 
micropillar (~160 nm in diameter) exhibits a strength ~3.7 GPa and a 
plasticity > 25%. Besides, the cleavage cracking and the GB cracking 
behavior occurred in bulk sample vanish in micropillars. Mechanisms 
for crack initiation and propagation in RHEA under compression are 
discussed, which well explain the size effect on failure behavior. The 
present findings reveal the sample size effect on the strength and failure 
in RHEA, especially the mechanisms for crack initiation under 
compression, which would be helpful for manipulating length scale and 
microstructure to design high strength RHEA with high resistance for 
cracking.
