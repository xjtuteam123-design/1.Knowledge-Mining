Oxidation Behavior of Mo Containing Alloys 
E. Dokumacı a, B. Önay b 
Department of Metallurgical and Materials Engineering, 
Faculty of Engineering, Dokuz Eylul University, Buca, Izmir 35160, Turkey 
aesra.dokumaci@deu.edu.tr, bbulent.onay@deu.edu.tr 
Keywords: Oxidation, Ni alloys, Molybdenum, Arc-melting, SEM. 
Abstract. In this study, high temperature (T>500 oC) oxidation behavior of two commercial Ni-base 
alloys and two experimental alloys, all containing more than 10% Molybdenum, is investigated. 
Experimental alloys were prepared from high purity materials using an arc-melting furnace under a 
protective environment. During tests, samples were exposed to the stagnant air environment of a 
high temperature furnace for predetermined times. Extent of oxidation was determined from sample 
mass change measurements as well as morphological and chemical analyses of the oxidation 
products. For analyses, a scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with backscattered electron 
(BE) and energy dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) detectors was used. Crystalline phases formed in 
the product scales over the alloy samples were identified by an x-ray diffractometer (XRD). 
Preliminary results indicate that although Mo in the alloy prevents the development of a protective 
oxide scale at the alloy surface, presence of alloying elements such as Cr or Al can decrease this 
negative effect of Mo on oxide scale formation. 
Introduction 
Refractory metal-base alloys with high melting points and relatively low densities are desired as 
structural materials for use at temperatures above the current operating temperatures of the Ni-base 
superalloys [1-3]. However, one major drawback for the use of refractory alloys, such as those 
based on Mo, is their poor oxidation resistance in oxygen-containing environments, like air, even at 
temperatures as low as 600 oC [4]. High temperature oxidation of these materials results in the 
formation of gaseous oxide products and oxide volatilization prevents the formation of protective 
scales that would otherwise prevent further material loss [5,6]. Thus, methods of improving 
oxidation resistance of such alloys are urgently needed and this objective can be achieved by the 
development of improved coatings or new oxidation-resistant refractory alloys [7,8]. 
The authors have been involved in a government-funded project on the development of oxidation 
resistant refractory metal alloys. Besides the production and characterization of experimental alloys 
rich in Mo, oxidation behavior of two commercial Ni base alloys; C-276 and C-22 were studied. 
These Ni alloys are not considered as high temperature materials but they are chosen for the study 
because they contain more than 10% Mo (by weight) and different amounts of Cr in their chemical 
compositions. Thus, it was expected that oxidation behavior of these commercial alloys might yield 
useful information about the effects of Mo and other alloying elements on the oxidation behavior of 
alloys that are much richer in Molybdenum. 
In this paper, primarily, the results of the high temperature oxidation experiments conducted, in air, 
on the two commercial Ni alloys are discussed. Also, preliminary results of the oxidation studies 
conducted on the experimental Mo-base alloys are presented.

For the discontinuous oxidation tests conducted on these alloys, 2 mm thick, disc-shaped samples 
were cut from milled alloy products. Before testing, two large surfaces of the samples were lightly 
ground using 800 grit SiC paper, cleaned ultrasonically in acetone, dried and weighed using a 
balance with 0.1 mg sensitivity. Each alloy sample was then hung inside a ceramic crucible to be 
able to collect any corrosion product spalled off their surfaces during testing. Crucibles were placed 
inside a furnace for oxidation under the ambient air environment. 
Two types of tests conducted on these Ni alloys. In the first group of tests, a single sample of each 
alloy were heated to 500 oC and kept at this temperature for 30 minutes. Then, power to the furnace 
was turned off and samples were cooled to room temperature in the furnace. After cooling, samples 
were removed from their crucibles and weighed. Corrosion products found in the crucibles were 
collected and also weighed. Sample surfaces and corrosion products were examined by SEM and 
EDS. Later, the same samples were put back into the furnace, in their separate crucibles, for another 
30 minutes of oxidation, this time at 700 oC. This type of testing and analysis procedure was 
repeated, for the same samples, at temperatures 800 oC, 900 oC and 1150 oC. 
In the second group of tests, C-276 and C-22 alloys were oxidized at 800 and 1150 oC, in 7 
consecutive cycles, for a total of 42 hours. For each test, new alloy samples were used. Each test 
cycle was started by heating the samples to the test temperature, keeping them at temperature for 6 
hours before cooling them to room temperature inside the furnace. At the end of 42 hours, 
corrosion products found in the crucibles were collected and weighed. Sample surfaces and 
corrosion products were examined by SEM/ EDS and XRD. 
Experimental alloys. Two, Mo-rich experimental alloys were produced by melting slugs or pieces 
of the constituent elements with 99.95 % or better purity. Pure metal samples were cleaned with 
acetone and weighed before charging into the furnace. Melting was done inside the water-cooled 
copper moulds of an arc melting furnace using a non-consumable tungsten electrode under an argon 
atmosphere. The button-shaped ingots produced were re-melted several times to enhance the 
compositional homogeneity of the final product. 
For oxidation tests, alloy samples were put, side by side, inside a ceramic boat and the boat was 
placed in the hot zone of a muffle furnace preheated to 500 oC. Samples were exposed to 
temperatures between 500 and 800 oC with increments of 100 oC. At each temperature, samples were 
kept in the furnace for 30 minutes. After exposure, samples were removed from the furnace and 
cooled for weighing. Later, the temperature of the furnace was increased and the samples were 
returned back to the furnace for oxidation at the next higher temperature. 
Because of either the small size or the brittle nature of the alloy buttons, samples used in the 
oxidation tests had irregular shapes. Thus, the evaluation of their oxidation resistances are done

Results and Discussion 
Ni alloys. The first group of tests conducted on the C-22 and C-276 alloys showed that weight 
changes after oxidation in air at temperatures less than 1150 oC were not significant. At 1150 oC, 
however, weight changes and scale spallation from the alloy substrates started to become 
noticeable. For example, after 6 hours of oxidation at 1150 oC, C-276 and C-22 samples had 0.0003 
and 0.0008 g/cm 2 of weight gains, respectively. The amount of scale spallation observed from the 
alloy samples were 0.0047 g for the C-276 sample and 0.0004 g for the C-22 sample. It was noted 
that the C-276 alloy which has a larger Mo content and a lower Cr content had more scale 
spallation. 
As seen in the SEM photographs of Fig. 1, scale spallation from the C-276 alloy sample was 
relatively more extensive. Also, the backscattered electron (BE) image of the scale spallation area 
suggested that spallation took place at locations where the C-276 alloy substrate was enriched with 
heavy elements (bright regions in Fig. 1b). EDS analyses conducted in the region marked ‘B” in 
Fig. 1b, showed that the bright regions are rich in Mo and Ni with small amounts of Cr and W 
whereas the darker region marked “A” contained, primarily, chromium and oxygen.

In Fig. 2(a) SEM picture of the deposits observed at the inner walls of the ceramic crucible 
containing the C-276 sample during the 42 hour-long tests was shown. The bright appearance of the 
deposits in the BEI mode, indicated the presence of heavy elements. EDS analyses of the deposits 
showed that they contained mostly Mo with some W and oxygen. However, similar deposits were 
not found inside the crucible containing the C-22 alloy sample. 
The only way for the deposits to form at the crucible surfaces was by the transport of Mo and W 
from the sample during the test. Since the melting points of the oxides of Mo and W are lower than 
those of the pure elements, it is reasonable to hypothesize that Mo and W in the C-276 alloy were 
first oxidized and the oxide vapors evaporated and condensed at the crucible walls surrounding the 
sample. The higher Mo content and the lower Cr content of the C-276 alloy could be the reason for 
the formation and transfer of refractory metal oxides from this alloy. The higher Cr content of the 
C-22 alloy must have enabled the formation of a Cr-rich scale which prevented the formation of the 
refractory metal oxides. The higher weight gain observed for the C-276 sample after the long-term 
test at 1150 oC, was an indication that the scale formed on this alloy was less protective. These 
explanations are supported also by the XRD data obtained from the sample surfaces after the test at 
1150 oC which showed the presence of NiCr 2O4 and Cr 2O3 phases over the C-22 sample surface. As 
for the C-276 sample, NiCr 2O4 and NiMoO 4 spinel phases, NiO and Cr 2O3 were detected. 
The comparatively higher amount of scale spallation observed for the Mo-rich C-276 alloy in this 
work can be explained also by the same hypothesis. Evaporation of the volatile oxides of Mo in the 
alloy must have enhanced the spallation of the corrosion products over the sample surface.

Another evidence for the observed negative effects of Mo on the oxidation of Ni alloys came from 
the investigation of the as-received alloys’ microstructures. It was found that both C-276 and C-22 
alloys contained some precipitates (intermetallic phases) that were rich in Mo and Ni. Precipitates 
were larger in size in the case of the C–276 alloy but were much smaller and more uniformly 
distributed in the case of the C-22 alloy. In Fig. 3a, one of such precipitates in the C-276 
is shown. To observe the effect of such precipitates on alloy oxidation, the C-276 alloy sample in 
Fig. 3a was exposed to air at 900 oC for an hour. Upon oxidation, the precipitate did not change its 
shape (Fig. 3b) but preferentially oxidized to form an oxide scale that was thicker than the scale 
formed over the surrounding areas. EDS analyses showed that the scale over the precipitate had Ni, 
Mo and oxygen whereas the scale over the surrounding areas was rich in Cr. Based on the above 
hypothesis, it is reasonable to think that growth and vaporization of this Mo-rich oxide layer over 
the precipitates destroyed the mechanical integrity of the more protective Cr and Ni-containing 
scale formed around or above the precipitates. 
Experimental Alloys. The alloys discussed here were produced with the purpose of observing the 
effect of alloying elements on weight changes and nature of corrosion products formed during 
oxidation of Mo-base experimental alloys. The first alloying element added to pure Mo was 
Niobium (Nb). This element was chosen because its melting point is close to that of Mo and that the 
binary alloy system is isomorphous. Also, the lower density of Nb enables the production of lighter 
Mo-rich materials [10-12]. The other alloying element investigated was Aluminum. This element is 
used as an alloying element in high temperature oxidation resistant materials since, under the proper 
conditions, Al promotes the formation of an oxidation resistant, protective oxide scale over the alloy 
surfaces. 
The 53.5%Mo and 46.5% Nb (by weight) binary alloy produced, initially, was considered to be 
ductile since it was possible to saw the cast ingot without fracturing. However, the Al-containing 
second experimental alloy (54Mo-46Nb-6Al) was brittle. Thus, it was difficult to machine properly 
shaped test samples from this alloy ingot. 
Samples of these experimental alloys were oxidized in air as explained in the Experimental Section. 
Together with the alloy samples, a pure Mo sample was also exposed to the same environmental 
conditions. Until the test at 700 oC, the two alloy samples and the Mo sample did not have 
significant weight changes. However at 700 oC, significant weight loss was measured for the pure

binary alloy, the scale formed over this sample was more uniform. The scale, however, contained 
cracks. 
The low temperature tests conducted on the experimental alloys showed that addition of Nb and Al 
to Mo were able to modify the oxide scale morphology of the pure Mo sample and prevent the 
formation of colored deposits which formed during its oxidation. However, the alloys had weight 
changes that were considered to reach unacceptable values at higher temperatures. For this reason, 
unlike the case for the Ni-base commercial alloys, oxidation tests at temperatures higher than 800 oC 
were not conducted on the experimental alloys.

Conclusions 
Major findings of this work can be summarized as follows: 
1. Weight changes measured for the commercial Ni-base alloys; C-276 and C-22 after their 
oxidation, in air, were not significant at temperatures less than 1000 oC. Corrosion product scales 
formed over both alloys, at those temperatures, were adherent. 
2. At 1150 oC, adverse effects of Mo in the C-276 alloy were observed in the form of severe scale 
spallation and removal of Mo by volatilization. It is concluded that the lower Cr and the higher Mo 
content of this alloy prevented the development of a protective scale over the alloy. 
3. Preferential oxidation of the Mo-rich precipitates present in the Ni alloys accelerated scale 
spallation, especially, from the C-276 alloy which had coarse precipitates and a higher Mo content. 
The C-22 alloy was less affected by the presence of Mo in the alloy because this alloy had a 
relatively higher Cr content and a more uniformly distributed precipitates. 
4. The low temperature oxidation tests conducted on the two Mo-rich binary and the ternary alloys 
produced by arc-melting showed that Nb and Al additions modify the morphology of the oxide 
scale of the pure Mo and prevent the formation of colored deposits which form during oxidation. 
However, scales formed over these alloys were voluminous and had poor mechanical properties.
