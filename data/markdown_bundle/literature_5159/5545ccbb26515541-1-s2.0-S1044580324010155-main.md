1. Introduction
Aluminum alloys have gained extensive usage as lightweight struc -

to their exceptional properties, including high specific strength, corro -

modern industries has resulted in a growing demand for aluminum al -

Conventional manufacturing processes for aluminum alloy compo -
nents typically involve casting followed by machining. However, this 
approach suffers from long production cycles and low yield. To address 
these challenges, a promising manufacturing method called wire-arc 
directed energy deposition (WA-DED) has emerged. WA-DED utilizes 
an arc as a heat source to melt and deposit filler wire layer by layer, 
following a predefined path. This technology offers remarkable advan -
tages, including high deposition efficiency and material utilization rate, 
making it particularly suitable for the cost-effective and rapid produc -
tion of large-scale metal components. In recent years, researchers have 
dedicated their efforts to exploring the microstructure and properties of 
heat-resistant aluminum alloys, specifically Al
–

high-temperature strength remains relatively low and fails to meet the 
requirements of certain components. To address this limitation, a novel 
Al-Cu-based high strength heat-resistant aluminum alloy wire, named 
205C, was developed in this study specifically for the WA-DED process. 
A single-pass multilayer thin-walled component was fabricated using the 
cold metal transfer (CMT) process. The microstructure and high- 
temperature mechanical properties were systematically investigated, 
presenting a fresh perspective for WA-DED heat-resistant aluminum 
alloys with enhanced high-temperature properties.
2. Experimental materials and procedures
2.1. Wire processing
To produce the 205C parent alloy, the induction furnace was utilized 
for smelting the aluminum alloy, followed by transferring the molten 
alloy into a holding furnace at a temperature of 740 
◦
C. Argon gas 
blowing was employed for refining purposes, and the alloy underwent a 
standing treatment for 30 min. Subsequently, ingots with a diameter of 
110 mm were obtained using a continuous casting method. The obtained 
ingots were then subjected to the hot extrusion method, resulting in the 
production of rods with a diameter of φ 9.5 mm. These rods underwent a 
meticulous process involving rolling, drawing, ultrasonic cleaning, and 
chemical passivation. Ultimately, φ 1.2 mm coiled wires were obtained.

2.2. WA-DED process and heat treatment
The WA-DED process was carried out using an ABB welding robotic 
system equipped with a CMT power source, as depicted in Fig. 1 a. A 
single-lane, multi-layer thin-walled component measuring 240 mm ×

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure analysis
Fig. 2 illustrates the microstructure of the WA-DED 205C aluminum 
alloy under the AD and T6 heat-treated conditions. The microstructure 
of the YOZ, XOZ and XOY sections in the AD sample exhibited equiaxed 
grains, as depicted in Fig. 2 a, b, c and d. However, there were variations 
in grain size. The interlayer region displayed a fine equiaxed grain zone 
(FEGZ), while the intralayer region exhibited a relatively coarse equi -
axed grain zone (CEGZ). This distinction can be attributed to the 
supercooling of the liquid phase at the solid-liquid interface and the 
nucleation process during the solidification of the liquid metal in the 
molten pool. The interlayer zone corresponds to the remelted area where 
the top of the previous deposited layer intertwines with the bottom of 
the currently deposited layer. This region experiences a significant 
temperature gradient and rapid cooling, resulting in substantial sub -
cooling. Additionally, the Zr (wt% = 0.30) and Ti (wt% = 0.19) alloying 
elements added in the wire formed Al
3
Zr and Al
3
Ti phases within the 
grains during the solidification. The crystal structures of Al
3
Zr, Al
3
Ti and 
α -Al all belong to face-centered cubic structures [ 25 ]. Furthermore, the 
lattice parameters of Al
3
Zr (a = 0.4110 nm) and Al
3
Ti (a = 0.3836 nm) 
are very close to that of α -Al (a = 0.40488 nm) [ 25 , 26 ]. The low lattice 
mismatch between Al
3
Zr or Al
3
Ti and α -Al are conducive to the het -
erogeneous nucleation [ 27 ]. The Al
3
Zr, Al
3
Ti and unmelted grain sur -
faces acting as the heterogeneous nucleation cores are beneficial to 
forming equiaxed grains [ 28 , 29 ]. On the other hand, the intralayer re -
gion experiences a reduced temperature gradient, leading to a slower 
cooling rate, decreased supercooling, and a lower number of nucleation 
sites. Consequently, relatively coarse equiaxed grains are formed.
Following the T6 heat treatment, the morphology and size of grain in 
the sample did not undergo significant changes. However, the features of 
grain boundaries became less prominent, as shown in Fig. 2 e, f, g and h. 
This is attributed to the dissolution of most second phases along the 
grain boundaries into the α -Al matrix during high-temperature solid 
solution treatment.
To further analyze the microstructure of the WA-DED 205C 
aluminum alloy, the morphology, grain size, and orientation distribu -
tion of the AD and T6 heat-treated samples were examined using EBSD, 
as depicted in Fig. 3 . The microstructure of both AD and T6 samples 
exhibited a combination of coarse and fine equiaxed grains, as shown in 
Fig. 3 a and d, which align with the observations made through OM. 
Based on statistical analysis, the average grain size of the AD and T6 
samples was determined to be 20.5 μ m and 23.4 μ m, respectively 
( Fig. 3 b and e). The grain size slightly increased after T6 heat treatment. 
The maximum texture index values for the AD and T6 samples were 1.92 
and 1.75, respectively ( Fig. 3 c and f). This low texture index value 
corresponds to the equiaxed grain morphology observed in the samples.
Further insight into the microstructure was obtained through BSE 
images, EDS face scan and point scan analysis of the elements, as shown 
in Fig. 4 . The AD sample ’ s microstructure primarily consisted of an α -Al 
matrix with second phases distributed continuously along the grain 
boundaries. The distribution of alloying elements exhibited significant 
heterogeneity ( Fig. 4 a). The A1 second phase mainly comprised Al and 
Cu elements, with an atomic ratio close to 2:1, indicating its formation 
during the solidification process as the Al
2
Cu phase. The B1 second 
phase was composed of Al, Cu, Mn, and Ni elements, suggesting it might 
be a poly-eutectic phase. Additionally, the C1 second phase contained 
Al, Cu, and Ni elements, indicating it could be the Al-Cu-Ni phase (Ni- 
rich phase).
Upon undergoing T6 heat treatment, the microstructure of the WA- 
DED 205C aluminum alloy underwent significant transformations, 
leading to a more homogeneous distribution of elements, as illustrated 
in Fig. 4 b. This change can be attributed to the dissolution of a majority 
of cyclic or semi-cyclic second phases that were initially distributed 
along the grain boundaries into the α -Al matrix. The EDS point-scan 
analysis revealed that the B2 second phase primarily consisted of Al, 
Cu, and Mn elements, indicating its composition as a Mn-rich phase. 
Similarly, the C2 second phase was composed of Al, Cu, and Ni elements, 
representing a Ni-rich phase. Under the T6 heat-treated condition, the 
morphology of these Ni-rich and Mn-rich phases became more distinct. 
The Ni-rich phase appeared as dispersed blocks, while the Mn-rich phase 
exhibited elongated rods, consistent with the findings of the study 
conducted by Chen et al. [ 15 ]. Following the high-temperature solid 
solution heat treatment, these stable second phases play a beneficial role 
in impeding migration and sliding of grain boundaries, and dislocation 
motion under high-temperature conditions. As a result, the alloy ’ s high- 
temperature strength is enhanced.

3.3. Mechanical properties
3.3.1. Tensile properties
The tensile properties of the WA-DED 205C aluminum alloy were 
evaluated under room temperature and 300

high-temperature were named T6 and HT, respectively. At room tem -
perature tension condition, the AD samples exhibited a UTS of 295 MPa, 
YS of 205 MPa, and El of 8.1 % in the horizontal direction, while in the 
vertical direction, the corresponding values were 271 MPa, 194 MPa, 
and 5.2 %, respectively. The mechanical properties were superior in the 
horizontal direction compared to the vertical direction. Following the 
T6 heat treatment, the UTS, YS, and El of the alloy improved to 444 MPa, 
353 MPa, and 9.4 % in the horizontal direction, and 428 MPa, 347 MPa, 
and 8.0 % in the vertical direction, respectively. Once again, the me -
chanical properties were more favorable in the horizontal direction. 
Although the microstructure analysis revealed equiaxed grains, which 
theoretically suggests isotropic mechanical properties, it was observed 
in this study that the vertical samples exhibited lower mechanical 
properties, possibly due to the presence of interlayer pores [ 51 ]. In 
vertical samples, the effective bearing area was reduced due to the 
presence of interlayer pores, leading to the reduction of mechanical 
properties [ 52 , 53 ]. The T6 heat treatment led to significant improve -
ments in both strength and plasticity, primarily attributed to 
precipitation strengthening, which is the major strengthening mecha -
nism in Al
–

3.3.2. Fracture morphologies
Fig. 12 illustrates the fracture morphology of the AD, T6 and HT 
tensile samples. The fracture surfaces of the AD samples ( Fig. 12 a and b) 
exhibited a combination of tear ridges, dimples, and cleavage planes, 
indicating a mixed ductile-brittle fracture behavior. Furthermore, 
microcracks were observed along the grain boundaries. Upon 
undergoing the T6 treatment, the fracture surfaces displayed a signifi -
cant increase in the number of tear ridges and dimples, while no 
microcracks were observed ( Fig. 12 c and d). This fracture morphology 
indicated a predominantly ductile fracture behavior. Under the 300 
◦
C 
condition, the fracture surfaces appeared relatively flat, however, 
distinct dimples and a few cleavage planes were still evident ( Fig. 12 e 
and f). This fracture morphology exhibited characteristics of a mixed 
ductile-brittle fracture. Moreover, pore defects were detected on the 
fracture surfaces of samples in all conditions. These defects reduced the 
effective loading area and could serve as sources of cracks, ultimately 
leading to a decrease in the tensile properties.
4. Conclusions
