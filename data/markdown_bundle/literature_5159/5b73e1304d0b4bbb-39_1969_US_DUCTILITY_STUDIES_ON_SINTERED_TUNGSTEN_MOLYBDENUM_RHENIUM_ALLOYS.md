STRACT
A Brinell brittleness test developed at Los Alamos several years 
ago has undergone considerable experimentation vith regard to optimum 
specimen diameter and thickness. A new, slightly larger, specimen 
size was used for more sensitive ductility measurements of sintered 
W-Mo-Re alloys with 0 to 8 at. $ Re. A possible heat treatment for 
such alloys to restore ductility normally lost through grain growth 
appears evident.
1. INTRODUCTION
In 1963, a Los Alamos report^- described a new 
brittleness test particularly applicable to powder 
metallurgy materials and presented results attained 
at that time. This test is convenient and useful in 
evaluating sintered tungsten-base alloys.
The test was called a brittleness test because 
it applies primarily to alloys and cermets that ex­
hibit little or no ductility in conventional tensile 
testing. The test appears to generate tensile stresses 
through an applied compressive load and gives a large 
range of values over a small range of ductility or 
plasticity. The validity of the test seemed assured 
when the trend of results matched common experience 
with tungsten and molybdenum and correlated with oth­
er workers' bend tests on W-Re sheet. The test also 
positively indicated the improved ductility of sin­
tered tantalum which had been "deoxidized" by fixing 
the contained tantalum oxide with small additions of
yttrium. Some results of the test on sintered W-Mo-
2Re alloys have been published. Reports by Fuhrman 
3 1*and Holden J indicated that other investigators had 
used the test successfully to measure the ductility 
of cermets with a niobium matrix.
In 1967, two patents were issued to Scruggs for
molybdenum and tungsten composites with improved
5room-temperature ductility. The ductility data for
these patents were obtained by this same test, and 
the patents cite the publication given as Reference 
1 of this report.
During 1966, this brittleness test was investi­
gated further with respect to the effect of specimen 
size. The resulting increased sensitivity permitted 
the test to be used with greater precision than be­
fore on sintered W-Mo-Re alloys containing 0 to 8 at. 
$ Re. The correlation of specimen size and sensitiv­
ity and the ensuing tests on alloys form the major 
part of this report, 
g. THE BRINELL BRimENESS TEST
The Brinell brittleness test was originally de­
veloped with sintered specimens about 3/8 in. in di ­
ameter x l/8 in. thick. The bottom of the specimen 
is ground flat to avoid premature cracking caused by 
a concave surface resting on the Brinell anvil. The 
specimen is centered under the 10-mm-diam ball of a 
hydraulic Brinell tester, and a load is gradually 
applied until the specimen cracks. With subsequent 
duplicate specimens, the diameter, d, of the impres­
sion in millimeters is read at approximately l/£ and 
3A of the breaking load. The values of obtained 
are hardness readings. These values should be near­
ly the same for each specimen, but may increase 
slightly with load owing to work-hardening or closing 
of the pores in a porous disk.

3.6 Disk Ductility and Tensile Elongation
Where disk ductility numbers are very high 
(such as with W-Mo-Re alloys containing 17 to 25 at. 
$ Re) we thought that some tensile test elongation 
could be obtained. However, the only attempt to re­
late these two tests resulted in zero elongation 
(brittle break) for the tensile specimens. We noted, 
also, that the disk specimens sintered in the same 
furnace runs with the tensile bars were below par in 
ductility.

## Page 14

Thus, in comparing test methods, there are prob­
lems other than the specimen and test differences.
The ductility of these tungsten-base alloys is re­
lated not only to rhenium content but also to the 
amount of interstitial elements. Proper sintering 
of a small charge of disks to reduce interstitial 
content is much easier than similar purification of 
a larger charge of tensile bars. The tensile test 
is also very sensitive to surface imperfections 
while the disk test appears insensitive, perhaps due 
to combined, rather than purely tensile, stresses.
A sidelight on the preparation of the tensile 
bars tends to confirm one of the undertones of this 
report: small amounts of rhenium do reduce the brit­
tleness of sintered w/Mo alloys. Of the t-8 sintered 
rods that were ground to tensile bar shape, three, 
all containing only 2 at. $ Re or less, broke in 
grinding.
Although there were twice as many rods with 3 
to IT at. $ Re content, none of them broke in grind­
ing.
The Brinell disk test is sufficiently sensitive 
to show that materials with near-zero tensile elon­
gation can differ greatly in brittleness, fragility, 
ductility, or plasticity under certain conditions.
It, CONCLUSIONS
(1) The new information obtained on this test 
substantiates its originally established validity as 
a means for classifying materials with varying de­
grees of brittleness.
(2) Photographs show that as the ductility re­
vealed by this test increases, the specimen is capa­
ble of absorbing more local and general plastic de­
formation before failure.
(3) The O.itlO-in.-diam disks (as-pressed) first 
used in this test still seem satisfactory for mate­
rials in the test's upper ductility range. Increas­
ing the disk thickness from 0-125 to about 0. l40 to
0.150 in. with the above diameter should further im­
prove test consistency, but this has not been tried 
with fairly ductile materials.
(4) Experiments with specimens of up to 1-0-in.
diam showed that disks of about 0.500-in. diam x
0. 175. to 0.200-in. thickness (as-pressed) improved 
the test's sensitivity and consistency for the most 
brittle and intermediate parts of the test range.
With such sintered (2350°C) W-Mo-Re disks, the duc­
tility increased steadily as the rhenium content was 
raised progressively from 0 to 8 at. $.
(5) Use of the test on W-Mo-Re alloys has in­
