Abstract 
 
A mathematical model to predict creep properties of Ni-based superalloys at intermediate temperature r anges based on 
microstructure, process and creep condition, and ma terial properties has been developed by modifying a nd combining 
previous models. Modifications are made so that as many necessary model parameters as possible, whose values have 
been obtained from experimental measurement of cree p properties, are determined from CALPHAD (CALculat ion of 
PHAse Diagrams) thermodynamic calculations. The mod el can reproduce minimum creep rate, creep rupture time, and 
time to specific strain for a wide range of Ni-base d superalloys, correlating well with experimental d ata without any 
further creep experiments. The applicability and li mitation of the model as an alloy design tool for n ew Ni-based 
superalloys with improved creep properties are discussed in this paper. 
 
Keywords: Creep, Metallic Material, Analytic functions, Ni-based superalloys, Thermodynamic calculation

1. Introduction 
Ni-based superalloys have been widely used in aero engines and other modern turbine engines over the l ast several 
decades owing to their good high-temperature tensil e, creep, and fatigue properties (Sims and Hagel, 1 972; Sims, 1984; 
Pollock and Tin, 2006). Due to the ever-increasing demand for extending the life time of superalloys a t high temperatures, 
a great amount of research effort has been made to improve creep properties. This includes adding a wi de range of 
alloying elements (such as Co, Cr, Mo, Re, Ru, W, N b, Ta, Ti, B, C, Hf, Zr) to the base Ni-Al alloys ( Sims and Hagel, 
1972; Sims, 1984; Pollock and Tin, 2006). However, since creep properties take a long time to investig ate during the 
development of new superalloys, the use of computat ional techniques for prediction of creep properties such as minimum 
creep rate, creep strain and creep rupture time is desirable. 
Empirical creep models proposed up to now have been mainly for the estimation of creep strain and time from 
relatively brief creep experiments (Li, 1963; Ferra ri, 1976; Dieter, 1986; Dyson and Gibbons, 1987; Lu pinc et al., 2005). 
The minimum creep rate, an important variable in th e empirical models, is mainly presented in a functi onal form of stress 
and temperature, and is estimated by fitting experi mental data (Ajaja et al., 1980; Nabarro and Villie rs, 1995; Kassner et 
al., 2007; Kassner, 2015). However, most models for minimum creep rate do not consider microstructural factors such as 
size, distribution, and fraction of γ′ precipitates. 
 A microstructure-based creep model (Dyson, 2009; B asirat et al., 2012; Zhu et al., 2012; Le Graverend et al., 2014) 
has been developed from continuum mechanics, rather recently. These models predict creep properties in functional 
forms of not only stress and temperature but also m icrostructural factors (such as dislocation density , precipitate size, 
inter-particle spacing of precipitates and volume f raction of precipitates). Although these models are practically suitable 
for analyses of experimental creep properties, some model parameter values should be determined only b y fitting to 
experimental creep data, and an application to new alloys is limited if experimental information is no t available for those 
new alloys. 
For a more efficient alloy design to improve creep properties, one needs a model that can estimate cre ep properties 
without any further creep experiments. From this pe rspective, Saunders et al. (2004) show a computatio nal scheme to 
estimate minimum creep rate and creep rupture time by using CALPHAD thermodynamic calculations without creep 
experiments. However, the model does not consider m icrostructural factors such as inter-particle spaci ng of γ′ 
precipitates and grain size. Development of a compu tational approach that considers microstructural fa ctors is needed, 
and an attempt at such an approach is presented here. 
The purpose of the present study is to develop a cr eep model that considers microstructural factors bu t which does not 
need further experimental measurement of creep prop erties, and thus can be used for designing new Ni-b ased superalloys 
based on computational approaches. Our creep model has been achieved by combining earlier creep models with 
modifications, and can be used for prediction of cr eep properties using model parameters obtained from thermodynamic 
calculations and existing creep data. In Sec. 2, it will be explained how the minimum creep rate, an i mportant variable in 
the present model, is developed in a functional for m of stress, temperature, and microstructural facto rs. The procedure of 
determining model parameters for the minimum creep rate and other parameters for creep strain and time will be detailed 
in Sec. 3 and Sec. 4. Comparisons between calculate d and experimental creep properties under various c reep conditions 
and microstructural factors along with applicability and limits of the model will be covered in Sec. 5.

2. Minimum creep rate model 
The creep process has three stages (Dieter, 1986). The first stage is primary creep, a region of incre asing creep 
resistance with an increase in dislocation density. The second stage is secondary creep, a region of constant creep rate due 
to the competing processes of strain hardening and recovery. The average creep rate during secondary c reep is called 
minimum creep rate. The third stage of creep, terti ary creep, is a region where cracks are initiated f rom voids, necking 
and brittle regions such as grain boundary, carbide , oxidation, and topologically close-packed (TCP) p hases. Also, the 
period of tertiary creep is often related to metall urgical changes such as precipitate coarsening, gra in recrystallization or 
diffusional phase changes (Dieter, 1986). 
Li (1963) proposes a functional form for creep stra in and time during primary and secondary creep. Als o, Dyson and 
Gibbons (1987), and Lupinc et al. (1995) propose a relation between creep rate and strain by fitting e xperimental data 
during secondary and tertiary creep. In the present study, creep properties are predicted by combining the previously 
proposed models. In those models, the minimum creep rate is an important variable, affected by various microstructural 
factors. 
For an elaborate determination of minimum creep rat e, the relation between minimum creep rate and micr ostructural 
factors needs to be understood. The minimum creep r ate has been related to stacking fault energy (Moha med and 
Langdon, 1974; Dieter, 1986), the morphology of γ′ precipitates (Ajaja et al., 1980; Bhowal et al., 1 990; Zhu et al., 2012), 
and the grain size (Dieter, 1986; Nabarro and Villiers, 1995; Kassner, 2015). 
Creep is influenced by the cross-slip of perfect di slocations and dislocation climb in the γ matrix, for which partial 
dislocations should be recombined into a perfect dislocation. At high temperatures (T > 0.5T m) that correspond to most of 
the service temperatures of superalloys, the lattic e self-diffusion is known to be the dominant rate-c ontrolling mechanism 
of the dislocation climb (Sherby and Miller, 1979; Nabarro and Villiers, 1995; Kassner, 2015). The eas e of the 
recombination of the partial dislocations, however, is affected by the size of stacking fault energy t hat determines the 
distance between partial dislocations. It is also k nown (Tian et al., 2014) that the stacking fault en ergy has an effect on 
the operating deformation mechanism (either an isolated fault mechanism or a micro-twin mechanism). However, it is not 
clearly known how the different mechanisms have eff ects on the resultant creep properties. What is kno wn concerning 
the effect of stacking fault energy on the creep pr operties is that the minimum creep rate increases w ith increasing 
stacking fault energy, in more detail, proportional to the cube of stacking fault energy (Mohamed and Langdon, 1974).

3. Parameters for the minimum creep rate model 
The present minimum creep rate model involves vario us parameters related to materials or microstructur al properties 
(volume fraction of γ′ precipitates, diffusivity, shear modulus, burgers vector, inter-particle spacing of γ′ precipitates, 
stacking fault energy, back stress and grain size) determined by the constitution or process condition of the material, in 
addition to model parameters (A 1 and A 2 in Eq.(6)) that can be determined by fitting existing experimental creep data. 
The volume fraction of γ′ precipitates is dependent on the temperature as we ll as the alloy composition. In the present 
study, the volume fraction of γ′ precipitates is obtained from thermodynamic calcul ations (Thermo-Calc.; Saunders et al., 
2000) at the last heat treatment temperatures in in dividual processes. Diffusivity, shear modulus, bur gers vector and 
stacking fault energy are formulated in a functional form of composition of γ and/or γ′ phase, which can be estimated also 
from thermodynamic calculations, as will be describ ed later on in this section. Back stress is given a value that 
corresponds to the Orowan stress, or 0.75/g+0+6, depending on the relative size of the two terms a s mentioned already in the 
previous section. Here, the Orowan stress is estima ted from a theoretical formula (Bhowal et al., 1990 ). The remaining 
material or microstructure related parameters are t he inter-particle spacing of γ′ precipitates and the grain size. Those 
properties are not determined only from alloy compo sitions, but are highly dependent on the process co ndition (Bhowal 
et al., 1990; Locq et al., 2004; Viswanathan et al. , 2005). So far, it is not trivial to predict those properties accurately 
using thermodynamic and kinetic simulation techniqu es. Therefore, in the present study, those properti es are left as 
externally given input parameters, of which values are assigned from literature data when fitting othe r model parameters 
to experimental creep data or are given arbitrary t arget values when predicting creep properties for u nknown alloys 
during an alloy design. The values of model paramet ers /g18+7/g+86w and /g18+7/g+870 are obtained by fitting existing experimental 
minimum creep rate data for René 95 (Bhowal et al., 1990). The /g18+7/g+86w and /g18+7/g+870 parameter values determined by this way 
are used for prediction of minimum creep rate of ot her arbitrary alloys. The transferability of /g18+7/g+86w and /g18+7/g+870 parameter 
values into other alloys has been carefully checked during the present study, as will be described in Sec. 5.

Even with the above-mentioned reasons for the poor description of creep curves in Fig. 16, there are s ome inherent 
technical limitations in the present model. During creep deformation, various microstructural changes are expected even 
at intermediate temperatures as well as increased d islocation density and crack initiation on TCP phas es boundaries 
(Pessah et al., 1992; V olek et al., 2006; Reed et a l., 2007), prior particle boundaries (PPBs) (Wanhon g et al., 1995), 
carbides (Wasson et al., 2008) and oxides (Dennison et al., 1978). Even though the damage constant int roduced in Eq. 
(41) (Dyson and Gibbons, 1987) is supposed to cover all the above-mentioned factors, it is thought to be insufficient to 
describe the whole effects using one model parameter. Creep properties are also affected by solute segregation to stacking 
faults in γ (Kassner, 2015) or γ/γ′ interface (Warren et al., 1998; Wu et al., 2007), misfit strain on γ/γ′ interfaces (Sato et 
al., 2008), and serration of grain boundary (Kearse y et al., 2000), which are not covered in the prese nt model. In addition 
to the above-mentioned limitations, more decisively, it should be emphasized that the present model co nsiders only mean 
radius and mean inter-particle spacing of γ′ precipitates, while such size distributions in rea l superalloys are generally 
multi-modal (with different average values between secondary and tertiary γ′ precipitates). Therefore, taking into account 
the multi-modal size distributions in radius and in ter-particle spacing of γ′ precipitates should be an area for future 
studies. 
Even though there exists a certain limitation, the present creep model reproduces minimum creep rate, creep rupture 
time, time to specific strain and dislocation-parti cle bypass mechanisms for various Ni-based superall oys in reasonable 
agreement with experimental information, and can be used for a design of new alloys with improved cree p properties at 
intermediate temperatures. The applicability of the present computational model would be strengthened if simulation 
techniques for the multi-modal microstructural evol ution during thermal processes and the effects of a lloying elements 
are combined. 
 
6. Conclusion 
It has been shown that the present creep model can reproduce the minimum creep rate of a wide range of Ni-based 
superalloys with good correlation to experimental d ata, and can further reproduce creep rupture time a nd time to specific 
strain well within an order of magnitude at intermediate temperatures. 
The advantage of the present model is that it uses computational approaches to obtain materials depend ent model 
parameters and does not require any further creep e xperiments. Even though there is a limitation in th e applicability of 
the model in predicting the creep rupture strain an d full creep strain-time curve, as a predictive mod el that considers 
microstructural factors, but does not need any furt her experimental measurement of creep properties, t he present model 
can be used for designing new Ni-based superalloys with improved creep properties at intermediate temperatures.

Title: An Intermediate Temperature Creep Model for Ni-based superalloys 
 
Authors: Young-Kwang Kim, Donghoon Kim, Hong-Kyu Kim, Chang-Seok Oh, and Byeong-Joo Lee 
 
Highlights 
 
/circle6 We propose a creep model considering various micros tructural factors at intermediate 
temperature. 
/circle6 The model has been achieved by combining earlier creep models with modifications. 
/circle6 The model parameters are obtained from thermodynamic calculations and existing creep data. 
/circle6 The model can reproduce minimum creep rate, rupture time and time to specific strain well. 
/circle6 The advantage of the model is that it does not require any further creep experiments.
