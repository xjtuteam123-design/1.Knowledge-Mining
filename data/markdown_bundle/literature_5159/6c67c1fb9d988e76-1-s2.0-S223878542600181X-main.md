ABSTRACT
Tantalum (Ta) and its alloys have recently gained significant recognition in both scientific research and industrial 
applications due to their exceptional properties, including high melting point, excellent biocompatibility, and 
superior corrosion resistance. Additive manufacturing (AM) offers unprecedented freedom in fabricating com -
plex, high-performance Ta components with minimal material waste, making it a transformative approach for 
high-value applications. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in AM of Ta and Ta-based alloys, 
covering feedstock characteristics, process – structure – property relationships, and application-specific perfor -
mance. Key aspects discussed include the influence of powder morphology and impurity content on AM quality, 
outlines processing window optimization strategies for high energy beam and kinetic-energy based techniques, 
that consistently yield near-fully dense components ( > 99.5 %), and highlights post-processing methods to 
enhance mechanical properties, exemplified by HIP treatments that elevate the fatigue limit to ~300 MPa. 
Special attention is given to the design of porous Ta architectures for biomedical implants, where AM enables 
patient-specific geometries with improved osseointegration. The review also surveys the development of Ta – W, 
Ta – Nb, and Ta – Ti alloy systems for high-temperature and biomedical applications. Remaining challenges and 
future perspectives in powder reuse, alloy design, and long-term implant performance are also outlined to guide 
further development in this rapidly evolving field.
1. Introduction
As a rare refractory metal, tantalum (Ta), has a crustal abundance of 
0.0002 % and commonly occurs in association with niobium (Nb). 
Although Australia and Brazil possess the world's richest tantalum ore 
reserves, the Great Lakes Region of Central Africa, including the Dem -
ocratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda, Ethiopia and Mozambique, re -
mains the most crucial global supplier of tantalum raw materials [ 1 , 2 ]. 
The unique combination of properties of Ta makes it indispensable for 
critical applications ranging from chemical processing equipment and 
high-temperature components in aerospace to permanent biomedical 
implants such as orthopedic and cranial devices [ 3 – 5 ]. Especially in the 
biomedical field, the bio-inertness and ability to promote osseointe -
gration have established it as the most promising candidate material for 
bone defect repair and joint arthroplasty [ 6 , 7 ]. However, pure tantalum 
exhibits inherent limitations, such as a relatively high density, an elastic 
modulus still significantly higher than that of human bone, and insuf -
ficient mechanical strength for the most demanding load-bearing or 
ultra-high-temperature scenarios. These limitations underscore the ne -
cessity of alloying tantalum with other elements to tailor its properties 
for specific applications [ 8 – 11 ].
However, the high melting point and high susceptibility to oxidation 
pose significant challenges for manufacturing through conventional 
techniques such as melting and powder metallurgy [ 12 – 16 ], which 
usually lead to high energy consumption and cost. The traditional 
machining processes, including milling and turning, usually result in 
low material utilization and high waste of the expensive material. 
Furthermore, these conventional methods struggle to fabricate the 
complex, thin-walled and three-dimensional porous architectures that 
are crucial for biomedical implants to reduce stiffness mismatch with 
natural bone and to facilitate bone ingrowth and vascularization [ 17 ].

From an engineering perspective, the selection among these AM

throughput, cost, and resolution. LPBF offers the highest practical 
feature resolution (~50 – 100 μ m), making it the preferred choice for 
intricate biomedical implants. However, the rapid cooling rates often

The intersection of AM and tantalum is, however, a domain marked 
by both great promise and distinct scientific and technical challenges. 
The extreme thermal cycles inherent to fusion-based AM processes (e.g., 
rapid melting and solidification) interact intricately with tantalum's 
high melting point and thermal conductivity, making the establishment 
of stable, defect-free processing windows non-trivial [ 25 ]. The 
solid-state bonding mechanism in techniques like cold spray presents a 
different set of challenges related to particle kinetics and deformation 
[ 26 , 27 ]. Furthermore, the quality of the final part is profoundly sensitive 
to feedstock characteristics, especially powder morphology and inter -
stitial impurity contents, such as oxygen and nitrogen, which strongly 
influence flowability, laser absorption, melt pool dynamics, and ulti -
mately, mechanical properties and biocompatibility [ 28 , 29 ]. For Ta al -
loys, additional complexities arise from the large differences in physical 
properties, including melting point and density, between Ta and the 
alloying elements, posing significant challenges for homogeneous in-situ 
alloying during AM [ 10 ].

2. Feedstocks
Based on compatibility with different AM techniques, the raw ma -
terials for AM can be primarily classified into two main categories: 
powder feedstocks and wire feedstocks, with powder serving as the 
predominant raw material for the AM of Ta and its alloys.
2.1. Irregular-shaped powder
Initial exploration into LPBF of pure Ta employed hydrogenation- 
dehydrogenation (HDH) powder as feedstock, as documented in the 
early-stage research [ 33 ]. The irregular particle morphology inherent to 
HDH powders fundamentally limited their flowability and packing 
density, imposing critical constraints on powder layer uniformity during 
spreading. While parametric optimization (laser power = 300 W, scan -
ning speed = 100 mm/s, layer thickness = 30 μ m, hatch distance = 230 
μ m) enabled the relative density exceeding 97 % in the as-built sample, 
metallographic analysis revealed persistent intra-granular porosity. 
These microstructural anomalies precipitated catastrophic embrittle -
ment, with tensile elongation of 2 % - a stark contrast to the typical 
ductility of wrought Ta (20 %) [ 34 ] and powder metallurgy Ta (27 % – 
43 %) [ 15 ].
In efforts to improve powder morphology, Li et al. [ 35 ] investigated 
the effectiveness of fluidization and jet milling techniques for reshaping 
irregular Ta powders, the corresponding schematics of which are shown 
in Fig. 3 . Their results demonstrated that while fluidization proved 
ineffective for modifying Ta powder morphology, jet milling success -
fully smoothed particle edges and improved powder flowability to 11.1 
s/50g (Hall flow rate), meeting basic LPBF feedstock requirements. 
However, their study revealed that the high-velocity collisions during jet 
milling introduced heterogeneous oxidation films (~5.1 nm thick, cor -
responding to ~0.08 wt% oxygen). This oxidation significantly reduced 
the laser absorptivity from 0.413 (for spheroidized powder) to 0.366, 
necessitating higher energy input to compensate for the energy coupling 
loss and achieve optimal densification. This result highlights that for 
laser-based AM, powder surface chemistry (specifically oxidation state) 
is a critical variable governing the energy absorption efficiency and the 
stability of the processing window.
However, it is noteworthy that irregularly shaped Ta powder has 
demonstrated unexpectedly high performance in cold spray additive 
manufacturing. According to the study by Cui et al. [ 36 ], under identical 
spraying conditions, the samples fabricated with irregular Ta powders 
achieved a higher deposition efficiency of 86 % compared to 61 % for 
spherical powders, and exhibited a superior tensile strength of 350 MPa 
(2.1 times that of spherical Ta powders) and superior ablation resis -
tance. These results are consistent with the systematic study by Chu et al. 
[ 37 ], which reported that angular Ta powder exhibited a deposition 
efficiency as high as 97 %, along with high cohesion strength and 
excellent corrosion resistance, outperforming its spherical counterparts. 
The enhanced performance of irregular powders is attributed to distinct 
mechanistic advantages verified by Chu et al. [ 37 ]. First, regarding 
deposition efficiency, the higher drag coefficient of angular powders 
enables significantly greater in-flight velocities (~638 m/s vs. ~564 
m/s), providing higher kinetic energy for adhesion. Second, regarding 
bonding strength, unlike spherical particles which rely on mechanical 
interlocking, the sharp edges of angular powders facilitate oxide frag -
mentation to form true metallurgical bonds; this is quantitatively evi -
denced by a drastic reduction in corrosion current density (from 510 
nA/cm
2

2
), indicating a dense, defect-free interface. 
Moreover, considering that HDH Ta powders typically cost only 
one-tenth of spherical plasma-atomized powders, such irregular pow -
ders offer a compelling combination of high performance and low cost 
for industrial applications.

2.2. Spherical powder
Due to the high melting temperature, conventional gas atomization 
techniques – including vacuum induction gas atomization (VIGA) and 
electrode induction gas atomization (EIGA) – face significant challenges 
in producing spherical Ta powders. Currently, spherical Ta powder 
production primarily employs two established techniques: plasma 
rotating electrode process (PREP) and plasma spheroidization (PS), the 
schematics of which are illustrated in Fig. 4 .
PREP involves melting the tip of a rapidly spinning metallic electrode 
using a plasma arc, where centrifugal force disintegrates the molten 
material into fine droplets that solidify into spherical particles in inert 
atmosphere. The PREP-produced spherical Ta powder exhibits high 
sphericity, absence of satellite particles, no hollow powder, and low 
impurity content [ 40 – 42 ]. It is noteworthy that the PREP technique is 
employed not only for producing spherical Ta powder but also for 
fabricating Ta alloy [ 43 , 44 ] and even Ta-containing refractory 
high-entropy alloy (RHEA) [ 45 ] spherical powders ( Fig. 5 ). However, 
scalability is constrained by the mechanical trade-off between electrode 
rod diameter and rotation speed. Consequently, the yield of fine powder 
(particle size below 45 μ m) is relatively low. This low yield serves as a 
primary cost driver, making PREP-prepared Ta powder (typically 
coarser) particularly suitable for electron beam powder bed fusion to 
mitigate the smoke effect caused by electrostatic charging, whereas the 
limited availability of fines restricts their economic viability for LPBF 
which generally demands finer particle size distributions. The enter -
prises utilizing the PREP technology for spherical Ta powder production 
include Northwest Institute for Nonferrous Metal Research and Sailong 
Metal in China.
For PS technology, the irregularly shaped powder is fed into the 
plasma torch using a carrier gas, and the particles rapidly melt under 
high temperature and coalesce into spherical shapes due to surface 
tension, followed by rapid cooling and solidification. The particle size 
and impurity contents of the PS-produced Ta powder are primarily

Synthesizing the findings from Sections 2.1 and 2.2 , a clear distinc -
tion emerges regarding how feedstock characteristics dictate process -
ability across different AM routes. For fusion-based processes (LPBF and 
EB-PBF), the presence of impurities, particularly oxygen, is detrimental 
not only to the final mechanical properties but also to the processing 
physics (e.g., reduced laser absorption). Furthermore, these processes 
impose strict requirements on particle size distribution, i.e. finer pow -
ders for LPBF resolution versus coarser powders for EB-PBF stability. In 
contrast, solid-state cold spray exhibits a unique tolerance for feedstock 
variations, effectively utilizing irregular powders and overcoming sur -
face oxidation through kinetic impact and adiabatic shear instability, 
thereby decoupling the stringent dependency on powder sphericity and 
purity required by fusion-based techniques.
2.3. Wire feedstock
To date, research on wire-based AM of Ta remains limited, however, 
wire-feed AM technologies demonstrate significant potential for future 
development due to its superior material utilization, higher deposition 
rates and lower equipment investment compared to powder-based AM 
systems [ 53 – 56 ], particularly for large-scale Ta component fabrication 
in biomedical and chemical processing applications where the excep -
tional corrosion resistance is required. Marinelli et al. [ 57 ] fabricated Ta 
samples using WAAM technique with 1.2 mm diameter Ta wires, 
achieving a deposition rate of 2.7 kg/h. Fig. 6 presents the employed 
WAAM set-up and the as-deposited wall structures. Their experimental 
results revealed significant anisotropy in both the microstructure and 
mechanical properties of the as-deposited samples: the microstructure 
consisted of columnar grains preferentially aligned along the deposition 
direction, while the horizontal orientation exhibited substantially 
higher yield strength, ultimate tensile strength and elongation compared 
to the vertical orientation. In their subsequent study [ 58 ], the intro -
duction of inter-pass cold rolling during the layer-by-layer deposition 
process of WAAM induced localized plastic deformation, resulting in 
equiaxed grains with random orientations and significantly improving 
the isotropy of the mechanical properties. Mechanistically, guaranteeing 
this isotropy requires strict process controls: the rolling load (e.g., 50 
kN) must be sufficient to generate a plastic deformation zone deeper 
than the deposited layer height. Furthermore, the outcome is highly 
sensitive to the thermal cycle, as the grain refinement relies on the heat 
input from the subsequent deposition pass to trigger static recrystalli -
zation within the deformed, energy-rich microstructure.
2.4. Impurity for AM-Ta feedstocks
The impurities discussed in this article primarily refer to non- 
metallic elements such as oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), carbon (C) and 
hydrogen (H), etc. These elements predominantly dissolve as interstitial 
atoms within the body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice of Ta, inducing 
substantial lattice distortion that significantly enhances the strength 
through solid solution strength mechanism [ 59 ].
Among these impurity elements, oxygen content has received the 
most extensive research attention from scholars. Tan et al. [ 60 ] sys -
tematically investigated the effects of oxygen content of the raw pow -
ders on the microstructure and mechanical properties of LPBF-processed 
Ta by employing spherical Ta powders with varying oxygen concen -
trations (150 ppm, 320 ppm and 470 ppm) while maintaining identical 
processing parameters. Their findings demonstrated that while oxygen 
pickup during the LPBF process was minimal (no more than 40 ppm), a 
clear oxygen-dependent trend had been confirmed: increasing oxygen 
content resulted in elevated tensile strength (from 391 MPa to 581 MPa),

usually reused, a practice that often leads to gradual accumulation of 
non-metallic impurities during recycling. The studies conducted by Song

oxidation reaction on the powder surface caused by the laser radiation, 
rather than the exposure to air. The 16 times of reuse led to the oxygen

exhibited distinct oxygen-dependent behaviors: tensile strength fol -

to 11 reuse cycles), whereas fracture elongation displayed a strict in -

to 11.7 % across the studied range. In the study by Guo et al. [ 64 ], it was 
found that through 30 cycles of powder reuse in EB-PBF of Ta, the ox -

the powder remained stable without significant deterioration. Concur -

notable variation, while the elongation decreased from 49.3 % to 32.0

3. Additive manufacturing of pure Ta
The additive manufacturing of pure Ta enables the direct fabrication 
of complex components, but its success hinges on overcoming challenges 
unique to the material's properties. This section systematically reviews 
the key aspects of AM pure Ta: the establishment of stable processing 
windows across different techniques (Section 3.1 ), post-processing 
treatments for microstructural and mechanical enhancement (Section 
3.2 ), the design and performance of porous architectures for biomedical 
applications (Section 3.3 ), and their subsequent translation into clinical 
practice (Section 3.4 ).
3.1. Processing window optimization
The additive manufacturing of tantalum is fundamentally governed 
by its intrinsic properties, namely an exceptionally high melting point 
and high thermal conductivity. These properties define a narrow stable 
parameter space necessary to achieve full density and sound micro -
structure. For melt-based techniques (e.g., LPBF, EB-PBF, LMD), the core 
challenge lies in balancing sufficient energy input to overcome high melt 
viscosity against excessive heat that drives defects. In contrast, solid- 
state deposition (e.g., CS) circumvents melting altogether, with its 
window dictated by kinetic energy for particle bonding. This section 
synthesizes the established paradigms for processing window optimi -
zation in Ta AM, examining how critical parameters are adjusted to 
concurrently manage density, defect formation, and microstructural 
development for tailored mechanical performance.

VED and densification of LPBF-processed Ta via varying the laser power 
and scanning speed, highlighting the balling phenomenon and micro -
porosity formation at high scanning speeds due to elevated melt pool 
surface tension. They demonstrated that reducing speed extended melt 
pool lifetime, enhances Marangoni flow, and improved wettability, 
pushing relative density above 99 %. Shi et al. [ 66 ] refined the bound of 
the processing window by utilizing RF plasma-spheroidized powder, 
achieving a near-theoretical relative density of 99.96 % at an energy

precisely control thermal gradients and cooling rates. This advanced

weakening, reduction of dislocation pile-ups, and mitigation of residual 
stress, and hence a superior balance of tensile strength and ductility. 
Conceptually, both double-layer scanning and laser remelting are 
implementations of multi-pass strategies, with the former being a 
fixed-parameter method and the latter representing a flexible, thermally 
engineered approach.

metric. Comparing to the LPBF optimization focusing on micron-scale 
melt pool stability, LMD entails significantly higher energy inputs and 
larger thermal masses via scaling up using a coaxial powder feed system.

varying laser power (1600 – 1800 W) to adjust the VED. As listed in

Specifically, the EBSD and FSD insets in Fig. 9 reveal millimeter-scale

This significant coarsening could be attributed to the prolonged molten 
pool lifetime and reduced cooling rate during LMD process. Despite the 
coarser microstructure, the LMD process remains acceptable and crucial 
for fabricating large-scale tantalum components in aerospace and 
chemical industries where fast near-net shaping is prioritized over the 
ultra-fine grains required for small, high-stress parts [ 62 ]. Furthermore, 
potential routes to mitigate the resultant anisotropy and improve fatigue 
performance include optimized scanning strategies (e.g. path rotation) 
to disrupt columnar growth and post-process heat treatments (e.g. hot 
isostatic pressing) to induce stress relief or recrystallization.

3.2. Post-processing heat treatment
Although the optimization of in-situ processing windows for high 
energy beam and kinetic-energy based additive manufacturing is critical 
for minimizing defects, the intrinsic rapid cooling rates and severe 
plastic deformation inevitably result in high residual stresses in the as- 
built state. Consequently, post-processing heat treatment, such as vac -
uum annealing [ 61 , 65 , 79 ] and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) [ 80 , 81 ], are 
frequently employed to achieve further densification and tailor me -
chanical performance.
Zhou et al. [ 79 ] investigated the microstructural evolution of 
LPBF-processed tantalum under a triple heat-treatment regime. They 
observed that the heat treatment effectively eliminated the epitaxial 
columnar grain structure typical of the as-built state, resulting in a 
mesostructure composed of equiaxed grains. Notably, they overcame the 
classical strength-ductility trade-off by preserving Hall-Petch strength -
ening via fine cellular grains while converting columnar grains to 
equiaxed ones. This yielded a synergistic “ high strength and toughness ” 
with a tensile strength exceeding 750 MPa and ductility surpassing 12 
%.
While vacuum annealing is effective for microstructural tailoring, 
HIP is often required to heal physical defects such as lack-of-fusion voids 
through the simultaneous application of high temperature and pressure. 
Qin et al. [ 81 ] revealed that the efficacy of HIP was highly sensitive to 
both temperature and component geometry. For solid tantalum blocks, 
they found that a 1350 
◦
C treatment (at 150 MPa) successfully closed 
internal micropores, achieving near-full density ( > 99 %) and inducing a 
crystallographic texture shift from the as-built {111} orientation to a 
{100} preference, accompanied by solid-solution strengthening from 
trace oxygen uptake. Conversely, for porous tantalum scaffolds, this 
high-temperature regime proved detrimental due to excessive oxidation 
and embrittlement of the fine struts [ 80 ]. Instead, an optimized 
lower-temperature HIP at 850 
◦
C was found superior for porous struc -
tures, improving compressive strength via recovery mechanisms 
without compromising ductility.

mizing HIP to enhance cyclic fatigue resistance. In the study by Qin et al.

3.3. Porous structure optimization
Notwithstanding the considerable progress in the additive 
manufacturing of tantalum, its elastic modulus (approximately 186 GPa) 
remains substantially higher than that of human cancellous and cortical 
bone. This mechanical incompatibility can induce a pronounced stress- 
shielding effect, whereby the implant bears the predominant mechanical 
load, consequently leading to resorption and eventual loosening of the 
adjacent bone tissue due to inadequate stress stimulation. Furthermore, 
solid, non-porous configurations inherently obstruct the infiltration and 
proliferation of new bone tissue and vascular networks, thus compro -
mising biological fixation. In response to these limitations, the strategic 
design and fabrication of porous tantalum architectures have surfaced as 
a critical research frontier in orthopaedic implantology. The integration 
of meticulously engineered porous networks not only facilitates a sig -
nificant reduction in the apparent elastic modulus of the construct to 
better approximate that of the native bone but also establishes a three- 
dimensionally interconnected microenvironment that is highly condu -
cive to osseointegration [ 83 , 84 ].
In pioneering studies establishing the viability of additively manu -
factured porous tantalum for biomedical implants, a prevalent approach 
has been the replication of well-characterized structures previously 
validated through conventional fabrication techniques. Yang et al. [ 85 ] 
employed LPBF to fabricate biomimetic trabecular porous tantalum 
scaffolds and conducted a comparative analysis with trabecular 
tantalum produced by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) (exemplified by 
Zimmer Trabecular Metal ™ ). Their findings confirmed that LPBF is 
capable of producing trabecular architectures possessing comparable 
porosity (70.1 %) and interconnectivity (99.99 %) to their 
CVD-fabricated counterparts, whilst simultaneously achieving superior 
pore sizes and strut diameters. The mechanical properties of these 
LPBF-manufactured scaffolds were identified to be intermediate be -
tween those of cancellous and cortical bone, underscoring their 
considerable promise as bone repair materials.
Beyond biomimetic trabecular designs, other periodic porous archi -
tectures based on strut-like elements have garnered significant research 
interest [ 86 ]. The design of such porous structures necessitates the 
simultaneous consideration of three principal factors: porosity, pore 
size, and unit cell topology. Guo et al. [ 87 ] fabricated porous tantalum 
scaffolds with dodecahedral unit cells ( Fig. 12 (a)) and systematically 
modulated their porosity from 69.0 % to 77.8 % by precise adjustment of 
the electron beam current within the EB-PBF process. This deliberate 
reduction in porosity was correlated with a concomitant increase in 
compressive yield strength and elastic modulus, and the relationship 
between elastic modulus and relative density was found to adhere to the 
Gibson-Ashby model. In a separate study, Gao et al. [ 88 ] utilized LPBF to 
fabricate cubic-lattice Ta scaffolds ( Fig. 12 (b)) with porosities ranging 
from 65 % to 85 % and pore sizes of 362 – 703 μ m. A decrease in porosity 
was associated with an increase in compressive strength from 17.6 ±
0.5 MPa to 40.5 ± 0.8 MPa and elastic modulus from 0.7 ± 0.2 GPa to 
1.5 ± 0.6 GPa. Finite element analysis (FEA) identified stress concen -
tration on vertical struts oriented at 45
◦
, indicating that strut diameter is 
a critical parameter for enhancing load-bearing capacity. Li et al. [ 89 ] 
investigated porous tantalum structures based on body-centered cubic 
(BCC) and its deformed variant (BCC-D) unit cells ( Fig. 12 (c)), fabricated 
via LPBF. Their work revealed that the BCC structure displayed nearly 
isotropic mechanical properties, whereas the BCC-D topology exhibited 
significant anisotropy, demonstrating a 44.1 % and 42.2 % higher elastic 
modulus and yield strength, respectively, along the building direction 
compared to the BCC structure. Finite element analysis attributed a 
transition in the compressive failure mode of the BCC-D structure to 
pronounced stress concentration at its strut nodes.
To mitigate the mechanical degradation stemming from stress con -
centration, researchers have turned to porous architectures based on 
triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) unit cell designs. Ni et al. [ 69 ] 
demonstrated that at equivalent porosity levels, the TPMS structure 
( Fig. 12 (d)) exhibited a compressive strength exceeding that of bench -
mark structures by over 30 % and an elastic modulus higher by over 95 
%. Crucially, the TPMS scaffolds withstood 10
6 
fatigue cycles without 
failure, a performance in stark contrast to rhombic dodecahedron 
(RDOD) scaffolds, which fractured under fatigue testing. Finite element 
analysis ascribed this superior performance to the seamless, continuous 
shell architecture characteristic of TPMS designs, which promotes a 
more uniform distribution of stress and effectively eliminates the stress 
concentrations inherently present at the strut junctions of conventional 
strut-based RDOD designs.
3.4. Clinical applications of 3D-Printed porous tantalum in orthopedic 
implants
Additively manufactured porous tantalum has recently been intro -
duced into orthopaedic practice to address bone defects and complex 
reconstructions across multiple joints. While conventionally manufac -
tured trabecular Ta (produced via CVD on vitreous carbon) has set the 
gold standard for osteointegration, it is generally limited to standard 
geometric shapes or uniform porosity. In contrast, additive 
manufacturing enables patient specific geometries and functionally 
controlled porous architectures to be implemented in both load bearing 
and small bone applications. In the following subsections, we summarize 
reported uses of AM porous Ta implants in the hip, knee, wrist, proximal 
radius and femur, highlighting where AM specifically overcomes the 
geometric and structural limitations of conventional implants.

where its trabecular like architecture and bone matched stiffness have 
translated into good clinical performance. Conventionally manufactured 
porous Ta acetabular components and augments have demonstrated 
excellent long-term fixation and osseointegration in primary and revi -
sion total hip arthroplasty (THA), especially in compromised bone stock 
and complex acetabular defects [ 90 ]. These successes provide a ratio -
nale for using additive manufacturing (AM) to further exploit the

Ta augments and custom prostheses in extreme cases. In Charcot neu -
roarthropathy of the knee, large 3D printed porous Ta augments have 
been used to fill massive segmental defects and support constrained TKA 
constructs, achieving restoration of alignment and stability, pain relief 
and early radiographic signs of biological fixation [ 95 ]. The precise 
anatomical matching enabled by AM was critical in these cases to restore 
joint line alignment without relying on modular combinations of stan -

been implanted after one stage debridement to replace deficient tibial or 
femoral segments, with early follow up suggesting infection control, 
functional improvement and stable fixation [ 96 ]. Together, these re -

useful options for managing extensive bone loss and complex revision 
TKA [ 94–96 ].

impossible to machine from bulk porous Ta without compromising 
structural integrity. Based on CT derived three dimensional re -

patient specific porous Ta implants are shaped to reproduce native bone 
geometry and articular contours, and are manufactured with a highly 
porous trabecular like architecture. Early reports describe stable fixa -

with radiographic evidence of osseointegration and no implant migra -

stem and neck interface promote biological fixation. The one-piece

the interface weakness risks of modular assemblies. Case level follow up

stable implant positioning with bone ingrowth around the porous re -

scaffolds have been combined with Masquelet's induced membrane 
technique for reconstruction of infective segmental femoral defects [ 99 ].

placement, a patient specific Ta scaffold is implanted within the induced 
membrane and stabilized with internal fixation. The scaffold is designed 
from CT data to match the defect and femoral canal and incorporates an 
interconnected porous architecture with approximately 70 % porosity 
and ~350 μ m pore size to balance strength and osteoconductivity. 
Clinical outcomes have demonstrated infection control, progressive 
bone ingrowth into the porous Ta and segmental union with acceptable 
alignment and function [ 99 ]. Together, these examples underscore the 
unique versatility of AM porous Ta for reconstructing small carpal and 
radial bones as well as large infected femoral defects [ 97–99 ].

the reconstructive paradigm from “fitting the bone to the implant” to 
“fitting the implant to the bone”. Evidence, although mostly limited to 
case reports and small series, consistently suggests stable fixation, 
functional improvement and radiographic signs of osseointegration in 
hip, knee and non-weight bearing sites. These convergent findings

4. Additive manufacturing of Ta alloys
Although additively manufactured pure Ta exhibits excellent 
biocompatibility and corrosion resistance, its mechanical performance 
still shows inherent limitations, such as relatively high density, elastic 
modulus mismatch with host bone, and suboptimal mechanical strength 
in demanding load-bearing scenarios. These constraints highlight the 
necessity of alloying Ta to further tailor its mechanical behavior, 
microstructural stability and biological response for specific clinical 
indications. In recent years, several Ta-based alloy systems have been 
developed for additive manufacturing, among which Ta – W, Ta – Nb and 
Ta – Ti alloys are the most representative. The following subsections 
therefore focus on these three alloy systems, summarizing their pro -
cessing characteristics, microstructure evolution, mechanical properties 
and biological performance in the context of additive manufacturing.
4.1. Ta – W alloys
Tantalum has emerged as a potential candidate material for high- 
temperature components in various applications including rocket noz -
zles, combustion chambers, gas turbines, and nuclear reactors. However, 
the widespread application is constrained by its intrinsically low me -
chanical strength [ 100 ]. According to the first principle analysis [ 101 ], 
W atoms dispersed in Ta lattice suppress vacancy clustering by 
enhancing repulsive W-vacancy interactions, which delays vacancy 
nucleation and inhibits void formation. The addition of W to Ta forms a 
substitutional solid solution that significantly enhances the alloy's me -
chanical properties at room and elevated temperature [ 102 – 104 ]. Zhang 
et al. [ 105 ] demonstrated through both theoretical predictions and 
experimental tests that the compressive strength of Ta – W alloys at both 
room temperature and elevated temperatures is significantly enhanced 
by an increase in W content ( Fig. 15 ). However, the increase in strength 
becomes relatively limited when the W content surpasses 20 %. Notably, 
the Ta – 20W alloy exhibits a compressive yield strength exceeding 200 
MPa at 2000 
◦
C, along with good compressive plasticity at room tem -
perature and formability. Simultaneously, Ta – W alloys exhibit advan -
tageous characteristics including excellent corrosion resistance, superior 
wear resistance, and low evaporation rates at elevated temperatures, 
making them particularly suitable for aerospace and military applica -
tions [ 106 , 107 ].
To date, research on the additive manufacturing of Ta – W alloys re -
mains relatively limited, and the attentions are mainly focused on 
optimizing the laser processing window and mechanical properties. Fan

4.2. Ta – Nb alloys
Owing to the exceptional biocompatibility exhibited by both ele -
ments, Ta – Nb alloys are widely employed in the field of biomedical 
implants. Although research on the additive manufacturing of Ta – Nb 
alloys remains limited to date, studies exploring the in vitro and in vivo 
biocompatibility of porous Ta – Nb alloy materials have been reported in 
the literature [ 9 , 111 ]. In the study carried out by Miao et al. [ 111 ], 
Ta – 10 %Nb alloy scaffolds with high porosity (68.0 % ± 0.41 %) and 
three-dimensionally interconnected pore structures were fabricated 
using a sponge impregnation and powder metallurgy sintering process. 
Subsequent cell culture experiments with the rat MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic 
cells demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, with significantly 
enhanced cell attachment and proliferation. The material's porous ar -
chitecture, secondary porosity, and rough surface topography collec -
tively increased the specific surface area, thereby promoting protein 
adsorption and cellular adhesion. Another publication [ 9 ] from the same 
research group investigated the influence of Nb content on the me -
chanical properties and biocompatibility of Ta – Nb alloys (as shown in 
Fig. 16 ). Their results indicated that porous Ta – 15Nb alloys exhibited 
superior in vitro cytocompatibility and osteogenic activity, along with 
higher pull-out force compared to other compositions.

unmelted Ta particles, which significantly contributed to the stable 
mechanical performance.

feedstock selection. A detailed examination of the literature summarized

elemental powder printing route are prone to microstructural in -

tantalum particles and pronounced elemental segregation, making them 
suitable only for experimental screening. In contrast, pre-alloyed pow -

for reliable, scalable manufacturing. Therefore, only data from pre- 
alloyed or fully homogenized samples should be considered represen -
tative of the alloy's intrinsic performance for critical load-bearing 
applications.

LPBF-fabricated Ta – Ti alloys exhibit significantly higher strength 
compared to their as-cast counterparts, primarily attributed to grain 
refinement in the LPBF-processed samples.
In the pursuit of durable orthopedic solutions, the synergy between 
mechanical resilience and direct osseointegration remains a primary 
objective. A recent study by Liu et al. [ 124 ] successfully formulated a 
fatigue-resistant Ti-50 wt%Ta alloy via LPBF, effectively bridging the 
gap between the lightweight nature of Ti and the superior bioactivity of 
Ta. It was demonstrated that by optimizing laser parameters to eliminate 
unmelted Ta defects, the alloy not only achieved a fatigue life signifi -
cantly surpassing that of pure Ti but also facilitated superior cell 
attachment through its unique process-induced surface topography 
( Fig. 19 ).
Beyond mechanical properties and direct structural integration, the 
biological response to Ta – Ti alloys has also garnered increasing atten -
tion. Recent pioneering work has shifted focus from simple osseointe -
gration to materials-guided tissue engineering, highlighting the 
influence of the Ta – Ti oxide interface on the immune microenviron -
ment. It was reported that the optimized Ta/Ti ratio promotes the po -
larization of macrophages towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype 
via the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, thereby creating a favorable 
osteogenic immune microenvironment that accelerates bone regenera -
tion [ 118 ], the mechanism of which is illustrated in Fig. 20 . Conse -
quently, the next generation of Ta – Ti bone implants will likely rely on 
not only utilizing porous topologies for mechanical compatibility and 
tissue ingrowth, but also leveraging the alloy's intrinsic surface chem -
istry to regulate immune responses for enhanced healing.
However, establishing these biological advantages as robust design 
arguments requires rigorous experimental validation. Specifically, since 
the enhanced cell attachment in Fig. 19 is partially attributed to process- 
induced topography [ 124 ], future studies should employ surface treat -
ment controls to decouple the biochemical contribution of Ta from 
physical roughness effects. Similarly, the immunomodulatory mecha -
nism shown in Fig. 20 relies on a specific surface oxide composition 
[ 118 ], therefore, its long-term stability and ion release behavior must be 
verified under dynamic physiological conditions to ensure the claimed 
osteogenic immune microenvironment persists in vivo .

and functional applications. While the feasibility of fabricating high-

To elevate the field beyond current capabilities, future research must

(1) Feedstock Supply Chain (Priority: High): The high cost and 
scarcity of spherical tantalum powder are the primary barriers to 
entry.
Milestone (1 – 3 years): Develop scalable plasma spheroidization or 
electrode induction melting gas atomization (EIGA) routes specifically 
for refractory metals to produce pre-alloyed Ta-based powders with 
oxygen content < 0.05 wt% and achieve a cost reduction of at least 30 % 
compared to current market prices. 
(2) Powder Reuse and Quality Control (Priority: High): There is a 
lack of data regarding how repeated recycling affects the oxygen 
pickup and printability of tantalum, which is highly sensitive to 
oxidation.
Milestone (1 – 2 years): Establish quantitative powder degradation 
models that correlate chemical changes (oxygen/nitrogen uptake) with 
flowability and final part mechanical properties, defining clear rejection 
thresholds for recycled powder. 
(3) In-Situ Monitoring and Intelligent Control (Priority: Me -
dium-High): Due to the high melting point and thermal con -
ductivity of tantalum, standard processing windows are narrow.

(5) Long-Term Biomedical Evidence (Priority: Long-Term):

feeding technology for ultrafine powders, enabling consistent 
feeding and effective dispersion of ultra-fine, low-density 
powders.

plasma, resulting in successful commercialization of research findings.

addressing powder suitability evaluation and clarifying the influence of powder charac -
teristics (e.g., particle size distribution, oxygen content) on the performance of laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) components.

performance in additively manufactured materials such as stainless steel, tantalum, and 
tungsten through hot isostatic pressing (HIP) and high-pressure heat treatment.
