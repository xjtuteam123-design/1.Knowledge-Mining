BRIEF COMMUNICATION
A Single Tensile Test for TRIP–TWIP Mechanisms
High-Throughput Mapping in a Compositionally
Graded Ti–Nb Alloy
CLE´MENCE FONTAINE, THOMAS CORRE, LOI ¨C PERRIE` RE,
HUGO P. VAN LANDEGHEM, DANIEL GALY, THOMAS BELFORTI,
PHILIPPE VERMAUT, FAN SUN, FRE ´DE´RIC PRIMA, and LOLA LILENSTEN
Compositional boundaries of transformation-induced plasticity and mechanical twinning
(TRIP/TWIP) activity in the Ti–Nb system are determined using a chemically graded sample
containing 18–30 at. pct Nb, prepared via diﬀusion couple. Deformation mechanisms triggered
by a single tensile test are analyzed by EBSD and EDX. TRIP activity is resolved in the range
22–25 at. pct Nb and TWIP activity in the range 23–28 at. pct Nb, showing the possibility to
chemically map deformation mechanisms with a single tensile test.

I. INTRODUCTION
TRANSFORMATION-INDUCED plasticity
(TRIP)/twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) Ti alloys
provide interesting mechanical properties such as supe-
rior toughness, strain hardening, and ductility. [1–4]
Triggering these speciﬁc deformation mechanisms
depends mainly on the metastability level of the b-phase,
inﬂuenced by the content of b stabilizing elements and
more generally by the composition of the
b-metastable alloy. [1] It also depends, for instance, on
the size and the orientation of the b-grains in the
microstructure,[5,6] the deformation temperature [7] and
the precipitation of the x-phase.[8] The use of predictive
empirical parameters, such as Mo eq
[9] or e/a,[10] or else
the use of a semi-empirical technique, the d-electron
alloy design,
[11] or even the use of ab initio calculation
methods[12,13] are existing solutions to guide the occur-
rence of TRIP/TWIP eﬀects. But these methods display
some limitations (only the inﬂuence of composition is
considered, for example) and rely on experimental
databases with sometimes incomplete information
regarding the full description of the samples.
[14] Hence,
more experimental work is required to increase the
accuracy of these methods and to validate or consolidate
the predictive results, ultimately allowing for easier and
more accurate alloy design.
The use of chemically graded material is a well-known
technique to accumulate quickly and eﬃciently an
important amount of experimental data by scanning a
wide range of compositions and can accelerate the
development of b-metastable Ti alloys.
[15] This has been
done in the Ti–Nb–Zr–O and Ti–Mo systems, where the
graded samples microstructure was analyzed by electron
microscopy, and their mechanical properties were ana-
lyzed by micro-indentation and resonant ultrasound
spectroscopy.
[16,17] A recent study by our group also
used micro-indentation and nano-indentation on a
10-mm-wide compositionally graded Ti–Nb prepared
by SPS, to identify not only the mechanical properties
but also the deformation mechanisms, and more specif-
ically the compositional ranges of TRIP/TWIP eﬀects
triggered locally using microhardness.
[18] However, this
eﬀective method may have limitations in terms of
accuracy of results and applicability to smaller compo-
sition gradients, as it is limited by the size of the
microhardness tip which locally deforms a certain
composition of the graded sample. Besides, it requires
individual analysis of many indents, leading to extensive
experimental investigation. In the present study, tensile
loading is applied to deform a graded sample in the
millimeter range to trigger the deformation mechanisms,
proposing a direct and quicker deformation approach

In this paper, the objective is to provide a ‘‘sin-
gle-shot’’ overview of the local deformation mechanisms
by co-deformation of a microstructure spread on a large
range of chemical compositions. The tensile direction is
applied perpendicular to the gradient direction leading
to isostrain loading of all compositions, to trigger the
various deformation mechanisms and observe their
compositional limits. To this end, a chemically graded
Ti–Nb is studied, prepared by diﬀusion couple, a
technique previously successfully applied.
[21] A large
range of compositions from 18 to 30 at. pct Nb is
selected, encompassing all the deformation mechanisms
available in the Ti–Nb system according to the literature
and, speciﬁcally, the TRIP eﬀect between 22 and 25.5
at. pct, and the TWIP eﬀect between 22 and 28
at. pct.
[22–30]
The Ti–Nb-graded sample was prepared from
Ti–18Nb and Ti–30Nb alloys (at. pct). The initial
buttons were obtained by arc melting, starting from
pure metals (Nb 99.95 pct, Ti plates containing below
250 ppm Fe and below 600 ppm oxygen), melted 5 times
to ensure chemical homogeneity, and then cut with
parallel faces to form cubes of 1 cm
2 of section each and
height between 0.5 and 1 cm. The cubes were assembled
after mirror polishing the faces in contact and wrapped
in a Ta foil. The solid-state bonding step was carried out
in a creep test machine at 950 /C176 C, under an Ar–2 pct H
2
ﬂow with a pressure ﬁxed between 3 and 5 mbar. During
the 1-hour isothermal hold, a constant compressive load
for a pressure of about 10 MPa was applied using a
weight system. After cooling, the two cubes were
perfectly welded. The proper diﬀusion treatment was
performed under dynamic vacuum (5 9 10
/C05 mbar).
The sample was again wrapped in a Ta foil and heat
treated at 1500 /C176 C for 24 hours, with heating and
cooling ramps of 7 /C176 C/min. Additional thermal treat-
ment in the b-domain at 800 /C176 C for 15 min under
vacuum was subsequently performed, followed by water
quenching. The sample was then sliced and cold-rolled
with 85 pct thickness reduction, with the rolling direc-
tion (RD) perpendicular to the direction of the compo-
sition gradient. The recrystallization treatment was
performed at 1100 /C176 C for 30 min under He ﬂow. The
sample wrapped in Ta foil was in a glass tube directly
inserted in the hot furnace. The sample was then
air-quenched by removing the glass tube from the
furnace, without breaking the vacuum inside the tube.
The sample was lastly put for 90 seconds in a salt bath
at 800 /C176 C and then water-quenched to ensure a
quenched b-metastable state for subsequent character-
ization. The thermomechanical process after the
solid-state bonding step is summarized in Figure 1(a).
All thermal treatments are done above the b-transus
temperature that being comprised between about 590 /C176 C
for Ti–30 Nb and 650 /C176 C for Ti–18Nb.
The tensile sample with dimensions provided in
Figure 1(b) was ground using SiC papers and cut using
a high-pressure water jet cutter. The tests were per-
formed with an INSTRON 5584 tensile machine
equipped with a 10-kN load cell and with an imposed
displacement speed of 0.2 mm/min. A random speckle is
sprayed on the specimen in order to measure the
deformation ﬁeld with Digital Image Correlation
(DIC), as shown in Figure 1(c). The grayscale images
are taken every two seconds using a 29 Mpix camera,
giving a physical pixel size of around 12 microns. The
DIC is performed with the open source software
Ufreckels, using a ﬁnite element formulation with
bi-linear Q4 elements.
[31] The force–displacement curve
was recorded, and the test was manually stopped once a
suﬃcient plastic deformation regime was reached.
Sample preparation for the chemical and microstruc-
tural characterizations included mechanical grinding
with SiC papers and OPS solution for pre-deformation
analysis and additional electropolishing at 11 /C176 C with a
solution of 2-butoxyethanol, methanol, perchloric acid,
and hydrochloric acid for post-deformation. The com-
position gradient was quantitatively assessed by
Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) analyses in a
Zeiss ﬁeld emission gun Scanning Electron Microscopy
(SEM) at 15 kV. The PhiRhoZ model and pure Nb and
Ti standards were used. The oxygen content in the whole
sample was evaluated by an elemental analyzer for the
last stage (after b solution treatment at 800 /C176 C for
1.5 minutes). The amount of oxygen in the whole ﬁnal
material is 800 ppm (mass pct). The microstructure of
the sample was characterized before and after the tensile
test with Zeiss Merlin SEM operated at 20 kV and with
a 9-nA probe current, using a Bruker eFlash HD
electron back-scatter diﬀraction (EBSD) system. Data
were processed with OIM software. The microstructure
was also observed using ForeScatter Detectors (FSD)
imaging with Zeiss Merlin SEM with a 0.02- lm step
size.

The graded tensile specimen is then deformed by
tensile test, with tensile direction parallel to the RD [see
Figure 1(c)], corresponding to a parallel setup approach.
The force–displacement curve is plotted in Figure 3(a).
A non-linear behavior is observed, with two successive
slope breaks. It can be related to the graded nature of
the sample, as the yield strength and the elastic behavior
of an alloy is drastically dependent on the chemical
composition. A clear change in mechanical behavior is
observed after a displacement of 0.4 mm, suggesting
that all compositions are deforming plastically. The
image correlation analysis of the ﬁnal state in
Figure 3(b) shows a relatively homogeneous
deformation, except a slight increase on the left side
(pure Ti–18Nb side). The force–strain curve is plotted
on Figure 3(c) for the speciﬁc area containing the
composition gradient (2.7 9 0.91 mm

of the curve suggests that all this region is plastically
deformed. As the macroscopic stress of the graded
sample cannot be obtained due to the parallel tensile
model (gradient orthogonal to the loading direction),
the stress–strain curve cannot be evaluated. This does
not prevent to study the active deformation mechanisms
which are the topic of interest in this study.
EBSD maps of the tensile specimen post-deformation

being horizontal in this ﬁgure. The composition evolves
from the bottom to the top of the ﬁgure on a vertical line
(along TD), but is constant along one horizontal line in
the sample (along RD, parallel to the tensile direction).
One can notice that the inter-graduation spacing for the
composition is not constant, as the gradient is not linear
[see Figure 2(a)]. The initial microstructural boundaries

identiﬁed in Figure 2, are reminded by the white-dashed

the TRIP eﬀect. The larger dimension of the stress-in-

with respect to the smaller size below the micrometer for
quenched a¢¢, allows to characterize stress-induced a¢¢
also below 24 at. pct, where quenched martensite is
present. This is exempliﬁed by a larger magniﬁcation

22 and 24 at. pct of Nb, quenched a¢¢ and stress-induced

TWIP eﬀect. Mechanical twins, easily identiﬁed, are
observed from 23 at. pct Nb to 28 at. pct Nb [green

This could be attributed to their Schmid factor; how-

transmission of plasticity from one grain to another,
leading to a dependence of a grain’s mechanical twin-

The results obtained by the tensile deformation and
characterization of the graded Ti–Nb sample corre-
spond to the literature data mentioned at the beginning.
The slight discrepancies with the TWIP eﬀect being
available from 22-at. pct Nb in the literature and the
TRIP eﬀect being available up to 25.5-at. pct Nb could
be attributed to the oxygen content of the graded
sample. By decreasing M
s and Mf, oxygen also has an
impact on the b-metastable phase composition and thus
on the TRIP and TWIP critical triggering stresses. [34–36]
Mostly, an increase in oxygen is known to stabilize the
b-phase with respect to the quenched a¢¢ martensite, and
thus, for a given composition, to shift the deformation
mechanism from TRIP to TRIP/TWIP to TWIP to
dislocation glide. Hence, the oxygen content of the
present sample can rationalize that the measured com-
positional limits observed for the deformation mecha-
nisms are slightly shifted toward lower Nb contents,
compared to oxygen-free samples. Moreover, as previ-
ously mentioned, TRIP and TWIP eﬀects reported in

The tensile deformation method therefore permits to
identify compositional zones of interest to target speciﬁc
deformation mechanisms and even allows to get a ﬁrst
insight on the inﬂuence of the orientation on the
deformation mechanism, on a 1.5 mm gradient. In
order to compare the results given by the two methods,
the microhardness method was also applied to the
diﬀusion couple Ti–Nb sample of the present article,
following the procedure performed in Reference 18,
using a 200-g microhardness load. No TWIP eﬀect was
detected around the indents, no matter the composition,
and the compositional domain displaying TRIP eﬀect
showed large uncertainties due to the indent size with
respect to the gradient size. Therefore, not only the
results for the tensile test are more accurate for the
micrometer scale sample, but also a unique tensile
deformation—‘‘single-shot’’ probing—is quicker than
several indentations along the gradient. By changing the
composition of the graded sample and expanding
toward more complex end-compositions, this technique
will allow to quickly characterize new systems. Only the
processing route [Figure 1(a)] will have to be adapted
from one system to another, taking into account their
characteristics (diﬀusion coeﬃcients, recrystallization
behavior, b-transus).
Overall, the tensile test applied to a graded sample,
combined with EBSD analysis is proven to be a quick
and eﬃcient way to characterize the compositional
ranges of TRIP and TWIP deformation mechanisms.
This unprecedented approach ensures reliable data,
especially considering that all compositions of the
sample undergo the same processing route. This fast
and easy method, successfully applied to the Ti–Nb
system as a proof of concept in the present study, could
easily be transferred to other Ti b-metastable systems,
allowing to collect more homogeneous data and better
rationalize the mechanical behavior and its prediction in
such systems.
