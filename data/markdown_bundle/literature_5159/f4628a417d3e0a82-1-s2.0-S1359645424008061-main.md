ABSTRACT
Refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) have emerged as a promising class of structural materials, 
demonstrating exceptional mechanical performance in aggressive environments. However, the complex atomic 
environments, significant lattice distortion, and vast compositional space of RCCAs present challenges to un -
derstanding the mechanisms that govern structure-property relationships. In this study, we explore the dislo -
cation mechanisms in three model quaternary RCCAs, namely Mo25Nb10Ta25W40 (at. %), 
Mo25Nb25Ta25W25, and Mo25Nb40Ta25W10 using large-scale atomistic simulations and machine learning 
based Spectral Neighbor Analysis Potential. Our atomistic simulations examine how the chemical composition 
and local ordering influence the mobility of both edge and screw dislocations, and how lattice distortion and 
diffuse anti-phase boundary energy (DAPBE) affect dislocation behaviors during nanostructural evolution. 
Notably, with the increase in Nb concentration in the model RCCAs, both DAPBE and lattice distortion are 
simultaneously enhanced as the chemical short-range order (CSRO) evolves into nanoscale B2 precipitates. This 
evolution results in high lattice distortion due to the lattice mismatch between B2 precipitates and the random 
matrix. Consequently, B2 nanoprecipitates provide a stronger pinning effect, hindering edge dislocation motion 
while promoting cross-slip of screw dislocations, leading to a reduced screw-to-edge ratio in slip resistance and 
mobility discrepancy. These findings offer valuable insights into dislocation behaviors and interactions with 
ordered precipitates, highlighting the importance of exploring non-equiatomic compositions and advancing 
beyond CSRO in RCCAs. This study has implications for optimizing alloy compositions and processing methods 
for superior performance in aggressive environments.
1. Introduction
In pursuit of advanced materials capable of withstanding extreme 
conditions, alloy innovation has become imperative to meet the esca -
lating demands of the energy, transportation, and aerospace sectors. 
Among these breakthrough materials, refractory complex concentrated 
alloys (RCCAs) with a body-centered-cubic (BCC) structure have 
demonstrated exceptional mechanical properties at elevated tempera -
tures, outperforming traditional superalloys [ 1-3 ]. For instance, equia -
tomic MoNbTaW RCCAs can maintain an impressive yield strength of 
400 MPa at temperatures exceeding 1200 
◦
C [ 4 , 5 ]. Recent developments 
in equiatomic RCCAs have led to noticeable property enhancements 
[ 6-8 ], such as increased high-temperature strength and specific strength 
of 140 MPa/(g/cm
3

◦

improved room-temperature ductility in HfNbTiV [ 10 ]. Additionally, 
complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) offer a vast compositional space, 
characterized by chemical fluctuations [ 11 ], non-equiatomic concen -
trations [ 12-14 ], and chemical short-range ordering (CSRO) [ 15-19 ], 
making them a largely unexplored frontier in alloy development for 
harsh environments.

RCCAs exhibit distinct deformation behaviors compared to simple 
and dilute BCC metals due to their chemical complexity. In traditional 
BCC metals, edge dislocations typically display much higher mobility 
than screw dislocations. Consequently, the dominant deformation 
mechanism in BCC metals involves thermally activated kink-pair 
nucleation of screw dislocations [ 20 , 21 ]. However, the complex 
atomic environment induced by multiple principal components in 
RCCAs fundamentally alters the energy landscape on which dislocation 
moves, thus changing dislocation behaviors [ 22-24 ]. For screw dislo -
cations, such complex atomic environments increase the concentration 
of equilibrium kinks on screw dislocation lines, potentially reducing the 
importance of kink-pair nucleation observed in pure/dilute BCC metals. 
As a result, the mechanical behaviors could be governed by kink 
migration, rather than kink-pair nucleation, across a wide range of 
temperatures [ 24-26 ]. For edge dislocations, the mobility may become 
comparable to that of screw dislocations due to chemical complexity, a 
factor that likely plays a significant role in RCCA mechanical behaviors 
[ 27 , 28 ]. For example, Chen and Xu et al. identified a new deformation 
mechanism termed nanoscale segment detrapping in edge dislocations, 
attributing it to local inhomogeneities and reducing the mobility gap 
between edge and screw dislocations [ 27 , 29 ]. Lee, Kubilay, and Maresca 
et al. proposed that the strength of RCCAs is primarily influenced by 
edge dislocations, supporting their assertion with experimental evidence 
[ 22 , 30 , 31 ]. They also developed an edge-dislocation-based model to 
predict RCCA strength. Therefore, a deeper understanding of edge and 
screw dislocation behaviors is hypothesized to be crucial in BCC RCCAs 
to optimize their mechanical behaviors.
The strengthening mechanisms in RCCAs, which impede dislocation 
motion, can involve two key factors: promoting lattice distortion [ 32 ] 
and increasing diffuse anti-phase boundary energy (DAPBE) [ 33 , 34 ]. 
The random field of solutes in these high-concentration alloys induces 
significant lattice distortion and atomic strains, which interact with the 
dislocation strain field and act as obstacles to dislocation motion [ 32 ,
35 ]. On the other hand, the interaction among constituent elements 
leads to the emergence of local chemical ordering, e.g., CSRO. When 
dislocations encounter CSRO, the creation of DAPBE demands addi -
tional strength. However, a challenge arises as increasing DAPBE 
through the formation of CSRO often accompanies a reduction in lattice 
distortion in CCAs [ 15 , 16 , 36 , 37 ]. The redistribution of chemical species 
into more compact configurations reduces the local elastic fluctuation. 
The conflicting impacts of DAPBE and lattice distortion have been 
observed in both FCC and BCC CCAs. For instance, Yin et al. discovered 
that the CSRO in the equiatomic MoNbTaW diminishes the resistance to 
edge dislocation while concurrently bolstering resistance against screw 
dislocation [ 36 ]. Antillon et al. found that the formation of CSRO led to a 
decrease in lattice distortion, resulting in a moderate strengthening ef -
fect on edge dislocations and a prominent strengthening effect on screw 
dislocation [ 16 ]. Therefore, a salient question arises: Can we strategi -
cally tune the composition and ordering state of RCCAs to achieve both 
high DAPBE and substantial lattice distortion? This strategy could 
enhance resistance to edge dislocation motion and potentially reduce 
the screw-to-edge ratio in slip resistance.
In this study, we investigate the behaviors of screw and edge dislo -
cations in three model quaternary RCCAs, namely Mo25Nb10Ta25W40 
(at. %, Nb10), Mo25Nb25Ta25W25 (at. %, Nb25), and Mo25N -
b40Ta25W10 (at. %, Nb40) at room temperature using large-scale 
atomistic simulations. These RCCAs are represented using a machine 
learning-based Spectral Neighbor Analysis Potential (SNAP). Chemically 
random cells are annealed employing a hybrid Molecular dynamics 
(MD)/Monte Carlo (MC) approach to examine the evolution of atomic 
environment and nanostructure. Additionally, atomistic simulations of 
dislocation motion are conducted to study the impact of chemical 
composition and local ordering on the behaviors of both edge and screw 
dislocations. We delve into the underlying mechanisms, specifically how 
lattice distortion and DAPBE affect these dislocation behaviors due to 
the evolution of nanostructure. We identify a unique case in the Nb40 
model alloy, where the DAPBE and lattice distortion are simultaneously 
enhanced as the CSRO evolves into nanoscale B2 precipitates. This 
evolution leads to high lattice distortion due to the lattice misfit between 
the B2 precipitate and the random matrix. Consequently, the B2 nano -
precipitates offer a stronger pinning effect for edge dislocation move -
ment while promoting the cross-slip of screw dislocations, which 
reduces the screw-to-edge ratio in slip resistance.
2. Methodologies
2.1. Machine learning potential
A neural network regression of Spectral Neighbor Analysis Potential 
(NN-SNAP), developed for the MoNbTaW alloy system [ 38 ], has been 
utilized to conduct the atomistic simulations in this study using 
LAMMPS software. Specifically, SNAP was employed to characterize 
local atomic environments. With a band limit (2jmax) set to 6 and 30 
bispectrum components per SNAP descriptor, a two-hidden-layer arti -
ficial neural network with 30 neurons in each layer was trained to map 
the SNAP descriptors to energy, force, and stress. The training dataset 
comprised 5564 atomic structures, including 5529 configurations from a 
previous study [ 39 ], augmented with data for large elastic strains. The 
model demonstrated strong performance on the training dataset, 
achieving mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 3.76 meV/atom in energy, 0.1 
eV/Å in force, and 0.537 GPa in stress. In validation datasets, MAEs were 
9.039 meV/atom in energy, 0.117 eV/Å in force, and 0.641 GPa in 
stress, respectively. The entire training process has been implemented 
using the Python package PyXtal_FF [ 40 ], and the model was integrated 
into the ML-IAP package within the LAMMPS software [ 41 ].
We have compared the generalized stacking fault energies (GSFEs) in 
the {110}, {112} and {123} planes along the 〈 111 〉 direction between 
our NN-SNAP potential and DFT calculations for the four unary alloys, 
three random quaternary RCCAs and the MoTa B2 phase. The supercells 
used for computing the GSFEs with NN-SNAP contained 81,000 atoms 
for the {110} and {112} planes, and 81,900 atoms for the {123} plane. 
The supercell was divided into two halves along z-axis, with the lower 
half remaining fixed and the upper half incrementally displaced in the 
〈 111 〉 direction. The GSFE was determined by calculating the energy 
difference in the relaxed structures per unit area of the slip plane before 
and after the displacement. When the displacement equals one Burgers 
vector on the {110} plane, the GSFEs are identified as the DAPBEs. The 
simulations were conducted at 0 K, and to account for local chemical 
variations, the GSFEs were calculated ten times for the random qua -
ternary alloys. Fig. 1 (a-c) demonstrate that the NN-SNAP potential is in 
good agreement with DFT energy calculations for the pure element 
constituents, multicomponent alloys and the ordered MoTa B2 phase. 
Additionally, in the random solid solutions, NN-SNAP predicted a stable 
symmetric screw dislocation core along the entire dislocation line length 
for Nb10 ( Fig. 1 (d)), Nb25 ( Fig. 1 (e)) and Nb40 ( Fig. 1 (f)). And the 
thermodynamics benchmarks of the NN-SNAP on the formation en -
thalpies of various binary ordered phases in the MoNbTaW RCCA system 
are shown in Fig. 2 . A good agreement between NN-SNAP and DFT 
calculations is observed. These findings support the use of NN-SNAP 
potentials for investigating microstructural evolution, defect ener -
getics and dislocation mechanisms in the model RCCAs. The details of 
the DFT calculations can be found in the supplementary materials.

layers of atoms in the y direction (denoted as the shear region in Fig. 3 ),

20 MPa per 2 ps. The bottom two layers of atoms in the y direction (a 
fixed region in Fig. 3 ) were fixed, and the shear deformation was carried 
out under the NVT ensemble. The shear stress was held for 50 ps after 
reaching the target value, while the dislocation positions were recorded.

Note that we have developed a Python package, SROTuner, to tune 
the atomic configurations based on WCPs to obtain large samples for 
dislocation simulations. The SROTuner adjusts the local atomic config -
uration within a given sample to match the specified WCPs. During 
tuning, it randomly swaps two atoms and computes the WCPs after each 
swap. If the WCPs after the swap are closer to the target WCPs, SRO -
Tuner accepts the swap. This process continues until the WCPs of the 
sample match the input WCPs. The SROTuner takes around 4 h on a local 
computer to generate the large samples (~500, 000 atoms) with similar 
WCPs to the small samples (e.g., 8192 atoms) subjected to 1 M MC steps. 
In contrast, generating these large samples using hybrid MD/MC simu -
lations may take weeks even on a supercomputer with 64 cores. 
Therefore, applying the SROTuner could significantly reduce the 
computational cost for generating large samples with ordered structures 
for MD simulations. The SROTuner codes are available on our GitHub. 
To validate the similarities between the samples generated by the 
SROTuner and those by MD/MC, we examine the differences in WCPs, 
potential energy after minimization and radial distribution functions of 
each pair between the samples. The results, which can be found in 
supplementary Fig. S7-S11, confirm that the samples generated by the 
SROTuner show negligible differences in WCPs, potential energy and

3.1. Atomic configurations and nanostructures of model RCCAs
Fig. 4 (a-c) displays the atomic configurations of the three model 
RCCAs upon the local ordering process after 1 M MC steps. Two key 
observations arise from these nanostructures: the formation of Mo-Ta 
pairs, and the emergence of Mo-Ta B2 nanoclusters in the Nb25 and 
Nb40 alloys. To facilitate a quantitative comparison of the nano -
structures across different samples, we calculate the WCPs and the 
number of B2 units. A B2 unit refers to a locally ordered Mo-Ta BCC 
structure, where 8 Ta atoms surround a central Mo atom, and vice versa. 
Fig. 4 (d) illustrates the WCPs of the Mo-Ta pair at different MC steps. 
Across all three alloys, the WCPs of Mo-Ta pairs consistently decrease as 
the MC steps increase, with the Nb40_1M sample having the largest 
absolute value. Correspondingly, the number of B2 units ( Fig. 4 (e)) in -
creases with the advancement of MC steps, with Nb40_1M having the 
highest count. Given that the concentrations of Mo and Ta remain the 
same in all three alloys, the increase in Nb concentration promotes the 
formation of CSRO and subsequently gives rise to the appearance of the 
MoTa B2 nanoclusters. The WCPs for all pairs can be found in Fig. S13 
and S14 of the supplementary material. Moreover, we also examined the

3.3. Edge dislocation mobility
Fig. 6 presents the velocities of edge dislocations as a function of the 
applied shear stresses for three different degrees of ordering in (a) Nb10, 
(b) Nb25, and (c) Nb40, respectively. The results indicate that the local 
ordering exhibits minimal impact on edge dislocation mobility in the 
Nb10 and Nb25 alloys, as the velocity curves for the three ordering 
states of each alloy nearly overlap. However, in the Nb40 alloys, the 
edge dislocation velocities decrease with increased local ordering. This 
effect is particularly notable in Nb40_1M, where a ~250 MPa increase in 
shear stress is required compared to a random state, as evidenced by the 
shift of the velocity curve.
The trend in edge dislocation mobility can be attributed to the var -
iations in DAPBE and lattice distortion, as shown in Fig. 5 . For Nb10 and 
Nb25, local ordering leads to opposite trends in DAPBE and lattice 
distortion, which counteract each other, thereby diminishing the 
strengthening effect, and resulting in minimal change in edge disloca -
tion mobility. A similar opposite trend is also observed in Nb40 during 
the initial stage of ordering. However, in Nb40_1M, where both high 
DAPBE and lattice distortion occur simultaneously, a pronounced 
strengthening effect is observed, resulting in a significant decrease in 
edge dislocation mobility ( Fig. 6 (c)).
Fig. 7 illustrates the mechanism of the edge dislocation movement at 
various stress levels in Nb40_1M alloys. Due to the rugged nature of the 
atomic potential energy environment, local dislocation lines tend to 
curve to fit into sites with lower energy, resulting in an initially tortuous 
morphology, as shown in Fig. 7 (a). When the applied shear stress is 
small (e.g., 610 MPa), as shown in Fig. 7 (b), the tortuous dislocation 
barely moves, remaining pinned by low energy configurations along the 
line, resulting in a dislocation velocity of zero. As the applied shear 
stress increases to 700 MPa, the edge dislocation begins to move. As 
illustrated in Fig. 7 (c), the central segment of the dislocation line bows 
out, gradually detrapping from the low-energy configurations. This 
mode of edge dislocation movement, named “ nanoscale segment 
detrapping ” , has also been observed in Co16.67Fe36.67Ni16.67Ti30 
CCAs [ 27 ]. Due to the nanoscale inhomogeneities in RCCAs, some seg -
ments of the dislocation line initially situated at sites with higher energy 
are favorable to overcome the local energy barrier, leading to the 
nanoscale segment detrapping. With a further increase in shear stress to 
870 MPa, the edge dislocation glides nearly as a straight line, as

The key mechanisms of screw dislocation motions are summarized in 
Fig. 9 , using the Nb40_random alloy used as an example. Firstly, at a 
relatively lower stress level (~ 1220 MPa), the screw dislocation moves 
via kink-pair nucleation and propagation. Due to the complex atomic 
environments, the kink-pair can nucleate on both the maximum resolve 
shear stress plane (MRSSP) and non-MRSSP, leading to the formation of 
the cross-kinks, as shown in Fig. 9 (a). Secondly, increasing the shear 
stress to 1390 MPa allows the cross-kink to be broken by forming va -
cancy and interstitial, refer to Fig. 9 (b). This mechanism requires 
overcoming an extra energy barrier, such as vacancy formation energy 
or interstitial formation energy, to break the cross-kink. Further 
increasing the stress to 2180 MPa results in the cross-kink being broken 
by forming large debris, such as clusters of atoms with amorphous 
structures or dislocation loops, beyond the point defects, refer to Fig. 9
(c). It is also noted that the key screw dislocation mechanisms do not 
change significantly among the three model RCCAs, but decreasing Nb 
concentration increases the stress required to activate the three defor -
mation mechanisms. From the random to 20 K ordering state, the pri -
mary change is the increase in the kink-pair nucleation energy barrier, 
lowering the probability of nucleation. As a result, at a lower stress level 
(e.g., 1050 MPa in Nb40_20K alloy), the motion of the screw dislocation 
is dominated by the kink-pair nucleation, as shown in Fig. 9 (d). Changes 
in the energy barrier of kink-pair nucleation also affect the cross-slip 
behavior, which is discussed in the next paragraph.

along the x-axis (i.e., [121] direction in the MRSSP). At the end of the 
simulation (~ 50 ps), the dislocation core moves to (346.9 Å, 35.0 Å). A 
cross-slip ratio is calculated by the ratio of the displacement along the y- 
axis to the displacement along the x-axis, as shown in Fig. 10 (a).
Fig. 10 (b) summarizes the cross-slip ratio in all the model RCCAs. In 
both Nb10 and Nb25 alloys, the cross-slip ratio decreases with increased 
ordering (MC steps), a trend also noted in Wang et al. ’ s work [ 24 ]. This 
decrease can be attributed to an increase in the energy barrier for 
kink-pair nucleation as the material evolves from a random to an or -
dered state, reducing the likelihood of nucleation at non-MRSSP sites 
and thereby decreasing the cross-slip tendency. In the Nb40 alloys, the 
ratio decreases from the random state to the 20 K state but increases 
from the 20 K to the 1 M state. This increase may result from the for -
mation of MoTa B2 nanoclusters.
3.5. Edge and screw dislocation discrepancy
Fig. 11 shows the screw-to-edge ratio in critical resolved shear stress 
(CRSS) of the three model RCCAs at various ordering states. The CRSS is 
evaluated as the stress at which the velocity reaches 2 Å/ps for edge 
dislocations and 1 Å/ps for screw dislocations. When compared to pure 
BCC metals, where the CRSS ratio is ~ 9 for Nb and 15 for Mo [ 36 ], the 
model RCCAs exhibit a much lower value between 2 ~ 3. This reduction 
can be attributed to the fact that the large lattice distortion and complex 
local atomic environment greatly slow down the edge dislocation 
mobility. Within the RCCA samples, two key observations are made. 
First, the CRSS ratio increases with ordering in Nb10 and Nb25 samples. 
Second, the CRSS ratio shows a noticeable decrease in Nb40_1M samples 
compared to others. In the Nb10 and Nb25 samples, ordering has little 
influence on edge dislocation mobility while slowing down the screw 
dislocation, increasing the CRSS ratio. In contrast, in the Nb40_1M 
sample, the high DAPBE and lattice distortion significantly slow down 
the edge dislocation movement, leading to a decrease in the CRSS ratio. 
The reduction in the screw-to-edge CRSS ratio, or the mobility differ -
ence, is encouraging, as it could promote dislocation interactions and 
multiplications, which are beneficial for ductility [ 46 , 47 ].

4.3. Influence of matrix concentration on dislocation and B2 interaction
To further explore the interaction between ordered B2 nanocluster 
and dislocations, we utilize simple models incorporating a single cubic 
MoTa B2 precipitate (30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å) within Nb and NbW alloys. 
The selection of precipitate size and shape is based on spatial and sta -
tistical analysis of the Nb40_1M sample. The calculated sphericity, a 
measure of how closely a shape approximates a perfect sphere [ 56 ], 
indicates that the B2 precipitates more closely resemble cubes than 
spheres. Additionally, the distribution of the edge lengths of these 
cubic-like precipitates supports an edge length of 30 Å as a representa -
tive size. Our focus is specifically on examining the influence of the B2 
precipitate on the mechanisms of edge and screw dislocation motions. 
The chosen alloys, characterized by varying lattice and modulus mis -
matches, allow us to explore how these mismatches affect dislocation 
motion, and potentially guide alloy design in RCCAs. As summarized in 
Table 2 , the Nb matrix exhibits a larger mismatch with the B2 structure 
in both lattice constant and shear modulus, while the NbW alloy shows a 
moderate difference. Through these models, we seek to answer: 1) how 
does the degree of mismatch affect the dislocation motion? 2) What are 
the differences in interactions between edge and screw dislocations with 
B2 structures?
Fig. 15 (a) illustrates the edge dislocation velocity under different 
shear stresses in the Nb and NbW alloys, both with and without the 
MoTa B2 precipitates. It is evident that the B2 precipitate exerts a 
significantly stronger pinning effect on edge dislocation in the Nb metal 
compared to the NbW alloy. Fig. 15 (b) and (c) depict the interactions 
between the B2 precipitates and edge dislocations in the Nb and NbW 
alloys, respectively, at a few critical moments. In the Nb matrix, the B2 
precipitate acts as a strong pinning site for edge dislocation motion. 
Notably, a significant bow-out is observed before the edge dislocation 
can cut through, resulting in a pronounced strengthening effect. On the 
other hand, in the NbW matrix, the B2 precipitate behaves as a relatively 
weak pinning site, and the bow-out of the edge dislocation is not 
observed.
Fig. 16 (a) shows the screw dislocation velocity under different shear 
stresses in the Nb and NbW alloys, with and without the presence of B2 
precipitates, respectively. Interestingly, no pronounced strengthening 
effect is observed in either alloy with the B2 precipitates. Further in -
sights into the interactions between the B2 precipitate and screw 
dislocation are presented in Fig. 16(b) for the Nb matrix and in Fig. 16 (c) 
for the NbW matrix, revealing distinct screw dislocation mechanisms. In 
the Nb matrix, the screw dislocation cross-slips and bypasses the B2 
precipitate instead of cutting through it. The bypassing mechanism 
suggests that the presence of nanoscale B2 precipitates could promote 
the cross-slips of screw dislocation. Conversely, in the NbW alloy, the 
screw dislocation motion remains in the plane, cutting through the B2 
precipitates to continue slipping. When compared to the edge motion 
dislocation in NbW alloy, the increase in slip resistance is relatively 
small for screw dislocations, potentially due to their reduced sensitivity 
to lattice mismatch. The bypassing mechanism found in the Nb_B2 alloys 
( Fig. 16 (b)) is particularly noteworthy. Firstly, this mechanism explains 
the increased cross-slip ratio in the Nb40_1M sample ( Fig. 10 (b)), sug -
gesting that the formation of the long-ranged B2 nanoclusters enhances 
the screw cross-slip. Secondly, the increased cross-slip ratio can be 
critical to the mechanical performance of RCCAs. Cross-slip may trigger 
multiple dislocation slip systems, helping to avoid planar-slip and 
localized deformation bands, thereby giving rise to a higher work 
hardening rate and ductility [ 10 , 54 , 55 ].

With the development of long-range ordering, the nanoprecipitates 
act as strong pinning sites, significantly slowing down the edge

• Screw dislocation mobility and mechanisms. The screw dislocation 
motion is characterized by kink-pair nucleation and propagation. In 
the random alloy, the complex atomic environments enable the kink

cross-slip. The development of CSRO increases the energy barrier of 
kink-pair nucleation, resulting in planar screw dislocation motion. 
Interestingly, with the development of nanoprecipitates, the likeli -
hood of cross-slip is enhanced again as screw dislocations bypass the 
strong nano-obstacles, resulting in non-planar slip.
• Implications of B2 nanoprecipitates: The formation of B2 nano -
precipitates results in a stronger pinning effect on edge dislocation 
movement while promoting cross-slip of screw dislocations. This 
dual effect contributes to a reduction in the screw-to-edge ratio in 
slip resistance and discrepancy in mobility, highlighting a complex 
interplay between nanostructural evolution and dislocation 
mechanisms.
Among the model RCCAs, the Nb40 alloy uniquely exhibits simul -
taneous enhancement of DAPBE and lattice distortion as CSRO evolves 
into nanoscale B2 precipitates. This evolution leads to high lattice 
distortion due to the lattice misfit between the B2 precipitates and the 
random matrix. These findings provide valuable insights into shaping 
dislocation behaviors and interactions with ordered precipitates, 
emphasizing the importance of exploring non-equiatomic compositions 
and advancing beyond CSRO in RCCAs. By understanding the intricate 
interactions between nanostructures and dislocations, this study con -
tributes to optimizing alloy compositions and processing methods for 
superior performance in high-temperature and high-stress 
environments.
