abstract
Nb–1 wt%Zr–0.1 wt%C was hot deformed in vacuum in uniaxial compression to a true strain of about 0.6
in the temperature range 700–1700 /C176C and strain rate range 10 /C0 3–10 s/C0 1. From the stress, temperature
and strain rate data iso-strain rate sensitivity contour maps were generated. Two distinct regimes were
identiﬁed in the processing map: one with zero to negative strain rate sensitivity ranging from 700 to
900 /C176C at all the strain rates, and second a high strain rate sensitivity domain around 1700 /C176C and
10
/C0 3 s/C0 1. In the ﬁrst regime shear bands were seen within grains indicating strain localization. On the
basis of detailed metallographic investigation and kinetic analysis, it was established that the domain
corresponding to strain rate sensitivity 0.2 is of dynamic recrystallization. At deformation conditions
away from this domain the microstructure was found to be heterogeneous with some grain boundaries
showing wavy nature, some small grains located at these prior grain boundaries and near absence of new
grains within larger grains, suggesting partial/incomplete dynamic recrystallization. A stress exponent of
ﬁve and activation volume of 200–500 b
3 within this domain suggests a dislocation based mechanism
during dynamic recrystallization.

1. Introduction
Nb alloys have been studied for their possible use in high
temperature nuclear reactors, as they retain strength at relatively
high temperatures, have long term thermal creep strength, good
compatibility with liquid alkali metals, and relatively low neutron
absorption cross-section [1–4]. Of the various Nb alloys, Nb–1 wt%Zr
is one of the earlier alloys to be studied and still remains an alloy of
interest as a potential material for advanced nuclear reactors [5–8].
Zr provides solid solution strengthening to the Nb matrix. A variant
of this alloy contains 0.1 wt%C to provide dispersion strengthening
due to Nb and Zr carbides[9]. Compared to other Nb alloys and other
Mo and Ta alloys, Nb–1Zr alloys have a lower strength. As with other
refractory alloys their ambient to warm temperature strength is
inﬂuenced by the impurity content. However, the advantage of
Nb–Zr alloys is that they are easier to fabricate as compared to Mo
and Ta alloys. Investigations on both Nb–1Zr and Nb–1Zr–0.1C
carried out in 1980s and 1990s dealt primarily with precipitation
characteristics in the carbon containing alloy [10–15] and the creep
behavior for both alloys [16–24]. The creep studies on these alloys
can be classiﬁed as creep to low strains by Titran and co-workers
[16–19] and creep at higher stresses to steady state by Mukherjee
and co-workers [20–23]. Some studies on the general mechanical
behavior of Nb–Zr alloys at elevated temperature have also been
carried out, but these cover a narrow strain rate and temperature
regime [7,25]. Apart from these studies there does not appear to
be any comprehensive study available in open literature on the
hot deformation behavior of Nb–1 wt%Zr or Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy.
The aim of the present investigation was to study the hot deforma-
tion behavior of Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy over a wide range of tempera-
tures and strain rates with a view to optimize the hot workability
as well as to identify the mechanisms of deformation at these
temperatures. Such a study would be beneﬁcial both for designing
the hot working processes and for controlling the microstructure
of the alloy during bulk forming.
2. Experimental
Wrought Nb–1 wt%Zr–0.1 wt%C alloy obtained as annealed
(1200 /C176C for 1 h) rods of 5 mm and 12 mm diameter was used in this
work (nominal composition in wt%, Zr-1, C-0.1). Isothermal uniaxial
compression tests were conducted on two types of cylindrical

700 to 1400 /C176C at strain rates from 0.002 to 10 s
/C0 1, and the other of

carried out in 10 /C0 4 mbar vacuum to a true strain of 0.5 for tests up

From the r vs. e plots, the variation of r with T at constant strain

strain or at steady state) is found to change signiﬁcantly with strain
rate and temperature, indicating that the ﬂow behavior is thermally
activated. Further, it is seen that with increasing temperature the
strain rate sensitivity m ¼ D ln r=D ln _e (the slope of the log r vs.
log _e plot) increases and is strain rate dependent. During hot defor-
mation two types of restoration processes namely dynamic recovery
(DRV) and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) can occur to cancel out
the effects of work hardening [28]. In DRV, the generation and accu-
mulation of dislocation due to work hardening is continuously offset
by dislocation rearrangement and annihilation. This results in a
steady state ﬂow behavior after brief initial hardening. When DRX
is the restoration process, the ﬂow curve rises initially as a result
of work hardening and recovery processes to a peak value. It has
been shown [28] that a critical value of strain is required for the
nucleation of relatively dislocation free regions initiating the DRX
process. Subsequently the ﬂow stress reaches a peak and then with
increasing strain drops to a steady state value. The softening (exhib-
ited by the decrease of ﬂow stress) beyond peak stress results from
the large scale removal of dislocations by the growth of dislocation
free regions. Although the characteristics of ﬂow stress can be used
to identify DRX, microstructural investigations are required to
conﬁrm the underlying restoration mechanisms during high
temperature deformation [28].
3.2. Processing maps
In recent years, processing maps have been used to model the
hot deformation behavior and to understand the microstructural
evolution [26,28–31]. The principle and basis of this approach have
been described elsewhere [32]. In this study the processing maps
were constructed from the r, T, _e data which are converted to a
contour map of strain rate sensitivity m in a frame of T and _e as
shown in Fig. 3 for a strain of 0.5. The contour numbers in Fig. 3
represent m and the shaded portion corresponds to negative m.
The processing map exhibits a single domain starting at about
1300 /C176C and a maximum m at 1700 /C176C and 0.002 s/C0 1. In this domain
m increases from slightly positive to about 0.24 with increasing
temperature and decreasing strain rate. Since m is signiﬁcantly
greater than zero in this domain, the hot worked product is not ex-
pected to exhibit ﬂow instability. In addition, at temperatures low-
er than 1000 /C176C and at all strain rates, m is found to be negative
(the shaded portion). A possible reason for the negative m at low
strain rates could be due to dynamic strain aging (DSA) as has been
observed in Nb–1Zr alloy at 800 /C176C [8]. A consequence of the neg-
ative m could be that the material experiences strain localization.
The negative m at high strain rates is often correlated with ﬂow
instability due to adiabatic heating.

3.5. Grain size – Z relation
A characteristic feature of DRX is the inverse relation of the
steady state grain size with Z as has been reported in several alloy
systems undergoing DRX [26,29,33,39]. A similar plot for the pres-
ent data is shown in Fig. 10 for only the recrystallized grain sizes. It
is seen that recrystallized grain size is inversely related to Z and ap-
pears linear on a log–log scale. This conﬁrms the occurrence of DRX
in this domain. It has also been shown that steady state grain size
is independent of the initial grain size and depends only on Z [33].
Thus grain reﬁnement can be carried out at high Z values and grain
coarsening will take place at low Z values.
4. Summary and conclusions
The processing map obtained on Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy deformed in
the temperature range of 700–1700 /C176C and the strain rate range
10/C0 3–10 s/C0 1 showed two distinctly different domains: (1) a zero to
negative strain rate sensitivity domain from 700 to 900 /C176C at all
the strain rates and (2) a high strain rate sensitivity domain from
1300 to 1700 /C176C with a maximum at 1700 /C176C and 10/C0 3 s/C0 1. The ﬁrst
regime of negative strain rate sensitivity showed strain localization
both at the macro and microscopic levels as seen at 800 /C176C and
10/C0 2 s/C0 1. The strain-temperature conditions corresponding to the
ﬁrst regime should be avoided during hot working. At conditions
outside the domain such as 1400 /C176C and 0.01 s /C0 1 wavy boundaries
were observed indicating precursors to DRX. At 1500 /C176C – 0.01 s /C0 1,
1600 /C176C – 0.01 s/C0 1 and 1700 /C176C – 0.1 s/C0 1 wavy boundaries and small
grains within large grains were seen indicating incomplete DRX. At
conditions within the domain equiaxed grains were observed whose
grain size varied inversely with temperature compensated strain
rate Z, thereby conﬁrming the occurrence of DRX. Thus is it sug-
gested that any hot working carried out on Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy
should be carried out at 1600 /C176C or beyond and preferably at lower
strain rates. However higher strain rates too may result in complete
DRX if hot working is done to larger strains. In industrial practice
Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy has been hot worked successfully at tempera-
tures in the range 1500–650 /C176C [9,10,14].
On the basis of the above observations the following conclu-
sions can be made.
(1) Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy exhibits dynamic recrystallization over a
wide range of strain rates (10
/C0 3–10/C0 1 s/C0 1) and temperatures
greater than 1400 /C176C. The dynamically recrystallized grain
size can be varied by deforming the material at the appropri-
ate temperature and strain rate within the domain.
(2) Nb–1Zr–0.1C alloy exhibits a domain of strain localization at
temperatures below 1000 /C176C and at all strain rates employed
in this study. These deformation conditions should be
avoided during industrial hot working.
