ABSTRACT
Oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) superalloys are widely used in aerospace and nuclear reactors due to their
exceptional creep strength and fatigue crack resistance at elevated temperature. In this work, an ODS NiCrFeY
alloy for high temperature applications was fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF). The microstructure
and mechanical properties at room and elevated temperature of the L-PBF processed ODS NiCrFeY alloy were
investigated. It was demonstrated that equiaxed grains with large fraction of low angle grain boundaries and
cellular structures with high density of dislocations were obtained in the as-built alloy. Dispersed Y
2O3 nano-
particles were in-situ formed in the ODS NiCrFeY alloy. Most of the Y 2O3 nanoparticles were located at the
cellular walls and pinned with a large number of dislocations. Due to the strengthening effects by dispersed Y 2O3
nanoparticles and ﬁne cellular structures, the ODS NiCrFeY alloy showed high ultimate tensile strength of 810,
560 and 250 MPa at room temperature, 600 /C14 C and 800 /C14 C, respectively.
1. Introduction
Oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) superalloys are extensively used
in the nuclear reactors and aerospace structural parts owing to their
excellent radiation resistance and extraordinary mechanical properties at
high-temperature. The oxide nanoparticles with high-temperature ther-
mal stability uniformly distributed in nickel matrix of ODS superalloys
can pin dislocations and accommodate point defects caused by neutron
radiation [1–3]. ODS superalloys are usually fabricated by powder met-
allurgy (PM) [ 4,5], such as spark plasma sintering (SPS) [ 6,7], hot
isostatic pressing (HIP) [ 8–10] or hot extrusion (HE) [ 11–13]. However,
the forming process of PM methods generally involves multiple complex
steps and long production cycle, which makes it dif ﬁcult to produce
complicated structures and limits the more extensive application of ODS
superalloys in large and complex components [ 8,14–17].
Additive manufacturing (AM) technology is an extraordinary assis-
tant in the fabrication of engineering materials. It has become a focus of
research in recent years owing to its high design freedom, the ability to
manufacture complex structure, and the excellent property of the fabri-
cated parts [ 18,19]. AM has been successfully applied to iron-based al-
loys, nickel-based alloys, titanium-based alloys, aluminum-based alloys
and high-entropy alloys [ 18,20–24]. AM is also used to produce ODS
superalloys for the advantages of rapid cooling rate, which contribute to
the homogeneous dispersion of oxide particles in nickel matrix and
consequently improve the alloy performance [ 25].
With high precision and design freedom, laser powder bed fusion (L-
PBF) has been used to produce ODS alloys. Kenel et al. prepared Y 2O3
nano-particles-contained ODS titanium-based superalloys with different
laser parameters. It was reported that cracking frequency was negatively
related to the radiant exposure upon L-PBF, and the crack initiation was
restrained by optimizing laser parameters [ 26]. Song et al. found 1%
addition of Y
2O3 signiﬁcantly improved the hardness and strength of
L-PBF IN 718 owing to the grain reﬁnement and dispersion strengthening
[25]. Guo et al. conducted oxidation experiments on L-PBF IN738LC

3.2. Microstructural analysis
3.2.1. Microstructure with overstated and degraded VED
Fig. 3 shows several defects formed at different laser VED. The
densiﬁcation of the specimens was signiﬁcantly affected by the laser VED
[35]. Violent vibration occurred inside the molten pool at exorbitant
temperature due to the overstated VED, so a large number of gases were
trapped in the molten pool during the solidi ﬁcation process. As shown in
Fig. 3 a, the pores were formed in apparently spherical shape when the
gas is not discharged in time in the molten pool. Irregular cavity pores
were formed at low energy densities due to the presence of incompletely
melted powder, as shown in Fig. 3 d. It was authenticated that neither
overstated nor degraded VED was bene ﬁcial to ameliorating the density
of specimens, which was consistent with the results in the studies by Ni
et al. and Guo et al. [ 34,36].

3.4. Microstructure and compositions of ODS NiCrFeY alloy
Fig. 6 a shows the inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) map of specimen A3 in
cross section. Fig. 6 c is the IPF map in longitudinal section, showing a
typical ﬁsh scale structure. The grains in the as-built NiCrFeY alloy are
near-equiaxed and stretched in longitudinal section. Counted in the
longitudinal section, the average size of the grains is approximately 46
μm, while it is about 54 μm measured in cross section. The heat dissi-
pation of the molten pool in L-PBF was conducted through the deposited
solid layer toward the substrate. The great thermal gradient along the
building direction facilitated the epitaxial growth of grains and resulted
in the stretched grain morphology. The distribution of high angle grain
boundaries (HAGBs) and low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) was
superimposed on the SEM micrograph, as shown in Fig. 6 b and d. Ad-
ditive manufacturing involves a process analogue to cyclic annealing
heat treatment. The previously solidi ﬁed layer partially or completely
remelted when formed a new single layer, which led to the recrystalli-
zation of grains to form part of the HAGBs. However, the laser beam
moved quickly in the forming region of powder bed, resulting in insuf-
ﬁcient remelting of the previously solidi ﬁed layer and uncompleted
recrystallization. Therefore, residual LAGBs were formed [ 39,40]. The
existence of a large number of LAGBs facilitated abundant subgrains,
which corresponded to the large number of cellular structures ( Fig. 4 ).
Interestingly, the histogram in Fig. 6g shows a high proportion of LAGBs
in the as-built specimens, with 66% LAGBs in cross section and 44%
LAGBs in longitudinal section, respectively. The existence of LAGBs
played a certain role in improving the strength of the matrix. Fig. 6e is the
PF map for the building orientation, showing that a lower-intensity
texture existed along building orientation. The rapid cooling rate in
L-PBF favored the formation of ﬁne grains rather than epitaxial growth.
The presence of oxide particles was also bene ﬁcial to restraining the
growth of grains, and changing the growth orientation of crystal,
resulting in no high-strong texture along building direction.
3.5. Mechanical properties at room and elevated temperatures
The ambient-temperature engineering stress /C0 strain curves of L-PBF
built specimens with different process parameters are shown in Fig. 7a.
The comprehensive mechanical properties of specimen A3 were
signiﬁcantly higher than those of the other specimens. Specimen A3
showed the ultimate tensile strength ( σUTS) of 815 MPa, yield strength
(σ0.2) of 697 MPa and elongation ( δ) of 16.7%, respectively. In contrast,
both specimens A2 and A4 with similar energy densities as specimen A3
exhibited appropriate strength but far lower elongation than specimen
A3. In addition, specimens A1 and A5 exhibited a lower strength
compared to specimen A3. As mentioned above, an excessive or insuf-
ﬁcient VED brought negative effects. On one hand, an excessive VED
was generally favorable for defects, such as spherical pores. The
spherical pores were attributed to the inert gas atmosphere in the
process chamber to prevent oxidation or dissolution during the melting
process. On the other hand, an insuf ﬁcient VED led to incomplete melt
in bed powder layer, which induced defects, such as crack sources. The
tensile properties of the specimens with different parameters from
Fig. 7a are shown in Fig. 7b. The comprehensive mechanical properties
of specimen A3 were better than those of the L-PBF built ODS nickel-
based superalloy and MA754 alloy [ 3,41] In order to verify the
anisotropy of mechanical properties, the A3 longitudinal specimens
processed with the same parameters were taken in the tensile test. The
A3 longitudinal specimen exhibited the mechanical properties with the
σUTS of 760 MPa, σ0.2 of 603 MPa and δ of 19.9%, respectively. As
shown in Fig. 6 , microstructural anisotropy existed in L-PBF NiCrFeY
alloy as evidenced by the epitaxial growth along building direction
[42]. However, the effect of anisotropy on the mechanical properties
was non-ignorable. For example, the UTS of transverse and longitudinal
specimens were 815 MPa and 760 MPa, respectively. The engineering
stress/C0 strain curves of specimens A2, A3, A4 and B3 at elevated tem-
perature of 600 /C14 Ca r es h o w ni nFig. 7 c. The σUTS and δ of specimen A3
at 600 /C14 C are 563 MPa and 21%, respectively. When further increasing
the testing temperature, as shown in Fig. 7 d, a large decrease in
strength and increase in elongation can be observed, while σUTS and δ of
specimen A3 were 256 MPa and 23.6%, respectively, which showed
comparable mechanical properties to the well-known MA754 ODS su-

4. Discussion
4.1. Densi ﬁcation behavior
Additive manufacturing is the process of interaction between laser
and metal powder. In this work, spherical powder with an average par-
ticle size of 30 μm, which was bene ﬁcial to the complete melting of ad-
ditive manufacturing, was used (as shown in Fig. 1b). With the change of
VED, the microstructure of the specimen also changed. Dong et al.
developed the numerical simulation model, and showed that the molten
pool size, depth and width increased with VED [43]. The width and depth
of the molten pool were limited owing to the insuf ﬁcient VED, which led
to lack-of-fusion (as shown in Fig. 3 d). The temperature gradient
exhibited a Gaussian distribution from the center to the edge of the
molten pool in the rapid solidi ﬁcation process [ 25]. The capillary force
and Marangoni convection were promoted with the increase of VED [ 44,
45], which exhibited good ﬂowability to reduce defects, such as pores
and cavities [ 25]. As shown in Fig. 4 a and c, some residual pores were
attributed to the inert gas atmosphere in the process chamber to prevent
oxidation or gases dissolved in alloy powders degassed during melting
process [46]. More gases were dif ﬁcult to expel from the molten powder
due to the violent vibration of the molten pool with the excessive VED,

4.2. Characterization of oxide particles
Fig. 8 shows the bright- ﬁeld TEM micrograph of specimen A3, which
conﬁrmed that Y 2O3 particles effectively introduced into the L-PBF-built
ODS NiCrFeY alloy were observed. As shown in Fig. 8a, there were two
sets of bright and dark particles. Dark area was Y 2O3 particles analyzed
by Kikuchi line and TEM analysis, whereas white area infers to pores due
to the absence of Kikuchi line. Pores were formed during the twin-jet
electro-polishing process due to the brittle characteristics of Y 2O3 parti-
cles. The detailed HAADF-STEM image and the corresponding EDX map
for oxides of yttrium are shown in Fig. 8. As presented in Fig. 8a, a small
number of oxides of yttrium were formed in nickel matrix with average
sizes of approximately 50 nm. From Fig. 8 a, the yttrium and oxygen el-
ements tended to be enriched in the dark particles, whereas no segre-
gation of nickel, chromium and iron elements was observed. A high-
resolution TEM image of the Y 2O3 particle was taken along the same
zone axis, as inserted in Fig. 8b. The Y2O3 particles were conﬁrmed by the
selected area electron diffraction in Fig. 8 c. It has been previously re-
ported that the ﬁne and uniformly dispersed nano-oxide particles were
formed during the in-situ heat treatment stage of deposition [ 47,48],
which were consistent with the particles in this work. The volume frac-
tion of Y 2O3 particles introduced in Fig. 8 a was 0.53% by using IPP
software, which indicated that 61% of the yttrium was converted into
yttrium oxide particles.
4.3. Distribution and deformation mechanism of oxide particles
The distribution and characteristics of the particles were further
revealed in the bright-ﬁeld TEM micrograph of specimen A3 (as shown in
Fig. 9 ). The cell separation network structure where a large number of
dislocations entangled with each other and formed a dislocation wall
(hereinafter referred to as the cellular structure, as shown in Fig. 9a and
b) within a grain was observed, which was similar to the discovery of
Wang et al. and Li et al. [ 38,49]. Cellular structure is a dislocation wall
formed by a large number of dislocations entangled with each other.
According to Wang et al., the presence of cellular structure can enhance
the yield strength of the specimen without the sacri ﬁce of ductility.
Fig. 9a and b show both cellular structures, where the Y2O3 particles with
a size of approximately 50 nm were found.
Two forms of particles are shown in Fig. 9b, in which the bright one
was caused by the falling of Y 2O3 particles during electrolytic double
spray thinning while the black one was the remaining Y 2O3 particles.
These Y2O3 particles mostly appeared in the cellular structure boundary
inside the grains, where the dislocations were incurred and entangled
with particles, as shown in Fig. 9 a and b. According to Orowan's law
[50–53], the ﬁnely dispersed distribution of Y2O3 particles contributes to
the properties of the specimen by pinning dislocations and grain
boundaries. Fig. 9c shows that the Y2O3 particles, accompanied by a large
number of dislocations, were mainly located at the boundary of the
cellular structures, partially inside two adjacent cellular structures. These
well-dispersed Y 2O3 particles shown in the magni ﬁcation of Fig. 9 d
pinned the dislocations and grain boundaries, making it more difﬁcult for

As reported by Saereh et al. [ 55], the Gibbs free energy of Y –O
compounds is the lowest at any temperature compared to chrome and
iron oxides. As a result of the fact that the metallicity of yttrium is the
strongest, yttrium is more likely to combine with oxygen to form stable
oxides in the L-PBF process. Thermodynamically, there is a trend for
yttrium to bond with oxygen and form stable oxides. Under the in ﬂuence
of a high-energy laser beam, the molten pool vibrated violently, which
helped the oxygen in the chamber diffuse freely to the edge of the molten
pool. Due to the lower density, yttrium ﬂoated in the molten pool and
then combined with the oxygen to form Y 2O3 at the top of the molten
pool, and then, a small amount of oxygen in the molten pool also com-
bined with yttrium to form Y 2O3. Marangoni convection in the molten
pool accelerated the rearrangement of Y 2O3 nanoparticles, enabling the
particles to be uniformly dispersed in the matrix [ 44,55].
As shown in Fig. 10 , along the building direction, epitaxial growth
was formed during the L-PBF process and the cellular structures inside
the grains were shown in the magni ﬁcation area, where the Y 2O3 parti-
cles were located at the boundary, pinning a large number of disloca-
tions. According to the Orowan mechanism, nanoparticles improve the
properties of alloys by pinning dislocations. Consequently, owing to the
reliable performance at elevated temperature, Y 2O3 particles located at
grain boundaries further improved the properties of alloys at high tem-
perature by pinning grain boundaries to inhibit grain growth.
5. Conclusions
In summary, ODS NiCrFeY alloy was fabricated by L-PBF. The defects
distribution and strengthening mechanism of LAGBs and Y 2O3 on me-
chanical properties are elucidated as follows:
1. Nearly full density with porosity less than 0.4% was obtained in the L-
PBF-produced ODS NiCrFeY alloy. Metallurgical defects like lack-of-
fusion and pores were observed in the as-built alloy. The amounts
of lack-of-fusion and irregular pores increased with the decrease of
laser VED, while the spherical pores increased with the increase of
VED.
2. Equiaxed grains with stretched morphology were obtained in the as-
built alloy. The average grain size is 46 μm measured in the cross
section. A large fraction of LAGBs (~66% of the total grain bound-
aries) was observed in the L-PBF-processed ODS NiCrFeY alloy.
3. Y 2O3 particles were found in the ODS NiCrFeY alloy during the L-PBF
progress. The Y 2O3 particles are formed in situ by the reaction of
