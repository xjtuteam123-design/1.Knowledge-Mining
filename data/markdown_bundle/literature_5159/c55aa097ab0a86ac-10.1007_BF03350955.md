Mechanical, Elastic, and Structural Properties of Alloys 
of Ru-Ta High-Temperature Intermetallic Compounds 
R.L. FLEISCHER, R.D. FIELD, and c.L. BRIANT 
Screening tests of binary high-temperature intermetallic compounds showed the Ru-Ta L10 
structure to have room-temperature impact resistance and high-temperature strength. Twenty 
alloys based on RUllTa9 have been studied for microhardness, elastic modulus, impact strength, 
and, in two cases, compressional stress-strain behavior. L10 lamallae are commonly seen, and 
their character on fracture surfaces correlates with relative toughness. The B2 phase is present 
in some alloys, also a twinned lamellar structure, and the beginnings of a martensitic transfor­
mation to the L12 form were observed in the transmission electron microscope at 93 K. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
FOR structural use at high temperature, intermetallic 
compounds are being tested with potential aerospace and 
engine applications in mind. Recent interest in these 
compounds[\·2.3] is based on the expectations of high spe­
cific strength and specific modulus at elevated temper­
atures and the existence of hundreds of mechanically 
untested binary compounds. 14] Recently, diverse binary 
high-temperature materials have received extensive 
attention. [5.6] 
The stiffnesses of intermetallics are often greater than 
those of the metals from which they are formed, [7] and 
usually these materials maintain their strength with in­
creasing temperature better than do disordered alloys.18.9] 
Many of them have low specific gravities that produce 
high specific stiffness and specific strength, two quan­
tities that are critical to most uses in aircraft and rotating 
parts. In contrast, this paper reports results on variants 
of a compound with a high specific gravity (14.4), 
RUllT~. For use in an engine, such a material could po­
tentially be employed only if it allowed an increase in 
peak temperature. There the increase in engine weight 
might be more than compensated for by an increase in 
thrust. [4] 
The L10 compound RUllTa9 was shown to be strong 
to high temperature, 15] and some nearby compositions are 
also tough at room temperature. This alloy is somewhat 
similar in properties to another L10 compound with de­
sirable mechanical properties, IrNb, but is less expen­
sive than iridium.

III. PROCEDURE 
A. Preparation of Samples 
Samples were arc melted into disk-shaped ingots using 
as high-purity components as were reasonably available. 
The purities were 99.98 pct Ru and Ta, 99.99 pct Nb, 
Ti, and V, and 99.998 pet Co and Fe. Samples were 
annealed at l350 °C for 20 hours in Ar-filled Si02 en­
velopes along with a small piece of Y to take up oxygen. 
All samples were electromachined and polished to 1.0 x 
0.5 x 0.5 cm, then given mechanical tests. The base 
composition RUllTa9 gave lattice parameters a = 0.4275 
(±0.0003) nm and c = 0.3376 (±0.0002) nm, as com­
pared to 0.4271 and 0.3395 listed by Villars and 
Calvert.[ll]

D. Phases Present 
On the binary phase diagram, there are two phases 
that are adjacent on either side of stoichiometry to the 
LIo phase. On the Ra-rich side, a Ru-rich body-centered 
cubic (bcc) Ta exists for Ta > 54 or 55 at. pct; on the 
other side, a B2 structure exists for Ta < 42 pct and T < 
1667 °e. The phase fractions of the majority phases for 
alloys and the melting temperatures for the binary com­

generally agree remarkably well with the nominal

B. Chisel Toughness 
The toughnesses are given in Table I. In a search of 
many crystal structures for tough binary alloys,!5] only 
three of 29 had toughnesses 2:2. In this work, designed 
to explore the composition range that includes tough ter­
nary (or higher order) alloys, more than half of the alloys 
in Table I are of toughness 2:2, and five of eight related 
alloys for which we have limited data in unpublished 
work are similarly tough. Those whose hardness values 
are given in Figures 1 through 3 tend to be tough, and 
those in Figures 4 and 5 are brittle. In binary alloys, ma­
terials on the Ta-rich side of stoichiometry are tougher.

D. Compressional Stress-Strain Measurements 
Two representative samples (of intermediate tough­
ness and different solute additions) were chosen for stress­
strain tests. IM-43, Fe1ORu45Ta45, and IM-46, 
COlORu45 Ta45, were compressed at room temperature to 
maximum load (a stress at which cracks began to prop­
agate). Plastic strains to fracture were 0.003 and 0.005 
at 900 and 680 MPa, respectively. Thus, there was lim­
ited but readily measured plasticity. Chisel toughnesses 
were 1 for IM-43 and 2+ for IM-46; more plasticity than 
we have observed here usually occurs for samples that 
have higher toughness values. For example, Al47Ru53 + 
0.5 at. pct B, which has a toughness value of 3, gave a 
strain of 0.34 in compression.!13]

VII. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 
The special property of this alloy series is the consid­
erable toughness that many of the compositions have at 
room temperature. Table I shows that no systematic re-
METALLURGICAL TRANSACTIONS A 
lation exists between the volume fraction of second phases 
and the toughness. However, the observed lamellar 
structure that is common to the Llo crystals is like that 
of the IrNb alloys and is correlated with rougher fracture 
surfaces and higher toughness. In some alloys, as de­
scribed in Section VI, the RuTa B2 structure also occurs 
and undergoes a martensitic transformation into the Llu 
structure. No generalization can as yet be made as to 
whether this transformation is beneficial.
