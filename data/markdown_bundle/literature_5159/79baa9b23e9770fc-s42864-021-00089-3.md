Abstract
Tungsten and its alloys with high strength, thermal conductivity, and plasma radiation resistance are highly desirable for 
applications in medical, electronic and also nuclear facilities. However, the high melting point and high ductile–brittle 
transition temperature limit the fabrication of complex parts by using traditional methods, such as powder metallurgy. As 
a kind of additive manufacturing technology, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) was used to fabricate tungsten and its alloys 
in recent years because of its high energy density and low action time. This paper reviewed the recent progress of LPBF 
fabricated tungsten. Two main challenges, which are the lack of density and cracking problem, are focused. The methods 
to solve these problems are reviewed. In terms of improving the density, decreasing oxygen content, optimizing the process 
parameter, and adopting spherical powder were proved effective. In terms of cracking problem, the method of alloying, such 
as adding secondary phase nanoparticles and alloy elements, could significantly reduce the cracking density. However, the 
crack problem has not been completely solved up to now. Finally, future developments and potential new research directions 
for LPBF tungsten are proposed.
Keywords Tungsten · Additive manufacturing · Densification · Cracking
1 Introduction
Since the twentieth century, tungsten has been recognized 
as a typical refractory metal and widely used in medical, 
electronic and military fields including rocket nozzle, armor-
piercing projectile, and X-ray grating due to its excellent 
high-temperature mechanical properties, high thermal con-
ductivity and good oxidation resistance [1]. Tungsten is also 
selected as the plasma-facing material for future nuclear 
fusion reactor due to its good plasma radiation resistance 
[2]. Due to the high melting point and high ductile–brittle 
transition temperature (DBTT), traditional methods such as 
powder metallurgy (PM), spark plasma sintering (SPS) and 
chemical vapor deposition (CVD) are usually used to fabri-
cate tungsten materials. However, these methods are usually 
difficult to manufacture complex parts. In addition, the high 
hardness and high brittleness of tungsten at room tempera-
ture make it difficult for post-machining by the traditional 
processing methods, which greatly limits the application of 
tungsten materials.
In recent years, additive manufacturing, also known as 
three-dimensional (3D) printing, has grown rapidly and has 
been applied in many engineering fields. Compared with the 
traditional “subtractive” manufacturing methods, additive 
manufacturing techniques are more capable of manufactur -
ing customized, high-value, and complex-shaped metal parts. 
This feature attracts lots of interest in manufacturing tungsten 
parts with complex structures using additive manufacturing 
techniques. The techniques used for metal additive manufac-
turing mainly fall into two categories, which are direct energy 
deposition (DED) and powder bed fusion (PBF). The two 
categories can be further distinguished by the primary heat 
source like laser, electron beam, plasma arc, and gas metal arc.
Among these additive manufacturing techniques, laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) was used to fabricate refractory

However, up to date, the manufacture of tungsten and

be solved. These materials have been observed to be lack 
of density and cracking. With the aim to provide reference

compares two main challenges when manufacturing tungsten 
or tungsten alloy using LPBF including densification and 
cracking problem. Section 2 focuses on the densification of 
tungsten. The cracking problem is also reviewed in Sect. 3.
2 Densification of tungsten
In theory, all metals can be fabricated by LPBF. However, 
due to the different physical properties (such as laser absorp-
tivity, surface tension and viscosity of the molten metal), 
the processing window of each material varies by a large 
extent [3]. Due to the unique physical properties of tungsten 
(high melting point, high density, high thermal conductivity, 
large surface tension and viscosity of solution), which result 
in insufficient melting or instability in the molten pool, it 
is hard to obtain a high-density tungsten part using LPBF. 
Therefore, in the early research on additive manufacturing 
of pure tungsten, the main concern is the densification of 
tungsten.
In 2012, the first paper on LPBF of pure tungsten was 
published by Nanyang Technological University cooperated 
with Huazhong University of Science and Technology and 
Heilongjiang Institute of Science and Technology [4 ]. In 
this report, the authors found with the proper energy input, 
nanocrystalline tungsten can be obtained using the LPBF, 
and the dense parts were formed at the low laser scanning 
speed. Although different processing parameters (laser 
power: 180–200 W, scanning speed: 25–75 mm·s −1, scan-
ning distance: 0.05–0.1 mm, layer thickness: 0.05–0.07 mm) 
were used, the highest density obtained was only 82%. In 
the same year, Deprez et al. [5 ] used pure tungsten as raw 
material to fabricate complex-shaped medical collimator by 
LPBF and a final density of 89.92 ± 0.50% was obtained. 
For pure tungsten, a large number of follow-up researches 
showed that there are three effective methods to improve 
the density: adjusting the processing parameters, adopting 
spherical powder, and controlling oxygen content [6–9].
2.1 Adjusting the processing parameter
In the LPBF forming process, spreading and solidification 
of the melt droplet compete with each other. Compared with 
typical materials with good printability, like steel and tita-
nium, tungsten has the characteristics of high melting point, 
high thermal conductivity, high surface tension and high vis-
cosity. Therefore, the spreading speed of pure tungsten drop-
let is slow, while the solidification rate is very fast. Tung-
sten melt solidifies before it spreads, which makes tungsten 
prone for balling. Balling further affects the subsequent 
powder deposition and solidification process, resulting in a 
large number of residual pores in the formed tungsten parts 
(Fig.  1) [6]. Therefore, balling of pure tungsten in LPBF 
forming process is the main reason for lacking density.
Balling is a common phenomenon in LPBF forming 
process. When developing a new material via LPBF, it is 
necessary to explore a suitable process window to avoid ball-
ing [3]. Researchers found that the balling of pure tungsten 
is directly related to the input laser energy density [7 , 8].

Enneti et al. [7 ] found that with the relatively low laser 
powder (90 W), the theoretical density of fabricated tung-
sten parts increased with the increased energy density, as 
illustrated in Fig.  2. And the samples formed with the low 
energy density experienced severe balling. Wang et al. [8 ] 
also showed that when the point distance and hatch distance 
were set, the dense zone was obtained at high laser power 
(Fig.  3). Reducing the scanning speed and increasing the 
laser power can result in larger temperature gradient, higher 
molten pool temperature and better melt spreading condi -
tion, to reduce the occurrence of balling and increase the 
density to a certain extent [4, 7].

The earlier results showed that it was difficult to produce 
tungsten parts with over 90% relative density only by 
optimizing the processing parameters [4 –7]. It was found 
later, using spherical powder was a very effective method 
to increase the density. Wang et al. [8 ] showed by com-
bining spherical powder that was plasma spheroidized 
from the polygonal powder with the optimization of pro-
cessing parameters, the fluidity of the powders increased

2.4 Changing the laser mode
In addition, the relative density of LPBFed pure tungsten 
can be further improved by changing the laser mode, such as 
femtosecond laser [10–14]. Compared with the continuous 
laser or pulse laser, the femtosecond laser can produce peak 
power of several orders of magnitude higher, and raise the 
temperature of the exposed area over 6000 °C [14], so that 
tungsten can be fully melted. Bai et al. [10] fabricated pure 
tungsten parts with a density of 99% using the femtosecond 
laser. However, femtosecond laser has the disadvantages of 
short life, slow forming speed and unable to form large parts, 
which has certain limitations in practical application.
3 Cracking in LPBFed tungsten
By adopting spherical powder, the relative density of pure 
tungsten formed by LPBF can reach more than 95% under 
reasonable processing parameters. Besides the difficulty in 
densification, however, the formation of microcracks caused 
by the generation of large residual stress during the cooling 
process of LPBE becomes another critical problem limiting 
the practical application of LPBFed tungsten parts. Mer -
celis et al. [15] and Kruth et al. [16] discussed the mecha-
nism of the residual stress generation in detail as follows: 
first, when the laser interacted with the material surface, 
the material was heated and generated a high-temperature 
gradient surrounding the laser spot, which made the mate-
rial expand. During the process of expansion, if the internal 
stress exceeded the yield strength of the material, the plastic 
deformation area produced tensile stress on the surrounding 
materials during cooling. Second, the laser melted area on

This residual stress was particularly unfavorable for the

tered cubic (BCC) metal, the plastic deformation of tungsten 
depended on the movement of 1/2 ⟨111⟩ screw dislocations, 
but they were difficult to move under applied stress due to 
the lack of close-packed plane [17]. Compared with edge 
dislocation and mixed dislocation, screw dislocation had the 
worst mobility at low temperature. At ambient temperature, 
the thermal fluctuation under external force resulted in a 
double-kink on a straight dislocation. It needed to climb over 
the Peierls’ barrier and then moved to an adjacent minimum 
energy position [18]. However, the thermal fluctuation of 
dislocations was unfavorable below the critical temperature. 
This critical temperature corresponded to the DBTT. For 
tungsten, the DBTT was as high as 200–400 °C [1]. During

high-speed camera, and further confirmed that cracks in 
LPBFed tungsten were related to ductile–brittle transition

Furthermore, it was found that the grain boundary was 
also the main factor leading to the brittleness of tungsten as 
the cracks were typically observed along the grain bound-
ary [18]. During solidification, impurity elements such as 
O, P or C in W tended to segregate at the grain bounda -
ries, which was one of the main reasons for low strength of 
the grain boundaries [20]. Although the solubility of these 
impurities in W matrix was very small (< 1 μg·g−1) at room 
temperature, the solubility increased greatly at eutectic 
temperature (100–1000 μg·g −1). Among these impurities, 
O might be the most damaging impurity element. Kurishita 
et al. [21] showed that the DBTT of tungsten materials could 
be reduced to about 200 K by reducing the oxygen content 
of 0.6‰ during the forming process. In the LPBF process, 
although the working chamber is filled with protective gas 
to control the adverse effect of oxidation in the forming pro-
cess, oxygen cannot be discharged inevitably. In addition, a 
small amount of oxygen impurities is adsorbed in the pow -
der of LPBF. Therefore, during the heating process, a large 
amount of residual oxygen impurities in the chamber and 
powder enter the tungsten matrix, resulting in the weaken-
ing of the tungsten grain boundary. Wang et al. [22, 23] 
proposed that due to the existence of oxygen, tungsten oxide 
was generated during the LPBF process. Under high temper-
ature, tungsten oxide reached the boiling point and formed 
a large number of nanopores that segregated at the grain 
boundaries and became the crack initiation sites, which pro-
moted the crack propagation along the grain boundaries, as 
shown in Fig. 5. To solve the cracking problem in LPBF of 
tungsten, researchers have tried many different approaches.
3.1 Adjusting processing parameters
To suppress cracking in LPBFed pure tungsten, two aspects 
should be considered which are, reducing the residual stress 
and reducing the DBTT. Studies have been made to satisfy 
these two aspects by adjusting the processing parameters.

The results of Guo et al. [24] and Sidambe et al. [25] 
showed that increasing laser energy density could reduce 
microcracks and increase the density of fabricated parts. 
Wen et al. [26] obtained the tungsten parts with a relative 
density of 98.71% by optimizing the processing parameters. 
With the increase of scanning speed and the decrease of 
linear energy density, the number of cracks decreased, and 
the length and width of cracks also decreased, but microc-
racks still existed. Tan et al. [27] showed that reducing the 
thickness of powder layer helped to reduce cracks. When 
high power density laser is exposed to relatively thin powder 
layer, the lower layer remelted, thus reducing the tempera-
ture gradient between layers. Substrate heating is another 
effective method to reduce the thermal gradient during 
LPBF process. Muller et al. [28, 29] showed that by heating 
the substrate to up to 1000 ℃ during LPBF of pure tungsten, 
the crack density in the fabricated parts decreased. How -
ever, this method still cannot eliminate cracking in LPB-
Fed tungsten. The cracks in LPBFed tungsten occurred in 
a very short time (< 10 ms) after laser exposure, so it was 
difficult to adjust the local temperature and stress distribu-
tion by changing the scanning strategy and other processing 
parameters [19]. In addition, Wang et al. [22] calculated the 
crack criterion of pure tungsten during the LPBF process, 
and the estimated surface temperature difference far exceeds 
the maximum endurable temperature. Therefore, it is almost 
impossible to solve the cracking issue using pure tungsten.
3.2 Alloying
As we demonstrated in the Sect.  3.1, complete elimination 
of cracking cannot be achieved by simply adjusting the 
process parameters. Since the ductile–brittle transition in 
LPBF process is the fundamental cause of crack formation, 
improving the plasticity of tungsten at room temperature 
is essential to solve the cracking problem. This is typically 
achieved through the metal alloy. At present, there are two 
main research directions of tungsten alloying: improving dis-
location mobility of tungsten itself, and purifying the impu-
rities on the grain boundaries to increase the grain boundary 
strength. The alloying of tungsten is mainly divided into two 
ways: one is to use the principle of dispersion strengthen -
ing to add secondary phase nanoparticles, typically referring 
to as extrinsic toughening; the other is to add other alloy

3.2.1 W–ZrC alloy
The use of secondary phase nanoparticles to enhance tung-
sten has been widely used in powder metallurgy. Nano-
particles like TiC, La2O3, Y2O3 and ZrC have been added 
to tungsten to improve their plasticity and thermal shock 
resistance [21, 28–30]. Due to the Zener pinning, the sec-
ondary phase nanoparticles can greatly refine the grain 
size of tungsten and improve the strength and toughness of 
tungsten materials. The average grain size of the W–ZrC 
alloy developed by Xie et al. [31, 32] was as low as 1 µm, 
the yield strength was 2.35 GPa compared to 1.2 GPa of 
commercial rolled tungsten, and DBTT was decreased from 
473 to 373 K. In addition to grain refinement, the secondary 
phase nanoparticles can also be used as an oxygen scavenger 
for tungsten. In W–ZrC alloy fabricated by powder metal-
lurgy method, the dispersed ZrC particles could react with 
the free oxygen impurities at the grain boundary to form 
W–Zr–Cx–Oy particles with good high-temperature stabil -
ity. The particles could form a semi-coherent structure with 
the tungsten matrix at the grain boundaries, connect the 
tungsten grains and strengthen the grain boundaries [32]. 
Different from powder metallurgy, metal experienced melt-
ing and solidification during LPBF process. The existence 
of secondary phase nanoparticles could improve the het-
erogeneous nucleation during the solidification, which was 
helpful to refine grains and suppress hot cracks. Martin et al. 
[33] changed the solidification behavior of Al7075 alloy 
during LPBF by adding TiB2 nanoparticles. As a kind of

0.5 wt% ZrC nanoparticles (Fig. 6). With the addition of ZrC

obviously refined, as shown in Fig.  7 [34]. For pure tung-

high-temperature gradient in LPBF process was favorable 
for grains to grow epitaxially along the temperature gradient, 
resulting in coarse columnar crystal with a long axis parallel 
to the building direction. In W–ZrC alloy, however, a large 
number of heterogeneous nucleation sites were introduced 
into the matrix, which reduced the energy barrier required 
for grain nucleation, and thus promoted the heterogeneous 
nucleation of a large number of new grains, to refine the 
grains. In addition, the secondary phase nanoparticles also 
inhibited the grain growth in terms of Zener pinning. LPBF 
was a process that experiences multiple thermal cycles. 
When the upper layer powder was melted by the laser, 
there was a certain heat-affected zone in the solidified part 
of the lower layer. The material in this area was remelted 
or annealed due to the energy input from the upper layer, 
resulting in grain coarsening or recrystallization. With the 
addition of secondary phase nanoparticles, however, the 
migration of the grain boundary was hindered by secondary 
phase nanoparticles and the grain boundary migration speed 
was reduced. Thus, the grain growth was restrained and the 
grains were refined.

tive path for crack propagation, so a large number of inter -
granular cracks were formed in pure tungsten along the grain 
boundaries of columnar grains. In W–ZrC alloy, although 
there were still columnar grains growing along the building 
direction, the grain size was obviously refined. The length of 
columnar grain was significantly reduced, and the shape was 
more curved, which lead to the shortening of crack propa-
gation path and the increasing of crack propagation resist-
ance. In addition, Shinozaki et al. [35] found that coarse 
columnar grains were more prone to cracking because the

3.2.2 W–Ta alloy
Qi et al. [38] showed that the addition of V and VI group ele-
ments could change the Fermi level in tungsten and molyb-
denum, reduce the Peierls’ energy barrier, and improve the 
dislocation nucleation and mobility after calculating the 
fracture behaviour of refractory metals with BCC struc-
ture. The toughening of tungsten by alloying with another 
metal has been widely studied in powder metallurgy. The 
main alloy compositions include tungsten tantalum [39–41], 
tungsten vanadium [42, 43], tungsten rhenium [44–46], and 
tungsten niobium [47]. However, due to the high melting 
point and low diffusivity of tungsten, the tungsten alloys 
fabricated by powder metallurgy usually have regional ele-
ment segregation. By LPBF, the true solid solution can be 
obtained because of the fully melting. Based on the above 
reasons, Ta was added as an intrinsic toughening element to 
LPBFed pure tungsten to suppress the cracking [23, 48]. The 
first problem that needs to be confirmed for LPBFed W–Ta 
alloy was in what form does Ta exist in the matrix. The study 
from Wang et al. [23] first demonstrated the XRD results 
after the LPBF process and no corresponding peaks of Ta 
and its compounds were found in W–6wt%Ta alloy (Fig. 9), 
indicating that Ta powder was completely melted and mixed 
with tungsten powder during LPBF process. Li et al. [ 49] 
further strengthened this founding by comparing the peak

While coarse columnar grains were most common in pure 
tungsten, a large number of equiaxed fine grains were found 
in the W–Ta alloy, and the average grain size in LPBFed 
W–Ta was significantly reduced [48]. In W–Ta alloy, the 
existence of Ta changed the solidification behavior of the 
material. Upon solidification, a constitutional undercooling 
condition could be formed in front of the solid–liquid inter-
face because of the different melting points between W and 
Ta, which was energetically favorable for the new grains to 
nucleate. As a result, decreased grain size was observed in 
W–Ta [9, 48]. In addition, in LPBFed alloys, active elements 
often reacted with oxygen and formed oxide nanoparticles 
in situ and dispersed in the matrix [52–54]. Compared with 
tungsten, tantalum was also easier to react with oxygen to 
produce a large number of tantalum oxide nanoparticles, 
which were entangled with dislocations and distributed in 
the matrix, as shown in Fig. 11 [48].
The grain refinement and the reduction of oxygen impuri-
ties caused by Ta addition could inhibit the cracking, which 
was similar to LPBFed W–ZrC alloy. The dislocation cells 
found in W–Ta alloy also had benefit to inhibit the crack -
ing. The dislocation cells acted as stress-bearing units under 
the external load, which could disperse the load stress uni-
formly and increase the yield strength of the material [23, 
48]. Wang et al. [23] measured the plastic deformation zone

the plasticity of pure W. Two grains with similar crystal-

orientation difference was measured. The results showed that 
the plastic deformation zone of pure tungsten was 13.6 μm2

3.3 Post‑processing
The above methods were tried to reduce the cracks in the 
LPBF forming process. Post-treatment is usually needed 
to eliminate the defects in the LPBFed metal parts. For 
example, hot isostatic pressing (HIP) is a commonly used 
method for densification. Chen et al. [55] showed that a sig-
nificant part of the cracks formed in LPBFed pure tung-
sten could be mechanically healed during HIP process (as 
shown in Fig. 15). However, the crack problem was not been 
completely solved by HIP. And the related research about 
post-treatment of LPBFed tungsten is rare and it should be 
strengthened further.

5 Summary and prospects
Tungsten is a refractory metal with wide application pros-
pects and has been used in several important components 
that experience extreme environments, such as plasma-fac-
ing material in International Thermonuclear Experimental 
Reactor (ITER). With the gradual increasing requirement 
in engineering fields, the needs of complex tungsten com-
ponents become more urgent. As it is difficult to achieve by 
traditional processing methods, LPBF technology provides 
the possibility to solve this problem. In recent years, great 
progress has been made in fabricating pure tungsten and its 
alloys using LPBF technique, but there are still a number 
of unsolvable problems including densification and cark -
ing. The most critical problem is cracking. The existence 
of microcracks will seriously limit the application of this 
technology. For example, when LPBFed pure tungsten is 
irradiated by He ion, abnormal fuzz structure would grow at 
the crack edge, making it questionable to be used as plasma-
facing material [49].
Although researchers have tried to solve the cracking 
problem of LPBFed pure tungsten from different aspects 
including alloying, adjusting process parameters and post-
processing, it is still unable to completely eliminate crack -
ing. In summary, the existing research results provide 4 
directions for future researches: (i) ductile–brittle transition 
is the most fundamental cause of the cracking, so reducing 
the DBTT is the key; (ii) grain boundary is the weakest point 
in polycrystalline tungsten, and it is necessary to increase 
the strength of grain boundary by reducing the oxygen 
impurities; (iii) increasing the ductility of the tungsten and 
increase the crack propagation resistance; (iv) reducing the 
temperature gradient and the solidification rate during the 
LPBF process.
Due to the problems of raw powder preparation and 
formability, the LPBF of tungsten alloy is very limited, 
and there are only a few reports about alloying with Ta and 
ZrC. However, new alloy systems like W–Re and W–TaC 
are currently under development and have shown promising 
results. In addition, advanced LPBF machine equipped with 
high-power laser and high-temperature building plate also 
offers the possibility to solve the cracking problem. Moreo-
ver, some existing computing and simulation methods like 
dislocation dynamics and finite element analysis could also 
be used to guide the LPBF process of tungsten. In the future, 
it is not impossible to solve the cracking problem in LPBFed 
tungsten by considering all factors that is helpful to crack 
suppression.
