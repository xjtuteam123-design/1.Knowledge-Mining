ABSTRACT
Integration of twinning and hierarchical microstructure into a face-centered cubic (FCC) matrix is a novel 
research approach for advancing high-temperature alloys, enhancing strength without the need for costly heavy 
elements. Here, a three-level B2 phase was incorporated into a NiCoCrFe high-entropy alloy (HEA) matrix, of -
fering a high strengthening effect at room temperature and a good resistance to moderate-temperature softening 
while preserving the low stacking fault energy of the FCC matrix. The resulting NiCoCrFeAl
0.3
Si
0.3 
HEA exhibited 
stable yield strength, strain hardening, and deformation twinning over a broad temperature ranging from 77 to 
973 K. By establishing a yield strength model based on the various strengthening mechanisms, the study high -
lighted the important role of the three-level B2 phase in the exceptional mechanical properties of the alloy across 
a wide temperature range. These findings present a promising avenue for the advancement of high-temperature 
structural materials.
1. Introduction
Developing high-performance structural materials for high- 
temperature environments and overcoming the limitations of existing 
superalloys is a primary objective for materials science community. 
These materials must demonstrate exceptional microstructural stability, 
resistance to high-temperature softening, and protection against oxida -
tion. Recent studies indicate that high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have the 
potential to supplant nickel-based superalloys as the next generation of 
high-temperature materials [1 – 3] . HEAs offer slow grain growth and 
remarkable phase stability in high-temperature conditions due to their 
sluggish diffusion and high entropy effect, making them promising for 
applications at elevated temperatures [4 – 6] . For example, a face- 
centered cubic (FCC) CoCrFeNi nanocrystal HEA displayed signifi -
cantly slow grain growth even at temperatures as high as 0.68 T

Furthermore, the sluggish diffusion observed in (NiCoFeCr)

NiAl intermetallic compounds have been considered a promising 
option for structural applications at high temperatures due to their 
appealing properties, including a high melting point (1949 K), good 
thermal conductivity (76 W/m ⋅ K), low density (5.9 g/cm
3
), and excel -
lent oxidation/corrosion resistance up to 1673 K [23,24] . The FCC 
matrix reinforced by NiAl-B2 phase, particularly the high-entropy ma -
trix reinforced by high-entropy B2 precipitation, shows potential for 
outstanding mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. The pre -
cipitation of B2 phase in the FCC matrix significantly enhances the al -
loy ’ s strength and work-hardening ability while maintaining ductility 
[25,26] . Moreover, incorporating B2 phase into the M/HE FCC matrix 
notably enhances fatigue life [27] and cryogenic temperature perfor -
mance [28,29] . Recent studies have investigated the mechanical 
behavior of B2-strengthened FCC HEAs over a wide temperature range. 
The findings indicate that the B2 phase ensures high tensile yield 
strengths and excellent ductility from cryogenic to high temperatures, 
with no significant brittleness at intermediate temperatures [30,31] . 
Furthermore, introducing deformation twins (DTs) into the high- 
temperature deformation in superalloys can provide work hardening 
to counteract thermal softening and intermediate-temperature brittle -
ness, representing a crucial area of superalloy research [32] . For 
instance, Qi et al. [33] observed a transition in the deformation mech -
anism of a Ni-Co based superalloy from the stacking fault (SF) mecha -
nism to the DT one with increasing temperature. Similarly, Bai et al. 
[34] noted the formation of DTs in a Ta-added Ni-Co-Cr superalloy when 
deformed at 815 ℃ .
This paper focuses on optimizing the medium/high temperature 
mechanical properties of FCC HEA through microstructure design. The 
approach involves reducing the stacking fault energy (SFE) of the matrix 
to enhance work hardening via twins, thereby improving ultimate 
strength and ductility at high temperatures. Additionally, a B2 high- 
temperature strengthening phase is introduced and its distribution 
form is optimized to enhance high-temperature strength and resistance 
to intermediate-temperature brittleness of the alloy. The study involves 
adding a significant amount of Si (6.9 at.%) to FCC NiCoCrFe HEA to 
reduce the SFE, followed by the addition of Al to adjust the hierarchical 
B2 precipitation during heat treatment. Furthermore, the doping of Al 
and Si greatly enhances the high-temperature oxidation resistance of the 
alloy. The mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms of the 
resulting HEA, strengthened by the hierarchical B2 phase, were inves -
tigated over a wide temperature range. A prediction model for yield 
strength in a broad temperature range was established by quantifying 
the contribution of each strengthening mechanism.

The Al

0.3 
HEA displays a distinctive serrated flow behavior when 
subjected to deformation at temperatures of 523 K and 773 K. This 
phenomenon is commonly associated with dynamic strain aging (DSA), 
leading to stress anomalies at elevated temperatures [43,44] , as depic -
ted in Fig. 5 . Within this HEA, interactions between dislocations and 
solute atoms (or short-range ordered clusters) occur at specific strain 
rates. Typically, within a certain temperature range, the diffusion rate of 
solute atoms aligns with the dislocation sliding rate. Solute atoms can 
migrate to the moving dislocation for a defined period, causing accu -
mulation and hindrance of dislocation movement, resulting in a 
macroscopically observed serrated stress rise. With increasing applied 
load, when the stress on the dislocation exceeds a critical threshold, the 
dislocation can overcome solute resistance and continue to slide, leading 
to a macroscopic stress drop [43,45] . Moreover, as temperature rises, 
the serrated behavior of the Al
0.3
Si
0.3 
HEA shifts from C-type to B-type 
flow [43] . The presence of Al and Si in the alloy causes a noticeable 
decrease in the temperature range where the high-temperature serrated 
flow phenomenon occurs compared to the NiCoCrFe HEA, moving from 
above 673 K to below 523 K [10] . This shift is attributed to the higher 
solute concentration and increased dislocation pinning effect due to the 
addition of Al and Si, resulting in higher stress levels at lower 
temperatures.
3.4. Deformation microstructure
The typical post-tensile fracture deformation structure at room 
temperature is illustrated in Fig. 6 . The matrix shows high density dis -
locations, along with an increased presence of DTs and SFs, particularly 
in the vicinity of the B2 phase. This phenomenon can be attributed to the 
low SFE of the matrix and the elevated stress levels resulting from stress 
concentration near the precipitated phase. The B2 phase tends to contain 
more dislocations due to its lower hardness compared to the sigma phase 
or the laves phase, making it prone to dislocation cutting under high- 
stress deformation conditions [27,46] . Recent research indicates that 
under sufficiently high stress levels, shear of the B2 phase can occur at 
early stages of strain, such as at 3 % strain [28] . The highly deformed 
microstructure of the matrix poses challenges in distinguishing between 
primary and secondary B2 phases.
During cryogenic deformation, the matrix experiences significant 
deformation with a higher density of dislocations and DTs, resulting in 
diffraction spots forming a ring as shown in Fig. 7 (a). Additionally, the 
B2 phase undergoes substantial plastic deformation with abundant 
dislocation activity. However, due to the good toughness of the B2 
phase, no internal cracking occurs, unlike hard and brittle carbides or B2 
phase failure under higher stress [29,47] or sigma phase basically does 
not produce plastic deformation [46] . Regions away from the B2 phase 
display high density multistage DTs and SFs under cryogenic deforma -
tion, as illustrated in Fig. 7 (b). Additionally, fine and dense DTs, SFs, and 
narrow hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structures within SFs are 
observed in other regions, as depicted in Fig. 7 (c) and (d). These char -
acteristics, in conjunction with the three-order B2 phase, contribute to 
enhancing the matrix ’ s strength, providing excellent work hardening, 
and resistance to delayed fracture.

In addition, the presence of hierarchical precipitation in the alloy 
results in a finely divided structure with soft and hard regions. The size 
difference of the B2 phase with a three-level gradient contributes to 
further hardness variation, creating a larger strain gradient in the 
interphase region. This allows the hard B2 phase to deform fully without 
cracking, activating more deformation mechanisms in the soft matrix to 
achieve strain coordination. This dual effect maximizes the alloy ’ s 
deformation ability and strengthens it through mechanisms [27,71]
such as high-density dislocation strengthening, DT and SF strength -
ening. These factors help the alloy maintain high work hardening and 
strength over a wide temperature range. Despite the average stress of the 
973 K stretched sample being lower than the critical twinning stress, 
there are more DTs observed near the B2 phase due to significantly 
higher local stress within the alloy. The alloy contains a volume fraction 
of B2 phase, totaling 15 %. Each level of precipitation serves a specific 
purpose: the primary B2 phase boosts strength through deformation, 
while the secondary B2 phase anchors to the grain boundary to prevent 
softening and grain growth at high temperatures, especially at 973 K and 
above. Additionally, it continuously anchors the grain boundary 
through dynamic precipitation, contributing to increased softening 
resistance. Tertiary B2 precipitation is dispersed in the FCC matrix to 
reinforce it and prevent premature cracking caused by significant stress 
concentration due to the substantial hardness difference between the 
matrix and B2 phase [28] . Each level of B2 phase serves specific func -
tions, as illustrated in Fig. 16 . The presence of these B2 phases may 
enhance matrix stability at high temperatures, akin to how the presence 
of L1
2 
precipitates in nickel-based superalloys inhibits precipitate 
coarsening and improves matrix stability [72] . The high melting point 
(1676 ℃ ) of the NiAl-B2 phase, particularly the multi-component B2 
phase, contributes to its toughness and resistance to high temperature 
softening. This property enables the alloy to maintain high strength in 
elevated temperature environments, notably retaining the same high 
yield strength at 973 K as at 523 K during tensile testing. However, as 
strain increases, the dynamic growth of the B2 phase, especially the 
secondary B2 phase at grain boundaries and the tertiary B2 phase within 
grains, along with dislocation recovery in the matrix, leads to strain 
softening. Notably, the dynamic pinning of the B2 phase at grain 
boundaries prevents significant growth in matrix grain size and inhibits 
dynamic recrystallization of the FCC matrix. The introduction of hier -
archical B2 phases has been instrumental in high temperature 
strengthening and toughening of alloys. Future research will focus on 
developing B2 phases that exhibit greater resistance to high temperature 
softening and increasing the volume fraction of the B2 phase.
5. Conclusion
This study delves into the integration of deformation twins and high 
temperature strengthening phases into an FCC-based HEA to develop 
advanced alloys suitable for high-temperature conditions. The research 
effectively establishes a matrix with low SFE that incorporates multiple 
three-level B2 phases, leading to a distinctive and stable microstructure 
within a novel HEA. The study examines the initial microstructure, 
strengthening and deformation mechanisms, and the creation of a yield 
strength prediction model spanning a wide temperature range for the 
novel alloy. The following conclusions can be drawn:
