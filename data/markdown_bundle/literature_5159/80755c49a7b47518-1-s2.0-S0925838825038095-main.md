ABSTRACT
Nanophase Separation Sintering (NPSS) has been shown to be a viable method for rapidly consolidating re -
fractory alloys at temperatures and applied pressures lower than typically expected resulting in densified, ultra- 
fine-grained material. Using this technique, a W-5Cr binary alloy has been synthesized through high energy ball 
milling and direct current sintering and subjected to high temperature mechanical and oxidation testing. The 
results showed near 100 % densification of the alloy at a temperature of only 1150 
◦
C using DCS for 1 h. The 
sintered components sustained compressive strengths above 500 MPa when tested at 1000 
◦
C and exceeded 
1 GPa below 900 
◦
C. While the oxidation of tungsten alloys is often catastrophic, these chromium containing 
alloys were able to sustain protective chromia growth for short durations up to 1000 
◦
C. In both these mechanical 
and oxidative tests, we have shown that NPSS W-Cr alloys may be a viable alternative to similar tungsten alloys 
in extreme environment applications. Significant carbide and contaminant inclusions are believed to have 
induced brittle behavior in the material at intermediate and room temperatures, and their reduction is the subject 
of future works.
1. Introduction
As the operating conditions of critical engineering components trend 
towards more hostile environments due to extreme loading, tempera -
ture, and irradiation, the pool of metallic alloy systems which can satisfy 
demand grows ever smaller. While iron and nickel-based alloys have 
proven to be the backbone of high temperature structural materials, they 
generally have a core metallurgical limitation of 1000 
◦
C [1] . As oper -
ating temperatures increase beyond this limit and push towards the 
incipient melting points of major strengthening phases, as in the case of 
γ ʹ strengthened Ni-superalloys, there is little that can be done to main -
tain mechanical strength. The few alloy systems which will maintain 
their strength through the 1000 
◦
C barrier are those with melting points 
significantly greater than those of the transition metals, namely the re -
fractory group elements including W, Hf, Nb, Ta, Zr, and Mo.
The desirable elevated melting temperature of refractory metals 
poses a significant engineering challenge in the large scale 
manufacturing of refractory based alloys and to date, C-103, a Nb-Hf 
alloy, is one of the few which is employed on large scales [2] . 
Furthermore, many of these refractory alloys exhibit ductile-to-brittle 
transition temperatures well above the temperatures that a component 
might experience during machining and finishing, limiting the amount 
of post-processing which can be performed on the constructed compo -
nents. In this regard, powder metallurgy (PM) and additive 
manufacturing processes are attractive methods of producing refractory 
alloys at scale in near-net shapes. In order to consolidate these loose 
powders, techniques such as hot iso-static pressing (HIP) or direct cur -
rent sintering (DCS) which employ heat near the melting point of the 
material and external pressure to cause diffusional bonding between 
particles and removal of void space [3,4] . In the case of refractory metals 
such as tungsten, whose melting point exceeds 3400 
◦
C, the infrastruc -
ture needed to consolidate powder dies is still immense. From an alloy 
design perspective, the consolidation temperature needed for PM may 
be reduced through additions of tertiary phases. These additions have 
lower melting temperatures than the bulk material and provide inter -
facial wetting and bonding of the powder particles as well as enhance 
mass transport throughout their liquid phase, resulting in a densified 
component [5,6] . Tungsten Heavy Alloys (WHA) are commonly

A more recent advancement in powder metallurgy is the use of 
nanophase separation sintering (NPSS) to consolidate refractory alloys 
at reduced temperatures and ideally re-form a solid solution alloy 
without the complete phase separation observed in actively and liquid 
phase sintered alloys. The NPSS phenomena requires an alloy system 
that contains two immiscible phases at low temperature and a solid 
solution phase at high temperature, which is initially driven into solid 
solution through mechanical alloying and made into a green compact 
suitable for sintering. The green body is subjected to a non-isothermal 
sintering such that, initially, enough energy is provided for separation 
of the immiscible phases which coats the surface of the powder particles. 
As temperature is increased, diffusion bonding of the separated phases 
then occurs, providing an initial densification. As temperature is further 
increased above the miscibility solvus point, the dual phase consolidates 
re-forming a solid solution as a densified component and is finally 
quenched ( Fig. 1 ). The mechanical alloying performed to generate the 
alloyed powder has the added benefit of creating a nanocrystalline grain 
structure, which significantly increases the bulk diffusion rates via 
increased grain boundary density. This allows for rapid phase separation 
and re-incorporation, so long as the sintering cycle is completed in 
sufficient time [9] .
The first group of alloys which demonstrated this behavior included 
several binary refractory alloys, including the W-Cr system, and were 
investigated by Schuh et al. [10] . The W-Cr phase diagram, from which 
Fig. 1 is derived, exhibits a significant miscibility gap promoting phase 
separation at low temperatures and single-phase stability at elevated 
temperatures as described above. The particular alloy of study in this 
report is based on the W-5Cr (wt%) binary system.
One of the most detrimental prospects for using additively manu -
factured components is the high likelihood for post consolidation HIP- 
type processes needed to remove residual porosity and elemental 
segregation in the builds, adding time and complexity to the 
manufacturing processes. The NPSS pathway may provide a means for 
pressurelessly sintering components to near theoretical density, 
removing post process heat treatments altogether. The abbreviated 
thermal exposure used in NPSS also helps maintain the ultra-fine grain 
size of the mechanically alloyed powder which should improve the 
mechanical strength of the alloy and aid in low temperature ductility, a 
particular weakness in tungsten alloys [11] .
To further investigate the viability of NPSS processed W-Cr alloys for 
use in extreme environments, its physical properties at elevated tem -
peratures must be determined and compared to those produced more 
traditionally. The most readily comparative data for high temperature 
mechanical strength is high temperature compression, which will be 
carried out over the range of 900 – 1200 
◦
C. These testing conditions are 
equally valid for the expected thermal conditions where refractory al -
loys are already employed in advanced engineering assemblies [12] . 
While mechanical strength generally provides the basis for a new ma -

also pose a challenge from an oxidation standpoint. Refractory alloys 
tend to form non-protective and non-conformal oxides which ablate, 
reducing the aerodynamic and protective performance of the component

4. Discussion
NPSS alloys provide a promising future for tungsten alloys, specif -
ically in reducing the times and temperatures required to form a highly 
dense component. The average density of the various processing 
conditions was 16.25 g/cm
3 
approximately 95 % of the theoretical 
density, accounting for the large volume fraction of carbide phase. 
Compared to traditional tungsten alloys, the sintering temperatures for 
the NPSS alloys in this study are hundreds of degrees lower and the 
resulting grain sizes range from one to two orders of magnitude smaller 
[5,7,18] . Despite the reduced processing conditions, the resulting room 
temperature maximal compressive strength for the NPSS alloy (1.2 GPa) 
is still comparable to that of the WHAs (1.4 GPa) and will likely continue 
to increase as the impurities are removed and ductility improved [19] .

What cannot be overlooked is the significant quantity of carbides and 
oxides in the material as confirmed through XRD, EBSD, and APT. The 
more thermally intensive sintering of the R2 samples resulted in larger, 
globular carbide morphologies that may be akin to those graphite 
nodules seen in ductile cast irons [24] . Contrarily, the more finely 
dispersed carbide network found in R10 and R11 more closely resem -
bled that of flake type graphite in gray cast irons [24] . As has long been 
understood in ferrous metallurgy, the precipitated carbides act as 
nucleation sites for failure and the sharper interfaces associated with 
flaking may lead to stress concentrations inside the microstructure, 
resulting in a significant drop in fracture toughness and more abrupt, 
brittle failure under smaller applied loads such as what was observed in 
these tungsten alloys [25] . The ductile-to-brittle transition temperature 
for tungsten alloys is assumed to range from 25 
◦
C to 400 
◦
C [26] . 
From this work, it is unclear if the WCr alloy has increased this tem -
perature or the brittle nature is solely attributed to the carbide, oxide, 
and intermetallic networks. The effect of oxides incorporated during 
sintering or from the original powders themselves as well as iron im -
purities presumably inherited from the milling media is a common issue 
in powder metallurgy [27] and must be accounted for as these NPSS 
alloys are validated.

This study demonstrates a promising method for the fabrication of 
new tungsten alloys and composites, specifically in that NPSS provides 
an avenue for maintaining ultra fine grains in billets near their theo -

roles. The scalability of the NPSS phenomena to pressurelessly sin -
tered, complex geometries may unlock new novel alloys in the tradi -
tionally limited tungsten design space.
5. Conclusion
A WCr NPSS alloy was fabricated and evaluated for high temperature 
applications through mechanical and oxidation testing. The nano-phase 
sintering principles proved useful in densification and sintering of dense 
components at temperatures well below that of traditional tungsten al -
loys with comparable mechanical properties and yielded smaller 
average grain sizes. The addition of chromium allowed for the devel -
opment of a limited oxidation barrier that provided degradation resis -
tance up to 1000
◦
C, which is a significant improvement upon pure 
tungsten and rivals other tungsten alloy replacements. A large propor -
tion of carbon-based impurities were found within the material and were 
likely included during powder processing at the commercial vendor. 
These impurities are believed to have detrimentally effected the me -
chanical properties of the alloy and future work should aim towards 
cleaning powder precursors.
