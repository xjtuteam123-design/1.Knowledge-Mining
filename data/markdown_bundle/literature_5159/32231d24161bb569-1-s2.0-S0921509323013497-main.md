ABSTRACT 
Co-based and Ni-based superalloys have been widely used in aerospace, with much attention given to additive 
manufactured Ni-based superalloys. However, the equally important Co-based superalloys have received less 
attention. In this work, a Co-based Haynes 188 superalloy is fabricated using Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF). 
This research shows the impressive mechanical properties of Haynes 188 superalloy before and after heat 
treatment at both room and high temperatures for the first time. In the as-built specimen, arrayed stacking faults, 
Lomer – Cottrell locks, dislocations, and M
6
C/M
23
C
6 
eutectic carbides are found. The yield strength, ultimate 
tensile strength, and elongation of the as-built specimen are 642 MPa, 928 MPa, and 57.3 %, respectively. For the 
heat-treated specimen, the grain size increases, dislocation density reduces and the eutectic carbides dissolve into 
the matrix, which reduces the yield strength to 488 MPa. However, annealing twins are found in the heat-treated 
specimen, and the twin-twin interaction networks further enhance the pile-up of dislocations and work hard -
ening ability during the deformation, which leads to an increase in the ultimate tensile strength and elongation to 
1002 MPa and 68.1 %, respectively. This work provides an effective method to improve the mechanical prop -
erties of Co-based superalloys by additive manufacturing, which is essential for the development of high- 
performance superalloys and their manufacturing methods. 
1. Introduction 
The development of advanced gas turbine engines puts forward ur -
gent requirements for superalloys [ 1 ]. Co-based superalloys are broadly 
employed for gas turbine engine components such as after-burner 
components and combustor chambers, due to their excellent 
high-temperature strength, ductility, and good resistance to oxidizing 
environments [ 2 , 3 ]. With the increasing integration of gas turbine en -
gine components, traditional subtractive methods may face a great 
challenge due to time cost during the process [ 4 , 5 ]. The Co-based su -
peralloy is much more expensive than Ni-based alloys due to the high 
cost of Co. Therefore, Additive Manufacturing (AM) could have much 
more advantage in manufacturing Co-based alloys [ 6 ]. 
Additive manufacturing is a rapidly growing technology that has a 
great advantage in building complex structures with less time [ 7 ]. 
Laser-based additive manufacturing, which allows for innovative design 
and integral manufacturing, is a crucial strategic technology for inno -
vation and industrial sustainability. Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 
shows a significant feature of high manufacturing precision. As a 
near-net-shape technology, LPBF is able to create complex components 
in one step [ 8 ]. During the process of LPBF, a part is first prepared by a 
3D model, then built by melting down powders using a laser, to obtain a 
nearly full dense component [ 9 ]. 
Recently, the LPBF technology has been used to prepare superalloys 
including Inconel 625 [ 10 – 13 ], Inconel 718 [ 14 – 17 ], Hastelloy X 
[ 18 – 20 ], and Haynes 230 [ 21 , 22 ]. Most efforts are focused on elimi -
nating microcracks in these additive manufactured superalloys [ 23 , 24 ].

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure characterizations 
Fig. 2 shows laser parameters optimization maps for as-built Haynes 
188 alloys with different power and scanning speed. It is shown that 
increasing the laser energy density by decreasing the scanning speed or 
increasing the laser power will cause the splashing of powder [ 38 ]. 
Therefore, some pores appear, and the density of the as-built alloys will 
decrease. Otherwise, decreasing the laser energy density by increasing 
the scanning speed or decreasing the laser power will cause some 
powders to be un-melted. And the density of the as-built alloys will also 
be affected. Finally, a laser power of 230 W and a scanning speed of 650 
mm/s was chosen to prepare the as-built alloys. The Archimedes ’ 
method was further employed to assess the density of the specimen, 
revealing a density of 99.9 %. 
Fig. 3 shows Optical and SEM images of the as-built and heat-treated 
Haynes 188 alloys. The molten pool boundaries with typical arch-shape 
curves for as-built alloy are shown in Fig. 3 (a). Those molten pools 
overlapped along the building direction. The molten pools are with a 
width of around 90 – 150 μ m and a height of around 80 – 100 μ m. After the 
heat treatment, the trace of the molten pool boundary disappeared, as 
shown in Fig. 3 (b). Both as-built and heat-treated Haynes 188 alloys 
show fully dense microstructures without porosity or micro cracks. To 
further characterize the microstructure in more detail, SEM images of 
the specimens are shown. In Fig. 3 (c), the alloy shows epitaxial growth 
of columnar dendrites which is caused by the directional solidification 
growth during the layer-by-layer deposition process. The continuous 
growth of cellular structure and columnar dendrites cannot be impeded 
by the molten pool. The temperature gradient (G) and the grain growth 
rate (R) are two important factors in the rapid melting and solidification 
processes. The G/R ratio is able to determine the microstructure. During 
the LPBF process, a relatively high G/R ratio will lead to the formation of 
cellular structures [ 39 ]. Given that eutectic carbides are predominantly 
at the nanoscale in the as-built specimens, the constrained resolution of 
SEM hinders the effective characterization of these carbides. Conse -
quently, detailed carbide characterization has been addressed in the 
TEM section. After the heat treatment, a homogenized microstructure 
with dissolved dendrites is shown in Fig. 3 (d). Heat-treated Haynes 188 
alloy exhibits an equiaxed grain morphology, and the cellular structures 
disappeared. There are also some twins in heat Haynes 188 alloys, which 
are further confirmed by TEM. 
Fig. 4 shows the Inverse Pole Figures (IPF), Pole Figures (PF) and 
grain size distribution histograms for the as-built and heat-treated 
Haynes 188 alloys. In Fig. 4 (a), the as-built Haynes 188 alloy shows a 
columnar sub-grain structure. Solidification of columnar structure oc -
curs by epitaxial growth which starts on the un-melted layer. Due to the 
epitaxial growth from the previous layer, the coarse columnar grains 
grow across several molten pools. The epitaxial growth is typical during 
the LPBF process [ 40 – 42 ]. According to the PF maps, the as-built 
specimen shows a < 110 > //BD texture. After the heat treatment, the 
alloy features an equiaxed grain morphology and the grains are closely 
packed together, as shown in Fig. 4 (b). The < 110 > //BD texture of the 
heat-treated specimen is less obvious, and it shows a nearly random 
grain orientation distribution, which suggests that there is no strong 
texture in heat-treated Haynes 188 alloy. The change in grain size before 
and after the heat treatment has been investigated in Fig. 4 (e) – (f) in the 
present work. The results show that the grain size increased from 23.0 
μ m to 37.9 μ m after the heat treatment.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Hierarchical microstructure of as-built and heat-treated Haynes 188 
alloys 
In general, the changes in microstructure brought by heat treatment 
are responsible for changes in their mechanical properties [ 29 ]. The 
corresponding hierarchical microstructure evolution before and after 
the heat treatment is summarized in Fig. 13 . At the microscale, both the 
as-built and heat-treated specimens are predominantly composed of the 
γ phase, accompanied by a transition in grain morphology from 
columnar to equiaxed grains. At the nanoscale, the as-built samples 
exhibit a high density of dislocations, alongside the presence of carbides, 
stacking faults, and L-C locks. In the heat-treated samples, prominent 
annealing twins can be observed, along with some L-C locks and nano -
twins. The mechanism underlying the formation of the microstructure 
will be discussed in detail below.

lidification path are used to evaluate the phase formation mechanism of 
LPBF Haynes 188 alloy. In Fig. 14 (a), the liquid, γ , M

The TEM results of the heat-treated specimen after deformation are 
also shown in Fig. 17 to understand the enhancement effect of heat 
treatment on the work hardening ability and ultimate tensile strength. 
The twin-twin interaction networks between the annealing twins and 
deformation twins are found in Fig. 17 (a) – (b). The annealing twins are 
flat and straight, as shown in Fig. 17 (b), In the heat treatment process, 
the grains begin to absorb energy and go through a recrystallization 
process with the occurrence of the annealing twins [ 71 ]. With the pro -
ceed of deformation, the deformation twins will occur within the grain, 
which transforms the original unfavorable orientation into a new 
favorable orientation. The original annealing twins and deformation 
twins interact continuously to change the orientation and stimulate slip 
with the increase of ductility [ 72 ]. In addition, the reduction of residual 
stresses brought about by the annealing treatment releases the local 
stresses during the deformation of the sample, thus preventing prema -
ture fracture caused by excessive local stresses during the deformation. 
The twin-twin interaction networks between deformation twins of 
different orientations are seen in Fig. 17 (e) – (f), which contribute to the 
improvement of work hardening ability and ultimate tensile strength of 
the heat-treated specimen. A large number of deformation twins are 
generated within the austenite grains during deformation. The defor -
mation twins continuously cut the austenite matrix, and the dynamic 
Hall-Petch effect occurs, which plays the role of fine grain strengthening. 
The annealing effects on the mechanical properties of a FeCoCrNi high- 
entropy alloy prepared by selective laser melting have also been inves -
tigated [ 65 ], which shows that twin-twin interactions can effectively 
hinder dislocation and improve the ultimate tensile strength. In addi -
tion, the networks of deformation twins with different orientations 
decrease the average free range of dislocations and further hinder the 
movement of dislocations. As a result, the work-hardening ability and 
ultimate tensile strength of the Haynes 188 alloy are increased. 
5. Conclusions 
In this study, the microstructure and mechanical properties of as- 
built and heat-treated Haynes 188 alloys are investigated thoroughly. 
The main conclusions are drawn below. 
1) A crack-free Haynes 188 alloy is successfully fabricated using the 
LPBF process. Obvious micro-cracks and voids are hardly seen in the 
specimens. Columnar structures and < 110 > //BD textures are seen in 
as-built alloys, after the heat treatment, the morphology becomes 
more homogenous and the texture is weak. 
2) The as-built Haynes 188 alloy shows a high yield strength of 642 
MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 928 MPa, and an elongation of 
57.3 %, respectively. After the heat treatment, the yield strength is 
lowered to 488 MPa and ultimate tensile strength and elongation are 
increased to 1002 MPa and 68.1 %, respectively. The heat-treated 
Haynes 188 alloys have comparable mechanical properties in
