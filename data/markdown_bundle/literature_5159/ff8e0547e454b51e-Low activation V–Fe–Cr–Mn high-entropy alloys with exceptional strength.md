1. Introduction 
A cleaner, more efficient and affordable energy source is urgently 
required due to the rapid depletion of traditional non-renewable energy 
sources such as coal and oil, as well as serious energy loss, huge carbon 
emissions and environmental pollution problems [ 1 , 2 ]. Nuclear energy 
has received a wide attention as a sustainable energy source with a very 
low carbon emission [ 3 ]. For further improvements in nuclear reactor 
economics, safety, and proliferation resistance, a pre-proposal for a 
Generation IV nuclear reactor system was made at the beginning of this 
century, which was planned to be developed into a commercial 
deployment site at least by 2030 [ 4 ]. The designed Generation IV nu -
clear reactors will face more severe challenges such as higher temper -
atures, stronger neutron irradiation, and more corrosive environment 
compared to current nuclear reactors [ 5 ]. 
The alloys proposed for the structural materials of next-generation 
nuclear reactors are austenitic stainless steels, reduced activation 
ferritic/martensitic (RAFM) steels, oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) 
alloys, V alloys, etc. [ 6 – 11 ]. Austenitic stainless steels often have low 
volume swelling resistance under high temperature irradiation [ 6 ]. 
RAFM steels are prone to irradiation embrittlement and with high 
ductile-to-brittle transition temperatures after irradiation [ 7 , 8 ]. ODS 
alloys are expensive and difficult to scale up [ 9 ]. Typical V alloys 
(V – 4Cr – 4Ti) have excellent irradiation resistance but suffer from 
low-temperature irradiation embrittlement and poor high-temperature 
strength and creep resistance. These problems make them have a 
limited operation temperature window (400 – 700 
◦
C) [ 10 , 11 ]. There -
fore, it is urgent to develop new nuclear materials that can withstand the 
harsh environment of nuclear reactor systems. 
HEAs have received a lot of attention as a new type of material with 
excellent mechanical properties [ 12 – 14 ], outstanding radiation resis -
tance [ 15 – 17 ], high thermal stability [ 18 ], and significant corrosion 
resistance [ 19 ]. HEAs first came to attention in 2004 [ 20 ] and were 
originally defined as alloys of equal or near-atomic ratios containing five 
or more species of major elements [ 21 ]. In the past decade, HEAs have 
been gradually broadened in definition and are no longer limited by the 
number and ratio of elements. Additionally, their phase structure has 
become complex, including not only single-phase structures (BCC, FCC, 
HCP), but also dual-phase structures (FCC + BCC) and multi-phase 
structures containing intermetallic compounds [ 22 – 26 ]. 
Nuclear structural materials are exposed to high-energy neutron 
irradiation for a long time, making the materials undergo neutron-

The microhardness was measured by a Vickers hardness tester (FM-

data points were collected for each sample.

kN load cell. The size of the cylindrical specimens is 3 mm in diameter 
and 6 mm in height. The high-temperature compression tests were 
performed on a thermal simulation testing machine (GLEEBLE - 3500). 
The size of the cylindrical specimens is 3 mm in diameter and 3 mm in

4.1. Phase formation mechanisms 
The addition of V largely changes the thermodynamic parameters of 
HEAs, as displayed in Table 1 . The absolute value of the enthalpy of 
mixing increases significantly with the content of V, which leads to a 
substantial decrease in Ω . The present HEAs have Ω and δ values ( δ ≤
2.84%, Ω ≥ 6.09) within the range predicted for the formation of stable 
solid solutions ( δ ≤ 6.6%, Ω ≥ 1.1) [ 36 ]. However, σ and BCC phases are 
observed in the V
