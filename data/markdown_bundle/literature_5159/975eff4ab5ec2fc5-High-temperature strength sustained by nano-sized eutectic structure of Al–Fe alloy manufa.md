ABSTRACT 
This study investigated the high-temperature strength of a hypereutectic Al – Fe binary alloy (Al – 15%Fe) additive- 
manufactured by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF). The L-PBF Al – 15%Fe alloy exhibited superior yield strength to 
the other existing L-PBF processed alloys due to α -Al/Al
6
Fe eutectic structure with a nanoscale cellular 
morphology. The present results demonstrated the feasibility of using L-PBF processed Al – Fe alloys as high- 
temperature lightweight materials. 
1. Introduction 
Additive manufacturing (AM) technology, also referred to as 3D 
printing, is a description for advanced fabrication technologies that 
enable the automated manufacture of complex 3D geometries layer-by- 
layer using computer-aided design (CAD) [ 1 , 2 ]. Laser powder bed fusion 
(L-PBF) [ 3 ] is the most common additive manufacturing technology for 
a variety of metals [ 4 , 5 ], including lightweight aluminum alloys [ 6 – 9 ]. 
The L-PBF process for producing a 3D component consists of selective 
laser melting of raw material, followed by rapid solidification. This 
combination facilitates the significant refinement of microstructures 
[ 10 – 13 ]. At ambient temperature, the strength of L-PBF processed ma -
terials is much superior to that of materials manufactured by conven -
tional processes [ 14 – 16 ]. Currently, most “ processable ” Al alloys are 
commercial cast Al – Si alloys [ 10 , 17 – 20 ]. However, the strengths of 
these L-PBF processed alloys (e.g., AlSi10Mg [ 17 , 21 ]) decrease at tem -
peratures above 150 
◦
C. Hence, the development of novel Al alloys with 
high-temperature strength and processability is necessary for additive 
manufacturing of geometrically complex components for 
high-temperature applications (e.g., radial impellers in compressor 
system for turbochargers). The development of many high-temperature 
Al alloys (e.g., Al – Ni – Fe, Al – Ce – Mn, Al – Mg – Si( – Cu) – Sc – Zr) has recently 
been reported in the literature [ 22 ]. In a similar attempt to develop 
heat-resistant Al alloys, we employed L-PBF processes to manufacture 
binary Al – Fe alloy samples with near-eutectic and hypereutectic com -
positions [ 13 , 23 ]. Both resulting Al – Fe alloys exhibited refined 
microstructures containing nanoscale intermetallic Al – Fe phases [ 13 , 
23 ], with the alloy with the near-eutectic composition (Al – 2.5%Fe) 
exhibiting a relatively high strength ( > 300 MPa) at ambient tempera -
ture [ 24 ]. Intriguingly, long-term incubation at 300 
◦
C had a negligible 
effect on the strength of the alloy [ 24 ], implying that Al – Fe alloys pro -
duced by L-PBF processes are suitable for sustained operation at 
elevated temperatures. Given that its hardness is twice as high as that of 
the Al – 2.5%Fe alloy (approximately 200 HV) [ 23 ], the hypereutectic 
alloy (Al – 15%Fe) demonstrates the potential for superior mechanical 
performance at elevated temperatures. In the present study, we inves -
tigate the high-temperature strength of the L-PBF-processed Al – 15%Fe 
alloy further using a compression test, and identify the mechanism by 
which its strength is maintained at elevated temperatures using trans -
mission electron microscopy (TEM). Based on these experiments, we 
discuss the potential use of Al – Fe alloys as novel heat-resistant Al alloys 
suitable for additive manufacturing by L-PBF. 
2. Materials and methods

produce alloy powder with a hypereutectic composition of 15 mass% Fe 
in an Al – Fe binary system. From our measurements, the proportion of 
the chemical compositions (Fe: 15.2 mass% and Si: 0.09 mass%) in the 
initial powder was almost the same as that in the L-PBF processed 
samples. Rectangular alloy samples (approximate size: 15 mm × 15 mm 
× 10 mm) were manufactured using a metal 3D printing machine (3D

3. Results and discussion 
The representative multi-scale structure of the L-PBF processed Al – Fe 
alloy sample is presented in Fig. 1 . A microstructure indicating multiple 
overlapping melt pools can be observed at low magnifications ( Fig. 1 
(a)). No crack was found in the melt-pool structure of the Al – Fe alloy 
sample manufactured by optimized L-PBF process conditions [ 25 ] 
( Fig. 1 (b)). Examination at a higher magnification reveals the presence 
of numerous fine particles of Al – Fe intermetallic phases distributed 
homogeneously inside the melt pools, with relatively coarse particles 
localized along the melt-pool boundaries ( Fig. 1 (c)). These coarsened 
particles have been identified as θ -Al
13
Fe
4 
( mS 102) [ 26 ] by electron 
backscattered diffraction (EBSD) analysis [ 23 ]. Higher-resolution char -
acterization using X-ray analysis and TEM revealed a number of 
spherical Al
6
Fe particles with an orthorhombic structure ( oC28 [ 27 ]), 
ranging in size from approximately 50 nm – 400 nm. These particles were 
surrounded by nanoscale cells corresponding to the eutectic α -Al/Al
6
Fe 
microstructures inside the melt pools ( Fig. 1 (d)). We rationalize the 
presence of distinct structural characteristics at different length scales 
using the solidification sequence to form stable or metastable alloy 
phases. According to the phase diagrams calculated for the binary Al – Fe 
system ( Fig. 1 (e)), the θ -Al
13
Fe
4 
phase is formed in equilibrium condi -
tions, while the Al
6
Fe phase is formed in non-equilibrium conditions 
[ 28 ]. The sequence for the formation of the metastable Al
6
Fe phase and 
the stable θ -Al
13
Fe
4 
phase during the L-PBF process has been described 
in detail elsewhere [ 23 ]. 
Plots illustrating the compressive properties of the L-PBF-processed 
Al – Fe alloy specimens at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 2 . 
Representative nominal stress-plastic strain curves measured by 
compression tests are presented in Fig. 2 (a). The elastic loading region 
was estimated from the raw test data by assuming uniform deformation 
in the compressed specimens. At room temperature, yield occurs at 
approximately 540 MPa, followed by strain hardening. The specimen 
fractured at a plastic strain of 1.4% at this temperature. Although the 
yield strength of the alloy specimens decreased as the test temperature 
increased, the yield stress remained above 300 MPa at a temperature of 
300 
◦
C. The strain-hardening rate of the specimens appeared reduced at 
higher temperatures. A slight reduction in stress can be observed after 
the maximum, potentially because of the collapse of local pores and the 
formation of localized shear bands during compression. To compare 
their mechanical performances, we plotted the 0.2% proof stress of the 
alloys produced in this study as a function of test temperature, together 
with data for powder-metallurgy (PM)-based Al – Fe alloys [ 14 , 29 , 30 ] 
and L-PBF-processed AlSi10Mg (the most commonly-used Al alloy in 
L-PBF) [ 17 ] from the literature, as shown in Fig. 2 (b). At all test tem -
peratures, the L-PBF-processed Al – Fe alloy exhibited higher strength 
than the PM-processed alloy series [ 14 , 29 , 30 ]. In addition, the yield 
strength of the Al – 15Fe alloy (~340 MPa at 300 
◦
C) is superior to that of 
other Al-based multi-element alloys manufactured using L-PBF pro -
cesses (Al – 14Mg – 0.5Si – 0.3Sc – 0.2Zr: 120 MPa [ 31 ], Al – 5.7Ni: 140 MPa 
[ 16 ], Al – 10Ce – 8Mn: 150 MPa [ 32 ], Al – 12Si – 1.4Fe – 1.4Ni: 270 MPa 
[ 33 ]). These results clearly show that L-PBF processed Al – Fe alloys have 
a high potential to be employed in high-temperature applications.

6

◦

6

long-term exposure ( Fig. 4 (a)). In the aspect of practical applications, it 
is required to sustain the high strength of the materials after long-term 
exposure at elevated temperatures (in service). In order to maintain 
the sufficient strength of the L-PBF processed Al – Fe alloy even after the 
prolonged thermal incubation, it would be necessary to stabilize the 
metastable Al
6
Fe phase for improving the thermal stability of the 
nanoscale cellular α -Al/Al
6
Fe structure in the L-PBF processed Al – Fe 
alloy. As the metastable Al
6
Fe phase in this cellular morphology

◦
C 
( Fig. 4 (b and c)), it could be necessary to modify the alloy composition 
to improve the phase stability and the L-PBF processability of Al – Fe 
based alloys. 
4. Conclusions 
In summary, this study has demonstrated the feasibility of using L- 
PBF processed Al – Fe alloys as high-temperature lightweight materials. 
The high-temperature strength of the hypereutectic L-PBF processed 
binary Al – Fe alloy was much superior to those of Al alloys manufactured 
using conventional powder metallurgy [ 14 , 29 , 30 ], as well as of other 
L-PBF processed Al alloys [ 17 ]. Our results indicate that the nanoscale 
cellular structure corresponding to the eutectic α -Al/Al
6
Fe phases 
(formed by rapid solidification during the L-PBF process) could be 
responsible for the superior high-temperature strength. However, the 
degradation of strength after the long-term exposure at high tempera -
tures would be attributed to the moderately low thermal stability of the 
metastable Al
6
Fe phase in the Al – Fe binary alloys. In order to improve 
the thermal stability of the nanoscale Al
6
Fe phase for strengthening, it 
could be required to optimize Fe content or modify the alloy composi -
tion (e.g., adding third elements) of Al – Fe based alloys for the L-PBF 
process.
