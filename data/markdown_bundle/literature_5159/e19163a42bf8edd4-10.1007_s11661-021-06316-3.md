Effect of Ti Addition and Microstructural Evolution
on Toughening and Strengthening Behavior
of as Cast or Annealed Nb–Si–Mo Based
Hypoeutectic and Hypereutectic Alloys
KASTURI SALA and RAHUL MITRA
Room temperature fracture toughness along with compressive deformation behavior at both
room and high temperatures (900 /C176 C, 1000 /C176 C and 1100 /C176 C) has been evaluated for ternary or
quaternary hypoeutectic (Nb–12Si–5Mo and Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti) and hypereutectic
(Nb–19Si–5Mo and Nb–19Si–5Mo–20Ti) Nb-silicide based intermetallic alloys to examine
the eﬀects of composition, microstructure, and annealing (100 hours at 1500 /C176 C). On Ti-addition
and annealing, the fracture toughness has increased by up to ~ 75 and ~ 63 pct, respectively with
~ 14 MPa/C214 m being recorded for the annealed Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti alloy. Toughening is ascribed
to formation of non-lamellar eutectic with coarse Nb ss, which contributes to crack path
tortuosity by bridging, arrest, branching and deﬂection of cracks. The room temperature
compressive strengths are found as ~ 2200 to 2400 MPa for as-cast alloys, and ~ 1700 to 2000
MPa after annealing with the strength reduction being higher for the hypoeutectic compositions
due to larger Nb ss content. Further, the compressive ductility has varied from 5.7 to 6.5 pct. The
fracture surfaces obtained from room temperature compression tests have revealed evidence of
brittle failure with cleavage facets and river patterns in Nb ss along with its decohesion at
non-lamellar eutectic. The compressive yield stress decreases with increase in test temperature,
with the hypoeutectic alloys exhibiting higher strength retention indicating the predominant role
of solid solution strengthening of Nb ss. The ﬂow curves obtained from high temperature
compression tests show initial work hardening, followed by a steady state regime indicating
dynamic recovery involving the formation of low angle grain boundaries in the Nb ss,a s
conﬁrmed by electron backscattered diﬀraction of the annealed Nb–12Si–5Mo alloy compres-
sion tested at 1100 /C176 C.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE necessity for high temperature materials to be
used in high pressure jet engines with the maximum
operating temperature exceeding that of the Ni-based
superalloys has led to the development of refractory
metal silicide based intermetallic alloys such as Nb–Si
based alloys. [1] The two-phase microstructure in Nb–Si
alloys constituted by Nb 5Si3 and Nb ss (Nb-rich solid
solution) is believed to possess superior stability at high
temperatures.[2] Apart from having a relatively lower
density than Ni, the silicide phase in the Nb–Si alloys
contributes to creep and oxidation resistance at higher
temperature, whereas the Nb ss phase imparts fracture
toughness at room temperature. [3] Although the Nb–Si
alloys possess very high melting temperatures, their
applications are limited at room temperature due to the
inherent brittleness of the Nb 5Si3 phase. In addition, the
poor high temperature oxidation resistance is also a
major hurdle limiting the wide-spread application of this
alloy. As expected, the roadmaps for strengthening and
toughening are found to contradict one another in the
Nb-based alloys. [4] Achieving a balance between the
high-temperature strength, room temperature plasticity
and the toughness of the Nb based alloys is considered
as one of the most important challenges so far, so that
these materials are suitable for engineering applications.
Tailored microstructure with desirable combination of
mechanical properties at various temperatures is
achieved through the choice of suitable processing
method as well as optimized additions of speciﬁc

The addition of Mo not only contributes to toughen-
ing of the Nb–Si based alloys in the range of 5.6 to 8.8
MPapm by microstructural reﬁnement along with solid
solution strengthening, [11,12] but also improves the high

of Ti to the Nb–Si based alloys and by using directional
solidiﬁcation processing or hot extrusion, a signiﬁcant
improvement in fracture toughness has been
achieved.[14] For example, the addition of 10 at. pct Ti
to the Nb–20Si alloy has led to an enhanced fracture

adjusting and optimizing the concentration of Ti and
Mo in the Nb–Si alloys having both hypoeutectic and
hypereutectic compositions are considered to be bene-
ﬁcial for enhancement of both fracture toughness and
high temperature strength without aﬀecting the brittle to
ductile transition temperature (BDTT). [15]
A signiﬁcant research has been carried out with the
objective of attaining both high-temperature strength
and thermal stability of the Nb–Si alloys, by triggering
the partitioning of alloying elements (W or Mo) and
preferential stabilization of the desirable phase. [14,16]
Adding Mo and W have caused signiﬁcant solid solution
strengthening of Nb ss with the compressive strength of
Nb–18Si–10Ti–10Mo–15W prepared by directional
solidiﬁcation reaching 650 MPa at 1770 K. [14] It may
be noted that the strength of Nb–10Si alloy is about 400
MPa at 1470 K, and when the Si content is increased to
18 mol pct, along with simultaneous addition of 22 mol
pct Ti, the compressive yield strengths of about 1000
and 450 MPa have been recorded at 1470 K and 1690
K,[17] respectively. However, due to the presence of
brittle or semi-brittle intermetallic silicide phases, the
deformation and fracture behavior are reported to be
much more complicated. [18,19]
The microstructure obtained in the silicide-based
alloy after high temperature deformation contains the
signature of the dominant softening mechanisms, which
in turn depends on deformation conditions (tempera-
tures, strain rates, and strain) and the intrinsic worka-
bility.[20] Dynamic recovery is indicated by the
formation of subgrains within the deformed areas in
the microstructure, whereas dynamic recrystallization
usually results in the formation of new strain-free and
reﬁned grains.[21,22] Macroscopic cracks observed on the
fracture surfaces of the deformed samples can be a result
of adiabatic shear and ﬂow localization resulting in ﬂow
instability, which is also considered as one of the
softening mechanisms. [23] In an earlier study on the
high-temperature deformation of the Nb–10Si alloy, the
post-deformation microstructure examined by transmis-
sion electron microscopy has exhibited the presence of
subgrains in Nb ss and silicide phases. [24] Also, recrystal-
lized grains of Nb ss have been reported to develop in the
Nb–Si–Zr alloy subjected to high temperature deforma-
tion, based on the results of electron backscattered
diﬀraction (EBSD) analysis. [25] By EBSD analysis, it has
been possible to obtain a signiﬁcant insight into the
deformation mechanisms of the hypoeutectic alloys
containing Nb ss as the primary phase. [26]
Based on the survey of literature, it is noted that
limited work has focused on the high-temperature
deformation of ternary or quaternary Nb–Si–Mo based
alloys. In the present study, Ti has been chosen as the
alloying element for the Nb–Si–Mo system with Si
content of ~ 12 or 19 at. pct for comparative evaluation
of the deformation and fracture behavior of both
hypoeutectic or hypereutectic alloy compositions. The
investigated alloys were either as-cast or annealed at
1500 /C176 C for 100 hours, such that the eﬀect of
microstructural variation in these two conditions could
be investigated. The detailed results of comparative
study on microstructure, compositions and volume
fractions of constituent phases, microhardness of pri-
mary phase and eutectic, bulk hardness as well as
indentation fracture toughness in as-cast or variously
annealed conditions (60 or 100 hours at 1500 /C176 C) may be
found in a couple of recent publications. [12,27] Fracture
toughness tests have been carried out at the ambient
temperature with examination of crack paths and
micromechanisms of failure. Additionally, the compres-
sive deformation behavior of the alloys in as-cast or
annealed condition at both ambient and high temper-
atures (900 to 1100 /C176 C) has been investigated and
compared. The microstructural changes leading to
dynamic softening of the investigated alloys during high
temperature compression tests have been investigated
through the studies of post-deformation microstructure
and the orientation of the constituent phases (Nb ss). The
purpose of this work is to understand how the evolution
of microstructure aﬀects fracture toughness along with
failure mechanisms at room temperature, as well as
compressive strength, deformation and fracture behav-
ior at either room or high temperatures.
II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
A. Materials and Processing
The nominal compositions of both as-cast and
annealed (1500 /C176 C/100 hours) samples are presented in
Table I. High purity elemental powders (details given in
the earlier publications by the authors [12,27]) were
carefully mixed in appropriate proportions to obtain
the aforementioned compositions for 1 hour using
mortar and pestle. Subsequently, the powder mixtures
were cold-compacted to form pellets having nearly 10
mm diameter and approximately 8 mm height at a
pressure of 16 MPa. Several such pellets were next
subjected to arc-melting in argon atmosphere inside a
water-cooled copper crucible with the help of non-con-
sumable tungsten electrode. The ingots thus formed
were turned and remelted at least ﬁve times inside the
crucible to achieve chemical homogeneity. Annealing
was carried out in vacuum (10 /C0 5 Torr) on some of the
as-cast specimens in a horizontal tube furnace, having
argon atmosphere and annealed at 1500 /C176 C for 100
hours, to obtain equilibrium and homogeneity. For this
purpose, the samples wrapped in a Ta foil were placed

B. Microstructural Characterization and Evaluation
of Mechanical Properties
The samples for observation of microstructures and
mechanical testing were sectioned from the same place
in each alloy ingot by electro-discharge machining
(EDM). Microstructural studies were carried out on
metallographically polished and etched specimens of all
the alloys; the details of which are already described in a
couple of earlier publications. [12,27] The compositions of
the individual phases were analyzed by using wavelength
dispersive X-ray (WDS) spectroscopy on an electron
probe microanalyzer (EPMA, model JXA8230 super-
probe, JEOL, Japan). The quantitative analyses of the
constituent phases were carried out with the help of
EPMA by using high purity Nb, Si, Mo and Ti as
standards along with conventional matrix corrections
(ZAF).
Fracture toughness tests were carried out by following
the ASTM E399 [28] standard testing procedure, on
un-precracked single edge notch bend (SENB) speci-
mens having a 24 mm span length (total length = 30
mm), 6 mm width and 3 mm thickness. A straight notch
of about ~ 3 mm depth was inserted into each specimen
using EDM with 0.1 mm diameter wire. The fracture
toughness tests were performed on a Schimadzu Univer-
sal Testing Machine using a fabricated three-point bend
ﬁxture at a nominal crosshead speed of 0.1 mm/min in
air.
In order to determine the strength and ductility of
these alloys, uniaxial compression tests were carried out
on cylindrical specimens having 6 mm height and 4 mm
diameter at room temperature till fracture as well as at
elevated temperatures of 900 /C176 C, 1000 /C176 C and 1100 /C176 Ci n
open-air up to a strain of 0.1 at a strain rate of 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1
in a fast response hydraulic machine (Dartec with 100
kN capacity). The samples subjected to compression
tests had a height to diameter ratio of 1.5. Both ends of
the cylindrical compression samples were polished ﬂat
prior to the experiments. Further, the sample surfaces
were coated with rosin grease for compression tests for
lubrication at room temperature, and with a mixture of
powdered glass wool and ethanol to reduce friction and
avoid extensive oxidation of the samples during expo-
sure at high temperatures. The samples were heated to
the test temperature inside a resistance-heating furnace,
with its temperature being controlled by a thermocouple
feedback-controlled ac current. A K-type thermocouple
was kept near the sample surface to monitor and control
the test temperature within ± 1 /C176 C. The samples were
heated at a heating rate of 20 /C176 C per second to the test
temperature, and soaked for 3 min. Subsequently, the
samples were deformed in compression to a strain of 0.1,
and the compressive yield strength along with the
engineering strain values at these temperatures for all
the alloys were determined. The net displacement of the
specimen ends was used to calculate the compressive
strain. The deformed specimens were then quenched in
water to preserve the microstructure prevailing during
high temperature compressive deformation.
The fracture surfaces and post-deformation
microstructures of the compression-tested samples were
studied using a ﬁeld emission scanning electron micro-
scope (FESEM) (Carl Zeiss, Germany, Model: Supra 40
FESEM) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray
(EDS) micro-analyzer (Oxford Instruments Ltd., UK
Model: ISIS300). The FESEM was operated at an
acceleration voltage of 20 kV and with a working
distance in the range of 10-13 mm. The compres-
sion-tested specimens were sectioned longitudinally
parallel to the loading direction, and subsequently
polished mechanically using SiC-coated emery paper
(mesh-size varying from 800 to 3000). Final surface
preparation was carried out by polishing on cloth using
a suspension of colloidal silica, followed by ultrasonic
cleaning. The microstructures of the samples were
investigated using electron backscatter diﬀraction
(EBSD) technique in a ﬁeld emission scanning electron
microscope (FESEM) operated at 25 kV. Scan area and
step size were chosen on the basis of grain size. The scan
parameters were kept identical for all the alloys. A
TSL-OIM data collector and analysis software were
used for EBSD data acquisition and analysis.

C. Room Temperature Fracture Toughness
The bar charts depicting fracture toughness (K IC)
values obtained from three-point bend tests carried out
at room temperature for as-cast and annealed samples
are shown in Figure 3. The fracture toughness values of
the investigated as-cast samples are found to vary within
a wide range, 5.4 to 10.5 MPa pm, whereas those of the
annealed samples lie in the range of 8 to 14 MPa pm,
indicating a strong inﬂuence of both alloying and
annealing. The following inferences can be drawn from
the results in Figure 3: (i) Ti addition has resulted in a
sharp rise in fracture toughness of both as cast hypoeu-
tectic and hypereutectic compositions by nearly ~ 75 pct,
and (ii) the increase is more prominent after annealing
of all the investigated alloys. Compared to the as cast
condition, the Nb–12Si–5Mo–HT alloy has shown an
increment of ~63 pct, whereas the highest value of
fracture toughness has been exhibited by the
Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti–HT alloy (14 MPa pm). On the
other hand, increments of ~ 52 and ~ 17 pct have been
observed in the case of Nb–19Si–5Mo–HT and
Nb–19Si–5Mo–20Ti–HT alloys, respectively.
The crack initiation and propagation paths from the
notches in the cross-section images of the specimens
having failed in fracture toughness tests, are depicted in
Figures 4(a) through (f). It can be observed that in all
the cases, once the crack is initiated from the notch, it
is not necessarily propagated in a direction perpendic-
ular to the stress axis, indicating multiple deviations
from mode-I propagation. For instance, it can be
observed that the crack has propagated following a
zig-zag path characterized by frequent deﬂections in the
Nb–12Si–5Mo alloy (Figure 4(a)), while crack branch-
ing has been primarily observed in the
Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti alloy, shown in Figure 4(b). On
the other hand, the crack propagation paths in the
Nb–19Si–5Mo–20Ti alloy reveals deﬂections observed
at the interfaces between Nb ss and 5-3 silicide phases as
observed in Figure 4(c). For the annealed hypoeutectic
alloys, the crack path is found to be more tortuous as
the crack is deﬂected and branched more often,
absorbing more energy (Figures 4(d) through (f)). The
occurrence of crack branching may be attributed to the
interface between Nb ss and 5-3 silicide, providing an
alternative and energetically favorable way for the
crack to propagate. A major contribution to the
observed deﬂection of crack path (Figure 4(d)) comes
from the large diﬀerence between the elastic modulus
values of Nb ss and 5-3 silicide phases. In addition to
this, the increase in the size of the primary dendritic
Nbss phase observed after annealing has enhanced the
frequency of bridging of the crack during its propaga-
tion. Similar observations are found for Nb–12Si–5-
Mo–HT and Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti–HT alloys, where the
crack follows a straight path in the presence of 5-3
silicide phase, and is hindered by the presence of
coarsened non-lamellar eutectic mixture, or meanders
around the interface, as the Nb ss phase appears to form
a continuous network around the 5-3 silicide phase as
shown in Figures 4(d) through (f). Earlier studies have
also reported that the alignment and distribution of
tougher phases play a signiﬁcant role in hindering the
crack propagation. [30] Thus, it is inferred that the
interphase interfaces in non-lamellar eutectic, as well as
morphology, and sizes of the primary Nb ss dendrites
along with their volume fractions play an important
role in enhancing the fracture resistance of the inves-
tigated alloys.
The mechanism of fracture has been analyzed in
detail by examining the fracture surfaces presented in
Figure 5. The Nb ss phase of the examined alloys
exhibits distinct signature of cleavage fracture such as
river lines, cleavage steps and plastic stretching, while
the 5-3 silicide phase mainly exhibits smooth facets
without traces of plastic deformation (brittle failure) as
shown in Figures 5(a) through (d). Additionally,
microcrack formation has been observed in the brittle
5-3-silicide phase, as shown in Figure 5(a). Further, the
decohesion occurring at the Nb ss/5-3 silicide interface
as shown in Figure 5(c), can be attributed to the
increase in stress concentration causing their separation
from each other. Such decohesion at the Nb ss/5-3
silicide interfaces appears to have resulted in crack
deﬂection and branching, as observed in the cross-sec-
tional images depicted in Figure 4. However, the Nb ss
phase in the non-lamellar eutectic mixture undergoes
plastic stretching as observed in Figure 5(d), which
may be considered as an indication of limited ductility.
The small-sized facets may be considered to have
originated by the fracture of non-lamellar eutectic
mixture along with interphase separation. On the other
hand, relatively larger cleavage facets have been
observed on the fractured brittle 5-3 silicide phase
(see Figures 5(b), (c) and (d)).

IV. DISCUSSION
The results of the present study indicate that the
mechanical properties including fracture toughness and
high temperature strength retention have a direct
correlation with both composition and volume fraction
of the constituent phases in the microstructures of the
investigated Nb–Si–Mo based alloys.
A. Toughening Behavior at Room Temperature
Based on the measured values of room temperature
yield strength and fracture toughness obtained for the
investigated as cast or annealed alloys, the critical
specimen thickness required for a valid plane strain
fracture toughness test according to the standard ASTM
399-70T[34] is found from the relation, B = 2.5 * (K IC/
r0.2)2 to be between ~ 0.1 mm and ~ 0.2 mm. As these
values are less than the specimen thickness used for the
present study by an order of magnitude, the thickness of
the specimens used in the present study for fracture
toughness measurements is considered as appropriate
for plane-strain condition during crack initiation. Fur-
ther, the notch depth/width was ~ 0.5, according to the
aforementioned standard. Due to the formation of a
sharp crack in the brittle phase present ahead of the
notch-tip, with increase in tensile stress during applica-
tion of load, fatigue pre-cracking is often not considered
as necessary for fracture toughness tests on brittle
materials. Therefore, the results of the fracture tough-
ness tests carried out in the present study are considered
to be the material properties.
As expected, the trend observed for variation of
fracture toughness with alloy composition or annealing
in the present study is qualitatively very similar to that
observed for the indentation fracture toughness of the
investigated alloys, as reported by the same authors in
earlier publications. [12,27] The qualitative similarity of
the trend observed in the present study with that of
indentation fracture toughness can of course be ascribed
to identical micro-mechanisms of toughening being
operative. However, the results of fracture toughness
tests on the SENB specimens are found to be less than
those obtained through indentation by ~ 19 to 49 pct,
due to the heterogeneities of microstructures aﬀecting
the latter data to a larger extent.
The fracture toughness of the hypereutectic as cast
alloys are found to be lower than those having hypoeu-
tectic composition due to the presence of brittle primary
5-3 silicide phase in the former type of alloy. It is also
noticed that the fracture toughness observed for ternary
hypoeutectic and hypereutectic alloys has increased on
annealing by 63.3 and 51.9 pct, respectively. Further-
more, addition of 20 at. pct Ti has led to even more
signiﬁcant increase in the fracture toughness of both
as-cast hypoeutectic ( ~ 75.2 pct) and hypereutectic ( ~
74.1 pct) alloys, which is attributed to the changes in
microstructure with formation of either higher amount
of Nbss or non-lamellar eutectic along with formation of
b-Tiss (Table III). However, in case of the annealed
alloys, the diﬀerence in fracture toughness values caused
by Ti addition is found to be relatively less, i.e. an
increase of ~ 42.9 pct for the hypoeutectic composition
and ~ 34.1 pct for the hypereutectic composition, which
may be ascribed to the formation of b-Tiss along with
diﬀerences between the amounts of Nb ss or non-lamellar
eutectic, as well as possible intrinsic toughening of the
5-3-silicide phase as discussed later in this sec-
tion. Higher amount of coarsened primary ductile Nb ss
phase along with the non-lamellar eutectic has con-
tributed by arrest or bridging of the propagating cracks,
as illustrated in Figure 4. In the hypereutectic alloys, the
Nbss phase of the non-lamellar eutectic mixture appears
to have mainly contributed to the observed increase in
fracture toughness.
Chan et al.[35] have evaluated the dislocation mobility
inside both Nb ss and 5-3 silicide phase by computing the
Peierls–Nabarro (P–N) barrier energy (U P–N), a higher
value of which means relatively lower dislocation
mobility. Their results show that the U P-N of unalloyed
5-3 silicide phase is 9.8 J/m 2, whereas that of the
unalloyed Nb is lower by an order of magnitude ( ~1J /
m2),[34,35] which suggests that the activation energy
required for dislocation motion in the 5-3 silicide phase
is much higher than that in the Nb ss. It is well-estab-
lished that alloying Nb with Ti, lowers the anisotropic
shear modulus and (P-N) energy, thereby easing the
dislocation motion in the Nb ss.[15,35–37] Further, a study
by Chan et al has shown that the elastic modulus of
(Nb,Ti)5Si3 is less than that of Nb 5Si3, and decreases
further with increase in Ti content, which has been
ascribed to reduction of the bond order and d-orbital
energy levels of Nb(Ti)–Si atom clusters. In addition to
this, the complex stacking fault energy of the 5-3 silicide
is also reduced on alloying with Ti as shown by Chan
et al. ,[38,39] which in turn is considered to enhance the
fracture toughness. In this context, it has been proposed
that the ease of [001] partial dislocation mobility may
have enhanced the fracture toughness of the 5-3 sili-
cide.[40] Furthermore, a study by Wang and Pugh has
shown that the ratio of shear modulus to bulk modulus
(G/K, Pugh’s ratio) is also a deciding factor for fracture
toughness, and its value for the a-5-3 silicide is greater
than that for the b-5-3 silicide. [41] Hence, the increase in
the fracture toughness value on annealing of all the
investigated alloys may also be ascribed to the trans-
formation of b to a-5-3 silicide. Thus, the microstruc-
tural evolution during both processing and subsequent
annealing may be considered to have a signiﬁcant eﬀect
on fracture toughness.

B. Strengthening Behavior at Room Temperature
The relatively higher values of room temperature
compressive strength observed for the hypereutectic
as-cast alloys compared to those having hypoeutectic
compositions is attributed to the presence of hard
primary 5-3 silicide phase. Additionally, the presence
of lamellar eutectic mixture of Nb ss and 5-3 silicide
phases may be considered to have contributed to the
strength (2403 ± 10 MPa) of the Nb–19Si–5Mo alloy,
which is found as the highest at the ambient tempera-
ture. Within the lamellar eutectic, the presence of hard
and undeformable 5-3 silicide phase possessing complex
crystal structure and high Peierls stress creates a
constraint for plastic deformation of the ductile Nb ss
phase, leading to cleavage fracture with characteristic
facet as shown in Figure 7. As a result, the mean-free
path for dislocation motion within the ductile Nb ss
constituent is small. It is also intuitive that greater the
interfacial area between ductile Nb ss and 5-3 silicide
phases, stronger will be the inhibition to the movement
of dislocations. The spherodization of eutectic con-
stituents with conversion to non-lamellar morphology
due to annealing (discussed in detail earlier [12,27])i s
expected to lower the constraint for plastic deformation,
thereby contributing to the reduction of compressive
strength values of the investigated alloys.
The alloys possessing hypoeutectic compositions
(Nb–12Si–5Mo and Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti) with higher
amounts of ductile Nb ss phase (including the primary
phase constituent) have exhibited relatively lower values
of compressive strength, compared to the hypereutectic
alloys. Higher compressive strength of the Nb–12Si–5-
Mo–20Ti alloy than that recorded for the Nb–12Si–5Mo
alloy, despite having lower amounts of lamellar eutectic
(Table III) can be attributed not only to the presence of
relatively higher fraction of 5-3 silicides ( ~ 2.2 pct as
shown in Table III), but also the solid solution strength-
ening of the primary Nb ss by greater concentration
(diﬀerence of ~ 1.8 pct) of Mo present in it (Table II).
Moreover, the amount of primary Nb ss in the Ti-con-
taining quaternary alloy is higher than that in the
ternary Nb–12Si–5Mo alloy by ~ 23.2 pct (Table III).
The primary Nb ss is expected to deform and strain
harden by a larger amount owing to lower constraint
experienced within the eutectic, and therefore its solid
solution strengthening is considered to have contributed

alloy. Moreover, the microhardness of the eutectic

C. Flow Softening Behavior at Elevated Temperature
1. Variation of stress–strain behaviour and ﬂow stress
with alloy composition
Interestingly, the typical compressive stress strain
curves for all the as cast alloys compression-tested at 900
/C176 C to 1100 /C176 C exhibit evidence of limited work harden-
ing before steady-state behavior or softening is achieved.
Reaching of the steady state regime may be ascribed to
the process of dynamic recovery in the ductile Nb ss
phase, after the maximum limit of work-hardening is
reached.
Contrary to the behavior observed for most of the
alloys compression-tested at 900 /C176 C, signiﬁcant soften-
ing has been noticed after the maximum stress is reached
in case of the Nb–19Si–5Mo alloy, as shown in
Figure 9(a). Based on the results shown in Table III,
the volume fraction of the ductile Nb ss in the
Nb–19Si–5Mo is lower and the amount of net 5-3
silicide is higher with adequate amount of lamellar
eutectic mixture than that in other investigated alloys,
which suggests that the constraint to the plastic defor-
mation is maximum in this alloy. Therefore, lowering of
the ﬂow stress observed beyond a maximum value in
case of the Nb–19Si–5Mo alloy during compression-test
at 900 /C176 C may be ascribed to cracking of the brittle
5-3-silicide phase similar to that depicted in
Figure 10(b). Interestingly, during compression test at
1000 /C176 C (Figure 9(b)), the transition from the stage of
work hardening to steady-state is observed for the
Nb–19Si–5Mo alloy at a lower stress compared to that
for rest of the alloys. Owing to the maximum constraint
imposed by the brittle phase, the maximum limit of
work-hardening may have been reached in the Nb ss
grains of the Nb–19Si–5Mo alloy at a relatively lower
stress during compression test at 1000 /C176 C, which may
have triggered dynamic recovery. The process of
dynamic recovery is known to require a suﬃciently high
dislocation density for generation of inter-dislocation
stresses, whose magnitude should be large enough to
trigger their rearrangement in low angle boundaries. [58]
At 1100 /C176 C, the values of ﬂow stress recorded at a
given strain for the alloys having hypereutectic compo-
sition are found to be less than those observed for the
hypoeutectic alloys, with the observed diﬀerence being
greater after annealing compared to that in as-cast
condition (Figure 9(f)). Such a trend may be ascribed to
the strong role played by work hardening of the
solid-solution hardened primary Nb ss phase in the
hypoeutectic alloys towards compressive ﬂow. The
SEM images depicting the microstructures of the sam-
ples subjected to compression test at 1100 /C176 C indicate
evidence for arrest of cracks generated in the brittle 5-3
silicide phase by the neighboring ductile Nb ss (as shown
in Figure 10(e)), which is further suggestive of plastic
deformation with work-hardening ahead of crack tip.
Moreover, decohesion of coarse Nb ss in the non-lamel-
lar eutectic of the hypereutectic Nb–19Si–5Mo–20-
Ti–HT alloy is expected to have permitted partially
unconstrained plastic ﬂow in this phase, which may have
also contributed to work-hardening.
2. Variation of high temperature strength retention
with alloy composition
The plots in Figure 9(e) indicate a signiﬁcant decrease
in the values of compressive yield strength with increas-
ing temperature (900 /C176 C to 1100 /C176 C), which can be
ascribed to thermally activated deformation of the
bcc-Nbss phase. The strength retention has been quan-
tiﬁed in the present study by calculating the ratio of
compressive yield strength at a given temperature to that
obtained at room temperature, r0.2(T)/r0.2(RT). Com-
parison of the plots of r0.2(T)/r0.2(RT) against temper-
ature for hypoeutectic and hypereutectic alloys as shown
in Figures 16(a) and (b), respectively, leads to the
following inferences: (i) the strength retention of all
the investigated as-cast alloys decreases with increasing
temperature following a linear relationship; (ii) the
quaternary (Ti-containing) hypoeutectic alloy shows
much greater strength retention at all test temperatures
than the corresponding alloy with hypereutectic com-
position; (iii) the strength retention observed at 900 /C176 C
for the Ti-containing quaternary hypoeutectic alloy is
superior by ~ 10 pct to that observed in case of the
corresponding ternary alloy; and (iv) the strength
retention observed after annealing is less than that of
the corresponding as-cast alloy, with the diﬀerence being
greater for the hypoeutectic compositions.
The linear relationship observed for decrease of yield
strength of the investigated alloys with increase in
temperature closely resembles the trend reported earlier
for pure Nb sheet, [59,60] and therefore suggests that
deformation of the ductile Nb ss phase either as primary
phase or eutectic constituent has a predominant eﬀect.

3. Micro-texture based mechanism of ﬂow softening
based on EBSD analysis
The EBSD results shown in Figures 11, 12, 13, 14 and
15 suggest that the LAGBs have formed primarily by the
cross slip of dislocations, although a limited amount of
climb is also probable. It has been also reported that in
Nb with high stacking fault energy ( ~ 200 mJm/C0 2), cross
slip is an eﬀective process for facilitating dynamic
recovery.[15,63] It is understood that plastic deformation
leads to the continuous rotation of these LAGBs. This
proposition is further substantiated by the local lattice
rotation from [101] to [111] orientation in the Nb ss phase
of Nb–12Si–5Mo–HT alloy after deformation
(Figure 14(d)). Due to the constraint imposed by the
hard 5-3 silicide phase present in the eutectic mixture,
recovery is initiated through cross-slip after a limited
amount of work-hardening through dislocation gener-
ation in the Nb ss present both as constituent of
non-lamellar eutectic or the primary phase of the
Nb–12Si–5Mo–HT alloy. Thus, the stored energy in
the Nb ss phase during high temperature deformation is
mainly released by dynamic recovery with the formation
of these LAGBs at a relatively lower strain.
V. CONCLUSIONS
In the present work, a comparative study has been
carried out on the eﬀects of annealing, along with
volume fractions and compositions of the constituent
phases in the ternary Nb–Si–Mo and quaternary
Nb–Si–Mo–Ti alloys with hypoeutectic (Nb–12Si–5Mo
and Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti) and hypereutectic
(Nb–19Si–5Mo and Nb–19Si–20Ti–5Mo) compositions
on room temperature fracture toughness as well as
compressive deformation behavior at both ambient and
high temperatures (900 /C176 C to 1100 /C176 C). The conclusions
drawn from the present study are as follows:
1. The microstructure of as-cast alloys contains pri-
mary Nbss or 5-3 silicide depending on hypoeutectic
or hypereutectic composition, besides a eutectic
mixture of Nb ss and 5-3 silicide as well as an
additional b-Tiss phase in the Ti-containing quater-
nary alloys. After annealing, the phases have
remained same, but the volume fractions and phase
compositions have changed considerably. On
annealing, the morphology of eutectic has changed
from lamellar to non-lamellar.
2. The average room temperature fracture toughness
(KIC) determined by three-point bend testing of
SENB specimens is found to be in the range of ~ 5.4
to 10.5 MPa /C214 m for the as cast alloys, and is
increased on annealing to the range of ~ 9t o1 4
MPa/C214 m. The Nb ss phase with higher volume
fractions and spheroidal morphologies in the
post-anneal microstructures has contributed to
toughening by arrest or bridging of cracks. Deco-
hesion at interfaces between Nb ss and 5-3-silicide
phases have led to crack deflection and branching,
causing increased crack path tortuosity. The
appearance of river patterns on the Nb ss along with
facets in the 5-3 silicide phases present on the
fracture surfaces reveals a cleavage mode of failure.
3. The room temperature compressive strengths of the
annealed alloys lie in the range of 1700-2000 MPa,
which is less than the range of ~ 2200 to 2400 MPa
obtained for the as-cast alloys. The higher values
obtained for the hypereutectic alloys is attributed to
the presence of larger amounts of inherently brittle
5-3 silicide than that in the hypoeutectic alloys. The
compressive strength of the Nb–12Si–5Mo–20Ti
alloy is found to be comparable to that of the
hypereutectic ternary and quaternary alloys, which
is attributed to the presence of solid solution
strengthened Nb ss both as primary phase, as well
as the constituent of non-lamellar eutectic.
4. High-temperature compression tests have revealed
that the compressive yield strength decreases with
temperature following a linear relationship, which is
identical for all the as-cast alloys with the exception
of Nb–12Si–5Mo, and is suggestive of the predom-
inant role of softening of the Nb ss. The strength
retention at a given temperature is found to be
superior for the alloys with hypoeutectic composi-
tion due to the presence of solid solution hardened
Nbss as the predominant phase, whose deformation
is constrained by the harder but brittle 5-3-silicide.
5. The EBSD analysis of Nb–12Si–5Mo–HT alloy has
revealed that the Nb ss phase exhibits dynamic
recovery involving change in local lattice orienta-
tion with preference for the h111i pole after defor-
mation at 1100 /C176 C.
