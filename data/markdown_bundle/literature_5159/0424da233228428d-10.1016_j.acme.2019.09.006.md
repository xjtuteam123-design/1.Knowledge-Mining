1. Introduction
Titanium and titanium alloys are perfect structural materials
for aerospace and aircraft ﬁelds [1] owing to their outstanding
properties, such as low density, high speciﬁc strength, good
corrosion resistance, excellent creep resistance and low
density [2]. However, for many engineering applications, a
robust bonding technique is required to fabricate components
with a large size and complex shape. Diffusion bonding is of

To derive the higher mechanical properties of diffusion-bonded joints at a low bonding
temperature, ultrasonic impact treatment (UIT) was used to increase the dislocations and
reﬁne the microstructure of faying surfaces prior to diffusion bonding. The results show that a
deformation layer with a thickness of 30 mm formed near the impacted TC11 alloy surface,
with many dislocation tangles, substructures, and dislocation cells in this layer. Diffusion
bonding experiments were performed for both original samples and UIT samples. For the
original TC11 alloy, the average shear strength of the diffusion-bonded joints increased with
an increase in the bonding temperature, and the joint strength was 550 MPa for joints bonded
at 820 8C for 30 min under a pressure of 10 MPa. However, the average shear strength of the
TC11 alloy joints with UIT reached 560 MPa for the joint that was diffusion-bonded at 780 8C,
whereas it was only 370 MPa for the joint without UIT. Therefore, UIT of the alloy surface
resulted in a joint with a high strength at a relatively low bonding temperature, which may be
beneﬁcial to inhibit the grain growth caused by the high temperature bonding process.

precise joining method that can be used to manufacture
precision components and instruments [4]. Generally, a high
bonding temperature and pressure and low surface roughness
are necessary to form an effective diffusion interface for a high
joint strength. However, severe deformation could occur when
the base metal suffers from a very high bonding temperature
and pressure [5]. Considering the dimensional accuracy of a
diffusion-bonded joint, the macroscopic deformation (%) of
the entire assembly has been thought to be less than 5%, which
has been achieved by a lower bonding temperature and
pressure. However, to prevent grain growth, surface oxidation
of the work pieces [6], the diffusion temperature is often varied
from 0.6 to 0.8 times the absolute melting temperature for
metals and ceramics [7]. Therefore, methods to improve the
mechanical properties of diffusion-bonded joints lessening to
grain coarsening, a phase transition, and severe plastic
deformation (SPD) is still a challenge.
It is well-known that the process of atomic diffusion across
joint interfaces is the essence of diffusion bonding [8]. Under
the same experimental conditions, it has been reported that
atomic diffusion at the interface is accelerated when the grain
size was small [9–11], the density of grain boundaries and
dislocations of the parent alloy is high [12]. Compared to
conventional ﬁne-grained structures, metals and alloys with a
nano-grained microstructure, which have many dislocations
and grain boundaries, can signiﬁcantly reduce diffusion
bonding temperatures [13,14]. Hence, artiﬁcial preparation of
nanostructures prior to diffusion bonding has become an ideal
method to obtain a robust joint at a low diffusion bonding
temperature.
Severe plastic deformation (SPD), including high-speed
machining (HSM) [15], high-pressure torsion (HPT) [16],
equal channel angular pressing (ECAP) [17] and accumula-
tive roll bonding (ARB) [18] has been reported to produce
nanostructures, dislocations in metals and alloys [19]. Hu
et al. [20] studied the inﬂuence of high-pressure torsion on
the microstructure of TC4 alloys. Dislocation cells, disloca-
tion tangle zones, and uncondensed dislocation wall were
observed in the TC4 alloy after high pressure torsion. Zafari
et al. [21] studied the formation of a nano-crystalline b
structure in the metastable Ti-5553 alloy during high-
pressure torsion. The results indicated the formation of
ﬁne grains with a size below 50 nm, because the original b
grains were divided by the formation of thin a" plates during
stress-induced martensitic transformation. Unal et al. [22]
investigated the effect of severe shot peening on the
microstructural evolution of the Ti-6Al-4 V alloy. Shot
peening induced the formation of ultra- ﬁne grains, and
changed the grain orientation. Moreover, the surface
roughness of the alloy surface treated by shot peening
increased. Hence SPD has a signi ﬁcant inﬂuence on the
variation in the alloy microstructure based on these studies.
However, the equipment needed to achieve this variation is
large and expensive. Additionally, the aforementioned
surface treatment methods may be not suitable for sub-
strates of a limited size. The application of UIT is effective to
solve this problem. The advantages of UIT equipment are
that they are small and have a low cost, ﬂexible operation,
and no space or position restrictions.
Many studies have focused on the effect of UIT on the
microstructure variation of alloys. Vasylyev et al. [23] found
that many Ti-O-C-N compounds formed on the TC4 alloy
surface after UIT. Moreover, a high density of dislocations,
grain reﬁnement, and partial phase transformation was
conﬁrmed in the distorted region. Panin et al. [24] reported
that many low-angle boundaries, dislocation glide and
twinning formed in commercial titanium after UIT. The
ultrasonic surface modiﬁcation of SS304 austenitic stainless
steel was investigated by Chang Ye [25], and the results
showed that compressive residual stresses were generated
near the substrate surface and the nano crystalline layer with a
high martensite content. Yakushina et al. [9] studied the
inﬂuence of grain size on the mechanical properties of Ti-6Al-
4 V diffusion-bonded joints. Compared to coarse-grained
samples, the joint interfaces of the ultra-ﬁne-grained samples
had less chain pores and higher shear strength when they
were diffusion-bonded at a relatively low temperature of 7258C.
Sharma [26] used structural steel as the parent alloy to study the
effect of pre-friction on diffusion bonding. The authors found
that after pre-friction treatment, the oxides were removed and
deformation of the surface layer occurred. After diffusion
bonding, recovery of the deformed grains that underwent the
pre-friction treatment was observed near across the diffusion-
bonded interface by relieving the stored internal energy by
atomic diffusion. These investigations demonstrated that alloys
that undergo UIT can generate a severe plastic deformation layer.
Additionally, due to plastic deformation, the original lattice
arrangement was changed. Dislocations and twins have been
found in the deformation layer. As the dislocations slip and pile
up, dislocation tangles and dislocation walls form, which divide
the coarse grains into ﬁne grains. These dislocations and grain
boundaries serve as tunnels and accelerate the migration of
atoms at a relatively low temperature.
In this study, TC11 alloys were diffusion-bonded at
temperatures ranging from 7508C to 820 8C, and the inﬂuence
of temperature on the interface structure and shear strength of
the diffusion-bonded joints was investigated. Furthermore, to
lower the diffusion bonding temperature, UIT was performed
on the surface of the TC11 substrate. The effect of UIT on the
microstructure evolution of the surface of the TC11 alloy and
the diffusion-bonded joint was studied. The results reported
here are expected to provide guidelines for the development of
low-temperature diffusion bonding.

the energy transducer to the impact needle, and imparts
vibrational energy to the impact needle. The vibrational
frequency can be as high as 20 KHz. The diameter of the
impact needle, which is made of alloy steel, is 4 mm.
UIT of the TC11 plate was conducted in ambient atmo-
sphere. The frequency of the gun was approximately 18 KHz.
The current was 2.5 A, corresponding to 25 mm of the
vibrational amplitude. Before UIT, the TC11 plate was ﬁxed
on the workbench to be steady. Meanwhile, the impact gun
was ﬁxed on the ABB (IRB 6640) robot using a ﬂange (Fig. 1 b). To
ensure that the entire surface of the plate could be treated by
the impact gun, the walking path was pre-designed (Fig. 2). The
entire process is described as follows. First, the impact gun
moves to the origin point slowly. Meanwhile, the energy power
will supply energy to the impact gun. When the impact needle
contacts the plate, it will pound the surface of the metal plate
violently, causing severe plastic deformation on the plate. As
the impact gun moves along the pre-set path, a plastic
deformation track is formed. The impact gun moves at 1 mm/
s. When the ﬁrst impact was completed, the surface of the
plate consisted of many parallel tracks; thus, the surface was
rugged (Fig. 2a). The roughness of the surface barely met the
requirement for diffusion bonding. Therefore, when the ﬁrst
impact treatment was complete, another impact process
followed. However, the origin point moved to a distance of
0.5 mm compared with the pre-origin point in the Y-axis
direction. Therefore, the head of the impact needle was
located at the peak of the two adjacent tracks. When the
second impact treatment began, the peak of the tracks
underwent plastic deformation; thus, the surface of the plate
became smoother than that in the ﬁrst impact treatment
(Fig. 2b). Additionally, when the third impact treatment began,
the origin point was re-located at the pre-position. This
process was repeated 20 times, and the surface of the plate was
quite smooth at the end (Fig. 2d).
2.2. Diffusion bonding experiments
After UIT, the TC11 plate was cut into small samples using an
electric spark cutting machine. The sizes of the diffusion-
bonded samples were 10 mm /C2 10 mm /C2 4 mm (for the bot-
tom) and 7 mm /C2 7 mm /C2 4 mm (for the top). The faying
surfaces were manually sanded using SiC abrasive paper (up to
7000 grit). Then, the samples were cleaned in an ultrasonic
bath using acetone for approximately 15 min. For comparison,
non-UIT samples were prepared in the same manner as the
UIT samples. Diffusion bonding was performed in a vacuum
furnace (ZT-40-21Y). The diffusion bonding temperatures were
750 8C and 780 8C for the UIT samples and 750 8C, 780 8C, 800 8C,
and 820 8C for the non-UIT samples. The holding time was
30 min at a pressure of 10 MPa. Heating commenced when the
vacuum of the furnace reached 8.8 /C2 10/C04 Pa. The heating rate
was 10 8C/min, and the samples were cooled to room
temperature in the furnace.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Effect of UIT on the microstructure of the TC11 alloy
Fig. 3 compares the X-ray diffraction patterns of the original
TC11 alloy and the UIT sample at different depths. The results
show that the TC11 alloy was a binary phase alloy, which
consisted of an a-phase (hcp) and b-phase (bcc). Moreover, the
new phase did not form owing to the UIT, and there was no
distinct difference in the characteristic diffraction peaks.
However, a noticeable decrease in the diffraction peak
intensity was observed as a result of UIT. The diffraction peak
intensities of the a phase (2u = 388, 2u = 408) and b phase
(2u = 398, 2u = 578) near the UIT surface were much weaker than
that of the original TC11 alloy. It has been reported that the
diffraction peak intensity decreases as the average grain size
of the alloy becomes smaller [27]. Thus, the average grain size
of TC11 was smaller owing to severe deformation during UIT.
Furthermore, the inﬂuence of UIT on grain reﬁnement became
weak with increasing impact depth. Thus, the ﬁnest grains
formed near the UIT surface. When the impact depth reached
30 mm, the reduction in the diffraction peak intensity was not
signiﬁcant, indicating that the inﬂuence of UIT on the grain
reﬁnement was not pronounced.
Fig. 4 shows a comparison of the cross-sectional micro-
structure of the original TC11 alloy and UIT sample. As shown
in Figs. 4a and b, the microstructure of the original TC11
consists of two main phases: a large volume fraction with a
short strip a-phase matrix and a small amount of lamellar b-
phase. Fig. 4c represents the microstructure of the plastic
deformed zone near the surface of the UIT sample, of which
the impact zone was quite dim and the thickness of the impact
zone was approximately 30 mm. Fig. 4d shows the magniﬁed
microstructure of the impact zone. It is difﬁcult to distinguish
the a-phase and b-phase in the impact zone. Moreover, several
non-equilibrium defects, such as holes and grain boundaries,
are observed in this zone. According to G. Sharma [26], these
non-equilibrium defects serve as diffusion channels and
expedite the bonding process.
Fig. 5 shows the TEM images of the TC11 alloy without and
with UIT. Fig. 5a shows the microstructure of the intact TC11
sample. Grains of the a-phase with slender shapes and
interlayers of the b-phase can be distinguished. The b-phases
were much thinner than the a-phases. The widths of the b-
phases ranged from 100 nm to 200 nm, while those of the a-
phases ranged from 200 nm to 500 nm. Moreover, the b-phase
deposited more dislocations than the a-phase. This phenom-
enon can be explained from the viewpoint of the crystal
structure. The structure of the a-phase was a hexagonal close
packed structure (hcp) with only three slipping systems. Thus,
it was difﬁcult for the a-phases to produce dislocation slips.

3.2. Interfacial microstructure of the diffusion-bonded
TC11 alloy joint

interfacial microstructure of diffusion-bonded TC11 alloy

temperature. As shown in Fig. 6a, when the TC11 alloy was

sufﬁcient at such a low temperature. By increasing the
temperature to 780 8C, the bonded area increased, and this

shown in Figs. 6b and c. A sound joint without any micro-voids
at the interface could be obtained at the bonding temperature
of 820 8C, as observed in Fig. 6d. Thus, it could be concluded
that the improvement in the bonding temperature was

Fig. 7 shows the effect of UIT on the interface microstruc-
ture of the TC11 alloy joints that were diffusion-bonded at
750 8C and 780 8C. The bonding time was 30 min, and the
pressure was 10 MPa – the same as those used for the original

micro-voids. Moreover, the microstructure of the TC11 alloy
was ﬁner near the joint interface due to UIT after diffusion
bonding, as shown in Fig. 7b. More importantly, for the
impacted TC11 alloy, a complete bonding interface formed at
780 8C, as shown in Fig. 7c. Similarly, as shown by the
magniﬁed microstructure of the joint in Fig. 7d, a ﬁne-grained
layer with a thickness of approximately 20 mm was formed
close to the interface. Notably, a sound joint in the TC11 alloy
without UIT was obtained at the bonding temperature of
820 8C, which was 40 8C higher than that of the joint with UIT.
This result conﬁrmed that the diffusion bonding temperature
could be reduced by approximately 40 8C to obtain a complete
joint upon introducing UIT.
The reduction in the bonding temperature caused by UIT
can be explained as follows. As mentioned in Section 3.1, there
were many dislocations and dislocation cells formed in the
ultrasonically impacted surface of the TC11 substrate. It has
been reported that this irregular arrangement of crystal lattices
results in a high excess free distortion energy [29]. Atoms
located in the deformed zone were excited because they
deviated from the equilibrium position, thus enhancing the
mobility of the atoms. Under the combined actions of thermal
energy and distortion energy during diffusion bonding process,
atomic diffusion was accelerated, and the joint interface could
be formed at a relatively low temperature. Moreover, the shape
of the b-phase formed near the joint interface of the TC11 alloy
was different from that observed in the deformed layer after
UIT (Fig. 4d). This region is conﬁrmed to be retained deformed
zone. There were many white b-phase strips that re-formed
and randomly distributed on the a-phase matrix, indicating
that re-crystallization occurred in the deformed layer during
diffusion bonding process. As mentioned in Fig. 4d, both a and b
phases could not be distinguished after UIT. But high distortion
energy reserved in this region after UIT. At high temperature
during diffusion bonding, the distortion energy is unstable
which preferred to decline. Therefore, the distortion energy act
as driving force of recrystallization. And new b phase formation
is a symbol of recrystallization. Notably, the morphology of the
b-phase in the original TC11 alloy was slender, whereas the
new formed b-phases were ﬁne (with sizes below 1 mm) and
irregular. The re-crystallization of the b-phase in the ultrasoni-
cally impacted zone indicated that atomic diffusion was
accelerated during bonding process, which was beneﬁcial for
healing the micro-voids at the interface.
Fig. 8 shows the EBSD results of the initial TC11 alloy and
the diffusion-bonded TC11 joint with UIT at 750 8C for 30 min
at 10 MPa. As shown in Fig. 8a and c, the main orientations of
the a-phase in the initial TC11 alloy are (20) and (0001). Fig. 8d
shows an inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) image of the diffusion-
bonded TC11 joint, indicating that the primary orientation of
the a-phase that formed near the bonded interface was (20). In
contrast to the original TC11 substrate, an a-phase with a
(0001) orientation was hardly observed near the joint interface.
Furthermore, the b-phases were scattered at the grain
boundaries of the a-matrix after diffusion bonding. However,
the morphology of the b-phase became ﬁner and more
disperse compared to that in the original TC11 substrate,
according to Fig. 8e.

size of new b phases are quite small, which will act as barriers
during shear test. Thus the change of the microstructure may
do favor to the mechanical property of sample.
Fig. 10 shows the microhardness distributions across the
diffusion-bonded TC11 alloy joints without and with UIT. For
the diffusion-bonded joint without UIT, the variation in the
microhardness across the bonded interface was quite similar
for bonding temperatures from 750 8C to 820 8C. There was no
distinct hardness peak value at the interface, and the
maximum microhardness of the joint was approximately
298 /C6 8 HV0.2. However, for the ultrasonically impact-treated
TC11 joints, the microhardness at the interface was higher
than that of the TC11 substrate regardless of the bonding
temperature, and maximum hardness of the joint bonded at
750 8C was 344 HV0.2. According to the research of J. Wu et al.
[30], the value of microhardness in the deformed region was
quite high than the base metal due to the strain hardening.
Thus, for the diffusion bonded samples which have a fraction
of deformed regions have a higher microhardness in the joint
interfaces. Besides, it should be noticed that when the bonding
temperature is 780 8C, the peak value of microhardness has a
slightly decline. The reason may be contributed to the
recrystallization released more residual stress at high bonding
temperature. It should be noticed that the strain hardening
has a close relationship with residual stress. As the deform
region in here was caused by UIT, thus the type of residual
stress is compressive residual stress. Hence the strain
hardening do favor to the shear strength.

strength vary considerably for different bonding temperatures

4. Conclusions
In this study, the effect of bonding temperature and UIT on
diffusion-bonded TC11 alloy joints was investigated. Based on
the above results, some conclusions were derived as follows:
(1) UIT is an effective method to reﬁne the microstructure of a
TC11 faying surface. In this experiment, the depth of the
deformed layer was approximately 30 mm, and no distinct
phase transition occurred. TEM images showed that the
deformed layer contained a lot of dislocations, twinning
barriers, and dislocation cells. This type of microstructure
offers a high energy for recrystallization, which is favorable
for the diffusion bonding process.
(2) For the non-UIT diffusion-bonded joint, the interface
bonding ratio and the shear strength increased upon
increasing the bonding temperature from 750 8C to 820 8C.
When the diffusion bonding temperature was 820 8C, a
maximum shear strength of 550 MPa was obtained. The
typical fracture surface of the diffusion-bonded TC11 joints
mainly consisted of ductile fraction surface and a non-
bonded area. As the bonding temperature was increased,
the unbonded area gradually decreased
(3) For the ultrasonically impacted TC11 alloy, the shear
strength of the joint that was bonded at 780 8C was
approximately equal to that of the non-UIT joint that was
bonded at 820 8C, indicating that UIT could reduce the
diffusion bonding temperature.
Moreover, compared to non-UIT diffusion bonding sam-
ples, the joint interface of UIT samples has a peak value of
microhardness which is caused by work hardening during UIT.
