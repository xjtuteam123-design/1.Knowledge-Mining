Refractory tungsten (W) is notoriously brittle at room temperature, which restricts its workability and 
narrows the temperature range of critical applications. Here, we report a multi-scale microstructure mod- 
ulation strategy to achieve an excellent combination of low-temperature ductility and high strength in 
bulk pure W. Through fast two-step low temperature sintering of the activated W powders and high- 
energy-rate forging treatments, unique hierarchical microstructures were constructed in pure W, includ- 
ing lamellar elongated matrix grains, with profuse interior ﬁne sub-grains, and high density of mobile 
edge and mixed dislocations. At room temperature, the hierarchical structured bulk W exhibits a de- 
tectable tensile ductility and an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1.35 GPa. The high strength can be 
maintained at elevated temperatures, i.e., UTS > 1.0 GPa at 200 °C; it also has a remarkable tensile duc- 
tility of 15.3% at this temperature. These properties are signiﬁcantly better than those reported in bulk 
pure W and W alloys with second phase particles. The shielding and blunting effects from low-angle 
grain boundaries and highly mobile dislocations, and the lamellar structure reaped delamination tough- 
ening effect are the main mechanisms for the improved low-temperature ductility and strength. This 
study demonstrates a practical route to achieve attractive low-temperature strength-ductility synergy in 
bulk W without involving any alloying elements, and it is a feasible and low-cost pathway to design high 
performance refractory metals and alloys.

1. Introduction 
Tungsten (W), as a representative body-centered cubic (BCC) re- 
fractory metal, has a high melting point, high strength at elevated 
temperature, high thermal conductivity, and other exotic proper- 
ties, which render it ideal for many applications such as kinetic 
energy penetrators and plasma facing materials in fusion reactors 
[1–4] . However, W features an intrinsic brittleness and is prone to 
operational embrittlement by grain coarsening and neutron irradi- 
ation, which signiﬁcantly restricts its workability and narrows the 
temperature range of applications [5–7] . 
The intrinsic brittleness and poor ductility of W are believed 
to originate from its BCC crystalline structure with a very high 
Peierls stress, and the poor cohesion nature of grain boundaries 
(GBs). So a practical approach for enhancing the ductility is to al- 
loy with Re that enhances GB cohesion and improves the mobil-

core structure [ 8 , 9 ]. The rolled W-1.9Re and swaged W-25Re ex-

The reﬁnement of grain size is considered as a key technique

reached 588 MPa at 600 °C, as its grain size was reﬁned to 4.2 μm

has been made on improving the ductility by additions of alloy- 
ing solutes and nano particles. However, alloying makes material 
development more resource intensive. Meanwhile, alloyed materi- 
als with complicated compositions and synthesis procedures might 
be also more diﬃcult to recycle. Therefore, developing W struc- 
tures to achieve a superior combination of low-temperature ductil- 
ity and high strength while reducing the compositional complex- 
ity is highly desirable for the sustainability of this high-performing 
metal. 
In recent years, the concept of “plainiﬁcation of materials”
has been proposed, which provides a novel strategy to enhance 
the mechanical properties of metals by controlling the stable mi- 
crostructure at different length scales with less or no alloying. The 
grain reﬁnement induced by deformation techniques in metals and 
alloys is a well-known response. For pure W, various severe plastic 
deformation (SPD) techniques including drawing, extrusion, equal- 
channel angular pressing (ECAP), and high-pressure torsion (HPT) 
were used to elevate its properties at low temperatures [22] . For 
example, the cold-rolled 0.1 mm thick W foil and K-doped W foil 
are ductile at ambient temperature, with a UTS of ∼2 GPa and 
∼3 GPa at RT, respectively [23–25] . The ultraﬁne-grained W pro- 
duced by ECAP displayed an evident improvement in both strength 
and ductility compared with that of the primary coarse-grained 
W [26] . In addition, the drawn W wire has a BDT temperature 
of RT, with a UTS as high as 2.1 GPa [27] . Microstructure inves- 
tigations revealed that the “ﬁber” size in the drawn W wire was 
reﬁned to about 1 μm, and the grain size along the rolling direc- 
tion in the cold-rolled W foil and K-doped W foil was even reﬁned 
to less than 0.5 μm, which is crucial to the improvement of low- 
temperature ductility and strength [ 23-25 , 27 ]. Moreover, the het- 
erogeneous structures such as the lamella and ﬁbrous structures 
introduced by deformation processing were believed to be bene- 
ﬁcial for the combination of low-temperature ductility and high 
strength [27–29] . Nevertheless, the incompetent dimensions size of 
W foils or W wires limited their application as bulk components. 
These W products are all subjected to SPD treatment at a temper- 
ature lower than the recrystallization temperature of W, resulting 
in an absolute majority of high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) 
which are weak in relieving the strain incompatibility and crack 
sensitivity [30] . 
For the preparation of bulk W materials with reﬁned grains, 
other thermomechanical working techniques including rolling, 
swaging, and high-energy-rate forging (HERF) are usually adopted 
to enhance the low-temperature performance. Through rolling, the 
grain size of pure W was reﬁned to less than 10 μm, and the 
BDT temperature decreased to 275 °C [31] . The swaged pure W 
with a grain size of 3.96 μm exhibited obvious tensile ductility 
at 250 °C [32] . Through HERF, and the assistance of oxide disper- 
sion, a ﬁne-grained (1.3 μm) bulk W-Y 2 O 3 alloy was reported to 
have RT tensile ductility and high strength ( > 1 GPa) [29] . Wor- 
thy of note is that for the hot-rolled W-ZrC materials, the lami- 
nate grain structure and reﬁned sub-grains (1.03 μm) caused by 
the generated low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) yield a high UTS 
of 1058 MPa with a ductile fracture at 100 °C [2] . The BDT tem- 
perature has a linear reciprocal relationship with the LAGB den- 
sity, because LAGBs can act as source of dislocations and show 
better strain compatibility between W grains than that of HAGBs, 
thereby contributing positively to the low-temperature ductility 
[ 30 , 33 ]. In addition to reﬁning the sub-grains, thermomechanical 
working provides a possibility to regulate dislocations such as dis- 
location density and types in sub-grains by controlling the de- 
formation temperature and rate, which play an important role in 
strengthening and ductilizing W materials [ 34 , 35 ]. However, it is 
hard to reﬁne the grain size (sub-grain size) of bulk pure W to the 
micron or submicron scale, due to the grain coarsening in high- 
temperature sintering and rolling or swaging processes. Therefore, 
no bulk pure W with an excellent combination of RT ductility and 
ultrahigh strength has been prepared yet. 
Having all the factors into consideration, there is high capabil- 
ity by regulating microstructures to achieve bulk pure W materials 
with improved strength and low-temperature ductility simultane- 
ously. However, it remains a challenge for preparing commercial 
bulk pure W to high density while maintaining ﬁne grain struc- 
tures, due to insuﬃcient powder sintering activity, which requires 
a very high temperature sintering at above 2200 °C and hot rolling 
at > 1400 °C [36] . In this work, through sintering the activated 
W powders by a developed two-step method, as-sintered W in- 
gots with ﬁne grains were prepared, and by the subsequent warm 
working of HERF process, bulk HERF-W were produced with an 
excellent combination of low-temperature ductility and ultrahigh 
strength. The HERF technique was adopted because of its high for- 
mation velocity and capability to obtain desired microstructures. 
In addition, the deformation temperature, quantity and rate could 
be manipulated ﬂexibly during each step of HERF process [37] . 
We emphasize the microstructure regulation by controlling lamel- 
lar mother grains with ﬁne sub-grain structures, LAGBs, and dis- 
location density and types. This multi-scale microstructure modu- 
lation strategy proposed in this work can provide a reference for 
the preparation of other high-performance refractory metals and 
alloys. 
2. Experiment procedure 
2.1. Sample preparation 
Tungsten powders with an average particle size of 2 μm (pu- 
rity > 99.9% trace metals basis) purchased from Xiamen Golden 
Egret Company (Xiamen, China) were selected as precursors, and 
the chemical of as-received W powder is shown in Table 1 . Me- 
chanical milling was carried out by a planetary ball mill to im- 
prove the sintering activity of powders. W powders were milled 
in 500 mL tungsten carbide vessels ﬁlled with puriﬁed argon at 
a rotational speed of 280 rpm and a milling time of 4 h. To re- 
duce the introduction of impurities and maximize the ball milling 
capacity, tungsten carbide balls were used as the grinding media 
at a ball-to-powder weight ratio of 8:1. Then, the ball-milled pow- 
ders were loaded in a graphite mold with a diameter of 70 mm 
and underwent pre-compaction at a pressure of 15 MPa. Subse- 
quently, the compacted tungsten powder was further consolidated 
by hot-pressing sintering at a constant pressure of 30 MPa in high- 
vacuum ( < 10 −3 Pa). An optimized sintering procedure containing 
two main steps was employed, including a low-temperature sinter- 
ing at 1300 °C for 2 h followed by a moderate-temperature sinter- 
ing at 1800 °C for 1 h. Here, a sintering temperature of 1800 °C was 
considered a moderate temperature for the distinction with the 
conventional fabrication procedure of tungsten material, in which 
higher temperatures of more than 2200 °C were usually used in 
the industrial community [36] . The former low-temperature sin- 
tering stage at 1300 °C ensures preliminary densiﬁcation with the 
formation of abundant sintering necks between adjacent powder 
particles and prevents excessive grain growth. The later short-term 
moderate-temperature sintering at 1800 °C provides rapid densiﬁ- 
cation through the fast channel of the sintering neck and the co- 
ordination of pressure, which guarantees not only the high rela- 
tive density of the consolidated ingot but also a ﬁne-grain state 
in this sintered ingot. Subsequently, cylindrical samples with a di- 
ameter of 30 mm were cut from the sintered ingot and subjected 
to the HERF process. Different from traditional thermomechani- 
cal processing including hot rolling, swaging, and forging which 
are conducted at relatively high temperatures > 1400 °C at initial 
multi-passes to prevent sample cracking due to the limitation of

2.2. Microstructure analysis 
Microstructures of as-sintered and HERF-W specimens were 
characterized using a Zeiss SIGMA300 ﬁeld emission scanning elec- 
tron microscope (FESEM). The specimens were ﬁrstly ground us- 
ing standard 240, 40 0, 80 0, 150 0, and 20 0 0 grit SiC paper in se- 
quence. Then they were further polished down to 0.25 mm using 
diamond suspensions on a metallographic lapping machine (MP- 
1B) and electrolytically polished at a constant voltage of 11 V and 
a current density of 3 mA/mm 2 in 10% sodium hydroxide aqueous 
solution. Quantitative analysis of grain sizes, orientations, and mis- 
orientation distributions was carried out by electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD) with a CRYSTAL detector (Oxford Instruments, 
Oxfordshire, UK). The operating voltage was 15 kV with a scanning 
step size of 0.25 μm. The grain structure of specimens was fur- 
ther investigated using the backscattered electron (BSE) SEM im- 
ages with high crystal orientation (channeling) contrast. 5 keV in- 
cident electrons were used to improve the quality of BSE-SEM im- 
ages. Compared with EBSD, BSE mode can detect very small orien- 
tation variations even in a single grain (tenths of a degree) due 
to its higher sensitivity to the misorientation, although the ob- 
served orientation variations in BSE images cannot be quantiﬁed 
[38] . Thus, it is convenient for displaying LAGBs. Thin foils were 
ground to a thickness of 60 ∼100 μm for transmission electron mi- 
croscope (TEM) observations. Final thinning and perforation of foils 
were accomplished by a Tenupol-5 twin-jet electropolisher using a 
10% sodium hydroxide aqueous solution at ∼ 10 °C at an operating 
voltage of 10 V. The specimens were observed with an FEI Talos 
F20 0X TEM operating at 20 0 kV, and the TEM images were ac- 
quired in bright ﬁeld imaging mode. Dislocation density was de- 
termined from TEM images and evaluated by the grid intercept 
method [39] : ρ = ( N h / L h + N v / L v )/ t , where N h and N v are the num- 
bers of intersections between dislocations and grid lines, L h and L v 
are the total lengths of horizontal and vertical lines, respectively, 
and t is the specimen thickness [40] . In this work, the local speci- 
men thickness was determined from the convergent beam electron 
diffraction pattern analysis. It is worth mentioning that only dis- 
locations present in the grain interior were counted, while dense 
dislocation agglomerations near GBs and dislocation walls were not 
considered. More than 30 TEM images from various areas were ac- 
quired to evaluate the dislocation density.

3. Results 
3.1. Mechanical properties 
Tensile engineering stress-strain curves of as-sintered W and 
HERF-W at different testing temperatures ranging from RT to 600 
°C are shown in Fig. 2 , and the corresponding 0.2% offset yield 
strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), and total elongation 
(TE) are listed in Table 2 for expedite comparison. As shown in 
Fig. 2 (a), the as-sintered W shows a typical brittle fracture be- 
low 300 °C and a noticeable tensile strain at 400 °C, indicating a 
brittle-to-ductile transition (BDT) between 300 °C to 400 °C and 
nil ductility temperature (NDT) of about 300 °C. This NDT value is 
lower than most sintered W materials such as spark plasma sin- 
tered (SPSed) pure W, SPSed W-Y 2 O 3, and SPSed W-ZrC [ 20 , 41 , 42 ]. 
On the other hand, the as-sintered W shows limited strength and 
ductility. For instance, its UTS is below 380 MPa at all testing tem- 
peratures, and its TE is just 10% ∼13% even at temperatures above 
400 °C. However, a signiﬁcant improvement in the NDT, UTS, and 
TE is found for HERF-W, as shown in Fig. 2 (b). At room tem- 
perature, the HERF-W exhibits a detectable tensile ductility since 
its fracture strength (1354 MPa) lies beyond its yield strength 
(1302 MPa), and it shows an obvious ductile strain with a TE of 
4.2% at 100 °C, indicating its NDT of room temperature. This is the 
ﬁrst time that the detectable tensile strain at room temperature is 
reported in bulk pure W, despite some thin W foils ( < 1 mm) and 
W ﬁbers have NDTs lower than room temperature [ 25 , 27 , 43 ]. 
In addition, the strength of bulk HERF-W reaches a surprisingly 
high level at low- and elevated temperatures. For instance, the UTS 
of HERF-W is as high as 1.35 GPa at RT and it maintains an excel- 
lent strength at moderate temperatures, i.e., UTS > 1.0 GPa at 200 
°C, and > 840 MPa at 600 °C, which are much higher than those 
reported in bulk pure W and even W alloys with second phase par- 
ticles [ 16 , 43-45 ]. For example, the UTS of hot-rolled or hot-forged 
bulk W at 200 °C generally ranges from 600 MPa to 800 MPa, 
which is 200 - 400 MPa lower than that of the present HERF-W 
[ 16 , 43-45 ]. More notably, from a low temperature of 100 °C to a 
moderate 600 °C as shown in Fig. 2 (b), the UTS of our HERF-W 
is more than 100 MPa higher than that of recently reported high- 
performance W-0.5 wt.%Y 2 O 3 alloy with the nano-sized strength- 
ening dispersoids [29] . 
Beside the high strength, the plastic elongation of present 
HERF-W signiﬁcantly increases from 0.3% to 15.3% with the tem- 
perature increase from RT to 200 °C, indicating the signiﬁcant 
compatibility of low-temperature strength and ductility, breaking 
the strength-ductility trade-off in traditional bulk W materials. Fur- 
ther increasing the temperature to above 200 °C, the TE shows 
a slight decreasing trend. A similar temperature-dependent ten- 
sile elongation evolution was also reported by other researchers 
[ 29 , 46 , 47 ]. However, compared with most reported bulk W ma- 
terials [ 13 , 20 , 47-51 ], HERF-W still exhibits a good combination of 
strength (UTS ∼ 843 ± 21 MPa) and ductility (TE ∼ 10.2 ± 2%) at 
600 °C ( Fig. 2 (b)), suggesting an overall increase in strength with- 
out sacriﬁce of ductility.

terms of NDT vs. UTS and UTS vs. TE at 100 °C, respectively, with 
those of reported bulk pure W and W-based alloys prepared by 
sintering or other deformation-treating processes ( Fig. 3 (a) and 
(b)). The sintered W materials [ 20 , 21 , 41 , 42 , 48 , 52 , 53 ] generally dis- 
play high NDTs of 50 0–70 0 °C and low UTS of below 750 MPa

3.3. Fracture surfaces 
Visual evidence is obtained by observing the fracture surface of 
tensile specimens to explain the mechanisms responsible for opti- 
mized tensile properties Fig. 7 . depicts the fracture morphology of 
HERF-W after tensile tests at different temperatures. The predom- 
inant fracture mode of HERF-W at room temperature is transgran- 
ular cleavage of W grains, as shown in Fig. 7 (a). This is different 
from the intergranular fracture mode in some pure W materials 
[61] , suggesting the strengthened GBs through HERF processes. A 
small number of straight microcracks ( < 1 μm) are found along 
the elongated W grains. These microcracks originate from the high 
stress induced crack nucleation and tip propagation along HAGBs, 
since cracks follow the lowest energy path. This result is consis- 
tent with previous studies reported by Tran-Huu-Loi et al. [62] and 
Rupp et al. [31] . When the testing temperature increases to 100 
°C, grains are drawn and deformed along the tensile direction, re- 
sulting in delamination structures as shown in Fig. 7 (b). Delamina- 
tion indicates the anisotropic stress is relaxed ahead of advancing 
crack fronts and blunting the crack tip, thus leading to toughen- 
ing effects, and ﬁnally resulting in the decrease of NDTs and the 
increase of low-temperature ductility [ 34 , 63 ]. Further increasing 
the temperatures to above 200 °C, dense delamination with duc- 
tile avulsion governs the fracture mode of HERF-W, as illustrated 
in Fig. 7 (c) and (d). Interestingly, interface rupture and interlayer 
separation of W grains with the delamination structure appear at 
300 °C, and this phenomenon is exacerbated at 500 °C. Previous 
results show that delamination would compromise the toughness 
in the ductile fracture region to some extent [29] , which may be 
responsible for the slightly reduced elongation of HERF-W at high 
temperatures.

4. Discussion 
As mentioned above, the present bulk HERF-W shows excep- 
tional tensile properties, including outstanding YS and UTS, and ex- 
cellent low-temperature tensile ductility including room tempera- 
ture ductility, which are often missing in conventional bulk W ma- 
terials. To understand this peculiarity in HERF-W, we discuss the 
formation of tactically constructed microstructures, and the under- 
lying mechanisms leading to synergetic strengthening and ductiliz- 
ing effects. The grain size, GB types, lamellar grain structures, sub- 
structures, dislocation density and types, and dislocation interac- 
tion are discussed in detail. 
4.1. Microstructure reﬁnement 
Bulk W materials are usually prepared by powder metallurgy 
that includes two main steps of powder sintering and thermo- 
mechanical processing. In this work, the microstructural reﬁne- 
ment strategy is integrated into the whole preparation process. 
Firstly, the ball milling technique is used to process the raw pow- 
ders for two main reasons. Collisions and frictions between the 
powders and grinding media occur during the milling, and this 
interaction gradually smashes and combines the powders to ob- 
tain a larger speciﬁc surface area, reduce powder agglomeration, 
and improve dispersion [64] . In addition, this interaction creates 
abundant defects, effectively increasing the internal storage energy 
in milled powders, and in turn lowering the demanded external 
thermal energy during the densiﬁcation sintering, permitting low- 
temperature sintering to achieve a relatively high density. 
Beneﬁtting from the activated powder, under the subsequent 
rapid two-step low temperature and short period sintering with 
a constant pressure of 30 MPa, a high-density bulk W slab with a 
relative density of 95% is prepared. Fig. 8 displays the direct com- 
parison by EBSD analyses of microstructures of the hot-pressing 
sintered W and commercially sintered pure W ingots including 
GB types and grain size distributions. Both the sintered W mate- 
rials have no preferred orientation (texture) as indicated by the 
IPFs in Fig. 8 (a 1 ) and (b 1 ), and GBs are all HAGBs as denoted by 
the blue lines in Fig. 8 (a 2 ) and (b 2 ). In addition, our sintered W 
slab achieves ﬁne grain structures with an average grain size of 
8.9 μm, while the commercially sintered W has an average grain 
size of 38.6 μm, several times coarser than that of as-sintered W. 
This is because during the production of bulk pure W in indus- 
try, raw tungsten powder is consolidated directly by cold isostatic 
pressing after a simple batch mixing, and then the slab is sin- 
tered at high temperatures (2300 °C) for a long period (more than 
20 h) without pressure, leading to severely coarse grains with a 
limited relative density of 92%. In addition, our hot-pressing sin- 
tered W possesses a narrow grain size distribution ranging from 4 
to 16 μm, while commercially sintered W has a wide grain size 
distribution of 16 ∼ 96 μm, further conﬁrming the reﬁned grain 
structures in as-sintered W. Beneﬁtting from the ﬁne grain struc- 
tures and high relative density, our as-sintered W slab demon- 
strates tensile ductility at a relatively low temperature of 400 °C, 
which is among the lowest reported temperature for sintered W

4.3. Ductilizing mechanisms 
The failure of W due to low-temperature brittle fracture is one 
of the major limitations in practical applications. This work inves- 
tigates the underlying ductilisation mechanisms in HERF-W by an- 
alyzing the microstructures in aspects of reﬁned grains, GB types, 
lamellar grain structures, and dislocation density and types. 
It is generally believed that the segregation of impurities such 
as oxygen or phosphorous greatly weakens the cohesion of GBs, 
leading to intergranular fracture of W grains, which is one of 
the main reasons for the poor ductility of W materials [86–88] . 
Compared with commercial pure W and other reported W alloys, 
the HERF-W exhibits a much higher GB density due to the ﬁner 
mother grains and sub-grains. The impurities in ﬁne-grained W 
would redistribute and spread over a larger area owing to the 
presence of substantial GBs [ 89 , 90 ], which alleviates the negative 
effect of impurities in HERF-W and helps to enhance the low- 
temperature ductility. However, the brittleness induced by impu- 
rities segregation is also controversial. Gludovatz et al. [91] sug- 
gested that intergranular fracture may not be directly correlated 
to the presence of impurities, and Hofmann et al. [92] even found 
the segregation of carbon improves the cohesion of tungsten GB. 
Therefore, other factors like grain size and shape or strain compat-

and the proportion of LAGBs is less than 15.5%, further conﬁrm-

most of the generated GBs are LAGBs, and the fraction of LAGBs 
increases from 15.5% in as-sintered W to 62.2% in HERF-W on the 
cross-section surface, and even as high as 79.3% on the forging sur- 
face as shown in Fig. 12 (b) and (c). This signiﬁcant evolution of 
GB types has noticeable effects on the tensile ductility of HERF- 
W. Rupp et al. [31] reported that HAGBs are the easy pathways for 
crack propagation under stress loading conditions and become the 
short slab of intergranular brittle fracture. On the contrary, Teren- 
tyev et al. [94] emphasized the increased LABGs could shield and 
blunt the crack tips, thereby improving the tensile ductility and 
lowering the BDT temperature. They further demonstrated that as 
the fraction of LAGBs increases, the BDT temperature will reduce. 
In a word, the increase in LAGBs and the decrease in HAGBs frac- 
tion could introduce the shielding and blunting (anti-shielding) ef- 
fects, resulting in a lower BDT temperature. This is one of the main 
reasons why the BDT temperature is as low as 100 °C and the NDT 
is at room temperature in our bulk HERF-W. 
Compared to HAGBs, the LAGBs exhibit higher fracture resis- 
tance, which is long believed that LAGBs show better strain com- 
patibility between W grains than that of HAGBs [30] . According to 
von Mises principle, at low temperatures, when the total number 
of active independent slip systems is less than ﬁve, most BCC met- 
als cannot accommodate the arbitrary strain ﬁeld during strain- 
ing [95] , and cracks may nucleate and grow based on the strain 
incompatibility between grains, leading to intergranular fracture. 
However, simulation works indicate that LAGBs effectively reduce 
the requirement of independent slip systems in W and are able to 
better transfer strain between grains without fracture [33] . Three- 
point bending tests results on W bi-crystals also imply that LAGBs 
have higher fracture resistance compared to HAGBs [96] . Therefore, 
the high proportion of LAGBs signiﬁcantly alleviates the strain in- 
compatibility, thereby improving the ductility of W. Based on the 
crystallography analysis, the poor lattice coherency of HAGBs hin- 
ders the dislocation migration and leads to the dislocation pile-ups 
and tangles, which have been shown to be crack nucleation sites 
[97] . Whereas LAGBs have much more coherent crystal lattices and 
are equivalent to dislocations array at the boundary, since LAGBs 
have misorientation angles less than 10 ° in HERF-W. According to 
the selection rules for dislocation transfer at grain boundaries, re- 
ported by Clark et al. and Birnbaum et al. [ 98 , 99 ], boundaries par- 
allel to both slip planes and directions are the most favorable ori- 
entation for dislocation transfer, and the angle ( θ) between the in- 
coming and outgoing slip planes should be minimized when they 
are not parallel. Based on these selection rules, LAGBs provide a 
more ideal pathway for dislocations across boundaries and avoid 
stress concentration at GBs. As observed in Fig. 13 , some disloca- 
tions indicated by dotted red lines glide across the LAGBs. Similar 
effects have been reported on studies of interphase and inter-metal 
boundaries by Misra et al. [100–102] , and the eﬃcient slip transfer 
is suggested to be a key factor for the striking ductility of Ni-Fe-Al 
alloys at RT [ 101 , 102 ]. Moreover, in LAGBs, the segments of dis- 
location networks act as Frank–Read or Bardeen–Herring sources, 
providing sites for dislocation nucleation and enhancing the ductil- 
ity [34] . Models proposed by J.R. Rice et al. [ 103 , 104 ] describe the 
brittle-ductile transition as a nucleation-controlled and mobility- 
controlled event, revealing the key factors affecting the ductility of 
materials are the number of active dislocation sources and dislo- 
cation motion. Our recent study quantitatively shows that for BCC 
metals, the brittle-ductile transition is controlled by the ratio of 
screw to edge dislocation velocity [105] . A suﬃciently high relative 
mobility is a prerequisite for the coordinated movement of screw 
and edge segments to sustain dislocation multiplication. The re- 
search by Peter Gumbsch also emphasized the importance of active 
dislocation sources for ductility [106] . Increased LAGBs provide two 
main positive effects for the emission of dislocations: (i) the nucle- 
ation of dislocations, and then (ii) the dislocations glide, thereby 
improving the ductility of HERF-W. 
In addition to the increase in the proportion of LAGBs, free dis- 
locations within sub-grains also have a great inﬂuence on the ten- 
sile ductility of HERF-W. Generally, a high density of dislocations 
introduced during the plastic deformation hinders their further 
movement, leading to an enhanced strength but decreased duc- 
tility in BCC metals. However, extensive research results showed 
the improved ductility of W after thermomechanical processing 
[ 34 , 107 ], which is inconsistent with the basic principle of work 
hardening. Therefore, understanding the dislocation behaviors is 
essential to analyze their roles in improving ductility. As for the 
dislocation movement, it is necessary to understand the differences 
between the screw dislocations and edge dislocations in BCC met- 
als. The former has an inherently compact and non-planar core 
structure, contributing to a relatively high Peierls barrier [ 108 , 109 ]. 
Meanwhile, the motion of screw dislocation is closely related to 
the formation of thermally activated kink pairs [108] . Usually, a

traditional approach of preparing ﬁne-grained W using a bottom- 
up method through accelerated sintering to produce ﬁne grains 
with HAGBs, in this work, ﬁne sub-grains with LAGBs are formed 
through a gentle top-down dynamic recovery approach. This ap- 
proach not only improves the strength of W, but also enhances 
its ductility, because it has inherent effects such as blunting the 
crack tip, improving the compatibility between each W sub-grains, 
and providing an ideal pathway for dislocation crossing. More im- 
portantly, there is a high density of reserved free dislocations in 
sub-grains, acting as a dominant strengthening mechanism. What’s 
more, the high fraction of edge and mixed dislocations contributes 
to the improved ductility of HERF-W by shielding the crack tips 
and accommodating the strain. To sum up, by implementing the 
multi-level microstructural optimization, an excellent combination 
of low-temperature ductility and ultrahigh strength is achieved in 
our HERF-W. 
5. Conclusion 
In the present work, we fabricated bulk pure W products by 
sintering the activated powders with a developed two-step low 
temperature and short period sintering technique, and followed 
warm working of high-energy-rate forging (HERF) process. The 
bulk HERF-W exhibits an excellent low-temperature tensile duc- 
tility and high strength. The main results can be summarized as 
follows: 
(1) A substantial enhancement of tensile strength and ductility of 
bulk HERF-W is achieved. At room temperature, a detectable 
tensile ductility and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1.35 GPa 
are observed. At 200 °C, the UTS of HERF-W is still higher than 
1.0 GPa, and it has a remarkable tensile ductility with a total 
elongation (TE) of 15.3%, which are much higher than those of 
previously reported bulk pure W and even W alloys with sec- 
ond phase dispersion particles. These results indicate a signif- 
icant compatibility of ductility and strength in our HERF-W at 
low temperatures. Moreover, at an elevated temperature of 600 
°C, HERF-W exhibits a good combination of strength (UTS ∼
843 ± 21 MPa) and ductility (TE ∼ 10.2 ± 2%) as well. 
(2) The microstructure analyses indicate multi-scale reﬁned mi- 
crostructures in HERF-W. Reﬁned lamellar structured mother 
grains with an average grain thickness of ∼3.8 μm and length 
of ∼16.5 μm were formed. Fine sub-grains ( ∼1.3 μm) with 
low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) are formed in these mother 
grains through a mild dynamic recovery during the HERF pro- 
cessing. In addition, there is a moderate density of reserved 
free dislocations ( ∼ (9.5 ± 0.4) × 10 13 m −2 ) in sub-grains, and 
among them highly mobile edge and mixed dislocations are 
dominant ( ∼58.2%). 
(3) The excellent combination of low-temperature ductility and 
high strength comes from the multi-scale microstructure mod- 
ulation, including the grain size, GB types, lamellar grain 
structures, substructures, dislocation density, and types. The 
GB strengthening and dislocation strengthening is the main 
strengthening mechanism in our HERF-W. The improved strain 
compatibility by LAGBs and their shielding and blunting (anti- 
shielding) effects, highly mobile edge and mixed dislocations, 
and the lamellar structure reaped delamination toughening ef-
