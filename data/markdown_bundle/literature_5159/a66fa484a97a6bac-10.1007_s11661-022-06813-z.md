BRIEF COMMUNICATION
A Study of the High-Temperature Strength
of Titanium Alloys Using Nanoindentation
and Micro-cantilever Bending Tests
YUKIMI TANAKA, KOICHIRO HATTORI, and YOSHIHISA HARADA
To investigate the microscale plastic behavior of Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 titanium
alloys in high-temperature environment, nanoindentation and micro-cantilever bending tests
were performed at 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C. The strength of single a-grains of IMI-834 and Ti-6242S at
high temperatures is larger than that of Ti-6Al-4V, suggesting that solid-solution strengthening
is the mechanism that plays a critical role in high-temperature strengthening, regardless of the
microstructure complexity.

TITANIUM alloys have been used in high-temper-
ature environments such as aerospace engines. The
Ti-6Al-4V alloy, which is one of the most common
titanium alloys, has also been applied to fan and cooler
parts of compressors.
[1] Near a-Ti alloys, such as
Ti-6242S (Ti-6Al-2Sn-4Zr-2Mo-0.1Si) and IMI-834
(Ti-5.8Al-4Sn-3.5Zr-0.5Mo-0.7Nb-0.35Si), have been
developed to improve heat resistance based on
Ti-6Al-4V. The Ti-6Al-4V alloy is used in low-pressure
compressors up to temperatures of approximately
315 /C176 C, whereas Ti-6242S and IMI-834 alloys are used
in high-pressure compressors up to 550 /C176 C to 600 /C176 C.
[2,3]
The strength of titanium alloys in high-temperature
environments has been investigated using macroscale
mechanical testing, and the maximum operating tem-
peratures were 590 /C176 C, 520 /C176 C, and 300 /C176 C for IMI-834,
Ti-6242S, and Ti-6Al-4V alloys, respectively. [4] More-
over, the eﬀects of microstructure on the plastic behav-
ior of a material have been investigated by tracing the
slip line behavior after mechanical testing at
macroscale.
[5,6] Micromechanical testing is also an
eﬀective method for clarifying microstructure eﬀects
because it focuses on the speciﬁc grains, phases, and
boundaries.
[7–9] At high-temperature environment, the
plastic behavior is also aﬀected by microstructural
properties such as the grain size, shape, and volume
fraction of a phase, which have been conﬁrmed by
macro-mechanical testing and slip trace analysis.
[10,11]
However, the mechanical characteristics of a-grains and
grain boundaries at high-temperature is not investi-
gated. In this study, we conducted micromechanical
testing (nanoindentation and micro-cantilever bending
tests) at high-temperature environments to investigate
the microscale plastic behavior of Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S,
and IMI-834 titanium alloys. These tests were per-
formed at 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C. The elevated temperature
(350 /C176 C) was selected to compare the heat resistance of
the three alloys.
[4,12,13] To determine the crystal structure
of the grains at which plastic deformation occurred, the
samples were observed using scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) and analyzed using electron backscatter
diﬀraction (EBSD). We investigated the temperature
dependence of the strength, focusing on each grain and
the crystal structure.
The samples were cut from Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and
IMI-834 alloys. Each alloy was solution heat-treated
and aged as follows:
[1,14] Ti-6Al-4V—950 /C176 C/5 hour,
WQ + 700 /C176 C/2 hour, AC; Ti-6242S—980 /C176 C/1 hour,
AC + 590 /C176 C/8 hour, AC; and IMI-834 /C176 C—1015 /C176 C/
1 hour, AC + 700 /C176 C/2 hour, AC, where WQ and AC
stands for water-quenched and air-cooled, respectively.
Nanoindentation and bending tests were performed on
the same samples using commercial nanoindentation
testers (Hysitron, TI 950 TriboIndenter) with a Berko-
vich indenter at 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C. Nanoindentation
tests were conducted at eight locations for each temper-
ature and each alloy. The elastic modulus and hardness

Before and after the nanoindentation and bending tests,
measurements were taken using an EBSD detector
(Bruker, e-Flash) attached to a ﬁeld-emission SEM

Figure 1 shows an example of the EBSD analysis
results of the Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 alloys
under the indented locations. The image quality maps
describe the quality of the diﬀraction patterns. The
indented locations are indicated by the black triangles.
The IPFZ maps describe the crystal orientation with
respect to the z-direction, which is the same as that of
the indentation direction. The equiaxed a and lamella

the equiaxed and lamellar regions were mixed in

ical properties of each a-grain, indentation tests were
performed and the indentation depth was suﬃciently
small ( ~ 500 nm) with respect to the a-grain size.
Figure 2(a) shows the mean elastic modulus and
hardness values (including the results from various
c-axis angles, as shown in Figure 1) at each temperature
and alloy obtained by nanoindentation tests. The elastic
modulus at 350 /C176 C was lower than that at 25 /C176 C, and the
diﬀerence between 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C showed a similar
tendency for the three alloys. In addition, the hardness
values of the three alloys at 350 /C176 C were lower than
those at 25 /C176 C. The hardness at 350 /C176 C of Ti-6Al-4V was
lower than that of Ti-6242S and IMI-834, whereas the
hardness at 25 /C176 C was similar for the three alloys. The
diﬀerences in hardness between 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C for
Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 were 1.27 GPa, 0.83
GPa and 0.70 GPa, respectively, suggesting that the
resistance to plastic deformation at high temperatures of
IMI-834 and Ti-6242S is larger than that of the
Ti-6Al-4V alloy.

the elastic modulus and hardness: grains with small
c-axis angles indicate high elastic modulus and hardness,
and grains with large
c-axis angles indicate low elastic
modulus and hardness at room temperature. [8,17]

Although the indented locations contain grain interior
and are near the grain boundary (see Figure 1), the
results of indentation curves showed little variation.
This result indicates that the mechanical properties near
the grain boundary depends on the mechanical proper-
ties of grain interior, not the characteristic of the grain
boundary. Therefore, the diﬀerence in hardness at
high-temperature among the three alloys seems to be
caused by the grain interior strengthening rather than
grain boundary strengthening. The results suggest that
the plastic deformation of a single a-grain at high
temperatures of Ti-6Al-4V is larger than that of
Ti-6242S and IMI-834.
Titanium oxidizes at high-temperature environments
and forms an oxide ﬁlm. We assumed in our experiment
that some titanium oxide was formed at the titanium
alloy surface during nanoindentation tests at 350 /C176 C.
Generally, the oxidation makes titanium brittle, and
increases its hardness. However, the hardness of tita-
nium alloys at 350 /C176 C is lower than that at 25 /C176 C in this
study. Therefore, the eﬀects of oxidation on the
mechanical properties are small, and the nanoindenta-
tion results conﬁrm the temperature dependence of the
mechanical properties of the titanium alloys.
To evaluate the slip behavior of equiaxed a-grains in
Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 alloys, micro-can-
tilever bending tests were conducted. Figure 3(a) shows
a micro-cantilever with an equilateral triangular
cross-section fabricated using FIB milling. The load
was applied 30 lm from the base of the cantilever.
Bending tests were performed on six micro-cantilevers at
each temperature and for each alloy. The slip planes
were identiﬁed by the crystal orientation obtained from
the EBSD analysis, and the slip line from the SEM
observations. Figures 3(b)–(d) show SEM images of the
alloys after the micro-cantilever bending test. IPF maps
with respect to the y-direction (parallel to the cantilever
length and applied tensile stress direction at the upper
surface by bending tests) are also shown in
Figures 3(b)–(d). The main grains of the cantilevers

c-axis inclination angles between 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 Ci n

Figure 4(a) shows the stress–strain curves of the
micro-cantilevers shown in Figures 3(b)–(d). The gradi-
ent of the stress–strain curve in the elastic region (initial
linear region) at 350 /C176 C was similar to that at 25 /C176 C for
IMI-834 and Ti-6242S; however, the gradient at 350 /C176 C
was smaller than that at 25 /C176 C for Ti-6Al-4V. The yield
point at 350 /C176 C was smaller than that at 25 /C176 C for all
IMI-834, Ti-6242S, and Ti-6Al-4V alloys. Figure 4(b)
shows the mean elastic modulus and 0.2 pct proof stress
of the six micro-cantilevers obtained by bending tests.
The elastic modulus was calculated from the linear
ﬁtting of the stress–strain curve in the region where the
strain was 0.001–0.015. The 0.2 pct proof stress was
calculated from the intersection point of the stress–
strain curve and the line shifted by 0.002 from the linear
ﬁtting line in the elastic region. The elastic modulus at
350 /C176 C was smaller than that at 25 /C176 C for Ti-6Al-4V
compared with Ti-6242S and IMI-834. The 0.2 pct proof
stress at 350 /C176 C was also lower than that at 25 /C176 C for all
the three alloys. Moreover, the 0.2 pct proof stress at
350 /C176 C for Ti-6Al-4V was lower than that for Ti-6242S
and IMI-834, whereas the 0.2 pct proof stress at 25 /C176 C
was similar for the three alloys. The diﬀerences in the
mean 0.2 pct proof stress between 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C for
Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 were 627, 326, and
146 MPa, respectively. These results suggest that the
resistance to plastic deformation at high temperatures
for IMI-834 and Ti-6242S is larger than that for the
Ti-6Al-4V alloy even in the microscale.
The elastic moduli determined by nanoindentation
and bending tests at 25 /C176 C were approximately within
125–130 GPa for the IMI-834, Ti-6242S, and Ti-6Al-4V
alloys [Figures 2(b) and 3(b)]. At 350 /C176 C, the elastic
modulus obtained using nanoindentation tests was
approximately 110 GPa for all IMI-834, Ti-6242S, and
Ti-6Al-4V alloys, whereas the elastic modulus obtained
using bending tests at 350 /C176 C was approximately 110
GPa for Ti-6Al-4V and 125–130 GPa for IMI-834 and
Ti-6242S alloys. In macroscale experiments and simula-
tion results from other studies, the elastic modulus at
350 /C176 C was 10–20 GPa lower than that at room
temperature for Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834
alloys.
[12,18,19] Considering that the results of our
microscale experiment (nanoindentation tests) are sim-
ilar to those of macroscale experiments in previous
studies, elastic deformation at the macroscale is likely to
depend on the elastic modulus of each grain. Further
consideration is needed to clarify the diﬀerent tendencies
in the micro-cantilever bending tests.
The results of the hardness values in the nanoinden-
tation tests [Figure 2(a)] and the 0.2 pct proof stress in
the micro-cantilever bending tests [Figure 4(b)] suggest
that the resistance to plastic deformation of IMI-834
and Ti-6242S at high temperatures is larger than that of
Ti-6Al-4V alloys at the microscale level. The similar
temperature-induced strength diﬀerence between
nanoindentation (focusing on single a-grain) and
micro-cantilever bending test results (containing grain
boundaries) suggest that the cause of high-temperature
strengthening of IMI-834 and Ti-6242S is the increased
strength of each a-grain by solid-solution strengthening
rather than its complex microstructure. In addition, as
shown in Figure 3, the activated slip plane at grains
having similar crystal orientations with respect to the
stress direction was similar between 25 /C176 C and 350 /C176 C.
This suggests that the solid-solution elements function
as obstacles to slip at high temperatures in IMI-834 and
Ti-6242S alloys, whereas the slips are easily activated by
high temperatures in Ti-6Al-4V alloys. Ti-6242S and
IMI-834 alloys contain a higher content of solid-solu-
tion strengthening elements for a phase (Al, Sn and Zr)
than those in Ti-6Al-4V.
[4] Furthermore, diﬀusion
aﬀects the high-temperature strength; therefore, Mo
and Nb, which are slower diﬀusing elements in a phase,
may suppress the a phase deformation in Ti-6242S and
IMI-834.[1] In addition, silicon addition also aﬀects the
high-temperature strength. [20] Combining these factors
seems to increase the hardness of a-grain at 350 /C176 Ci n
Ti-6242S and IMI-834. The concentration of solid
solution is diﬀerent between the primary and secondary
a phases.[9,21] The hardness of primary and secondary a
phases might be diﬀerent at high temperature, which
could be explored in future research.
In summary, we performed micromechanical tests
(nanoindentation and micro-cantilever bending tests) on
Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and IMI-834 alloys to investigate
the microscale plastic behavior in a high-temperature
environment. The hardness values of Ti-6242S and
IMI-834 in the nanoindentation tests and 0.2 pct proof
stress in micro-cantilever bending tests at 350 /C176 C were
larger than those of Ti-6Al-4V alloys, whereas those at
25 /C176 C were similar among IMI-834, Ti-6242S, and
Ti-6Al-4V alloys. In the micro-cantilever bending test,
the activated slip planes of the Ti-6Al-4V, Ti-6242S, and
IMI-834 alloys did not diﬀer between room temperature
and high temperature conditions. The results suggest
that the strength of single a-grains at high temperatures
in Ti-6242S and IMI-834 is higher than that in
Ti-6Al-4V. Regardless of the complex microstructure
of titanium alloys, the solid-solution strengthening of
a-grain is the key mechanism in high-temperature
strengthening of Ti-6242S and IMI-834 alloys. These
insights into the high-temperature strengthening mech-
anism will contribute to the improvement of heat-resis-
tant titanium alloys.
