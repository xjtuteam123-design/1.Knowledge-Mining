ABSTRACT 
Hot cracking is a major challenge in the additive manufacturing of Ni-base superalloys. Most cracking indicators 
only consider the alloy composition and its effects on solidification behavior, strength, and ductility. We propose 
a new model that extends these classical cracking indicators by additionally incorporating the transient thermo- 
mechanical state during melting and solidification. The model is derived from insights into the cracking of thin 
layers and is mainly based on the elastic strain energy available to drive crack opening in a critical temperature 
interval. The underlying thermo-mechanical process simulations are implemented in a custom computational 
framework designed to efficiently model powder bed fusion. The model predictions are validated against 
experimentally measured crack densities in CMSX-4 processed by electron beam powder bed fusion. The pro -
posed model allows for rationalizing the dependence of cracking susceptibility on the thermal history, which is 
controlled by scan speed and beam power. Finally, the model is applied to develop a crack mitigation strategy by 
reducing the built-up strain energy density. This is achieved by heating the melting area to a high temperature 
before melting, thereby reducing the temperature gradient. 
1. Introduction 
Additive manufacturing (AM) has enabled the manufacturing of Ni- 
alloys with great freedom to tailor geometry and microstructure, making 
columnar or equiaxed grain structures [1,2] and even single crystals 
[3,4] possible. Ni-base superalloys have received significant attention in 
this regard due to the potential to fabricate chemically homogeneous [5] 
and creep-resistant [6] parts, which is enabled by rapid cooling resulting 
in a fine dendritic structure. The superalloys with the best mechanical 
properties employed in the hottest section of gas and jet engine turbines 
must withstand high stresses and temperatures while resisting oxidation 
and corrosion. These materials have a high volume fraction of γ ʹ phase, 
an L1
2

sible for their excellent high-temperature capability. However, this 
makes them poorly weldable and consequently difficult to process by 
AM, as they are prone to cracking. Electron beam powder bed fusion 
(PBF – EB) is an AM process that is particularly well suited to processing 
crack-sensitive alloys since the build temperature can be kept constantly 
above 1000 
◦
C, thereby reducing the thermal gradient and thermal 
stresses compared to the widespread laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB). 
Indeed, single crystals with a crack-free core of the Ni-base superalloy 
CMSX-4 [7] and other γ ʹ -rich superalloys [8] have been manufactured by 
PBF – EB, while crack-free manufacturing has not yet been achieved by 
PBF-LB [9] . However, manufacturing of complex geometries and fine 
structures is not yet possible. 
Generally, four cracking mechanisms are commonly observed in 
additively manufactured superalloys: solidification cracking, liquation 
cracking, strain-age cracking, and ductility-dip cracking. Strain-age 
cracking [10] and ductility-dip cracking [11,12] occur in the solid 
state at intermediate temperatures between 600 and 900 
◦
C. These 
mechanisms are less relevant to PBF-EB due to the high process 
temperature. 
Solidification cracking and liquation cracking involve the presence 
of a liquid phase between grain boundaries. Both can be classified as hot 
cracking since they occur at high temperatures. Solidification cracking 
occurs in the semi-solid state towards the end of solidification. Due to 
thermal shrinkage, small pores or liquid-filled cavities can act as crack 
initiation sites when dendrites are pulled apart [13] . Several models 
have been proposed to predict hot cracking [14] , partially with con -
tradictory results [15] . The simplest approach only considers the so -
lidification interval, i.e., the difference between solidus and liquidus 
temperatures. A narrow solidification interval is considered beneficial

The problem of hot cracking in AM has not yet been solved 
completely. Since many commercial alloys now processed by AM were 
developed initially for casting and are susceptible to this defect, exten -
sive efforts have been dedicated to developing alloys that resist cracking 
in AM. The development of Ni-base alloys has been focused on reducing 
the cracking susceptibility using thermodynamic calculations to esti -
mate solidification and strain-age cracking indicators. This approach led 
to the design of ExpAM by Zhou et al. [20] , ABD-850AM, and ABD- 
900AM by Tang et al. [21] . Harrison et al. [22] modified Hastelloy X, 
and Xu et al. [23] developed the novel alloy MAD542 by improving the 
solid solution strengthening to improve the alloys ’ strength in a critical 
temperature range. All these alloys could be produced crack-free by PBF- 
LB. However, some evidence suggests that classical hot cracking 
mechanisms cannot fully explain the cracking phenomena. Yu et al. [24] 
found that a combination of hot and strain age cracking caused cracks in 
prototype alloys. Furthermore, most of these newly developed alloys

Mar-M247, possess γ ′ solvus temperatures of nearly 1300 
◦

Cracking mechanisms might differ between these alloy classes. 
In a previous study [27] , we found that hot cracking criteria such as 
the freezing range or the Kou criterion are not well suited to developing 
Ni-base superalloys with high γ ʹ solvus temperature since alloys opti -
mized for low cracking susceptibility showed greater crack densities 
after remelting experiments than the already crack-sensitive Mar-M247. 
Contrarily, the liquidus and γ ʹ solvus temperatures offered better pre -
dictive capability. Furthermore, studies by Horst [25] , Volz [28] , and 
Jackson [29] found a significant increase in the thermal expansion co -
efficient of Ni- and Co-base superalloys near the γ ʹ solvus temperature. 
This should lead to strong material contraction during cooling, poten -
tially causing cracks to open. Thus, an in-depth understanding of the 
thermo-mechanical material behavior during the AM process is required 
to better comprehend cracking in γ ʹ -rich superalloys. 
The coupling of a thermo-mechanical analysis with crack formation 
has been attempted by several other researchers. Tran at al. [30] 
rationalized the cracking between support structures and the part in 
PBF-LB by evaluating the J integral based on the stress field around a 
crack simulated on the part scale. Nie et al [31] modeled the tempera -
ture and stress fields in a single laser melted track for various process 
parameters and explained an increased amount of cracks by a greater 
magnitude of the von Mises stress. Lu et al. [32] simulated the effect of 
different scanning strategies on the stresses in cuboidal specimens to 
offer a means of controlling stress-induced crack propagation. A 
computationally complex hot cracking model was developed by Ruan 
et al. [33] by coupling a thermo-mechanical finite element analysis with 
a cohesive phase-field fracture model that enabled predicting the loca -
tion of crack formation. Its applicability was however limited to short 
single scan paths due to the high computational cost. 
In this study, we use a detailed finite element simulation to model the 
mechanics of the PBF – EB process for different process and material 
parameters. By combining approaches from the fracture of thin films and 
hot cracking in welds, we propose a new cracking model for Ni-base 
superalloys based on the stored elastic strain energy density induced 
by the AM process. The strain energy density depends on the processing 
conditions as well as material parameters such as Young ’ s modulus and 
yield strength. The model is experimentally validated and applied by 
proposing crack mitigation strategies through modification of process 
strategies. 
2. Model description 
2.1. Cracking model 
For the development of a qualitative indicator that describes the 
cracking susceptibility of an alloy based on thermo-mechanical simu -
lations, we take inspiration from classical hot cracking models as well as 
from the fracture of thin films. To a certain extent, the melting of one 
layer is analogous to depositing a coating layer on top of a substrate 
since the resulting stress states are comparable: the deposited material 
cools down and shrinks on the substrate, resulting in tensile residual 
stresses. Cracks can initiate in thin coatings if two conditions are fulfilled 
simultaneously: a stress criterion requiring that the stress is higher than 
the material ’ s strength and an energy criterion requiring that enough 
potential energy be present to create a crack [34] . Fracture mechanics 
predict that opening of cracks is easier in thick layers than in thin layers 
for equal stress states since the energy available to create a crack in -
creases with the thickness of the layer [35] . Consequently, the critical 
energy density required for cracking is inversely proportional to the 
layer thickness. However, once the layer ’ s strength dominates, cracks 
initiate at a constant stress or energy density [36] . 
Applying this approach to additive manufacturing, we assume that a 
crack is likely to form if a high strain energy density is present in a thick 
region below the deposited material ’ s surface after the melting of a 
layer. Thus, a very thin crack-sensitive layer may suppress crack 
formation.

stresses and strains contributes to crack formation, as compression 
would counteract the crack opening. Furthermore, we only consider the

4.1. Thermo-mechanical material state during additive manufacturing 
The general thermo-mechanical state during PBF-EB of a simple 
cuboid is shown in Fig. 2 for 650 W beam power and 3000 mm s

hidden. The beam is scanned along a meandering pattern in the Y di -

The cuboid is built on top of a start plate, which extends from 0 to – 2.5 
mm in the Z direction. 
Several typical features characterize the material state. The top of the 
cuboid expands due to rapid heating by the electron beam. As it is 
constrained by the cooler material below, this hot region ( Fig. 2 d)) is in 
a compressive stress state ( Fig. 2 a)). By contrast, the hot expanded re -
gion stretches the cooler region below, causing a tensile stress state. As 
soon as the material ’ s yield strength is exceeded, it deforms plastically 
( Fig. 2 b)). The periodically varying plastic strain results from the line 
order melting, as the beam passes multiple times close to a single volume 
element. The solidified melt is also under tensile stress since the un -
derlying layers inhibit its thermal contraction. As the material cools 
down, it shrinks on the cooler material, leading to compressive stresses 
in the substrate. These effects of the temperature field on the stress states 
are known as the temperature gradient mechanism [55] .

The present cracking model was developed with γ ′ -rich superalloys 
and the characteristics of the PBF-EB process in mind and is not 
straightforwardly applicable to, e.g., PBF-LB. Due to the high process 
temperature and comparatively slow cooling rate, high amounts of γ ′ 
phase form during the PBF-EB build process [65] , whereas γ ′ formation 
is largely suppressed due to the high cooling rate in PBF-LB [9,66] . The 
absence of γ ′ precipitates during cooling will affect the alloy ’ s strength 
[67] and the thermal expansion, potentially removing the characteristic 
peak in CTE that is associated with γ ′ dissolution. This may be why most 
cracking models applied in developing Ni-base superalloys for PBF-LB 
do not take the γ ʹ phase into account or consider only its effect on 
strain age cracking susceptibility during heat treatment [21] . Xu et al. 
found that in PBF-LB, the γ ʹ fraction has a negligible influence on the 
cracking behavior and demonstrated crack-free processing of the γ ʹ -rich 
superalloy MAD542 [23] . However, at about 1180 
◦
C, this alloy still has 
a lower γ ʹ solvus temperature and phase fraction than the investigated 
CMSX-4, which remains challenging to process. Thus, the effect of γ ʹ on 
cracking appears to depend on the manufacturing process due to the 
varying heating and cooling rates and the resulting influence on the 
precipitation kinetics. 
It should be noted that the microstructure was not considered in the 
cracking model. Engineering of the grain structure has proved to be 
another way to mitigate cracking. On the one hand, the shrinkage strain 
is distributed over more interfaces by producing a fine equiaxed or 
columnar grain structure [19] so that no significant tensile strain can 
build up at any gain boundary. On the other hand, eliminating high- 
angle grain boundaries entirely by producing single crystals also 
removed hot cracks [8,51,68] . Since processing conditions affect 
stresses and microstructure simultaneously, both effects must be taken 
into account for designing optimal process strategies. 
6. Conclusions 
We present a new model based on a strain energy density criterion 
for cracking of Ni-base superalloys in electron beam powder bed fusion 
derived from the cracking mechanisms of thin films. The model was 
validated by comparing its predictions from process simulations with 
experimentally determined crack densities in CMSX-4 fabricated by PBF- 
EB. We draw the following conclusions: 
• The proposed cracking model explains the dependence of the crack 
density on energy input and scan speed qualitatively well by 
considering both the stored strain energy density and the extent of 
the crack-sensitive region. 
• Local variations in the crack density could be rationalized by eval -
uating the spatial distribution of the overall maximum strain energy 
density in the vulnerable temperature range. 
• The influence of the alloy composition on the cracking sensitivity is 
reproduced well by the proposed cracking model with the example of 
IN718. The alloy ’ s yield strength has the greatest effect as it de -
termines the stored strain energy density. 
• The description of process- and composition-dependent crack sensi -
tivity enables the development of crack mitigation strategies by 
optimizing alloys and processing strategies. In the case of γ ʹ -rich 
superalloys, reducing the temperature gradient by local preheating 
seems to be a promising approach.
