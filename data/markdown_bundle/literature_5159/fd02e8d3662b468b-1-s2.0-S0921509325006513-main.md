ABSTRACT
The extremely fast cooling rate of laser additive manufacturing (LAM) inhibits the precipitation of the γ ′ phase in 
Ni-based superalloys, necessitating subsequent heat treatment to induce γ ′ precipitation. However, traditional 
heat treatment processes often compromise the cellular microstructure strengthening inherent in additive 
manufacturing (AM) processes. This paper introduces a novel heat treatment regime that precipitates nanoscale 
γ ′ phase while preserving the cellular structure, achieving a balance of strength and ductility in Ni-based su -
peralloys processed by Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). Studies show that heat-treated samples have a higher 
density of nanoscale γ ′ phase compared to as-deposited sample. Although the cellular structure size increases 
with annealing temperature, it remains intact. Compared to as-deposited sample ( σ

a synergistic enhancement of strength and ductility in AM γ ′ -phase-strengthened Ni-based superalloys.
1. Introduction
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is one of the most widely used metal 
additive manufacturing (AM) technologies in industry and is renowned 
for its unparalleled design freedom, short production cycles, and high 
material utilization rates [ 1 , 2 ]. It has been extensively employed in the 
fabrication of complex hot-end components from Ni-based superalloys. 
However, the unique high cooling rates (1 – 40 × 10
6 
K/s) and high 
temperature gradients (10
6
– 10
7 
K/m) characteristic of LPBF can sup -
press precipitation of the γ ′ phase during the fabrication of 
γ ′ -phase-strengthened Ni-based superalloys [ 3 , 4 ], often necessitating 
post-processing to control the precipitation of the γ ′ phase [ 5 ]. This is 
crucial for achieving a performance comparable to or superior to that of 
hot-end components manufactured using conventional processes. 
Therefore, it is essential to develop a rational heat treatment regime to 
control the properties of LPBF-formed γ ′ -phase-strengthened Ni-based 
superalloys.
Existing heat treatment regimes for LPBF-formed γ ′ -phase-strength -
ened Ni-based superalloys, however, are generally derived from those 
used for traditionally cast and forged components, and often disrupt the 
unique cellular structures inherent to LPBF processes [ 6 – 10 ]. Early 
research indicated that the presence of cellular structures confers sig -
nificant advantages in terms of strength, corrosion resistance, and creep 
performance over conventional materials [ 11 – 13 ], and these structures 
are prevalent in LPBF-fabricated components across various alloys 
[ 14 – 16 ]. Moreover, the interaction between cellular structures and 
other deformation microstructures, such as twins, can induce additional 
mechanisms for energy dissipation during deformation, leading to

2.2. Heat treatment process
Introducing annealing twins into LPBF-formed components can 
achieve a balance between strength and ductility [ 25 , 26 ]. However, 
previous studies [ 27 ] found that the effect of introducing annealing 
twins was not ideal. This is because during the traditional heat treatment 
process of the alloy, the precipitation of the γ ′ phase is accompanied by a 
significant precipitation of the η phase, which completely eliminates the 
cellular structure, preventing the alloy from achieving the desired bal -
ance of strength and ductility. To preserve the cellular structure, pre -
cipitate the γ ′ phase, and avoid the precipitation of the η phase, this 
study designed a heat treatment regime suitable for the alloy based on 
the TTT curves calculated by JMatPro ( Fig. S1 ): initially, direct aging 
treatments were conducted for 2 h at 750

respectively, followed by an annealing treatment at 540

These three samples were named HT1, HT2, and HT3, and the 
as-deposited sample was named LPBFed.

4. Discussion
We have employed a unique heat treatment process that precipitates 
nanoscale γ ′ phase while retaining the cellular structure, achieving a 
synergy between strength and ductility. We investigated the reasons for 
this synergy from the evolution of the cellular structure and precipita -
tion phase, the strengthening mechanisms and their contribution to 
strength, and deformation mechanisms.
4.1. Evolution of cellular structure and precipitation phases
In the process of rapid solidification, the segregation of solutes re -
sults in the creation of dislocation cells through the pinning of disloca -
tions, and at the same time, rapid solidification suppresses the formation 
of the γ ′ phase. Consequently, changes in the microstructure that occur 
during heat treatment involve two main elements: the enlargement of 
dislocation cells and nucleation of the γ ′ phase.
Regarding the growth of dislocation cells, Ti as a primary segregating 
element results in a decrease in the SF energy within the cell walls 
( Fig. 11 (a), JMatPro), which is more pronounced in the HT2 sample, 
allowing screw dislocations to dominate the cell walls [ 47 ]. Initially, the 
segregating elements, acting as point defects with relatively low thermal 
activation energies, undergo short-circuit diffusion through dislocation 
channels at the beginning of the heat treatment [ 48 ], enabling disloca -
tions that were pinned by the segregating atoms to unpin. Concurrently, 
the movement of the segregating atoms promotes the climbing of dis -
locations, ultimately leading to the reaction and annihilation of dislo -
cations with opposite signs. This conclusion is well-supported by Fig. 11
(c). Second, from a thermodynamic perspective, the LPBFed sample is 
unstable (high residual stress), and given the correct thermodynamic 
conditions (such as increased temperature), it transforms towards a 
lower energy state. However, the energy of LAGBs is given by E =

4.3. Deformation mechanisms
4.3.1. Deformation twin formation
Parts fabricated via LPBF undergo repeated cycles of heating and 
cooling, leading to the formation of a distinctive cellular structure that 
differs from that formed during cold deformation processes. Previous 
research has revealed the following: first, this cellular structure acts as a 
"soft" barrier that can impede dislocation motion, though not 
completely, indicating a relatively weak obstructive effect [ 22 ]. 
Consequently, as the dislocations move, some are captured by the 
cellular structure. With continued external load, more dislocations are 
captured, and when similar dislocations glide on adjacent planes, DTs 
are formed. Second, the capturing capacity is limited and does not lead 
to extensive movable dislocation cross-slip [ 20 ]. According to the 
classical double cross-slip mechanism, twin nucleation is influenced by 
the size of the Frank – Read (F-R) source [ 67 ]. In this study, the inter -
spacing of nanometer-sized dispersed precipitates within the matrix can 
be considered as the F-R source size, which promotes the formation of 
twins. Ultimately, misorientation on the cell walls can generate defects 
that act as dislocation sources, which in turn promote the formation of 
nanoscale twins. This results in stable plastic flow during the deforma -
tion process, enhances strain hardening via the dynamic Hall – Petch 
effect, and delays the onset of necking [ 17 ]. To encapsulate, synergy 
between the cellular structure and the dispersed nanoscale γ ′ phase 
during deformation triggers the creation of SFs, L-C locks, and DTs. This 
interaction ensures that the heat-treated samples maintain a certain 
level of ductility ( Fig. 13 (c and d)).
4.3.2. Work hardening behavior
Fig. 7 (b) illustrates that the high ductility of the LPBFed specimen 
originates from nanoscale DTs triggered by high-density dislocations 
within the cellular structure, as well as its superior grain deformation 
coordination ability [ 11 ]. The presence of a step in the work-hardening 
curve of the heat-treated samples, as shown in Fig. 7 (b), indicates an 
obstruction encountered during plastic deformation. This is due to the 
presence of γ ′ -phase precipitates of differing sizes within the HT1, HT2, 
and HT3 samples. After tensile deformation, the cellular structure re -
mains stable, which is consistent with the results of Ding [ 5 ] and Voisin 
[ 22 ]. The presence of γ ′ phase within the cellular structure bolsters 
dislocation pinning, resulting in markedly increased local shear stress 
and the trapping of dislocations within the cells. The variation in plas -
ticity among HT1, HT2, and HT3 samples is likely due to variation in the 
diameter of the cellular structure and the γ ′ phase: on the one hand, γ ′ 
phase that is too small reduces the obstructive effect on dislocations, 
preventing sustained work hardening, as demonstrated by the necking 
tendency of HT1 in Fig. 7 (a); on the other hand, γ ′ phase that is too large 
causes severe lattice distortion, and a reduction in the dislocation cut -

5. Conclusions
This study demonstrates that specialized heat treatment can effec -
tively optimize the microstructure of LPBFed nickel-based superalloys, 
resulting in a significant enhancement of their mechanical properties. By 
creating a synergistic combination of cellular structures and nano- γ ′ 
precipitates, we achieved a remarkable balance of strength and ductility 
in the HT2 sample, with its yield strength increasing from 759 MPa to 
1299 MPa and tensile strength from 1034 MPa to 1517 MPa, while 
retaining 20.7 % elongation. The study also reveals that the deformation 
mechanisms in the heat-treated samples involve complex interactions 
between stacking faults, L-C locks, nano-twins, and the cellular structure 
with nano-precipitates. These findings not only highlight the effective -
ness of tailored heat treatments in enhancing the mechanical properties 
of LPBFed precipitation-strengthened nickel-based superalloys but also 
offer valuable insights for future research and development in this field. 
However, further investigation into properties like fatigue, corrosion, 
and creep, particularly within the composite structure of cellular 
structures and γ ′ phases, is needed for high-temperature applications.
