ABSTRACT 
In recent years, significant efforts have been made to develop high-performance alloys for engineering appli -
cations, including high-entropy alloys with lightweight and high ductility at room temperature and high strength 
at high temperature. In this work, a novel lightweight (NiAl)
55
(FeCrMoV)
45 
high-entropy alloy (HEA) was 
prepared using vacuum arc-melting. Its microstructure, compressive properties and deformation mechanisms 
were investigated. The results showed that the HEA is composed of BCC and B2 phases and has a low density of 
only 6.72 g/cm
3
. Its compressive yield strength, specific yield strength and fracture strain at room temperature 
are 1739.5 MPa, 258.9 MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g and 24.9%, respectively; and its yield strength and specific yield strength at 
1073 K and 1273 K are 950.7 MPa and 141.2 MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g, 421.8 MPa and 62.8 MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g, respectively, better 
than that of most HEAs, refractory HEAs (RHEAs) and conventional alloys reported previously. The plastic 
deformation of the HEA is governed by dislocation slip at both room and elevated temperatures. The high 
strength of the HEA can mainly be attributed to the addition of high melting point elements Mo and V. The 
excellent comprehensive mechanical properties of the HEA indicate its potential application prospects in high- 
temperature structures. 
1. Introduction 
With the rapid development of modern aviation and aerospace in -
dustries, the development of high-performance alloys with excellent 
mechanical properties has become increasingly urgent [ 1 ]. Over the past 
few decades, Ni-based alloys, as the most common metallic materials, 
have played an important role in extreme applications, such as aviation 
engines and gas turbines, due to their excellent properties at room and 
elevated temperatures. However, the strength of Ni-based alloys de -
creases rapidly when temperature exceeds 650 
◦
C due to the limitation 
of their melting points, which seriously restricted their further appli -
cations in aviation and space industries [ 2 ]. Therefore, it is imperative to 
develop next-generation high-performance alloys for such extreme ap -
plications with novel alloy design concepts. In the past decades, the 
development of high-entropy alloys (HEAs), which were first proposed 
by Yeh et al. [ 3 ] and Cantor et al. [ 4 ] and have been considered as a new 
type of alloys, has become a research focus. Compared with conven -
tional alloys, HEAs are composed of four to five or more main elements, 
and the atomic percentage of each main element ranging from 5% to 
35% [ 3 ]. This special alloy design strategy can not only enrich the types 
of metallic materials, but also provide ideas for breaking through the 
performance limits of traditional metallic materials. 
In the past decade, as a branch of HEAs, refractory high-entropy 
alloys (RHEAs) composed of high melting point elements (such as Mo, 
Ta, W, Nb, Hf, Zr, Ti and V) have attracted increasing interest [ 5 ], 
mainly due to their excellent properties at elevated temperature, such as 
high strength [ 6 ], excellent stability [ 7 ], and softening resistance [ 8 ], 
etc. For examples, Senkov et al. [ 9 ] reported two body-centered-cubic 
(BCC)-structured NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW RHEAs, which not only 
exhibit excellent softening resistance at temperatures ranging from 
600 
◦
C to 1200 
◦
C, but also have yield strengths exceeding 400 MPa at 
1600 
◦
C. It should be emphasized that the high-temperature yield 
strengths of these two RHEAs are significantly higher than that of con -
ventional Ni-based alloys. Unfortunately, the two RHEAs have very high 
densities ( > 12 g/cm
3
) and very low fracture strain ( < 3%) at room 
temperature, which largely restrict their practical engineering applica -
tions. To reduce the density of RHEAs without sacrificing high- 
temperature strength, replacing high-density elements Ta and W with

high-entropy alloy (HEA) is designed based on the 
following considerations: (1) the B2 phase composed of elements Ni and 
Al can not only improve the strength and oxidation resistance, but also 
significantly reduce the density [ 15 – 17 ]; (2) the BCC phase composed of 
elements Fe and Cr can form a coherent interface with B2 phase, which 
can improve the mechanical properties due to the small lattice mismatch 
[ 18 ]; and (3) adding high melting point elements Mo and V can not only 
effectively improve the high temperature strength of the alloy, but also 
avoid the formation of topological close-packed phase (TCP, mainly 
including σ and Laves phases) [ 19 – 21 ]. The microstructure, compressive 
properties at both room and elevated temperatures, and the corre -
sponding deformation mechanisms of the HEA are analyzed and dis -
cussed in details. 
2. Materials and methods 
The alloy ingot of (NiAl)
55
(FeCrMoV)
45

water-cooled copper crucible in an argon atmosphere. The above pro -

and the final ingot is approximately 100 g.

America) equipped with energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The TEM

thickness of 50 μ m, then ion-milled until perforation using a precision

4. Discussion 
4.1. Deformation mechanisms 
Generally, for dual-phase or multi-phase alloys, phase interface is 
very important for plastic deformation, because it can induce the initi -
ation of dislocations at the beginning of plastic deformation [ 33 , 40 ]. 
Figs. 7 (a) and (b) show respectively the low and high magnification TEM 
images of the HEA after compression test at room temperature, where 
many dislocations can be observed in the BCC phase, B2 phase and phase 
interface, indicating that the deformation mechanism is plastic defor -
mation governed by dislocation slip. Phase interface dislocations can 
effectively reduce the long-range internal stress induced by lattice 
mismatch [ 41 ]. In addition, the green ellipses in Fig. 7 (b) show that 
some dislocations slip across the phase interface. Fig. 7 (c) shows the 
HRTEM image of the phase interface and FFT patterns of different 
phases, where it can be seen that the atomic plane is continuous when 
crossing the interface, indicating insignificant phase interface mismatch. 
Fig. 7 (d) is the IFFT image at the phase interface in the region sur -
rounded by the pink square in Fig. 7 (c), where it can be seen that there 
still very few dislocations after deformation, attributed to insignificant 
phase interface mismatch. 
With the rapid development of aviation and aerospace industries, it 
is very important and meaningful to reveal the deformation mechanism 
of alloys working in high-temperature environments. Figs. 8 (a) and (b) 
show respectively the low and high magnification TEM images of the 
HEA after compression test at 1073 K. Similar to the room temperature 
test results shown in Fig. 7 , the plastic deformation of the HEA com -
pressed at 1073 K also take place by dislocation slip. Fig. 8 (c) shows the

5. Conclusions 
In this work, a novel lightweight (NiAl)
55
(FeCrMoV)
45 
high-entropy 
alloy (HEA) was designed and prepared with vacuum arc-melting. The 
microstructure, compressive mechanical properties and deformation 
mechanisms were investigated. Based on the experimental and analyt -
ical results, the main conclusions are summarized as follows: 
(1) The lightweight HEA is composed of BCC and B2 phases and has a 
density of only 6.72 g/cm
3
. The lattice mismatch at the interface 
between BCC and B2 phases is very small. 
(2) The HEA shows excellent mechanical properties when subjected 
to uniaxial compression at room and elevated temperatures, 
superior to most EHEAs, RHEAs and conventional alloys reported 
previously. Specifically, the yield strength, specific yield strength 
and fracture strain of the HEA at room temperature are 1739.5 
MPa, 258.9 MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g and 24.9%, respectively. The yield 
strength and specific yield strength of the HEA are 950.7 MPa and 
141.2 MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g at 1073 K, and are 421.8 MPa and 62.8 
MPa ⋅ cm
3
/g at 1273 K. 
(3) A large number of dislocations are observed in BCC and B2 phases 
after deformation at both room and elevated temperatures, 
indicating that the deformation mechanism is plastic deformation 
governed by dislocation slip.
