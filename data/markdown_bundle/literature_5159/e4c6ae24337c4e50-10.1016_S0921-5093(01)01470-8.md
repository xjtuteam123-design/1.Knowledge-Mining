The inﬂuence of high temperature exposure on the mechanical
performance of a /p103titanium aluminide alloy
S.K. Planck 1, A.H. Rosenberger *
Materials & Manufacturing Directorate , Air Force Research Laboratory , AFRL/MLLMN, Wright-Patterson AFB , OH 45433-7817, USA
Received 27 November 2000; received in revised form 27 April 2001
Abstract
The inﬂuence of a prolonged high temperature air exposure on the tensile and fatigue behavior of a /p103titanium aluminide
(Ti–46Al–2Nb–2Cr–1Mo–0.2B, at.%) has been examined as a function of temperature, ambient to 760 °C. The embrittled
surface layer has an effect on both properties. While the effect on tensile ductility diminishes as the interrogation temperature is
increased, the inﬂuence on fatigue strength is most pronounced at the intermediate temperature of 540 °C. This appears to be
linked to the minimum in fatigue crack growth resistance that is associated with this temperature regime. The effect of high
temperature exposure has been effectively modeled, based on the initial crack sizes observed on the fracture surfaces, using an
integration of the crack growth rate. However, the threshold observed in standard long crack specimens appears to be
non-conservative. Published by Elsevier Science B.V.

1. Introduction
Gamma titanium aluminide (TiAl) alloys have under-
gone extensive development over the past decade due to
their potential as a structural component in gas turbine
engines for future ﬁghter and commercial aircraft. The
technical advancement of turbine engines requires that
lighter weight, high temperature structures be intro-
duced to improve performance and efﬁciency. Lower
density alloys like /p103TiAls are pivotal in allowing this
advance [1,2]. Gamma TiAls have attractive properties
for high temperature structural applications that in-
clude a low density of approximately 3.8 g cm
−3, good
oxidation and burn resistance, high strength, modulus
retention, good oxidation and hot corrosion resistance,
and excellent creep properties [4]. The current /p103TiAl
alloys appear to possess useful mechanical properties
up to 760 °C (1400 °F), which extends the capabilities
of titanium-based alloys beyond that of conventional
/p97–/p98titanium alloys and promises to replace nickel-base
superalloys in some applications with a material having
one-half the density [3]. A number of components have
been identiﬁed for replacement with /p103TiAl including
compressor blades and vanes, turbine cover plates, and
low pressure turbine blades. The most aggressive appli-
cation for /p103TiAl alloys is for the rotating, fracture-crit-
ical compressor hardware including compressor disks
and integrally bladed rotors. In addition, a density of
half that of nickel-based superalloys promises dramatic
weight savings in large static structures such as dif-
fusers, ducts, and cases [5].
The successful application of /p103TiAl alloys to these
components requires, among many other things, a de-
tailed understanding of their mechanical performance
in the high temperature environment and a thorough
assessment of their durability and reliability. The effect
of high temperature air exposure on the tensile strength
and ductility has been examined on a number of /p103
alloys [6,7]. Kelly, et al. [6] found a reduction in ductil-
ity in a duplex material after exposures as short as 10 h
at 315 °C. However, specimens that had been exposed
at 650 °C for 16 h regained their ductility, when tested

2. Experimental
2.1. Material
The /p103TiAl (Ti –46Al–2Nb–2Cr–1Mo–0.2B, at.%)
used in this study was provided by the US Air Force [10].
Three hundred and ﬁfty-six millimeter diameter ingots
were prepared by single hearth plasma arc melting and
received a 6 to 1 primary reduction by isothermal
extrusion. Secondary reduction was achieved using
isothermal forging. Heat treatment of the forged alloy,
in air, involved exposure at 1300 °C for 25 min followed
b ya5m i ne x p osure at 1370 °C. After air cooling to
900 °C, that temperature was held for 24 h and then air
cooled to room temperature. This heat treatment pro-
duced a nearly lamellar microstructure, Fig. 1, with a
lamellar colony size of approximately 100 /p109m consisting
of equiaxed /p103and B2 at the colony boundaries.
2.2. Experimental techniques
Modiﬁed dog-bone tensile and fatigue specimens, 9.5
mm diameter, having diametrically opposed ﬂats reduc-
ing the gage section to 25 mm2 and yielding a 15 mm long
uniform gage length, were machined from the forged
ingots using electro-discharge machining followed by
low-stress grinding. Select specimens were subjected to
high temperature isothermal exposures in the as-ma-
chined state in a box furnace at 760 °C prior to testing.
The exposure temperature of 760 °C was chosen to
represent the most severe thermal condition to be expe-
rienced by /p103components in gas turbine engines. The
isothermal exposures here do not simulate the true
thermal cycling that engine components receive, but
experiments performed at 700 °C comparing the oxida-
tion behavior under isothermal and cyclic conditions,
indicates that no spallation of the oxide scale occurs at
this temperature [11,15]. Hence cyclic and isothermal
exposures should produce similar levels of environmental
damage. Some of the exposed specimens were
remachined, via low-stress grinding to remove approxi-
mately 100 /p109m of surface. All of the unexposed specimens
and those low-stress ground (LSG) after the high temper-
ature exposure were electropolished prior to fatigue
testing.
The mechanical testing was performed in air using a
servohydraulic test machine equipped with a four zone,
quartz lamp heating system that maintained the central
12 mm gage length of the specimen at the test tempera-
ture. Test temperature was measured and controlled
using four ‘loop’thermocouples [13]. The loop thermo-
couples consist of a twisted pair of thermoelements that
are looped around the specimen and held in contact with
the surface of the specimen by tension in the wires. This
technique eliminates the need to weld thermocouples to
the specimen and the possibility of a weld-induced crack
initiation sites.
Tensile tests were conducted in stroke control using a
crosshead rate to approximate a strain rate of 10
−4 m
(m s) −1. Tensile tests were performed under ambient,
540, and 760 °C temperature conditions and strain was
monitored using a high temperature, quartz rod exten-
someter. The specimens were allowed to soak at test
temperature for only 20 min prior to testing so the
additional exposure for the 760 °C tests is negligible. At
least two specimens used for each test condition.

The reduction in fatigue resistance by 70 –80 MPa at
540 °C is the most severe material performance debit
observed after the high temperature exposure — and
potentially of the greatest concern. This most severe
reduction in fatigue strength may be explained by con-
sidering the relative contributions of crack initiation /
early crack growth and fatigue crack propagation [23].
In this high cycle fatigue (HCF) regime, the total
fraction of fatigue life attributed to crack propagation
is typically small. That is, the processes of crack initia-
tion dominate the HCF life. However, one effect of
long time, high temperature exposure is to enhance
crack initiation due to the embrittled surface of the
specimen. In this case, the total fatigue life would be
more in ﬂuenced by the number of cycles required to
propagate the crack. The worst crack growth resistance
(i.e. highest growth rates and lowest threshold in the
low /p68K regime) occurs at the highest elevated tempera-
ture, where the environment assists the crack growth,
but at temperatures still below the ductile to brittle
transition temperature (DBTT) [21,24]. In this material,
that means that the crack growth resistance is less at
540 than 760 °C, Fig. 10. At temperatures greater than
the DBTT, growth rates tend to fall below the room
temperature behavior due to the increase in ductility
and toughness [3].
The exposed specimens tested in air at 540 °C experi-
enced the greatest amount of fatigue strength loss,

4. Discussion
The effect of exposure on the tensile behavior of this
/p103alloy tends to follow the general behavior of other /p103
alloys examined, [6,7]. That is, the most severe reduc-
tion in tensile ductility and strength occurs at room
temperature after high temperature air exposure, while
the exposure has little or no in ﬂuence on higher temper-
ature properties. This implies that the main area of
concern for the environmentally induced ductility debit
would be during inspection or service of the gas turbine
engine components, when they are more likely to be
stressed at low temperatures. The effect of exposure on
the fatigue behavior of this alloy is more complex and
does not follow this simple trend. The greatest reduc-
tion in fatigue performance after long term exposure is
at the intermediate temperature with a lesser effect at
higher temperatures and even less in ﬂuence at room
temperature.
There appear to be two phenomena taking place in
the tests conducted at room temperature. Both the
tension and fatigue results indicate that there is an
improvement in the material ’s performance after the
extended ‘heat treatment ’ at 760 °C. The ductility in-
creases from a baseline of 1.08% to 1.33 and 1.56%
after 50 and 500 h treatments at 760 °C, respectively.

exposure effect may simply be based on the resistance
to crack initiation — in the embrittled surface layer. It is
well known that the fracture toughness is dependent on
the orientation of the lamellar colony [26] — the fracture
toughness being only /p24 MPa/p10m, when the colony is

embrittled with oxygen. In this case, the size of the
initiation crack would be on the order of the mi-
crostructural constituent, but the stress level causing
crack initiation would be in ﬂuenced by the time of the
exposure.

analysis of Wright and Rosenberger [9] where the em-

5. Conclusions
Gamma TiAls are susceptible to reductions in room
temperature tensile ductility after extended high tem-
perature air exposure. The debit diminishes at moder-
ately elevated test temperatures, however, a slight
reduction in tensile strength remains.
The fatigue resistance is also diminished by extended
high temperature air exposure. This reduction is most
severe at intermediate temperatures, and does not di-
minish at moderately elevated temperatures. At the
highest temperature, 760 °C, there is a lesser reduction
the case of the 760 °C tests also indicates that there may
be a delay in crack growth at the start of the new stress
block. The fracture surface marking is quite distinct in
these specimens, due to the high temperature of the
test, and therefore a rather reliable measure of the
initial crack size. The generally anti-conservative nature
of the predictions, in the case of the 540 °C tests,
indicates that either the pop-in crack sizes are actually
larger and do not have enough time to develop a
reliable surface marking, or the true threshold is less
than 2.25 MPa /p10m. In either case, the threshold is
lower at 540 than at 760 °C, which is consistent with
the long crack growth rate data. Therefore a large

The effects of high temperature exposure are limited
to the surface of the specimens — under both tensile and
fatigue loading conditions. This is demonstrated by the
elimination of any effects of exposure upon removal of
the surface layer. High temperature exposure, however,
did not change any of the fracture or crack initiation
processes. Crack initiation, under fatigue loading, ap-
pears to be associated with lamellar colonies having the
lath normal oriented approximately parallel to the
loading axis.
A clear link between the minimum fatigue crack
growth resistance and the most severe effect of expo-
sure, at the intermediate temperature (540 °C), has been
demonstrated. The fatigue crack growth threshold con-
trols the fatigue resistance after the high temperature
exposure. The apparent threshold for the small pop-in
cracks is lower than determined using standard long
crack threshold techniques, but the upper portion of
the curve appears to accurately represent the growth of
naturally initiated cracks — once they attain a growth
rate of approximately 10
−8 m per cycle. The embrittle-
ment appears to affect crack initiation. The size of the
initial cracks is only weakly dependent on exposure
time and only at the highest temperature. Better under-
standing and assessment of the initiation and growth
threshold for small cracks are needed for the develop-
ment of a truly robust design system tailored to /p103
alloys.
