Novel Metastable Nonequiatomic Ti‑Zr‑Nb‑Mo Medium‑Entropy Alloys 
with High Yield‑Strength‑to‑Elastic‑Modulus Ratios
Ka‑Kin Wong1 · Hsueh‑Chuan Hsu2 · Shih‑Ching Wu2 · Wen‑Fu Ho1 
Received: 14 August 2021 / Accepted: 11 October 2021 / Published online: 31 March 2022 
© The Korean Institute of Metals and Materials 2022
Abstract 
In this study, novel nonequiatomic Ti-rich Ti-Zr-Nb-Mo medium-entropy alloys (Ti-MEAs) with a metastable body-centered 
cubic structure were designed using Mo equivalent theory ([Mo]eq) for biomedical applications. The Ti65-Zr20-Nb14-Mo1 
(at%; Ti65-M1) and Ti65-Zr18-Nb16-Mo1 (at%; Ti65-M2) exhibited high yield strength of 1188 and 1118 MPa, respectively. 
Notably, the moduli of Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 were as low as 67.4 and 61 GPa, respectively, which were considerably lower 
than those of commercial biomedical alloys and several biomedical HEAs/MEAs. Transmission electron microscopy images 
indicated that Ti65-M2 reached a metastable state, resulting in a low elastic modulus. Furthermore, the yield-strength-to-elas-
tic-modulus ratios (× 1000) of Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 were as high as 17.6 and 18.3, respectively, which were approximately 
three times greater than that of a commercial Ti-6Al-4V ELI implant. This study applied metastable theory for developing 
two metastable Ti-MEAs with low elastic moduli.
Keywords Medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) · Metastable phase · Ti-rich · Alloy design · Elastic modulus
1 Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted considerable 
research attention for applications in many fields. Although 
the four core effects of HEAs result in them possessing 
high strength, their moduli of elasticity are correspondingly 
increased. The elastic modulus is a critical material char -
acteristic of alloys used as biomedical implants. When the 
elastic modulus of an implant is considerably higher than 
that of human bone (≤ 30 GPa), a stress shielding effect 
would occur and cause atrophy of the surrounding bone tis-
sue and, consequently, implant failure [1 –3]. Few studies 
have investigated biomedical HEAs/medium-entropy alloys 
(MEAs). Although biomedical HEA and MEA alloys exhibit 
high strength and biocompatibility [4–7], most studies have 
not discussed their elastic modulus. Furthermore, no study 
has examined the theory underlying the conventional design 
of low-elastic-modulus titanium (Ti) alloys in relation to 
HEA and MEA systems.
Ti alloys are widely used as implant materials because 
of their low elastic moduli and high specific strength [8]. Ti 
alloys can be classified into α, α + β, and β types, and adding 
more β-stabilizing elements in such alloys results in a stable 
β-phase structure [8–13]. Over the past few decades, studies 
on Ti alloys have shifted their focus to metastable β-phase Ti 
alloys to achieve an even lower modulus [14]. Molybdenum 
equivalent ([Mo]eq) theory and the valence electron concen-
tration (VEC) are typically used to calculate the phase stabil-
ity of Ti alloys. The term [Mo]eq denotes the contribution 
of each alloy element to the β-phase stability of a Ti alloy. 
 [Mo]eq (wt%) = 1.0 Mo + 0.74 V + 0.50 W + 0.39 Nb + 0.28 
Ta + 2.2 Fe + 1.69 Cr + 0.85 Cu + 1.22 Ni + 1.57 Co + 1.69 
Mn + 0 Sn + 0 Zr-1.0 Al [15]. A metastable β-Ti alloy with 
a low modulus can be obtained by maintaining [Mo]eq at 
approximately 10 wt% [16–20]; this applies to alloys such as 
Ti-25Nb-8Sn (wt%; 52 GPa) [16], Ti-24Nb-4Zr-7.9Sn (wt%; 
42 GPa) [17], Ti-4.9Mo-6Sn-14.2Nb-9.3Zr (wt%; 56 GPa) 
[18], Ti-5.2Mo-6.5Sn-10Zr-10.2Nb (wt%; 43 GPa) [19], 
and Ti-8Mo-4Nb-2Zr (wt%; 35 GPa) [20]. Ikehata et al. 
[21] used first-principles calculations to study the effect of 
the VEC in a binary Ti alloy system on elastic modulus,

Three Ti-rich nonequiatomic Ti-Zr-Nb-Mo MEAs (Ti-
MEAs) were developed, namely Ti50-Zr25-Nb15-Mo10 
(at%; Ti50), Ti58-Zr 23-Nb 12-Mo 7 (at%; Ti58), and 
 Ti65-Zr20-Nb10-Mo5 (at%; Ti65), for biomedical applications 
[24]. Among them, Ti65, which had the highest Ti content, 
exhibited the lowest elastic modulus (86 GPa). Because of 
the lattice distortion effect of the HEA, the yield strength 
of Ti65 (approximately 1381 MPa) was higher than those 
of conventional biomedical alloys such as Ti-6Al-4V ELI 
[25], 316L SS [ 26], and Co-Cr-Mo [ 27]). However, the 
moduli of the three Ti-MEAs were still higher than that of 
human bone. The present study also revealed that the elastic 
modulus of an alloy may be affected by several thermody -
namic parameters, such as differences in the atomic radius 
(δ), melting point (TL), and mixing enthalpy (ΔHmix) of the 
constituents. In the Ti-Zr-Nb-Mo MEA system, δ has a sig-
nificantly greater influence on the elastic modulus of the 
alloy relative to the other thermodynamic parameters of the 
alloy. A large difference in the atomic radii of alloying ele-
ments leads to severe lattice distortion and the generation 
of an elastic stress field [28]. Consequently, the capacity 
of atoms for elastic deformation decreases, and the elastic 
modulus of the alloy increases.
To further reduce the elastic modulus of Ti65, the present 
study applied [Mo]eq theory to adjust the elemental com -
position of Ti65 and designed two metastable-BCC Ti-Zr-
Nb-Mo MEAs, namely Ti65-Zr20-Nb14-Mo1 (at%; Ti65-M1) 
and Ti65-Zr18-Nb16-Mo1 (at%; Ti65-M2). Metastable HEAs/
MEAs with low elastic moduli are yet to be reported for bio-
medical applications. In this study, the correlations between 
the thermodynamic parameters and elastic moduli of the Ti-
MEAs were investigated, the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of the two metastable Ti-MEAs were examined, 
and their mechanical properties were compared with those of 
Ti65 and several conventional biomedical alloys (Ti-6Al-4V 
ELI, 316L SS, and Co-Cr-Mo).
2 Experimental Procedures
Nonequiatomic Ti65-M1, and Ti65-M2 were prepared with 
a commercial arc-melting vacuum pressure casting system 
(A-028, DAWNSHINE, Taiwan) by using Ti (99.7 wt% 
pure), Zr (99.9 wt% pure), Nb (99.95 wt% pure), and Mo 
(99.95 wt% pure). The compositions of Ti65-M1 and Ti65-
M2 MEAs were Ti(49.13 wt%)-Zr(28.81 wt%)-Nb(20.54 
wt%)-Mo(1.52 wt%) and Ti(49.11 wt%)-Zr(25.92 wt%)-
Nb(23.46 wt%)-Mo(1.51 wt%), respectively. Because 
the content of Ti in the three Ti-MEAs was less than 50 
wt%, the traditional Ti-alloy system did not apply to these 
three alloys. Prior to the casting phase, alloy ingots were 
melted more than six times to homogenize the constitu-
ent elements. The dimensions of the as-cast samples were 
30 mm × 5 mm × 1 mm, and the samples were subjected 
to three-point bending tests. The central part of the sam-
ples was used for microstructural characterization. Before 
analyses were performed, the surfaces of all the alloys were 
mechanically polished according to standard metallographic 
procedures to achieve a final level of 0.06-μm SiO2 polishing 
suspension. First, the surfaces of the samples were cleaned 
with deionized water; subsequently, the samples were soaked 
in alcohol and cleaned using an ultrasonic cleaner. X-ray dif-
fraction (XRD), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and 
three-point bending tests were performed on the polished 
alloys. The alloys were etched in a solution of deionized 
water, nitric acid, and hydrofluoric acid at a volume ratio of 
80:15:5 for electron probe microanalysis (EPMA).
Phase analysis was conducted through XRD 
(D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation at 
40 kV, 40 mA, 2θ = 20°–90°, a scanning speed of 4°/min, 
and a step size of 0.02°/step. Grain orientation and phase 
structure analyses were performed through EBSD (7800F, 
JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. 
The grain size was measured according to the ASTM stand-
ard intercept method (ASTM E112-12). Finally, phase 
confirmation was performed through transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM; FEI Tecnai G2 F20, FEI Company, USA) 
and high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL, 
Japan). The TEM specimens were prepared using a focused 
ion beam (FIB; NX2000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The accel-
eration voltage was 200 kV for TEM, the dark current was 
94 μA, and the emission current was 103 μA. Selected area 
electron diffraction (SAED) and HR-TEM analyses were per-
formed to confirm the structure of the produced Ti-MEAs.
Chemical composition and elemental mapping analyses 
of as-cast alloys were performed through scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM; 6330, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) with energy-
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and EPMA (JXA-
8530F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) with wavelength-dispersive 
spectroscopy (WDS). The acceleration voltage was 15 kV 
for EDS-SEM and WDS-EPMA, the scanning time for point 
analysis was 3 min, the scanning time for line analysis was 
5 min, and the scanning time for mapping analysis was 
30 min. At least three samples of each Ti-MEA were ana-
lyzed to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the test results.

4 Discussion
4.1 Phase Structure
The XRD patterns (Fig. 1), phase maps (Fig. 2b), and TEM 
diffraction patterns (Fig.  3) revealed that both Ti65-M1 
and Ti65-M2 exhibited a single BCC structure. Further -
more, streaking (marked by blue arrows) was observed in 
the SAED images (Fig.  3a, c). This streaking is a general 
characteristic of most Ti and Zr alloys with the metasta -
ble BCC structure [38] and is typically accompanied by the 
appearance of the ω phase [38– 40]. Various causes have 
been speculated for streaking. Ho et al. [40] suggested that 
in the Ti-Mo alloy system, when the concentration of solute 
atoms increases to a certain level, asymmetric vibrations 
occur because of the formation of the ω phase or the insta-
bility of the BCC structure. These factors cause diffraction 
spots to deviate from the ideal position as well as the appear-
ance of streaking. Therefore, in metastable Ti alloys, the 
double diffraction of the β phase and the diffusion of the 
ω phase contribute to streaking. In the present study, no ω 
phase was observed in the microstructure images (EPMA 
and HR-TEM) and diffraction patterns (XRD and SAED). 
Therefore, the streaking in the SAED pattern of Ti65-M2 
was attributed to the double diffraction of the unstable lat-
tice structure. Thus, the appearance of streaking verified 
that Ti65-M2 might have reached a metastable state in the 
present study.
4.2 Microstructure
The as-cast Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 exhibited a similar equi-
axed grain structure and orientation. The larger grain size of 
Ti65-M2 may be related to its lower δ value; a lower δ value 
is associated with less severe lattice distortion and, conse-
quently, better diffusion of alloy atoms [41]. Furthermore, a 
low segregation degree in Ti65-M2 can be beneficial to its 
grain growth. The composition analysis of Ti65-M1 in the 
DR and ID regions revealed that the Nb and Mo contents 
were greater in the DR regions than in the ID regions. How-
ever, Zr content was greater in the ID regions than in the DR 
regions mainly because Nb and Mo have a higher melting 
point and smaller atomic radius relative to Zr. Moreover, 
because the MEAs contained a considerably greater amount 
of Ti relative to other alloying elements, the degree of Ti 
segregation was considerably lower than that of other ele-
ments. Notably, Ti65-M2 exhibited a lower degree of seg-
regation because the δ of Ti65-M2 was lower. A decrease in 
the δ value can reduce the degree of crystal lattice distortion, 
thereby enabling easier movement and stacking of atoms 
during diffusion.

4.3 Elastic Modulus
In the present study, the melting points of Ti65-M1, Ti65-
M2, and Ti65 are similar, but their elastic moduli varied sig-
nificantly. Thus, the melting points of alloys do not appear to 
be the main factor that affects the elastic moduli. Addition-
ally, the ΔHmix values of Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 are close to 
zero; thus, they tend not to form intermetallic compounds 
and precipitates. The Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 contained no 
intermetallic compounds and precipitates, which would 
otherwise increase their elastic modulus considerably. Fur -
thermore, a larger difference in the atomic radius of MEAs 
can enhance lattice distortion and result in the generation 
of a stronger local stress field, which leads to an increase in 
the elastic modulus of the alloy. Therefore, the lower elastic 
modulus of Ti65-M2 is mainly related to its low δ value.
Metastable β-Ti alloys exhibit a low elastic moduli. In 
the present study, streaking was observed in TEM-SAED 
(Fig.  3a, c), which proved that the structure of Ti65-M2 
reached a metastable state. The low elastic moduli of Ti65-
M1 and Ti65-M2 are also associated with their metastable 
states. The interatomic bonding force of alloys with meta -
stable β phase is weaker than those of alloys with stable 
β phase, hence a metastable alloy exhibits a lower elastic 
modulus relative to a stable alloy [14]. Therefore, low-elas-
tic-modulus Ti-MEAs can be achieved using metastable 
alloy designs. The relationships between the elastic moduli 
and [Mo]eq values of metastable Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2, Ti-
MEAs [24], biomedical β-Ti alloys (wt%) [37, 42, 43], and 
metastable β-Ti alloys (wt%) [16–20] are presented in Fig. 6. 
These alloys exhibited low elastic moduli, as indicated by 
the [Mo]eq values of the metastable Ti65-M1, Ti65-M2, and 
β-Ti alloys being almost 10 wt%.
5 Conclusions
In the present study, the [Mo]eq theory was applied to adjust 
the elemental composition of Ti65-Zr20-Nb10-Mo5 (at%). 
Two metastable low-elastic-modulus Ti-Zr-Nb-Mo medium-
entropy alloys, Ti65-Zr20-Nb14-Mo1 (at%; Ti65-M1) and 
 Ti65-Zr18-Nb16-Mo1 (at%; Ti65-M2), were developed. The 
XRD, EBSD, and TEM results revealed that both metastable 
Ti-MEAs exhibited a single BCC structure, equiaxed crys-
tals, and dendritic structures. Unique streaking was observed 
in the TEM-SAED images of Ti65-M2, which indicated that 
Ti65-M2 reached a metastable state. A composition analy -
sis revealed that a decrease in the δ value of an alloy can 
prevent severe segregation. In the three-point bending tests, 
Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 exhibited high yield strength (1188 
and 1118 MPa, respectively) because of the lattice distortion 
effect; they also exhibited low elastic moduli (67.4 and 61 
GPa, respectively). Furthermore, the low elastic modulus of 
Ti65-M2 is mainly related to its low δ value. This finding 
indicates that the [Mo]eq theory can be applied to alloying 
design in Ti-rich MEA systems and that low-elastic-modulus 
MEAs can be obtained by incorporating metastable states in 
an alloy. Therefore, the metastable Ti65-M1 and Ti65-M2 
developed in this study are promising materials for biomedi-
cal applications because of their high σ y/E ratios and low 
elastic moduli.
