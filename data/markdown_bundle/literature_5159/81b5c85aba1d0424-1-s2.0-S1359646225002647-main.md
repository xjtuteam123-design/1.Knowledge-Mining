ABSTRACT
Body centered cubic (bcc)-superalloys – exploiting a two-phase microstructure composed of bcc matrix 
strengthened by coherent ordered precipitates – are emerging as a promising class of materials for high- 
temperature applications. Such alloys offer significant advantages especially in iron- and refractory-based sys -
tems, due to high melting points and superior mechanical strength. However, they often face critical challenges 
including limited ductility and toughness, phase instability or environmental vulnerability. Developing these 
alloys requires an integrated approach, from design to property assessment, and an in-depth understanding of 
deformation and strengthening mechanisms operating in the main phases across different scales.
This viewpoint article addresses the reported deformation mechanisms in bcc-superalloys with a particular 
focus on precipitation strengthening and dislocation interactions, and it proposes future research directions. Key 
aspects such as the transition between mechanisms, the role of the antiphase boundary energy, and the effect of 
chemical complexity especially in advanced refractory alloys remain open questions.
Introduction
Body-centered cubic (bcc) superalloys refer to materials designed 
using a strategy akin to that of nickel-based superalloys and focusing on 
optimizing high-temperature performance through tailored composi -
tion, microstructure, and strengthening mechanisms. Whereas Ni-based 
superalloys feature a matrix with face-centered cubic (fcc) ( γ phase) and

Fundamental characteristics of the bcc lattice deformation and 
its derivative ordered phases
In bcc-superalloys, both strength and ductility are strongly influ -
enced by the inherent nature of the bcc lattice and its ordered phases. 
Dislocation motion in the bcc lattice is a thermally activated process 
controlled by lattice friction at least up to the athermal temperature of 
the mechanism. Screw a/2 < 111 > dislocations control the plastic 
deformation across a broad range of temperatures due to their non- 
planar core structure [ 26 ]. Consequently, bcc metals and alloys 
exhibit a strong temperature and orientation dependence of the critical 
resolved shear stress (CRSS) along with a relatively elevated DBTT 
[ 26 – 31 ]. Mechanical behavior is also extremely sensitive to interstitial 
impurities.
In bcc substitutional solid solutions, alloying induces hardening 
through effects on kink mobility and the formation of athermal barriers 
[ 32 – 34 ]. These mechanisms can lead to significantly higher CRSS 
especially in concentrated bcc alloys [ 35 ], without radical change of the 
deformation processes. Chemical complexity may significantly impact 
the energetics of the mechanisms, the temperature dependence of the 
strength, the edge-to-screw dislocation mobility, as well as ductility 
[ 36 – 41 ]. Notably, solid solution strengthening looks efficient at inter -
mediate temperatures up to 0.6T

generally achieved with ordered phases deriving from the bcc lattice.

Precipitation effects on dislocation mechanisms in bcc- 
superalloys. experimental details of the operating micro- 
mechanisms
The strengthening mechanisms originate from various dislocation- 
particle interactions and induce an increase of the mechanical resis -
tance by hindering dislocation motion. Differences in elastic moduli 
and/or atomic volume between matrix and particles cause long-range 
elastic strain field leading to modulus and coherency strengthening, 
respectively. On the contrary, hardening due to short-range effects oc -
curs when the dislocation core contacts the particle. In that case, an 
additional force is needed to push the dislocation through the ordered 
precipitate (i.e. shearing mechanism) thereby creating an APB and 
leading to order strengthening. These strengthening mechanisms are 
reviewed and discussed in sections 3.1 and 3.2 in relation to these alloys. 
At the atomic scale, the interaction between moving dislocations and 
particles depends not only on the particle strength but also on the vol -
ume fraction, the size, the lattice mismatch and the dispersion of the 
secondary phase. It is therefore convenient to distinguish between cut -
ting, looping or climbing (if temperature is sufficiently high) mecha -
nisms. In the case of coherent particles, shearing is possible in the 
underaged condition, but in restricted cases: small size and/or low APB 
energy. Precipitation hardening usually reaches a peak at a certain 
particle size for a constant phase fraction, as the strengthening mecha -
nism shifts from shearing to bypassing. In the case of bcc-superalloys both 
phenomena – cutting and dislocation bowing of the hardening secondary 
phase – have been evidenced experimentally.
Iron-based alloys reinforced with B2 and/or L2
1 
precipitates
The influence of the B2 phase on dislocation mechanisms remains the 
most extensively investigated in these alloys. Deformation should occur 
by the motion of single or super-dislocations (i.e. dislocation pairs), 
cutting the ordered particles when their size is sufficiently small. Particle 
shearing by super-dislocations is favored as the second dislocation 
eliminates the APB produced by the first, and the attraction between the 
two enhances the influence of the applied shear stress on the leading, 
enabling it to move at lower stresses. The effect has been described for 
ferritic superalloys with B2-NiAl precipitates using dislocation dynamics 
(DD) [ 62 ]: from calculations, single dislocations are unlikely to exist in 
such alloys, as super-dislocations – regardless of the nature of their 
coupling (i.e. weak or strong) – are predicted to provide a lower 
strengthening contribution. Dislocation pairs are then the marker that a 
shearing mechanism is prevailing, and such dislocations have been 
observed in several precipitation strengthened bcc alloys strained at 
room temperature (RT). Taillard and Pineau have observed 〈 111 〉
super-dislocations in confined {110} and/or {112} slip bands up to peak 
strength in Fe-19Cr( + Ni,Al) (wt. %) [ 63 ]. At the peak strength, the 
presence of dislocation pairs and loops suggests a transition to an Oro -
wan mechanism ( Fig. 1 a & b). Shearing has also been reported in 
Fe-19Cr-4Ni-2Al (wt. %) under low-amplitude cyclic deformation [ 64 ,
65 ] and after RT compression of Fe-23Ni-6Al (wt. %) containing small 
coherent B2-NiAl precipitates [ 66 , 67 ]. 〈 111 〉 super-dislocations appear

Precipitation strengthening in refractory alloys
The quest for refractory alloy systems exhibiting a two phase 
microstructure analogous to the duplex γ - γ’ found in Ni-based super -
alloys has been intense over the last 20 years especially since the 
emergence of the high-entropy concept [ 35 , 78 ]. Efforts have then been 
made to incorporate the B2 (NiAl, CoHf, CoZr, FeTi) or L2
1 
(Fe
2
AlTi, 
Ni
2
AlTi) phases in a bcc matrix composed of transition metals such as Cr, 
Nb, Mo, Ti or W [ 10 – 13,25,51,79,80 ]. In most cases, the mechanical 
properties exhibit attractive gains due to the presence of the hardening 
phase [ 72 ], but the deformation mechanisms of the duplex microstruc -
tures are not well understood. Strengthening mechanisms are widely 
discussed considering different theoretical contributions (see section 4), 
but observations remain scarce. Efforts should be focused in that direction 
in the near future. A more careful analysis of the deformed microstructures 
and the associated operative processes is required to understand and improve 
mechanical properties (strength and ductility – the latter being a major 
challenge) of such bcc-superalloys .
In concentrated refractory alloys containing a mix of Group IV (Ti, 
Zr, Hf) and Group V/VI (V, Nb, Mo, Ta, W) together with addition of Al, 
generally trigger the formation of spinodally decomposed microstruc -
tures with the joint presence of a bcc phase and chemically complex B2 
compound [ 16,22,24,81 – 84 ]. Recent reports show that the B2 phase can 
form in such Al-containing alloys through nucleation and growth [ 85 ,
86 ], while Ru-containing systems have also been shown to develop 
B2 phases via a similar mechanism [ 47 – 49 ], paving the way for the 
design of stable microstructures for high temperature applications. 
Recent efforts have been made to identify the operating strengthening 
mechanisms based on TEM observations. The bcc matrix usually 
deforms first with b = a /2 < 111 > dislocations in confined slip planes.

Challenges and perspectives
The previous sections have focused on reviewing the reported 
operative deformation processes and strengthening mechanisms in bcc- 
superalloys. Strengthening phenomena in ferritic alloys are qualitatively 
identified – especially under creep conditions – and are supported by 
strong experimental evidence. In refractory bcc-superalloys, much work 
remains to be done to fully understand the active deformation processes. 
While experimental evidence confirms shearing, the transition between 
cutting and by-passing remains poorly understood. Future work should 
aim to systematically assess the role of coherency loss, misfit strain, and 
interfacial segregation in such a transition, for example through tailored 
heat treatments and advanced characterization techniques (atom probe 
tomography, TEM).
The field is relatively new [ 11 , 13 , 17 , 79 , 80 ], and significant progress 
has been made recently in the exploration, design, and optimization of 
microstructures of promising compositions, but often at the expense of a 
thorough investigation of the underlying deformation mechanisms. 
There is a need to address simple fundamental questions , and we have 
identified few challenges listed in the following points. 
1. The understanding of strengthening mechanisms in refractory bcc- 
superalloys (Ti-, Cr-, W- and high-entropy-based) require capturing the 
behavior of both matrix (bcc structure) and ordered phases over a broad 
range of temperatures . This work has been done decades ago during 
the development of Ni-based superalloys [ 119 ]. While our qualita -
tive understanding of the operative deformation processes in dilute 
and concentrated bcc solid solutions has improved [ 33 , 38 , 41 , 120 ], 
the question for concentrated intermetallics is still open. First reports 
on the theme point in the right direction [ 19,61,85,121 ]. Preliminary 
investigations highlight the central role of chemical complexity in 
influencing the ordering energy. The increase in the number of 
constituent elements in the B2 phase strongly impacts the APB en -
ergy and 〈 111 〉 super-partials are observed in several cases [ 19 , 23 ,
61 , 89 ]. Then, a systematic study of the evolution of APB energy with 
composition and temperature in concentrated B2 compounds with 
complex site occupancies is essential. Disentangling the contribu -
tions of specific elemental interactions to APB energy remains chal -
lenging, but recent computational methods offer promising avenues 
[ 122 ]. Beyond that, the determination of active slip systems and 
CRSS should provide simple but crucial information on the defor -
mation behavior of the different phases. Moreover, it is often 
incorrectly assumed that the brittleness of refractory bcc-superalloys 
is solely due to the presence of B2 intermetallic compounds. The lack 
of ductility in these alloys is not necessarily intrinsic to the ordered 
B2 phase itself but may in some cases be more critically linked to 
plastic incompatibility between the matrix and possible hard inter -
metallic phases at grains boundaries [ 20 , 23 ].
2. Experimental data on elastic properties of the bcc-superalloys constituent 
phases are missing . The determination of such properties (Young ’ s 
modulus, shear modulus, elastic stiffness coefficients) is necessary to 
describe phase response to applied stress, but also to capture 
geometrical configurations of dislocations during deformation at 
high temperatures. It will certainly require the preparation of single 
crystals which is extremely challenging for refractory compositions.
3. Finally, we suggest reevaluating theories of particle hardening considering 
results on these bcc-superalloys . Except for bypassing processes (in the 
case of non-deformable particles), where the theory is well estab -
lished, our analysis of various reports suggests that equations used to 
evaluate strengthening contributions serve primarily as a semi- 
qualitative framework for interpreting experimental data. Existing 
strengthening models are often applied to bcc-superalloys with 
limited validation. The models are not completely rigorous (see our 
comments on modulus strengthening) and have been mostly estab -
lished for spherical particles. Such reevaluation of theories needs 
model alloys (with low content of interstitial impurities) to be pre -
pared, and microstructures carefully controlled (accurate composi -
tion of the phases, size, shape, volume fraction and precipitate 
distribution). The superposition of the different contributions 
(including solid solution strengthening, not discussed in this paper) 
remains an open question that will probably require coupling of 
phase-field simulations with dislocation dynamics.
