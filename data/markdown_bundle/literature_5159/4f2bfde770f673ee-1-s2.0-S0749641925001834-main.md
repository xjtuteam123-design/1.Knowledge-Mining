2004 ; Yeh et al., 2004 ). Their unique design flexibility has enabled the development of alloys with unprecedented property combi -
nations ( Senkov et al., 2018 ). Recent research has increasingly focused on body-centered cubic (BCC) refractory high-entropy alloys

To overcome the strength-ductility trade-off in RHEAs, researchers have proposed diverse design strategies. Sheikh et al. (2016)

uniform elongation in RHEAs.
Recent studies highlight the crucial role of deformation mechanisms — such as planar-slip, cross-slip, and kink band formation — in 
influencing the mechanical behavior of RHEAs ( Wang et al., 2023a ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2024a ). The activation of specific 
slip systems and their associated dislocation dynamics critically regulate stress distribution and strain accommodation during 
deformation ( Jian and Ren, 2024 ; Le ´on-C ´azares et al., 2024 ). A prototypical example is TiZrHfNbTa and VNbMoTaW RHEAs ( Tsuru

elements promotes the activation of {112} < 111 > dislocations, enhancing ductility. However, the exact contribution of slip planes to 
deformation remains unclear. The tensile deformation of TiZrHfNbTa alloys at room temperature is primarily governed by the sluggish 
motion of screw dislocations, which facilitate bulk plastic flow ( Couzini ´e et al., 2015 ). In high-temperature nanoindentation studies,

relative mobility of screw and edge dislocations in this alloy increases with temperature, although this temperature dependence is less 
pronounced compared to that in simple BCC metals. Additionally, localized ductility arises from collective arrays of edge dislocations 
forming tilt boundary pairs, commonly known as kink bands ( Chen et al., 2021a ; Wang et al., 2020 ). For example, in Nb

3.4. Statistical analysis of slip activity
To investigate the activation of slip systems during deformation, slip trace analysis was performed on the observed slip lines at 
various stages for Hf0, Hf5, and Hf10 alloys. The identification of the active slip systems within each grain during the plastic defor -
mation of polycrystalline metallic alloys was achieved using the Slip Trace - Modified Lattice Rotation Analysis (ST-MLRA) technique 
( Yang et al., 2022 ). First, grain orientations were determined via EBSD, and PFs for all potential slip planes were constructed. These PFs 
were then compared with the normals of the observed slip traces. The intersection points between the slip trace normals and the PFs 
provided insight into the most likely active slip systems. However, several challenges arose during the analysis: (i) ambiguity in slip 
plane identification — a single slip trace could correspond to multiple slip plane poles; and (ii) multiple possible slip directions — a 
single slip plane could accommodate several potential slip directions, complicating the determination of the exact active slip system.
Traditionally, previous studies have addressed these ambiguities by assuming that the slip system with the highest Schmid factor 
(SF) is the most likely to be activated ( Wan et al., 2022 ). While this assumption provides a useful approximation, it does not offer 
definitive proof of the actual slip system in operation. To overcome this limitation, grain rotation analysis was incorporated into this 
study for further refinement. Specifically, when a slip system is activated, the associated grain rotates along the rotation axis of that slip 
system. This grain rotation can be mathematically described using a rotation matrix and expressed as:

3.5. Deformation mechanism
To further elucidate the deformation mechanisms at finer scales, TEM analyses were conducted on the Hf0 and Hf10 alloys at 
various strain levels, as shown in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10 . Fig. 9 shows the dislocation behavior in the Hf0 alloy, captured using bright-field 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (BF-STEM) at strains of 2 %, 5 %, 9 %, and 14 % (fracture). In the early stages of plastic 
deformation (2 %, Fig. 9(a) and ( b )), dislocations primarily glide along planar-slip bands associated with favorably oriented slip 
systems (highlighted by red lines). Dislocations accumulate within these bands, leading to jamming and tangling, which ultimately 
evolve into high-density dislocation walls (HDDWs). This planar-slip-dominated deformation behavior is characteristic of RHEAs 
( Wang et al., 2023 b). Secondary slip system activation occurs when stress concentrations within the bands, induced by dislocation 
jamming, exceed a critical threshold. Escaping dislocations, as shown in the blue box in Fig. 9(a) , propagate along secondary slip 
bands, maintaining a planar-slip morphology. With increasing strain, the secondary planar-slip bands transform into HDDWs, which 
progressively widen, as depicted in Fig. 9(c) -( h ). Intersections between HDDWs on different slip planes form a grid-like Taylor lattice, 
marked by green quadrangles in Fig. 9(c) and ( d ). The shaded regions enclosed within these grids exhibit minimal dislocation activity, 
indicating locally undeformed areas. Additionally, as dislocation density increases with strain, the Taylor lattice size decreases, 
reflecting intensified dislocation interactions.
It is noteworthy that regularly arranged dislocation walls (indicated by red arrows) and dislocation networks (indicated by blue 
arrows) are observed in Fig. 9(a) and ( b ). These structures represent ordered configurations formed during recrystallization annealing 
through mechanisms such as cross-slip and climb. Some dislocation walls interconnect to form sub-crystalline boundaries, serving as 
intrinsic microstructural features. These pre-existing dislocation structures strongly interact with planar-slip dislocations generated 
during deformation, impeding their motion and leading to severe dislocation entanglement, as highlighted by yellow arrows in Fig. 9 
(b) . However, such dislocation walls and networks were observed only in isolated regions of a few samples, indicating that their 
contribution to the overall mechanical response of the alloys is limited and not dominant.

4.2. Slip plane preference
In conventional BCC alloys, the macroscopic slip plane is typically influenced by the loading axis, with < 111 > dislocations 
frequently cross-slipping between {110} and {112} planes. In Section 3.4 , we investigated the activation of macroscopic slip planes in 
the Hf0, Hf5, and Hf10 samples at 0.5 % and 2 % strain using in-situ EBSD experiments combined with the ST-MLRA method. Two 
unexpected findings emerged from the analysis: (i) activation of the {123} slip plane was detected, with the highest activation pro -
portion among all planes; and (ii) as Hf content increased, the activation proportion of the {112} slip plane rose, while that of the 
{110} slip plane declined.
Previous studies have proposed that slip traces on the {123} plane may arise from combined dislocation activity on the {110} and 
{112} planes, as the {123} plane represents an intermediate crystallographic orientation between them. Under certain stress condi -
tions, slip trace analysis might incorrectly attribute superimposed or varying dislocation slip paths to the {123} plane. Nevertheless, 
recent research on RHEAs ( Wang et al., 2020 ) has demonstrated that the critical resolved shear stresses (CRSS) required for dislocation 
motion on high-order planes such as {123}, {134}, or {145} can be comparable to, or even lower than, that on the {110} plane. This 
behavior facilitates extensive and uniform plastic deformation. Assuming comparable resistance to dislocation slip on the {110}, 
{112}, and {123} planes, the {123} slip systems are more likely to be activated due to their favorable geometric alignment ( Naveen 
Kumar et al., 2013 ). Specifically, the {123} < 111 > system offers 24 slip systems, in contrast to the 12 slip systems provided by either 
the {110} < 111 > or {112} < 111 > systems, making it particularly advantageous for accommodating plastic deformation. Due to the 
limitations inherent to TEM experiments, extensive statistical analysis was not feasible in this study. Thus, confirming whether the 
{123} slip system consistently exhibits the highest activation proportion would require comprehensive multi-grain analysis, which is 
beyond the scope of the current work.
This study focuses on how compositional variations influence the activation ratio of the {110} and {112} slip planes. The effect of 
alloy composition on slip plane preference has been well-established in Ti-based and Mg-based alloys ( Nieto-Valeiras et al., 2024 ). For 
example, Zhu et al. (2019) demonstrated that the addition of 0.47 wt.% Ca to Mg alloys promotes non-basal < a > slip, thereby 
enhancing ductility. Similarly, recent research on RHEAs has underscored the critical impact of slip plane preference on ductility. 
Tsuru et al. ( Tsuru et al., 2024 ) examined the macroscopic slip behavior of the ductile TiZrHfNbTa and the brittle VNbMoTaW alloys 
through microcolumn compression testing. Their results showed that {101} slip dominates in VNbMoTaW, while {112} slip is 
preferred in TiZrNbHfTa. Using first-principles calculations within the virtual crystal approximation scheme, they evaluated the 
Peierls energy surfaces of pure Nb, Nb67Zr33, and Nb50Zr50 alloys. These calculations revealed that increasing Zr content shifts the 
minimum energy path for screw dislocation motion toward the {112} plane, with full alignment at a Zr concentration of 50 at.%. This 
shift was attributed to the increased concentration of HCP elements, which promote activation of the {112} slip system.
Our study reveals that, even among HCP elements, Zr and Hf have distinct effects on {112} slip plane activation. Specifically, we 
find that increasing Hf content further enhances activation of the {112} slip system. This discrepancy may stem from the differences in 
HCP phase stability and tendencies between Zr and Hf. While our current study cannot fully elucidate the mechanism underlying the 
observed change in slip plane preference, we propose that future investigations should focus on analyzing the generalized stacking 
fault energy ( γ ) surface and dislocation core structures associated with Zr and Hf. Previous studies have shown that these factors can 
significantly influence slip plane preference. For example, Duesbery and Vitek (1997) explained the plastic anisotropy resulting from

4.3. Dislocation behavior
In Figs. 11 and 12 , we analyzed the dislocation behavior in the H0 and H10 samples at 2 % strain. The results indicate that dis -
locations are primarily distributed along the {110}, {112}, and {123} slip planes, with the dislocation character varied depending on 
the activated plane. On the {110} plane, dislocation lines were aligned parallel to each other and exhibited a pure screw dislocation 
character. On the {112} plane, in addition to pure screw dislocations, mixed-character dislocations were observed. These mixed 
dislocations deviated from the straight orientation characteristic of pure screw dislocations, displaying substantial tortuosity and non- 
screw segments along the dislocation line. In contrast, on the {123} plane, we observed edge dislocations oriented perpendicular to the 
Burgers vector, as well as mixed dislocations exhibiting wavy or curved slip traces, as shown in Fig. 11 (g3) and (h3). These non-linear 
dislocation paths are interpreted as a consequence of pronounced local lattice distortions and atomic-scale chemical fluctuations 
inherent to RHEAs ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ).
Our findings reveal a correlation between slip plane and dislocation character. Screw dislocations dominate on {110} and {112} 
planes, while edge and mixed dislocations are more prevalent on high-order planes such as {123}. This observation aligns with 
previous reports in BCC RHEAs ( Wang et al., 2020 ; Xu et al., 2021 ), where high-order planes were energetically favorable for edge 
dislocation glide due to reduced local slip resistances. Moreover, we observed that screw dislocations tend to maintain straight-line 
configurations on the {110} slip plane, whereas on the {112} plane, they exhibit more wavy characteristics. This morphological 
difference is associated with the asymmetry of the γ-surface energy and the diffuseness of the dislocation core structure. Due to the 
twinning-antitwinning asymmetry, dislocation cores on {112} planes are more prone to non-planar spreading ( Frederiksen and 
Jacobsen, 2003 ; Ventelon and Willaime, 2007 ), which facilitates cross-slip and kink pair formation, thereby promoting dislocation 
curvature ( Romaner et al., 2010 ; Tsuru et al., 2024 ). These results provide valuable insights into how dislocation types and slip planes 
interact to influence the deformation mechanisms in RHEAs.

The remarkable elongation and stable SHR observed during the stage of non-uniform deformation are associated with planar-slip 
behavior and the activation of multiple slip systems, as shown in Figs. 9 and 10 . During the uniform deformation stage, primary slip 
systems are activated, growing through planar-slip and gradually forming dislocation channels. As these primary channels become 
prevalent, dislocations accumulate and entangle within them, leading to the formation of HDDWs that localize strain and initiate the 
transition to non-uniform deformation. Stress concentrations within these HDDWs promote slip system transitions, enabling dislo -
cations to overflow from the primary channels through cross-slip and generate a series of secondary channels. The widespread dis -
tribution of secondary channels utilizes undeformed regions, thereby delocalizing strain and enhancing deformation uniformity. 
Moreover, interactions between primary and secondary channels contribute to sustaining the SHR ( Gao et al., 2023 ). With increasing 
strain, however, secondary channels progressively develop their own strain-localized HDDWs. The competition between homogenized 
deformation driven by slip system transitions and localized strain concentration within HDDWs governs the non-uniform deformation 
process, effectively mitigating instability and enabling exceptional ductility during non-uniform deformation.
Further discussion focuses on elucidating the influence of Hf addition on the ductility of the alloys, particularly in relation to its 
effects on dislocation behavior, slip activity, and microstructural evolution during deformation. As described in Section 3.3 , in-situ 
EBSD-KAM analysis was employed to calculate the GND density at various strain levels. The results indicate that the Hf10 alloy, 
which exhibited the highest fracture elongation, consistently showed the lowest GND density within the 2 %~10 % strain range. This 
finding is intriguing, as higher dislocation densities are generally associated with enhanced work-hardening capacity, which typically 
benefits ductility. However, it is important to recognize that KAM maps reflect local orientation gradients within grains ( Huang et al., 
2024 b). In some alloys with superior ductility, dislocation glide tends to be more uniform, and stress-relief mechanisms are more 
effective during deformation, reducing local orientation differences and thereby lowering KAM values. A comparative analysis of the 
deformation behavior between Hf0 and Hf10 alloys ( Figs. 9 and 10 ) reveals a notable difference in dislocation dynamics. In the Hf0 
alloy, dislocations accumulate within slip bands, forming HDDWs. With increasing strain, these HDDWs expand, intensifying local 
stress concentrations and increasing strain gradients within grains, ultimately leading to higher GND densities. In contrast, in the Hf10 
alloy, dislocations within primary channels are more prone to overflow through cross-slip, generating secondary slip bands. This 
overflow promotes more homogeneous deformation and reduces local strain gradients, thereby lowering the GND density.
Fig. 5 highlights the formation of kink bands in the Hf5 and Hf10 alloys, a feature not observed in Hf0. In polycrystalline materials, 
kink bands typically nucleate in regions of high dislocation density and propagate across entire grains, thereby alleviating local stress 
concentrations ( Li et al., 2021 ). Essentially, the formation of kink bands represents a non-conventional strain accommodation 
mechanism, wherein localized lattice rotation zones temporarily absorb the applied load. This behavior manifests as stress plateaus or

triggers several beneficial mechanisms to compensate for these effects: (i) Kink bands obstruct the primary slip systems and facilitate 
the activation of secondary slip systems, thereby broadening the plastic deformation region and SHR stage ( Chen et al., 2021a ). (ii) 
Their formation delays the onset of strain localization and prolongs the necking process, leading to a substantial increase in fracture 
strain, as clearly reflected in the tensile curves ( Fig. 3(a) ). Similar phenomena have been reported not only in RHEAs but also in 
high-strength BCC Ti alloys ( Sadeghpour et al., 2019 ). (iii) Kink bands help suppress crack initiation by increasing the characteristic 
spacing between voids ahead of crack tips, thereby enhancing damage tolerance ( Cook et al., 2024 ).
Based on the above analyses, the enhanced elongation observed during the non-uniform deformation stage can be attributed to two 
primary mechanisms: dislocation cross-slip and kink band formation. Both processes are closely linked to slip plane preference and 
dislocation behavior. Specifically, the addition of Hf promotes the activation of the {112} slip plane. Compared to the {110} slip plane, 
the screw dislocation core on the {112} plane is more diffuse, facilitating cross-slip. This allows dislocations to escape from the primary 
slip channels, thereby mitigating stress concentrations within HDDWs and improving the utilization of previously undeformed regions. 
Furthermore, we observed more mixed and edge dislocations on higher-order slip planes such as {112} and {123}, which provide 
favorable conditions for kink band formation. The kink bands observed in Hf-containing alloys facilitate the activation of multiple slip 
systems, delay the onset of necking, and suppress crack initiation, thereby significantly enhancing the fracture strain.

activity in RHEAs—highlighting the role of edge dislocations, deformation on high-order slip planes, and correlations between slip 
plane preference and dislocation character via simulations—a comprehensive understanding of slip plane preference, dislocation 
behavior, and the contribution of kink bands to plasticity remains limited. In this study, we employed the Ti

high-performance RHEAs.
5. Conclusion
In summary, our work demonstrates that rational compositional design can significantly enhance the non-uniform strain capacity 
and fracture resistance of RHEAs, while maintaining high strengths exceeding 1 GPa. These improvements are primarily attributed to 
the addition of Hf, which promotes activation of the {112} slip plane. Furthermore, our observations reveal distinct dislocation 
character associated with different slip planes: screw dislocations preferentially glide on {110} planes, whereas mixed and edge 
dislocations tend to activate on higher-order planes. These features facilitate cross-slip in the Hf10 alloy, mitigates dislocation pile-up 
within HDDWs, and redistribute plastic flow into undeformed regions. Additionally, the formation of kink bands was observed in Hf- 
containing alloys, which further enhances fracture resistance by redistributing local strain and delaying crack initiation. These findings 
suggest that tailoring slip plane preference and dislocation behavior through compositional tuning offers an effective strategy for 
optimizing the mechanical performance of RHEAs.
Based on the above findings, future work could focus on elucidating the activation mechanisms of the {112} and {123} slip planes 
in RHEAs. Advanced techniques such as micropillar compression/tension combined with in-situ TEM could be employed to investigate 
dislocation nucleation, multiplication, and motion on different slip planes. Furthermore, integrating density functional theory (DFT) 
and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to explore γ-surfaces, dislocation core structures, and CRSS of various slip systems in RHEAs 
would provide theoretical guidance for composition design.
