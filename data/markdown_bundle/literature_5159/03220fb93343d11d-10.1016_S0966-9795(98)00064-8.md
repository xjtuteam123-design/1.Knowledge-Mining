Abstract
The e/C128ect of Al alloying on the microstructure and properties of MoSi2 was investigated. MoSi 2–2.8 and 5.5 at% Al (1.5 and 3 wt
%, respectively) alloys showed a two phase microstructure comprising of -Al2O3 and MoSi 2–Al alloy, while the MoSi 2–9 at%
(5 wt %) Al alloy possessed a 3 phase microstructure: MoSi 2–Al alloy, Mo(Si,Al) 2 and-Al2O3. Al additions to MoSi 2 reduced the
SiO2 phase, which is detrimental for high temperature strength of MoSi 2, forming Al 2O3. The surplus Al entered into solid solution
with the MoSi 2 and altered the lattice parameters. The Al in solid solution was 0.5 at% in MoSi 2–2.8 at% Al alloy, 2.5 at% in
MoSi2–5.5 at% Al alloy and 3.1 at% in the MoSi 2–9 at% Al alloy. The fracture toughness underwent only a moderate improvement
in MoSi2–2.8 at% and 5.5 at% Al alloys, but exhibited a signiﬁcant 49% rise in the MoSi 2–9 at% Al alloy. Replacement of SiO 2 by
Al2O3 in MoSi 2–Al alloys led to a signiﬁcant improvement in the high temperature yield strength between 1100 C and 1250C.

1. Introduction
Molybdenum di-silicide (MoSi 2) has drawn world-
wide attention for its strong potential for elevated tem-
perature structural applications in the range of 1000–
1500
C, and is the material of intense research
throughout the last decade [1,2]. The attractive features
of this material are its high melting point of 2030
C and
outstanding oxidation resistance up to a temperature of
1700
C [3]. In addition, it undergoes plastic deforma-
tion above the ductile to brittle transition temperature
(DBTT) of 1100–1300
C [4,5], and can be machined
using electro-discharge machining. These are the two
important characteristic features, where MoSi 2 scores
advantage points over conventional ceramic structural
materials like SiC or Si
3N4.M o S i2 (-MoSi2 in the Mo–
Si binary phase diagram [6]) is a line compound stable
below 1900
C. It possesses a body centred tetragonal
unit cell (C11 b structure, I4/mmm space group) with
lattice parameters, a 0:3202 nm and c 0:7845 nm,
giving a c/a ratio of 2.45 as shown in Fig. 1. The C11 b
structured MoSi 2 is a long range ordered crystal made
of 3 b.c.c. lattices stacked and compressed along the c-
axis, where Mo and Si atoms are placed at the body
centres alternately. The Mo atom positions are 0, 0, 0
and 1/2, 1/2, 1/2 and Si atom positions are 0, 0, 1/3; 0, 0,
2/3; 1/2, 1/2, 1/6 and 1/2, 1/2, 5/6. -MoSi
2 undergoes
phase transformation at 1900C, forming -MoSi2 (C40
structure, P6 222 space group). Boettinger et al. [7] have
however concluded from an analysis of phase transfor-
mations that this phase is metastable.
The oxidation resistance of MoSi
2, like SiC, is due to
the protection from an impervious and continuously
adhering layer of SiO
2 between the temperatures of 700
and 1700C. Below 700 C, the SiO 2 layer is not con-
tinuous and this leads to penetration of oxygen and pest
disintegration in porous MoSi 2 product in the tempera-
ture range of 400–600 C [8,10]. Thus one of the chal-
lenges in MoSi 2 is to control the pest oxidation by
having density closer to the theoretical value [9] or by
adding proper alloying elements [11,12]. Stergiou et al.
[11] and Yanagihara et al. [13] have reported how Al
additions help in improving pest and oxidation resis-
tance of MoSi
2. Kisley and Kodash [14] have reported
that the resistance to oxidative scaling of Mo(SiAl) 2 is
superior to that of MoSi 2 because of formation of a
mullite coating.

MoSi2 has some other outstanding problems before it
can be widely used in structural applications. It is brittle
at room temperature with a low fracture toughness of
about 3–4 MPapm [15,17] and no ductility. Its high
temperature strength and creep resistance drops sharply
beyond 1200
C due to enhanced plasticity from activa-
tion of more than ﬁve independent slip systems [17,18].
However, the material can be softened at a lower tem-
perature due to presence of SiO
2 particles and ﬁlms at
the grain boundaries [5]. Even the fracture toughness
has been found to decrease with increase in temperature
above 1000
C due to the softening of SiO 2 phase and
weakening of grain boundaries [19,20]. Higher strength
at high temperature can thus be obtained by reducing or
eliminating the SiO
2 content, as in the reaction hot
pressed MoSi2 [21,22]. The other options are reinforcing
with ceramic phases like SiC [21–24] or by alloying.
C additions up to 2 wt%, which reduced the SiO 2
during hot pressing, and formed SiC particles in situ led
to considerable improvement in high temperature hard-
ness, fracture toughness and creep resistance [19,20,25].
Shah et al. [26] recommended Al additions to MoSi
2 to
increase its metallic character, as Al atoms would sub-
stitute Si atoms, and change the nature of bonding. The
Mo–Si bond is a mixture of covalent and ionic bonding,
with a partial metallic character [27]. Nesphor and
Samsanov [27] have evaluated the covalency of the Mo–
Si bond as a measure of the localization of valence
(d+s) electrons. The value of covalency was found to be
0.2, which is close to that of metallic phases. Hence, it is
obvious that an Al atom substituting the Si atom would
be quite e/C128ective in increasing the metallic character.
Costa e Silva and Kaufman [28] experimented with Al
additions of 2.5, 4.1 and 8.1 wt% ( &4.6, 7.4 and
14.2 at%, respectively) to MoSi
2. Al reacted with SiO 2
forming Al 2O3 and some of it went into solid solution.
In this way, in-situ deoxidation and alloying of MoSi 2
was achieved with or without any structural modiﬁca-
tion. Al 2O3 also possesses a coecient of thermal
expansion value similar to that of MoSi 2. Alloying with
8.1 wt% Al (14.2 at%) led to the formation of
Mo(Si,Al)
2, a hexagonal C40 structured phase [28], as
the predominant phase. There was a decrease in the
hardness, and a moderate improvement in room tem-
perature fracture toughness, with increase in concentra-
tion of Al.
The success achieved in improving the mechanical
properties and the oxidation resistance of MoSi
2 on
alloying with Al motivates further studies on under-
standing the e/C128ect of Al alloying on microstructure and
properties. In this investigation, Al additions of 2.8, 5.5
and 9 at% (which corresponds to 1.5, 3 and 5 wt%,
respectively) have been attempted on a commercial
grade MoSi
2. A comprehensive study of the resulting
microstructure and composition and its e/C128ect on the
room temperature and high temperature mechanical
properties is being reported in this paper. A preliminary
and brief report of this work was published as a part of
the paper in ref. [21], where some of the mechanical
properties were compared with the MoSi
2–20 vol% SiC
composite processed by conventional vacuum hot
pressing, reaction hot pressed MoSi 2, and the reaction
hot pressed MoSi 2–20 vol% SiC composite. This work
can be considered as an extension of what has been
reported earlier by the authors and other investigators
around the world in the literature on e/C128ect of Al alloy-
ing on the microstructure and properties of MoSi
2
[11,13,14,28].

3. Experimental
Hot pressed MoSi 2 and MoSi 2–2.8, 5.5 and 9 at% Al
alloy discs were sand blasted and polished on both sides
to remove the graphite layer. These were then sectioned
using electrodischarge machining or diamond cutting.
Density measurements were performed using Archi-
medes’ principle. The microstructure of the metallo-
graphically polished specimens was studied using
optical microscopy, scanning (SEM) and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) and electron probe micro-
analysis (EPMA). Polarized light optical microscopy
was used to study the grain size distribution and the
linear intercept method [31] was applied to estimate the
grain size. Quantitative analysis of chemical composi-
tion of di/C128erent phases and line scanning was performed
with the help of wavelength dispersive spectroscopy
(WDS) facility of the EPMA. X-ray di/C128raction (XRD)
analysis using Cu K
 radiation was carried out to
analyze the nature of phases formed. X-ray data was
further analyzed to evaluate the lattice parameters.
For the purpose of TEM, specimens were prepared by
mechanical thinning followed by argon ion milling.
TEM studies were performed on a Philips 430T
TEM using an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.
Electron di/C128raction analysis was used to identify the
phases.
Hardness was measured on the metallographically
polished specimens using Vicker’s diamond indentor
and loads of 20 and 30 kgf. The indentation process in
brittle materials, normally produces a controlled frac-
ture pattern consisting of a pyramidal indented region
with some localized cracking and plastic deformation,
plus two perpendicular radial cracks that appear on the
surface of the material as four cracks radiating outward
from the indentation corners. Positions of indentations
were kept at sucient distance from one another to
avoid neighbouring indent–crack interactions. Cracks
formed at the corners of indentations were examined to
understand the operative crack propagation and tough-
ening mechanisms. Imaging with polarized light optical
microscopy led to recording of the locations of the crack
paths with respect to the grains and grain boundaries.
For room temperature ﬂexural strength, samples of
dimensions, span ( S)=40 mm, thickness ( B)=5.0 mm
and width ( W)=5.0 mm were prepared by electro-

and edges. Single edge notched bend (SENB) specimens

6. Conclusions
The e/C128ect of Al alloying on microstructure, room
temperature mechanical properties and high tempera-
ture compressive yield strength was studied in this
investigation. This investigation has shown that:
1. Al reduced the SiO
2 content of MoSi 2 forming
Al2O3 through an in-situ displacement reaction
and this is obvious from observation of the
microstructure using polarized light optical micro-
scopy and EPMA. It is however dicult to add
just that much of Al, which is exactly sucient for
a complete replacement of SiO
2. Even though the
quantity of Al to be added could be calculated
from an estimate of oxygen content in MoSi
2, fur-
ther oxygen is likely to be picked up during pow-
der handling. The Al
2O3 particles remained
distributed throughout the matrix and at grain
boundaries as well as at grain interiors.
2. The surplus Al remaining after Al
2O3 formation
was alloyed with the MoSi 2, forming a substitu-
tional solid solution. The Al in solid solution in
MoSi
2 matrix was 0.5 at% in MoSi 2–2.8 at%
(1.5 wt%) Al alloy, 2.5 at% in MoSi 2–5.5 at%
(3 wt%) Al alloy and 3.1 at% in MoSi 2–9 at%
(5 wt%) Al alloy. MoSi 2–2.8 and 5.5 at% Al alloys
had microstructures containing MoSi 2 (C11b
structure with Al in solid solution) and -Al2O3
phase. MoSi 2–9 at% Al alloys possessed three
phases comprising of MoSi 2 (C11b structure with
Al in solid solution), Mo(Si,Al) 2 phase (C40
structure) and -Al2O3 phase. The concentration
of Al and Al/Si atomic ratio in the Mo(Si,Al) 2
phase were found to be 8.7 at% and 0.15%,
respectively. Al in solid solution not only alters
the lattice parameter and the nature of the intera-
tomic Mo–Si bonds in MoSi
2, but also a/C128ects
its mechanical behaviour at room and elevated
temperature.
3. The room temperature ﬂexural strength showed a
remarkable increase on alloying with 2.8 at% Al.
The room temperature fracture toughness exhib-
ited only a moderate improvement on 2.8 and
5.5 at% Al additions, but a remarkable 49%
increase with 9 at% Al alloying. Al
2O3 particles led
to toughening through the mechanism of crack
deﬂection. A signiﬁcant degree of crack branching
could also be observed along the paths of inden-
tation cracks. Indentation cracking pattern varied
from predominantly transgranular to inter-
granular nature with an increase in the Al con-
centration in solid solution in MoSi
2.
4. The high temperature yield strength also showed a
noticeable improvement on Al alloying between
1100 and 1250
C due to removal of the deleterious
SiO2 phase, which is responsible for softening at
elevated temperatures. Al alloying also led to ser-
rated stress-strain curve outputs during compres-
sion testing of MoSi
2–Al alloys at 1200 C and
above.
