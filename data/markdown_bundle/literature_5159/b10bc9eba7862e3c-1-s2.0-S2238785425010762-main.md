ABSTRACT
Most of the high-strength aluminium alloys for Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) in research are based on 
conventional hardenable Al – Cu, Al – Mg – Si and Al – Zn – Mg alloys that have issues with hot-cracking during the L- 
PBF process. This study investigates the effect of alloying elements on two novel Al – Cr – Mo-Sc-Zr alloys (Scan -
cromal ® variants) with a good, crack-free processability and strength-ductility combination. A layer thickness of 
100 μ m was used, highlighting the high productivity and good processability of Al – Cr alloys. Heat treatments and 
hardness measurements were conducted to investigate peak age heat treatment and revealed a relation between 
Sc/Zr-ratio and peak aged temperature. SEM observations of small precipitates at grain boundaries and fracture 
surfaces contributed to understanding the changing fracture mechanism from less ductile (2.4 – 10.2% elongation) 
to very ductile (14.1 – 19% elongation). Tensile tests at elevated temperatures and creep tests showed a positive 
effect of Cr and Mo on high temperature strength. High-temperature properties, especially creep results of Al – Cr 
alloys and Scalmalloy ® (Al – Mg – Mn-Sc-Zr) are presented for the first time and emphasize the increased heat- 
resistance by Cr and Mo. The threshold stress, below which theoretically no creep should occur, of the pre -
sented Scancromal ® variants is at least 5 times higher (142 – 189 MPa) compared to Scalmalloy ® (28 MPa). A 
comprehensive comparison of the results to literature values of AlSi10Mg, other Al – Cr alloys and high tem -
perature Al alloys gives valuable insight on how the aging response, microstructure, ductility and high- 
temperature strength of this type of alloy is influenced by its alloying elements.
1. Introduction
Because of the rapid solidification rates during Laser Powder Bed 
Fusion (L-PBF), conventional high-strength and age hardenable 2xxx, 
6xxx and 7xxx series Al alloys tend to hot cracking. One option to 
address this problem is adding grain refiners like Sc, Zr or Ti to avoid 
columnar grain growth and enable crack-free parts [ 1 , 2 ]. These grain 
refiners also enable strengths of more than 500 MPa due to formation of 
coherent L1

improve processability is adding Si to decrease the solidification range

when volatile elements like Mg and Zn are involved [ 9 , 10 ].
Scancromal ® was introduced in 2019 by Airbus Defence and Space 
GmbH as a material concept for additively manufactured Al – Cr-Sc-X- 
type alloys. It is based on the previous Scalmalloy ® Al – Mg-Sc-Zr-type 
alloy concept by the same originator. To address the problems with 
evaporation of Mg and Mn in Scalmalloy ® , these elements were 
replaced by 2.6 wt.% Cr in the first development attempt and resulted in

2. Methods
2.1. Powder and Laser Powder Bed Fusion
Three different powders were used in this work: Scalmalloy ® (in the 
following denoted as ‘MG ’ ), Scancromal ® with a high alloying element 
content (‘CR-high ’ ), and low alloying content (‘CR-low ’ ). Chemical 
compositions are given in Table 2 . All three powders were produced by 
Toyo Aluminium K.K., Japan. MG and CR-high were produced via inert 
gas atomization while CR-low was produced via centrifugal atomization 
in nitrogen. Powder particles were measured with an optical image 
analyser Camsizer X2 by Microtrac Retsch GmbH showing a near 
spherical powder particle shape with the resulting particle size distri -
bution in Table 3 and optical microscopy images in Fig. 1 . For the par -
ticle measurement, three samples per alloy with at least 1 million 
particles per sample were used. The coarser particle fraction of CR-low 
was intentionally chosen to be tailored for high build rate applications.
MG and CR-high samples were manufactured in argon atmosphere 
with a maximum oxygen level of 0.1% using a SLM Solutions 
SLM ® 125HL system equipped with a 400 W fiber laser. CR-low samples 
were manufactured in nitrogen atmosphere with a maximum oxygen 
level of 0.1% using a Concept Laser M2 upgraded to Series 5 system 
equipped with two 400 W fiber lasers. MG was printed with standard 
parameters and a layer thickness of 30 μ m, CR-high and CR-low were 
printed with a high layer thickness of 100 μ m. All process parameters are 
summarized in Appendix A . Relative density of each alloy was deter -
mined by image analysis of six optical micrographs resulting in 
> 99.93%, > 99.76% and > 99.55% for MG, CR-high and CR-low, 
respectively. None of the printed samples showed cracks.

As built hardness increases for both alloy types with the corre -
sponding Cr or Mg content, which is plausible because of two mecha -

and second, precipitation hardening (Orowan) by secondary and/or 
coarsened Al

process if Sc and/or Zr is added. Peak aged hardness due to additional 
secondary and/or coarsened Al
3
(Sc,Zr) precipitates does also increase 
with increasing alloying content, leading to two almost parallel regions 
for as built and peak aged Al – Mg. In contrast, as built and peak aged 
areas of Al – Cr begin to intersect at approx. 4 wt.% Cr. This could indi -
cate that Cr is involved in the formation of Al
3
(Sc,Zr) precipitates and 
therefore reduces the aging response. Atom probe measurements [ 11 ] 
support this assumption because of small amounts of Cr and Mo that 
were detected in the Al
3
(Sc,Zr) precipitates.
For a further increase in peak aged hardness, there are three options: 
(i) increasing solid solution hardeners Cr or Mo, (ii) introducing other 
solid solution hardeners additionally or to substitute (parts of) Cr and 
Mo, and (iii) increasing Sc and Zr to increase the precipitation hardening 
effect. A further increase of Cr or Mo could lead to detrimental phases 
and decrease tensile strength and ductility, which will be discussed in 
case of this study in Section 4.3 . Introducing other elements may be 
successful, but other effects in combination with Cr, Mo, Sc and Zr might 
occur that must be investigated. Adding more Sc and Zr might overcome 
the reducing effect of Cr and Mo on peak age hardness but is neither 
sustainable nor economical and therefore not industrially relevant. 
Therefore, the so far maximum achievable hardness value was reported 
for Al – Cr4 – Mo1.36-Sc0.78-Zr0.52 and is 169 HV [ 22 ].
Summing up the age hardening response, a high alloying content in 
Al – Cr alloys is not necessarily useful to increase strength and should be 
chosen carefully considering the application requirements of the final 
part. The visible limitation of hardness of Al – Cr alloys in Fig. 10 a, could 
come from a so far unknown relationship between Cr and Sc/Zr and may 
be proven or disproven in future studies by adding way more Cr. 
Another possibility to increase hardness is substituting (parts of) Cr with 
other elements.

5. Conclusion
A comprehensive study on heat treatment, mechanical properties at 
room and elevated temperatures and microstructure of two novel 
Al – Cr – Mo-Sc-Zr alloys with different compositions (Scancromal ® , ‘CR- 
high ’ and ‘CR-low ’ ) using a 100 μ m layer thickness has been conducted. 
All results have been compared to Al – Mg – Mn-Sc-Zr (Scalmalloy ® , 
‘MG ’ ), which was produced with a standard layer thickness of 30 μ m. 
The following conclusions can be drawn. 
• Sc/Zr ratio influences precipitation formation temperature of Al
3

Zr) particles and has to be considered when choosing an appropriate 
heat treatment.
• Peak aged hardness of CR-high and CR-low is 168 HV10 (330
◦

and 136 HV10 (400

this study with literature, a hardness of about 169 HV10 seems to be 
the achievable maximum for Al – Cr alloys for L-PBF without exces -
sively increasing Sc and Zr.
• Peak aged and in Z-direction, CR-high achieves an ultimate tensile 
strength of 462 MPa close to MG (516 MPa). CR-low has a signifi -
cantly lower ultimate tensile strength due to the lower alloying 
content (353 MPa) but its yield strength (327 MPa) exceeds heat 
treated AlSi10Mg.
• A comparison with literature values showed that there is a trend 
regarding Cr content and ductility of Al – Cr alloys: Ductility decreases 
with increasing Cr content in both Z- and XY-direction. The reason 
for this phenomenon – independently from decreasing ductility by 
solid solution hardening – could be precipitates on grain boundaries 
that change their shape from compact in CR-low to elongated in CR- 
high, act as stress concentrator and lead to more intergranular 
fracture instead of dimples.
• Tensile tests at elevated temperatures showed that Al – Cr alloys have 
a higher ultimate tensile strength (depending on alloying composi -

temperature seems to be lower compared to MG and similar
