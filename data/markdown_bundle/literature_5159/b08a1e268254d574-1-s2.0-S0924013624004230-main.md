ABSTRACT
Subgrain formation and its coupling with the α phase significantly improve the strength and toughness of tita -
nium alloys. To elucidate the effect of temperature changes on the formation mechanism of nano-subgrains in Ti- 
6V-5Al-5Mo-5Cr-3Nb-2Zr-0.2Si alloy, multi-directional forging was conducted at various temperatures, and 
finally quantified the contribution of subgrains and phase evolution to mechanical properties. The results 
revealed that the equiaxed α phase precipitates at the β grain boundary. As the temperature rises, the aspect ratio 
of the α phase increases, while its content decreases. The formation mechanism of nano-subgrains during multi- 
directional forging at 690 ℃ involves dislocation accumulation and the separation of torsional bands within the β 
grains. When the temperature increases, the deformation resistance decreases, eliminating the need for crystal 
torsion to reduce this resistance. Subsequently, high-density dislocations form dislocation walls, which delineate 
the boundaries of fine nano-subgrains. This random orientation of subgrains significantly enhances both the 
strength and toughness of the titanium alloy forged at 770 ℃ . Therefore, the tensile strength and fracture 
toughness of the alloy reach peak values of 1084.8 MPa and 54.18 MPa ⋅ m
1/2
, respectively. Microstructural 
analysis of the cracks reveals that the nano-subgrains effectively hinder their rapid propagation. Due to the 
coupled strengthening effect of subgrains and α phases, the tensile strength and fracture toughness of the tita -
nium alloy forged at 770 ℃ are increased by 26 % and 40 % respectively compared with the as-cast alloy.
1. Introduction
Forging stands out as the most direct method to enhance the me -
chanical properties of β -titanium alloy due to the alterations in crystal 
structure and dislocation density it induces. Techniques such as equal 
channel angular pressing [1,2] and single forging [3] introduce signif -
icant deformation but come with drawbacks, and the effect of defor -
mation on performance gain has given rise to new inspiration. Recently, 
multi-directional forging (MDF) has captured the attention of re -
searchers due to its equipment simplicity and capacity to impart higher 
cumulative strain. Zhang et al. [4] successfully achieved titanium alloys 
with tensile strength reaching 1214.5 MPa by precisely controlling the 
content and distribution of silicide through MDF. Ozerov et al. [5]
demonstrated that MDF notably improves the plasticity of titanium 
alloys without sacrificing their strength. It is important to highlight that 
the MDF of titanium alloys under optimal process parameters is pivotal 
for modulating their mechanical properties. Achieving a finer micro -
structure is essential for boosting mechanical properties, a challenge 
that has garnered considerable attention from researchers [6,7] . In 
comparison to β grains, β subgrains do not introduce boundaries with 
detrimental toughness, making them an ideal method for strengthening 
and toughening. MDF emerges as a crucial process in modulating these 
properties, necessitating a comprehensive investigation into its under -
lying mechanisms that govern microstructural evolution.
Ensuring MDF under optimal process parameters is crucial for 
adjusting their microstructural. Temperature plays a pivotal role in hot 
deformation processes, as it dictates the size, content, and type of phases 
present [8 – 10] . Precise temperature control is crucial, as it significantly

and β single-phase region).
The formation of nano-subgrains and the precipitation of α phase are 
important microstructure evolution processes in the MDF process, both 
of which can improve the tensile strength of titanium alloys. Different 
microstructures are prepared through different processing processes, 
which determine the properties of the material. Further attention should 
be devoted to the mechanisms of α phase precipitation, refinement, and 
subgrain formation during large deformations induced by MDF, as these 
processes forge a critical connection between the processing route and 
the mechanical properties of the material [21 – 24] . Xu et al. [25] re -

precipitated α phase exerts a strengthening effect in the as-cast titanium 
alloy [26] . Typically, phase and grain refinement, along with sub -
structure changes, occur concurrently during deformation. Therefore, 
the coupled strengthening effect between β subgrain formation and α 
phase precipitation warrants thorough investigation.
This study clarified the evolution of microstructure and mechanical 
properties during MDF, paying special attention to the effect of tem -
perature on crystal orientation and subcrystal formation mechanism, 
and quantified the contributions of α phase content and nano-subgrains 
to the mechanical properties. It provides a reference for realizing the 
strengthening and toughness of titanium alloys through the coupling of 
subgrains and α phases.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure and phase analysis
Fig. 1 presents the microstructure and phase composition of the as- 
cast titanium alloy. As depicted in Fig. 1 (a), the microstructure of the 
as-cast alloy is characterized by a single β phase, with an average grain 
size of approximately 380 μ m. Higher-resolution images reveal the 
presence of voids and other defects, which contribute to the reduced 
mechanical properties of the as-cast titanium alloy. The XRD analysis 
shown in Fig. 1 (b) indicates a single β peak with a narrow half-width, 
suggesting the absence of β subcrystals. Collectively, the microstruc -
tural and phase analyses confirm that the as-cast microstructure is 
composed solely of the β phase, without any β subgrains or α phase.
Fig. 2 is a comparative diagram of microstructure after forging at 
different temperatures. In Fig. 2 (a), the α phase with a content of about 
27 % after multi-directional forging at 690 ℃ is mainly distributed 
along the original β grain boundary. In the β grain, the α phase is not 
uniformly distributed at the edge and center, and the closer to the 
center, the lower its content. The enlarged image shows that the 
morphology is equiaxed with a diameter of about 2 – 3 μ m. During 
forging, dislocations are more easily to be blocked at the original β grain 
boundaries, which provides enough driving energy for α phase precip -
itation and equiaxed transformation. As the forging temperature is 
elevated to 730 ℃ ( Fig. 2 (b)), the content of α phase precipitating at the 
original β grain boundaries decreases. However, the morphology and 
size remain largely unchanged compared to the observations at 690 ℃ . 
The microstructure resulting from MDF at 770 ℃ is depicted in Fig. 2 (c), 
where most of the α phases maintain an equiaxed shape, while a few 
transforms into a continuous form. When the temperature reaches 810 
℃ , which is above the β transition temperature, only a continuous α 
phase is observed as shown in Fig. 2 (d). The results indicate that defects 
such as voids are eliminated after MDF, and the microstructure is 
composed of a mixture of α and β phases. As the deformation tempera -
ture increases, the content of the α phase decreases, while the β phase 
content increases accordingly.
The XRD results of the titanium alloy after MDF at various temper -
atures are presented in Fig. 3 . At 690 ℃ , XRD analysis reveals two 
distinct peaks corresponding to the α and β phases. As the temperature 
increases, the α phase gradually dissolves into the matrix, leaving only 
the β phase detectable. Although the α phase was previously charac -
terized in Fig. 2 , its diminishing content renders it increasingly difficult 
to identify by XRD [27,28] .
The β phase grains are counted in multiple fields of view, and the 
average size value is obtained. Fig. 4 shows that the average β grain size 
of titanium alloy forged at 690 ℃ decreases from 380 μ m in the as-cast 
state to 180 μ m, representing a decrease of 52.6 %. This reduction in 
grain size is attributed to the weakening of the driving force for β grain 
recrystallization as the forging temperature increases. In contrast, the 
average grain size of titanium alloy forged at 810 ℃ is 410 μ m.

at 690 ℃ is shown in Fig. 5 (a). The original β grain boundary and the α 
phase hinder the dislocation slip to form a dislocation wall and activate 
the dislocation slip in the surrounding β grains. The dislocation wall

walls and the β grains. In Fig. 9 (a), the dislocation wall shown by the red

connection lines at the curved interfaces, which contributes to pre- 
energy storage for subsequent recrystallization. The microstructure of 
the shear band situated at the β grain boundary is illustrated in Fig. 9 (b). 
It comprises the β grain boundary and the area enclosed by dislocation 
walls within the crystal. Similarly, dislocation walls accumulate pro -

of the β grains reveals the formation of nano-subgrains with dislocations

various positions exhibit distinct diffraction patterns. A higher disloca -

discernible corresponding diffraction rings.

4. Discussion
4.1. Subgrain formation mechanism
Fig. 12 summarizes the formation mechanism of two subgrains in 
titanium alloys based on the observed microstructure changes, where 
Fig. 12 (a and b) are the subgrain formation mechanisms at 690 ℃ , 730 
℃ and 770 ℃ , and Fig. 12 (c and d) are the subgrain formation mech -
anisms at 810 ℃ . In Fig. 12 (a), dislocations at the grain boundary extend 
into the grain, forming a dislocation wall. In Fig. 12 (b), subgrains form 
in the surrounding area between the dislocation wall and the original β 
grain boundary during continued deformation. Additionally, Figs. 7 (a) 
and 7 (b) show that there is a torsion band with an orientation different 
from that of the β crystal, caused by local plastic deformation, which 
leads to the decomposition of the β grain into several subgrains. Crystal 
rotation occurs within the torsion band to make plastic deformation 
easier, which means that lower deformation resistance is obtained [12, 
32] . In Fig. 12 (c and d), there is no need for torsion to adapt to local 
strain after matrix softening at higher temperature (810 ℃ ). Instead, 
many dispersed dislocations in β grains provide sufficient driving energy 
for subgrains nucleation. TEM results show that the newly formed sub -
grains inherit the highly random orientation of dislocations.

4.3. Crack propagation mechanism
Fig. 13 (a) shows the crack propagation morphology of a titanium 
alloy deformed at 690 ℃ after a toughness test. The crack continues to 
propagate around the equiaxed α phase, extends straightforwardly 
through the β phase, and eventually breaks completely. As shown in 
Fig. 13 (c), the crack is observed within the α phase at the β grain 
boundary but does not completely penetrate the sample. The nearly 
parallel slip bands extend through the β grain and across the grain 
boundary into the adjacent grains. This indicates that the subgrains 
hinder the generation and propagation of cracks, as evidenced in 
Figs. 5 – 10 , where a greater randomness of β phase orientation correlates 
with better toughness of the titanium alloy. As shown in Fig. 13 (d), after 
forging at 810 ℃ , there are no subgrains within the β grain. Grain 
boundary α phase does not cause cracks to deflect, but is quickly 
penetrated, resulting in a decrease in the toughness of the titanium alloy 
after deformation at 810 ℃ .
The crack propagation mechanism in titanium alloy is summarized in 
Fig. 14 . As shown in Fig. 14 (a), the microstructure contains α phases, 
which hinder and deflect cracks. This crack propagation mechanism is 
confirmed by the fracture morphology observed after forging at 690 ℃ . 
Specifically, the zigzag crack propagates along the phase boundary of 
the α phase located at the grain boundary. As the temperature increases, 
the blocking effect of the grain boundary α phases weakens. Even though 
the crack deflection path becomes relatively straighter, the titanium 
alloy exhibits optimal fracture toughness due to the high fracture 
resistance of randomly oriented subgrains within the β grains. As 
depicted in Fig. 14 (c), a decrease in dislocation density within the β 
grains is observed at a deformation temperature of 810 ℃ , resulting in a 
more direct crack propagation path within the β grains. Additionally, the 
increase in the aspect ratio of the α phase at the grain boundary reduces 
its pinning effect on the crack, allowing it to cross the grain boundary 
more directly, as observed in Figs. 13 (d) and 14 (c). Consequently, with 
the decrease in the volume fraction of the α phase, the crack propagates 
rapidly along the β grain boundary, exhibiting a long and tortuous 
morphology.
5. Conclusion
In this work, two distinct formation mechanisms of nano-subgrains 
are proposed, and their contributions to mechanical properties are dis -
cussed. The coupling effect of α phase and nano-subgrains on mechan -
ical properties is concerned, which provides a novel feasibility basis for 
forging modification of titanium alloys. The main conclusions are as 
follows: 
(1) The α phase precipitated during forging is gradually sparse along 
the β grain boundary. As the temperature increases, the content of 
the α phase at the grain boundary augments, while its size ele -
vates and the shape becomes more elongated.
(2) When the forging temperature is lower than 810 ℃ , with the 
increase of temperature, the content of β nano-subgrains in -
creases and the orientation becomes more random. The high 
density dislocation leads to the separation of β grains into fine, 
randomly oriented nano-subgrains. At 690 and 770 ℃ forging 
temperatures, the formation of torsion bands significantly re -
duces the deformation resistance encountered. It and accumu -
lated dislocations lead to the formation of nano-subgrains at the 
same time.
