1. Introduction 
Steels, aluminum alloys or nickel-based alloys as a few examples; 
material scientists in the 20th century were focused on individual ele -
ments whose properties have been adjusted by small amounts of alloying 
elements [ 1 ]. Around the turn of the millennium a new class of materials 
started to gain more and more attention, i.e. alloys with many elements, 
but none dominating. The term “ high entropy alloy ” has been intro -
duced and defined as an alloy with five or more elements and an atomic 
concentration of each element between 5 and 35% [ 2 ]. These re -
quirements lead to an increase of the configurational entropy in the 
crystal lattice, which was supposed to dominate and suppress the 
formation of intermetallic phases [ 3 ]. A solid solution single-phase 
microstructure is thus stabilized, from which the following interesting 
properties are expected [ 4 ]: A randomly distributed and concentrated 
solid solution shows severe lattice distortion, desirable for high me -
chanical strength by hindering the dislocation movement. Another 
aspect is the complicated diffusion through the crystal lattice, stabilizing 
the material at high temperatures. An impressive feature of 
face-centered cubic (fcc) high entropy alloys is their high ductility at 
cryogenic temperatures down to 77 K [ 5 ], but single-phase high entropy 
alloys on the other hand do not really fulfill the mentioned mechanisms 
to compete with commercially used alloys. Furthermore, hardly any 
alloys are described in the literature which have a single-phase

The metals with a purity of at least 99.99% are cleaned with ethanol

alloy remains in the liquid state. Withdrawing the melt through a water-

to directionally solidified and [001]-oriented grains. 
The rods, 110 mm long and 20 mm in diameter, are subjected to 
homogenization and annealing. In addition to the standard annealing for 
all alloys, alternative annealing is performed for the base- and Hf-alloy.

2.2. Microstructural observations

4. Conclusion 
Regarding the influence of high melting elements on the micro -
structure of the base alloy Al
10

25

8

and the associated 
mechanical properties at elevated temperatures, the following findings 
can be summarized: 
• To avoid eutectics at the grain boundaries, the homogenization 
temperature must be reduced when adding Hf, Zr or Y. As a result, 
the Heusler-phase from the as-cast state stays undissolved in the Hf- 
and Zr-alloy, and in the Y-alloy even the dendritic structure remains. 
In case of the Mo-alloy, subsequent annealing leads to fewer Heusler- 
needles and in case of the W-alloy, these are not precipitated at all. 
• The γ
′
-morphology changes as a function of the magnitude of the 
lattice misfit at the matrix/ γ
′
-interface. Hf and Zr accumulate in γ
′
and thus increase the positive misfit, which leads to sharp edges of 
the cubic γ
′
-particles. Mo causes spherical particles due to the 
opposite effect, which is less pronounced for W. Y is bound in 
additional phases and does not affect the matrix/ γ
′
-misfit. 
• Using a neural network, several thousand γ
′
-particles have been 
efficiently examined regarding their size and volume fraction in al -
loys with almost cubic γ
′
-precipitates. 
• When annealed at a higher temperature, the phase fractions shift. 
There are three times more Heusler-needles in the base alloy 
annealed at the higher temperature of 950 
◦
C, which causes bad 
mechanical tensile behavior. However, the fraction of only 3 vol% 
after standard annealing has a positive effect on the ultimate tensile 
strength compared to the two-phase W-alloy. 
• Five creep experiments on the base alloy lead to a stress exponent of 
10.8 at 750 
◦
C and an activation energy of creep of 486 kJ/mol in the 
range of 700 – 800 
◦
C at a stress of 425 MPa. 
• In contrast to the tensile behavior, 3 vol% of the needle-shaped 
Heusler-phase in the base alloy behave detrimentally on the creep 
resistance. 
• The Mo-alloy, due to round γ
′
-particles, and the Y-alloy, due to un -
dissolved dendrites, do not lead to any improvement of the time- 
independent and -dependent mechanical properties of the base 
alloy. They are instead improved by adding Hf and Zr, because the 
sharp-edged γ
′
-particles and the beneficial Heusler-phase from the 
as-cast state contribute to this behavior. 
• The best alloy from this study, the Hf-alloy, can be loaded with a 
stress twice or five times higher than Inconel 617 or Alloy 800 H at 
750 
◦
C to realize the same creep rates.
