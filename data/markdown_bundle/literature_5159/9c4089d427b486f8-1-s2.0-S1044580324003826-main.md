1. Introduction 
Molybdenum possesses both high physical and mechanical proper -
ties, including good structural stability, thermal conductivity and high 
strength at high temperature [ 1 ]. Highly stable body-centered cubic 
(BCC) structure makes multi-strengthening methods possible in molyb -
denum alloys. Efficient improvements of strength and creep resistance at 
high temperature could be obtained via solution strengthening (Zr, Ti 
[ 2 ], W [ 3 ], Si [ 4 ], and B [ 5 ]) and dispersion strengthening (Mo
2
TiC
2 
[ 6 ], 
HfC [ 7 ], La
2
O
3 
[ 8 ]). High ductile to brittle transition temperature 
(DBTT) in molybdenum (325 K), however, leads to low ductility in most 
of Mo alloys at room temperature (RT) [ 9 ]. Thus, developing Mo alloys 
with DBTT lower than RT is an essential way to obtain desirable me -
chanical properties. 
Sufficient experimental results have proven that adding large frac -
tion of rhenium could reduce DBTT of Mo
–
Re alloy to near 77 K, which 
leads to the enhancement in both ductility and strength at RT [ 10 ]. 
Comparing with ~4% elongation and ~ 300 MPa yield strength in pure 
Mo at RT, the elongation and yield strength of Mo
–
Re (35 at.%) alloy 
increase to 700 MPa and ~ 13%, respectively [ 10 ]. The benefits of Re to 
Mo were attributed to some suggestions, including increased interstitial 
(e.g. carbon and oxygen) solution [ 11 ] and decreased stacking faults 
energy that facilitates twinning deformation at low temperature [ 12 ]. 
As a candidate of heat pipe reactor core, some researches on the 
processes and mechanical properties of Mo
–
Re alloy with weight per -
centage 47.5% (Mo-47.5Re) have been reported [ 9 , 10 , 13 , 14 ]. Xu etal 
reported the anomalous strain-rate effect on ductility at room temper -
ature, i.e. elongation decreases as strain-rate decreases [ 9 ]. In addition, 
Mo-47.5 rhenium alloy exhibits good ductility as temperature down to 
liquid nitrogen temperature [ 10 ]. In previous research, Deformation

2. Experiments 
High purity molybdenum and rhenium powders were used to pre -
pare Mo-47.5Re (wt%) alloy via electron beam melting methods. 
Thermal extrusion with ratio 4:1 was given to reduce geometrical size 
and improve mechanical properties. Thermal annealing at 1600 
◦
C was 
conducted for 2 h to eliminate processed stress and finish full recrys -
tallization. The recrystallized samples were mechanically grinded and 
polished to obtain grain sizes under optical microscope, to obtain phase 
structure via X-ray diffraction (XRD) with 2 θ ranges from 20
◦
to 90
◦
. 
Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD JSM-7200F) was used to char -
acterize grain orientations. 
To study the microstructural evolutions of Mo-47.5Re alloy, RT 
compressions were tested with strain rate 1.67 × 10
 3 
/s. The diameter 
and height in compression test samples are 4 mm and 6 mm, respec -
tively. Furthermore, compressions were also conducted at 400 
◦
C, 
800 
◦
C and 1200 
◦
C with strain rate 1.67 × 10
 3 
/s and 1.67 × 10
 1 
/s, 
respectively. The final true strain was set at 30% for all compression 
tests. At least three specimens are tested for each sample to justify the 
results. Deformed features were characterized via EBSD and trans -
mission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F) at 200 kV. The 
samples for TEM tests were prepared via mechanical grinding and ion 
milling. The distribution of composed elements is detected by energy 
dispersive spectrometer (EDS) operating at TEM.

4. Conclusions 
We have studied the temperature and strain rate dependent micro -
structural evolutions of Mo-47.5Re alloy under compressions. Defor -
mation twins are observed after compression at RT, which has been 
carefully characterized. Twinning is pretty sensitive to temperature and 
is absent at 400 
◦
C. Screw dislocations move fast and dominate the 
plasticity of Mo-47.5Re alloy, suppressing the nucleation of deformation 
twins. Microstructures and mechanical responses are dependent on 
temperature and strain rate. Strain gradients in deformed sample are 
rely heavily on strain rate at 400 
◦
C. Comparing with compression at 
400 
◦
C, microstructures are less independent on strain rate at 800 
◦
C and 
1200 
◦
C. While, yield stresses are weak temperature dependent at low 
temperatures.
