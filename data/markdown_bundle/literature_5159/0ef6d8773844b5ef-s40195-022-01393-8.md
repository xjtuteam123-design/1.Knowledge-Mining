Abstract
Eutectic high-entropy alloys (EHEAs) that have superior formability are attractive for direct laser deposition technology. In 
this study, a regular-shaped bulk Ni32Co30Cr10Fe10Al18 EHEA without apparent pores and micro-cracks was successfully 
fabricated by direct laser deposition. The as-deposited alloy showed a high tensile strength of 1.3 GPa with a ductility of 
35% at ambient temperature and a tensile strength of 320 MPa at 760 °C. The deformation mechanisms of the as-deposited 
alloy at ambient and elevated temperatures were investigated by coupling the in-situ tensile test with a scanning electron 
microscope. It is revealed that the excellent combination of strength and ductility originated from the synergic effects of the 
FCC and B2 phases in eutectic lamellae. And the generation of cracks along phase boundaries restricted its high-temperature 
strength above 760 °C.
Keywords Direct laser deposition · Eutectic high-entropy alloys · Elevated-temperature properties · Deformation behaviors
1 Introduction
Direct laser deposition (DLD) technology, based on pow -
ders melted by a high-energy–density laser, is among the 
most widely used methods in metal additive manufacturing, 
which has possessed significant advantages in fabricating 
geometrically complex products [1 –4]. However, since the 
large temperature gradient during rapid solidification, the 
formability of alloys [2, 5] in DLD is generally restricted by 
the temperature gradient-induced cracks. In general, cracks 
are likely to occur in the solid–liquid mixing mushy zone 
due to the internal stress during solidification [6 ].
With excellent mechanical properties [7–9], high-entropy 
alloys have attracted extensive attention among developed 
printable alloys. And it has been found that the as-deposited 
HEAs can exhibit superior performance than the correspond-
ing as-cast counterparts, owing to different cooling rates of 
DLD process [10, 11]. In recent years, as a HEA of com-
bining design concept of eutectic alloys, the eutectic high-
entropy alloys (EHEAs) have shown excellent mechanical 
properties at room temperatures and microstructure stabil -
ity at high temperatures [12–17]. More importantly, EHEAs 
melts could be timely feed in the later solidification period 
since its good fluidity, which can avoid the formation of 
cracks [18]. Hence, there exists a significant advantage of 
eutectic high-entropy alloys in guaranteeing the formability 
of alloys, making EHEAs a promising alloy candidate for 
DLD.
Up to now, several bulk samples of EHEAs have been 
prepared by DLD or other laser metal deposition meth-
ods. Joseph et al. [19] explored the influence of Al con-
tent on the microstructure and mechanical properties of the 
 AlxCoCrFeNi2.1 HEAs prepared by LENS (laser engineered 
net shaping), showing the increase in B2 phase fraction and 
compressive strength along with increasing Al content. 
Vikram et al. [20] reported the LENS-printed AlCoCrFeNi2.1 
EHEA with FCC primary phase dendrite and FCC-BCC 
eutectic lamellae, as well as the good comprehensive perfor-
mance at room temperature and high-temperature stability.

Most of the previous research on the DLD-ed EHEAs 
focused on the investigations of microstructure and mechani-

deformation behaviors is still unclear, especially at ele -
vated temperatures, which are essential for further regulat-

 Ni32Co30Cr10Fe10Al18 (Ni32Al18 for abbreviation) EHEA 
was fabricated via DLD, in which the corresponding micro-
structures and mechanical performance were evaluated. The 
alloy can be well-printed and showed excellent mechanical 
properties at a broad range of temperatures. Particularly, the 
tensile test with in-situ SEM (scanning electron microscope) 
was carried out to reveal the deformation mechanisms in 
the as-deposited EHEA at ambient temperatures. The high-
temperature deformation behaviors were explored by analyz-
ing the evolution of microstructure and micro-cracks after 
deformations at different temperatures.
2 Materials and Methods
The raw Ni32Al18 powder was made of CoCrFeNi powder 
(prepared by plasma rotation electrode process, 50–150 μm) 
and Ni, Co, Al powders (prepared by gas atomization, 
50–75 μm) by mechanical mixing in a planetary ball mill 
for 8 h at a rotation speed of 300 rpm. AISI 316 stainless 
steel plates with dimensions of 140 mm × 60 mm × 10 mm 
were used as the substrate. The experiment was prepared 
by an LSF-VI laser additive manufacturing system with a 
semiconductor laser, a coaxial powder feeder and an inert 
atmosphere protection chamber (argon atmosphere with the 
oxygen content below 50 ppm). The processing parameters 
are listed in Table  1. The bi-directional deposition pattern 
was applied and the scanning orientation was deflected by 
90° between each successive layer, as shown in Fig. 1a. The 
as-deposited bulk Ni32Al18 EHEA with dimensions about 
20 mm × 25 mm × 60 mm was built, as shown in Fig. 1b.
The phase composition of the as-deposited Ni32Al18 
EHEA was characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (X'Pert 
PRO) with Cu Kα radiation at a scanning rate of 5° min−1, 
and a 2θ angle ranging from 20° to 130°. Backscattered 
electron (BSE) imaging and energy dispersive spectrom -
etry (EDS) mapping analyses, and electron backscatter dif-
fraction (EBSD) were carried out on a TESCAN MIRA3. 
Tensile tests at room and high temperatures were performed 
by a universal electronic tensile testing machine (TSMT 
EM6.504) at a strain rate of 1 × 10−3  s−1. High-tempera-
ture compression tests were performed by a Gleeble ther -
mal/force simulation testing machine with a strain rate of 
1 × 10−3  s−1 and a heating rate of 10 °C/s. The in-situ tensile 
test was conducted in SEM with a displacement strain rate 
of 1 × 10−3  s−1. All samples for mechanical performance 
experiments were cut by wire electrical discharge machin-
ing and were applied with loads along the building direction.
3 Results
3.1 Microstructures
Figure  2a shows the grain morphology of the DLD-ed 
 Ni32Al18 alloy. The long dendrite grains grew across mul-
tiple layers along the building direction, showing typical 
epitaxial growth features. On the sides of the sample, den-
drite growth directions were deflected to edges on both 
sides, respectively, due to the temperature gradient change. 
Figure 2b depicts the enlarged phase morphology, reveal -
ing the hypo-eutectic microstructure of the as-deposited 
alloy. Thus, the microstructure comprises primary dendrites 
epitaxially growing across multiple deposition layers and 
dual-phase lamellae in the inter-dendritic regions between 
columnar dendritic grains. Similar structures can be found in 
other studies adopting the rapid solidification process, such 
as the LENS-ed AlCoCrFeNi2.1 prepared by Vikram et al. 
[20] and directionally solidified AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 prepared 
by Zheng et al. [23]. Furthermore, deposited layers can be 
distinguished from each other by the presence of inter-layer 
regions with higher eutectic lamellae volume fraction, as 
displayed in Fig. 2c. And the secondary dendrites gradually 
become more obvious from the bottom to the top of each 
layer, due to the decreasing temperature gradient. The X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) result of the as-deposited Ni32Al18 alloy 
(Fig.  2d) shows the peaks corresponding to FCC and B2

3.2 Mechanical Properties
Figure 4a shows the room-temperature stress–strain curves 
of the as-deposited Ni32Al18 EHEA, comparing to conven-
tional as-cast counterpart [25]. Several DLD-ed samples 
were tested repeatedly, and all stress–strain curves are 
listed in Fig. S1. It is evident that the as-deposited alloy 
possesses excellent tensile properties with a yield strength 
of 516 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 1342 MPa, 
and an elongation of 36.3%. In addition, compared with 
the as-cast sample, a strength increment of ~ 20% (about 
240 MPa) and an elongation increment of ~ 125% (about 
20%) were achieved in the DLD-ed Ni32Al18 sample. The 
directional growth of FCC dendrites can effectively reduce 
the proportion of transverse interfaces and effectively pro-
motes the work hardening ability, improving the ultimate 
strength and elongation of the DLD-ed samples. However, 
the yield strength of DLD-ed samples decreased. It was 
previously shown that the yield strength of the dual-phase 
HEAs could be accurately predicted based on the yield 
strengths of the constituent phases using the rule of mix-
tures [19]. Compared with the as-cast alloy, the volume 
fraction of B2 phase in the as-deposited alloy is lower 
due to the existence of primary FCC phase, resulting in a 
reduction in yield strength [26]. Figure  4b compares the 
fracture tensile strength and elongation of this alloy with 
the present DLD additively manufactured HEAs, indicat-
ing that the as-deposited Ni32Al18 EHEA displayed an 
excellent combination of high strength and high ductility 
compared with other DLD-ed HEAs [21, 27–31].

4 Deformation Behaviors
4.1 Microstructure Evolution at Room Temperature
To reveal the correlation between microstructure and 
mechanical properties, an in-situ tensile test was conducted 
to investigate the deformation behavior of individual phases 
during tensile deformation. The in-situ stress–strain curve 
of the as-deposited Ni32Al18 EHEA is presented in Fig.  5a. 
The sudden decreases on the curve (marked by black arrows) 
represent a slight stress relaxation due to the pause in force 
loading for microstructure observation in SEM. After obtain-
ing the corresponding SEM images, the force loading was 
continued. The morphology of the observed area is shown 
in Fig. 5b, where the bright and dark zones are correspond-
ing to FCC and B2 phases, respectively, and no voids or 
cracks can be observed on the surface of the specimen. The 
observed area contains four characteristic regions. Regions 
1, 2 and 3 are eutectic microstructures whereas the lamel-
lae are approximately parallel, approximately vertical and at 
about 45° to the loading direction, respectively. Region 4 is 
the dendrite microstructure containing a coarse dendrite of 
the primary FCC phase.
In order to clarify the evolution of different microstruc-
tures during deformation, the detailed morphology images 
of four characteristic regions under different deformation are 
shown in Fig. 6. In the beginning, slip lines can be obviously 
observed in the eutectic lamellae (Fig. 6a1–c1) but few in the 
dendrite regions (Fig. 6d1). Specifically, in eutectic regions, 
the slip lines originated in the two-phase interface and sub-
sequently extended to the soft FCC phase. It demonstrates 
that the phase boundaries of eutectic layers are the origin of 
the dislocation heterogeneous nucleation which would acti-
vate the plastic deformation of the soft FCC phase [33]. The 
slip system is different in four regions. In region 1, there was 
a single slip and the slip lines can extend through the phase 
boundary to the B2 phase. In region 2, crossed and jagged-
like slip lines in Fig.  6b1 represent the cross slip system. 
Staggered and X-like slip lines in Fig. 6c1 represent the mul-
tiple slip system in region 3. In regions 2 and 3, the slip lines 
are confined within the FCC phase by phase boundaries. The 
slip transfer in phase boundary depends on the relationship 
between the slip plane and the interface in the activated slip 
systems or the dislocation vector. The interface allowing 
slip lines to pass through distributes strain to the B2 phase 
is beneficial to the collaborative deformation between the 
FCC and B2 phases, which ensures the alloy ductility [34].
When the strain reaches 8%, stress concentration hap-
pened near the phase boundary (bright white area in 
Fig. 6a2). Along with the increasing strain, a crack grew 
in the soft FCC phase (Fig.  6a3) and changed the direc-
tion of propagation when it expanded to the hard BCC 
phase (Fig.  6a4), demonstrating that the alternating soft-
hard (FCC-B2) phase layers effectively buffer the crack

4.2 Microstructure Evolution of High‑Temperature 
Deformation
To further explore the strong temperature dependence, high-
temperature compression tests were conducted to investigate 
the microstructure evolution after thermal deformation at 
different temperatures. The high-temperature compression 
stress–strain curves of the as-deposited Ni32Al18 EHEA are 
shown in Fig. 7a. It is observed that the flow stress rapidly 
increases to a peak value, and then gradually decreases to 
a relatively steady state, indicating that the main soften-
ing mechanisms here are dynamic recovery (DRV) and 
dynamic recrystallization (DRX) [36, 37]. At low strain, 
the dynamic recovery during this stage is not enough to 
balance the effects of work hardening, causing the stress 
to keep increasing. As strain increases, the annihilation of 
dislocation due to dynamic recrystallization offsets the work 
hardening effect and thus decreases the flow stress. When 
the steady stage reaches, the flow stress keeps a steady state 
due to the dynamic balance between the work hardening 
and dynamic softening. At 700 °C, 850 °C, 1000 °C and 
1150 °C, the compressive yield strengths are about 412 MPa, 
220 MPa, 90 MPa and 38 MPa, respectively. XRD patterns 
of as-deposited samples after high-temperature compression

We further checked the microstructures after hot com -
pressions to clarify the effect of deformation temperature on 
the structure. The microstructures of the longitudinal sec-
tions of each sample after high-temperature compression 
are shown in Fig.  7b–e. After compression at 700 °C, the 
structure is composed of elongated lamellae and dendrites 
interspersed with banded regions composed of broken grains 
(Fig. 7b). The phase boundaries of eutectic layers and the 
broken grains can effectively hinder dislocation motion to 
restrain softening process, maintaining the work harden-
ing ability and high-temperature strength at 700 °C. With 
the increase in temperature, the dendritic structure gradu-
ally disappears and the eutectic lamellae become shorter 
and coarser. Dendrite structure can be observed at 850 °C 
(Fig. 7c). When the temperature increased to 1000 °C, parts 
of the FCC and B2 phases transform into equiaxed grains, 
as shown in Fig.  7d. At 1150 °C, the equiaxed grain size 
increases obviously.
EBSD results of the samples of the as-deposited EHEA 
after thermal deformation at different temperatures are 
shown in Fig.  7f–i. At 1000 °C, new grains grew in the 
BCC lamellae (Fig. 7h), demonstrating a typical character -
istic of DRX [38, 39]. The proportion and grain diameter of 
recrystallized grains increased gradually with the increase in 
deformation temperature. At 1150 °C, the texture almost dis-
appeared and the BCC grains were uniformly distributed in 
the FCC phase. These results show that the transformation of 
dendrite structure to equiaxed crystal structure is intensified 
with the increase in temperature, indicating the acceleration 
of DRX grains growth by high temperature, resulting in the 
sharp softening of the material.
The effect of microstructure on mechanical proper -
ties can also be explained by researching the cracks. The 
crack morphologies near the tensile fracture surface at 
high temperature are shown in Fig. 8. At 760 °C, almost all 
cracks occurred at the transverse phase boundaries between 
the dendrite and eutectic structure. Some micropores are 
observed in the FCC phase dendrites and lamellae near the 
phase boundary (Fig.  8a), indicating that the microcracks 
appeared in the phase boundary and then extended in the 
soft FCC phase. The micropores in the dendrite area rapidly 
develop into long cracks at 45° to the load direction (Fig. 8b, 
c). Figure  8d shows that the cracks also appear near phase 
boundaries at 982 °C. However, the crack source increases

5 Conclusion
In this work, a compact Ni32Co30Cr10Fe10Al18 alloy with 
superior printability was successfully fabricated by DLD, 
proving the potential printability advantage of EHEAs in 
additive manufacturing. The microstructures of the as-
deposited alloy consist of typical epitaxial columnar crys-
tals growing along the deposition direction, in which the 
dendrites are FCC phase and the interdendritic eutectics 
are FCC + B2 phases. The printed sample shows excel -
lent mechanical properties at room temperature with a ten-
sile strength over 1.3 GPa and an elongation up to 35%. 
At 760 °C, the alloy exhibits a yield strength of 320 MPa 
with low density of 7.45 g/cm3. Based on the in-situ tensile 
test, the plastic co-deformation by slip transfer and phase 
strengthening effect at room temperature were visualized. 
However, at high temperatures the recrystallization and 
cracks propagation along phase boundaries induce the sof-
tening of the alloy with limited strength.
