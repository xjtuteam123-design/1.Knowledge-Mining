Increasing the thrust-weight ratio of aeroengines requires development of high-strength and stable high- 
temperature materials. A data-driven design of Ni-based turbine disc superalloys is performed to improve 
the yield strength to reach the target. Through ﬁrst-principles calculations determining the design super- 
alloy system, the theoretical models and Calculation of Phase Diagram (CALPHAD) screening composi- 
tions, and machine learning extrapolating prediction, 14 compositions are selected from 2,865,039 com- 
position combinations. Ni-17Cr-8Co-1Mo-1W-6Al-3Ti-1Nb-1Ta is selected to verify the design accuracy. 
Experimental tests prove that the designed alloy has trade-offs of microstructure with satisfying design 
targets, and then, the yield strength is higher in the designed alloy than in commercial superalloys, reach- 
ing 728 MPa at 850 °C. A scheme for increasing the performance of the designed alloy is proposed by 
discussing the strengthening mechanisms, machine learning process, and alloying chemistry effect. The 
cross-scale data-driven design is regarded as an accurate and eﬃcient way to design novel high-strength 
Ni-based turbine disc superalloys, whose signiﬁcance is the obvious reduction of trial-and-error tests.

1. Introduction 
Ni-based superalloys have excellent comprehensive proper- 
ties of strength maintenance, creep resistance, fatigue resistance 
and oxidation resistance in high-temperature environments above 
600 °C and are widely used in the ﬁelds of aerospace, nuclear 
power, and transportation [1–3] . Ni-based superalloys consist of 
the FCC-Ni matrix phase ( γ) and the L1 2 -Ni 3 Al precipitates ( γ’) 
obtained from solid state transformation; ∼50 vol% ordered γ’ 
precipitate phase coherently embedded into disordered γ matrix 
phase. High-temperature strengthening is analysed as two mech- 
anisms: (1) γ’ resisting Shockley partial dislocation shearing and 
(2) reducing the deformation rate by twin crystals of the {111} 
plane of adjacent γ’ [4–9] . Relying on excellent reinforcement abil-

In the service environment, the loading stress is higher in the 
turbine disc than in the turbine blade, e.g., the instantaneous lo- 
cal stress for the turbine disc of the Trent 800 engine can reach 
10 0 0 MPa at 650 °C [10] . Therefore, the properties of turbine discs 
pay great attention to yield strength. On the macroscopic level, 
the yield strength reﬂects the ability of the material to main-

on the microscopic level, the yield strength implies the ability to 
block dislocation motion to maintain the consistency of the mi- 
crostructure [11] . In service, the material will be considered to 
have failed if the material deformation exceeds the limit of di- 
mensional tolerance. Thus, the resistance of yield stress is vital 
for Ni-based turbine disc superalloys; the yield strength is re- 
garded as a critical metric to evaluate high-temperature failure re- 
sistance for turbine discs. Developing higher performance aircraft 
requires the engine to heave a higher thrust-weight ratio (ratio of 
engine thrust to engine gravity), which requires materials to have

In the process of designing novel Ni-based turbine disc superalloys, 
one of the most crucial requirements is raising the yield strength, 
which is essential to develop engines with high thrust-weight

High-performance Ni-based turbine disc superalloys have al- 
ways been the focus of development. Over the latter part of the 
twentieth century, a concerted development of composition and 
process dramatically promoted performance to be raised. The pro- 
cess, technology from wrought and precision casting of turbine 
discs to directional solidiﬁcation and single crystal technology of 
turbine blades, has provided effective support. Since the origi- 
nal resistance-oxidation of 80Ni20Cr, an effective way to improve 
the compositions is the application of alloying elements; e.g., Ni- 
monic80A was ﬁrst developed by adding Al and Ti to form γ’- 
Ni 3 (Al, Ti) to strengthen the γ-(Ni, Cr) matrix [ 13 , 14 ]. The current 
superalloys have simultaneously added alloying elements above 8 
species, to improve solid solution strengthening (Cr, Co, Mo, W, Re , 
and Ru), precipitate strengthening (Al, Ti, Nb, and Ta), and grain 
boundary strengthening (C, B, Zr, and Hf) [15] . However, too many 
alloying elements easily bring about unstable microstructures, e.g., 
forming deleterious phases, which requires optimizing the element 
concentration. As Reed et al. stressed, controlling 8 elements in the 
range of 0–10 wt% using a resolution of 1 wt% will have 10 8 al- 
loys to be prepared, tested and ranked [16] . Clearly, the empiric 
repeated test is awful weak when facing the huge composition 
space. Therefore, a vital point is how to balance the complex al- 
loying chemistry to ensure microstructure stability without losing 
strength [17] . In 1964, William and Slaney primarily proposed the 
PHACOMP method to evaluate the precipitate trend of the deleteri- 
ous σ phase [18] , which pioneers the application of computational 
design in the development of superalloys. Nevertheless, the PHA- 
COMP method gives no information on temperature-dependant sta- 
bility or phase boundaries, especially for other topologically close- 
packed phases except σ, e.g., μ and Laves. A milestone of super- 
alloy design is the application of CALPHAD to bridge the temper- 
ature and chemistry to phases by rapid and reliable calculation of 
phase equilibrium [13] . The turbine disc superalloy RR10 0 0 is the 
typical and classic case using CALPHAD to design a new superal- 
loy [19] . In 2009, Reed et al. summarized the CALPHAD and the- 
oretical model and proposed the ‘alloys-by-design’ method to de- 
sign superalloys with property trade-offs using theory-based com- 
putational modelling to replace trial-and-error tests [16] . Using the 
alloys-by-design method, turbine disc alloys (ABD-DX and ABD- 
DXI) [20] , single crystal blade alloys (ABD-1, ABD-2, and ABD-3) 
[20] , and additively manufactured alloys (ABD-850AM and ABD- 
900AM) [21] were designed with excellent properties. Therefore, 
computational modelling has become a crucial and effective way 
to design superalloys. However, the computational design still has 
a grand challenge—the usability of modelling on mechanical prop- 
erties (e.g., yield strength). The theoretical models constituted by 
the measured parameters show accurate prediction of the yield 
strength [22–25] . However, it is scarcely possible to scan the whole 
composition space to obtain all parameters; the experiment-based 
models only consider simplifying the parameters to predict the 
tendency [26] . 
Data-driven methods have gained great attention in accelerat- 
ing material design [ 27 , 28 ]. With the arrival of the big data era, 
the mode that combines big data and artiﬁcial intelligence is called 
the ‘fourth paradigm’ of science [29] , also known as the ‘fourth 
industrial revolution’ [30] . Compared with experimental methods, 
machine learning has the ability to automatically build models 
to achieve predictions of material properties. In 2016, Raccuglia 
et al. performed machine learning to analyse failed experiments 
to achieve new material discovery [31] , which proves that ma- 
chine learning has extraordinary potential in new material design. 
In addition, high-throughput calculations (ﬁrst-principles calcula- 
tions [32] and CALPHAD [13] ) are also introduced as eﬃcient ways 
for the Materials Genome Initiative to accelerate the development 
of novel materials [33] . Thereafter, data-driven materials design 
is also raised to a new height [34–38] . In the matter of super- 
alloys, the data-driven method has shown an accurate and eﬃ- 
cient ability to reduce trial-and-error tests [39] . Machine learn- 
ing can successfully achieve the improvement of a single target 
via adaptive design based on an eﬃcient global optimization al- 
gorithm [40] or automatic optimization design based on a genetic 
algorithm [11] . For the balanced design of multiple targets, ma- 
chine learning within uncertainty also shows potential and effec- 
tiveness [ 41 , 42 ]. Even for the microstructural characteristics of su- 
peralloys, deep learning [43] , text mining [44] , and symbolic re- 
gression [45] have been applied to reveal the physical mechanism. 
Meanwhile, high-throughput ﬁrst-principle calculations have been 
used to search for new ternary superalloys [46] ; high-throughput 
calculations of CALPHAD have been performed to screen the su- 
peralloys that are reinforced by γ’ + γ’’ [47] . With the contin- 
uous improvement of the thrust-weight ratio of aeroengines, the 
service environment is more severe, and the requirements of tem- 
perature bearing capacity and mechanical properties are also in- 
creasing. The data-driven method will become an effective support 
for the rapid design of novel Ni-based turbine disc superalloys by 
balancing multiple properties. 
Alloying is an effective way to improve properties by manipu- 
lating the microstructure. Especially by multicomponent alloying, 
the superimposition of the alloying effect shows advantages in im- 
proving properties [48] . Therefore, selecting elements that have a 
strengthening effect is vital. However, in traditional cases of super- 
alloy design, there is always a premise that the selection of alloy- 
ing elements relies on experimental experience, which leads to the 
maximum superimposition effect being diﬃcult to ﬁnd. For exam- 
ple, the experimental accuracy of alloying planar defects depends 
heavily on the type of elasticity theory applied [49] . Another factor 
is that alloying experimental effects are easily affected by chemical 
concentration. The ﬁrst-principle calculations can independently 
describe the alloying effect for each alloying element without be- 
ing affected by external factors. Each element that can improve 
precipitate strengthening can be determined by ﬁrst-principle cal- 
culations. However, ﬁrst-principle calculations can only determine 
elements and cannot analyse the phase fraction of a speciﬁc al- 
loy composition. The composition space of the determined element 
system can be very large, but many compositions do not form the 
γ + γ’ structure of superalloys, which can be solved by CALPHAD. 
CALPHAD can analyse the phase fraction under a determined com- 
position and temperature to screen alloys that have a γ + γ’ struc- 
ture. Nevertheless, both ﬁrst-principle calculations and CALPHAD 
have diﬃculty predicting the yield strength. Although the uniaxial 
strain by ﬁrst-principle calculations [50] or the theoretical model 
by CALPHAD [11] are used to analyse strength, the accuracy is in- 
suﬃcient for new alloy design. Therefore, machine learning is used 
to predict yield strength, relying on the strong ability of regular 
exploration. Machine learning makes up for the problem of theo- 
retical models in which many experimental parameters need to be 
measured. 
In the present work, a cross-scale data-driven procedure was 
performed to design superalloys with high yield strength at 850 °C. 
Fig. 1 is the workﬂow schematic of the data-driven design. First- 
principles calculations were primarily performed to determine su- 
peralloy systems with improved γ’ precipitate strengthening. Then, 
theoretical models and CALPHAD were used to screen superalloys 
through superalloy criteria. Next, machine learning and shear yield 
stress were applied to predict the yield strength. Finally, experi- 
mental veriﬁcation was carried out to assess the accuracy of the 
data-driven method.

2. Methodology 
In the process of superalloy design, ﬁrst-principles calculations, 
theoretical models, CALPHAD, and machine learning were per- 
formed. To clearly show all calculations, the nomenclature of the 
calculation parameters is listed in the Supplementary 1. 
2.1. System selected by ﬁrst-principles calculations

2.1.3. APB and SISF energies 
The planar defects in the microstructure of superalloys have 
vital importance for their properties. The typical planar de- 
fects include APB and SISF. Forming an APB is the condition in 
which a /2 [ ¯1 01 ] dislocation passes through γ/ γ’; therefore, another 
a /2 [ ¯1 01 ] dislocation is needed to pass through γ/ γ’ to recover the 
structure [10] . The structural energy of the APB directly affects the 
shear yield stress. A superalloy has a high E APB with a strong shear 
yield stress. The forming mechanical of SISF is a /3 [ ¯1 ¯1 2 ] entering 
γ’ from γ, leaving SISF behind and leading to a /6 [ ¯1 ¯1 2 ] in γ/ γ’. 
Reducing the SISF energy can abate the creep rate due to hinder- 
ing cross slip [55] . Overall, systems with high APB energy and low 
SISF energy beneﬁt the development of high-strength superalloys.

2.1.4. Calculation details 
A model was built by a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell, containing 32 
atoms. All calculations were performed with the Vienna Ab ini- 
tio Simulation Package [ 56 , 57 ] using a projector augmented wave 
pseudopotential. The cutoff energy was 500 eV, and then Bril- 
louin zone sampling used a 6 × 6 × 6 k -point mesh according 
to Monbhorst-Pack theory [58] . The energy convergence of elec- 
tronic self-consistency was 5 × 10 −7 eV/atom. Ionic relaxation was 
ﬁnished when forces on all of the atoms were below 0.01 eV/ ˚A. 
A convergence test was performed to keep the energy ﬂuctuation 
within 0.01 eV/atom and the lattice parameter ﬂuctuation within 
0.001 ˚A. Knowing that the Curie temperature of Ni 3 Al is 41.5 K 
(ferromagnetic transition to paramagnetic) [59] , the doping of TM 
elements may lead Ni 3 Al to paramagnetic at 0 K and even keep 
ferromagnetic at a high temperature; thus, the spin-polarized cal- 
culation was added into each system.

2.2.3. Processability 
The basic processability that should be considered includes 
plasticity and quench cracks. Some superalloys need to undergo 
rolling or wrought procedures, which requires superalloys to be 
plastic. In the Ni matrix, the concentrations of bcc-structure W 
and Mo affect plasticity, and increasing concentrations lead to a 
decrease in plasticity [12] . However, the total amount of Mo + W 
plays a vital role in improving strength [20] . Reed et al. proposed 
that the concentration of Mo + W can be 2 at.% −4 at.% to en- 
sure mechanical properties and avoid deleterious phases when the 
alloy has the high oxidation-resistant element Cr [20] . Guo also 
proposed that an alloy system may exist in the α-W phase when 
Mo + W > 4 at% [12] . Thus, the concentration of Mo + W was 
limited in the range of 2–4 at%. The other factor is quench crack, 
which is affected by T solvus and solidus temperature ( T solidus ) [68] . 
Supersolvus treatment is performed in a single γ phase to elim- 
inate primary γ’, a region from T solvus to T solidus ; therefore, rais- 
ing T solvus means increasing the supersolvus treatment tempera-

2.3.4. Uncertainty analysis improving extrapolations ability 
It is necessary to develop a method to improve the extrapola- 
tion ability in the process of alloy screening because most of the 
predicted alloys are far from the training dataset. Here, a method 
based on a normal distribution was proposed to analyse the un- 
certainty of extrapolation prediction [ 41 , 42 , 74 ]. The uncertainty, in 
other words, is the probability that the predicted value is located 
in the region that satisﬁes the target. Clearly, the regular distri- 
bution of big data accords with a normal distribution. However, 
a typical characteristic of material data is a small sample. An ef- 
fective method to break the limit of small sample is using the 
bootstrap method [75] , which adopts random sampling with repo- 
sitioning to create multiple analogue datasets to obtain a probabil- 
ity distribution. Fig. 5 shows the procedure of uncertainty analy- 
sis. Primarily, the D -size training dataset was subjected to boot- 
strap sampling N times, obtaining N datasets (the size of each new 
dataset was still d ). Then, the selected algorithm was used in N 
datasets to train N models. The explored alloy composition A was 
predicted in N datasets to obtain N predicted values. When N is 
suﬃciently large, the predicted values can be regarded as normally 
distributed; therefore, the mean and standard deviation can be cal- 
culated. Then, the probability that alloy A was more than the tar- 
get, namely, the area on the right side of the target value, can be 
calculated. The properties of the explored alloys can be selected by 
comparing the probability of each alloy. The design lower limit of 
the yield strength at 850 °C was placed at 700 MPa.

2.4. Experimental procedures 
A 50 g ingot sample was prepared by smelting 99.99% pure 
metals under the protection of high-purity argon; meanwhile, the 
proportioning error of each element was less than ± 0.2 at%. The 
solution heat treatment was carried out using 1160 °C/24 h/air 
cooling; then, the ageing treatment was made using 850 °C/8 h/air 
cooling. After polishing down the sample surfaces through 30 0 0- 
grit grinding paper, the samples were etched electrolytically in an 
alcoholic solution of 20 vol.% perchloric acid and 10 vol% glycerol. 
Grain size was counted through optical microscopy using the inter- 
cept method. Scanning electron microcopy (SEM) was performed to 
observe the microstructure, and then the volume fraction of γ’ and 
the size of γ’ were obtained by thresholding the SEM images. To 
measure T solvus and T solidus , differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) 
was performed using a heating rate of 10 °C/min. X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) was carried out with Cu K α radiation and a scanning speed 
of 2 °/min, to examine the phase stability. Compression tests were 
performed to measure 0.2% proof strength with a strain rate of 0.01 
s − 1 at 800 °C, 850 °C, and 10 0 0 °C. The density was measured via 
the Archimedes method.

4. Discussion 
For the application of turbine discs, superalloys must main- 
tain completeness with the assaults of centrifugal force and high- 
temperature airﬂow [12] . However, the service conditions of tur- 
bine discs are complex, embodied in the distribution difference of 
stress and temperature from hub to rim [83] . Thus, for the design 
of new superalloys, the overarching target is raising the upper limit 
of high-temperature mechanical properties; meanwhile, the ability 
to resist degradation, e.g., corrosion and oxidation, is equally sig- 
niﬁcant for maintaining completeness. To further comprehend the 
increase in performance, the strengthening mechanism, machine 
learning process, and alloying chemistry were discussed to guide 
the design of high-strength turbine disc polycrystalline Ni-based 
superalloys.

5. Conclusions 
A data-driven design method has been proposed, by which Ni- 
based turbine disc superalloys can be optimized to raise the yield 
strength. The following conclusions can be drawn from the present 
work: 
(1) Data-driven design was demonstrated to have utility in im- 
proving the yield strength of Ni-based turbine disc super- 
alloys. Only 14 compositions remained from 2864,039 com- 
positions, with 99.99995% of the compositions rejected. The 
yield strength at 850 °C is higher in the experimental XS 
alloy than in commercial IN10 0, Waspaloy, Udimet50 0, and 
Inconel713C. All requirement parameters of the XS alloy ac- 
cord with the design targets. 
(2) The Ni-Al-Co-Cr-Ti-Nb-Ta-Mo-W system selected by ﬁrst- 
principles calculations is proven to be a potential system 
with high yield strength. Beneﬁtting from Ta, Nb, and Ti on 
the improvement of V γ ’ and E APB , precipitate shearing has 
the highest contribution of 560.3 MPa for the XS alloy. The 
solid solution elements of Co, Cr, Mo, and W also provide a 
yield strength strengthening contribution of 157.4 MPa. The 
system can develop further new superalloys. 
(3) The combination of CALPHAD and the theoretical model 
shows eﬃcient and precise screening ability. Although the 
accuracies of the CALPHAD database and theoretical model 
control the quality of the design result, the simple and quick 
method can remove the obvious errors of traditional statis- 
tical methods. 
(4) The extrapolation prediction accuracy of machine learning is 
improved via feature transformation and uncertainty analy- 
sis. The key factors affecting yield strength were identiﬁed, 
by which the extrapolation ability of the superalloy features 
model is improved toward an R 2 of 0.68 compared with 
an R 2 of 0.25 for the composition/heat treatment model. 
The uncertainty analysis promotes the extrapolation accu- 
racy from 60% to 80%, which tremendously raises the accu- 
racy of ﬁnding high yield strength in the huge composition 
space. 
(5) The date-driven method can be used in different limits to 
satisfy various application situations. The application con- 
cept of date-driven design is perceived as a tendency to re- 
duce trial-and-error costs and then accelerate development 
of Ni-based turbine disc superalloys.
