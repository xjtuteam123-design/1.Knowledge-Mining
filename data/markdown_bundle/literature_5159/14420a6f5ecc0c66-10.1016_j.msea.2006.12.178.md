Effect of Mo on mechanical properties and
microstructure of Nb–Ti–C alloys
Rengen Ding, Huisheng Jiao 1, Ian P. Jones∗
Department of Metallurgy and Materials, University of Birmingham, Birmingham B15 2TT, UK
Received 6 June 2006; received in revised form 21 November 2006; accepted 5 December 2006
Abstract
The microstructures and mechanical properties of Nb–Ti–C alloys containing different Mo contents have been investigated. The alloy without
Mo contains Nb solid solution (Nbss), (Ti,Nb) 2C//H9252(Nbss) eutectic, (Ti,Nb) 2C and (Ti,Nb)C. The addition of Mo restrains the precipitation of
(Ti,Nb)C. Compression testing has been carried out at room temperature and 1473 K. The alloys showed good ductility at room temperature and
Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo produced a 0.2% offset yield stress of 300 MPa at 1473 K. The room temperature and high temperature strengths increase with
increasing Mo, but the addition of Mo decreases the room temperature ductility.

1. Introduction
Niobium-based alloys are one of the most promising refrac-
tory alloys for high temperature structural application in place
of Ni-based superalloys. Niobium has a very high melting point
of 2741 K, a relatively low density (8.57 g/cm
3) and good duc-
tility at room temperature. However, the strength of niobium
decreases substantially at temperatures above 1200 K. Various
approaches have hence been used to improve the strength of Nb
at elevated temperature, including solid solution strengthening
with Mo, W, Ta and Hf[1] and dispersion or composite strength-
ening with intermetallic compounds such as Nb
3Al and Nb5Si
[2], as well as with a carbide phase [3,4]. Jiao et al. [4] deter-
mined that the addition of TiC to Nb-based alloys increases the
strength at room and elevated temperatures, but that the strength
of simple Nb–Ti–C alloys at elevated temperature is not ade-
quate. Solid solution strengthening is also necessary. Mo appears
to be the most promising element for solid solution strength-
ening because Mo not only improves the strength of Nb, but
also does not easily form carbide. Therefore, the microstructures
and mechanical properties of Nb–Ti–C alloys with different Mo
contents have been investigated in this study.

2. Experimental procedure
Three alloys with nominal compositions Nb20Ti12.5C,
Nb20Ti12.5C10Mo and Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo (at.%) were pre-
pared by arc-melting and casting into buttons. Conventional
constant strain rate deformation with a strain rate of about 10
−4/s
was performed on all three as-cast alloys. High temperature com-
pression was carried out under vacuum. TEM foils were prepared
by electropolishing and examined in an FEI Tecnai F20. Various
phases’ compositions were measured using energy-dispersive
spectrometry (EDS) in transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
and wavelength-dispersive spectrometry (WDS) in scanning
electron microscopy (SEM).

3.2. Mechanical tests
Vickers hardness tests showed that the hardness increases
with increasing Mo content ( Table 2 ). The compression
stress–strain curves of the three alloys at room temperature
(Fig. 4 ) show that the addition of Mo improved signiﬁcantly
the yield strength of the alloys but decreased the ductility. The
0.2% offset strength of Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo is about 1000 MPa
at room temperature. The compression tests were interrupted for
the alloys containing Mo which failed when the strain reached
around 25%. Since the addition of Mo did not much change
the volume fractions of carbides, no Mo was detected in the
carbide and no Mo-rich phase was observed, the improve-
ment of strength in the alloys is mainly attributed to solid
solution strengthening by Mo. The effect of solid solution hard-
ening on the yield strength, σ, of the material is given by
σ = 2.5μ(δ)
4/3c [6]. where μ is the shear modulus (38 GPa
for Nb at room temperature), δ is a misﬁt parameter and c
is solute concentration. The misﬁt parameter δ is deﬁned as
δ = (1/a)(∂a/∂c) (5.81% for Nb–Mo binary alloys [7]) where
a is the lattice parameter of Nb. Therefore, the predicted val-
ues of solid solution hardening for 10 and 20 at.% Mo in Nb
alloys at room temperature are 215 and 430 MPa, respectively.
This is supported by the experimental results (300 and 420 MPa)
which are again in agreement with the study of Tan et al.
[1].

3.3. Deformation microstructure
After 30% compression at room temperature, the microstruc-
ture was examined using TEM. A high density of dislocations
can still be seen in the Nbss matrix ( Fig. 5 ), which endured
large scale deformation. The carbides in these alloys also expe-
rienced considerable deformation in compression. An image of
a deformed (Ti,Nb)C carbide is shown in the inset to Fig. 5.
A high density of lines is observed inside the carbide. Streaks
perpendicular to these features are clearly seen in the diffraction
pattern (inset to Fig. 5). These lines represent a high density of
microtwins.
Fig. 5. Bright ﬁeld TEM image obtained using g = 110 from Nb20Ti12.5C after
30% compression. A high density of dislocations is evident. Insets show the
defects in a deformed carbide and the SADP obtained from the deformed carbide.
4. Conclusions
Based on an investigation of the microstructure and mechan-
ical tests of Nb–Ti–C alloys with various Mo contents:
(i) Nb20Ti12.5C consists of Nb solid solution (Nbss),
(Ti,Nb)2C//H9252(Nbss) eutectic, primary irregularly shaped
(Ti,Nb)2C carbide and secondary needle shaped (Ti,Nb)C
carbide. In the alloys with Mo, no secondary (Ti,Nb)C
carbide was observed.
(ii) The 0.2% offset strength increases with increasing Mo
content, whereas the addition of Mo decreases the ductil-
ity. Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo appears to possess substantial high
temperature strength (300 MPa).
(iii) After deformation, a high density of dislocations was
observed in Nbss with a high density of microtwins in the
carbides.
