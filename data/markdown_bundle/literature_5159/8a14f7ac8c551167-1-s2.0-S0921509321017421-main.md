ABSTRACT 
Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) are increasingly attractive for potential high temperature applications. 
To further optimize the mechanical properties, here we introduce intermetallic silicides into a RHEA 
(TaMo
0.5
NbZrTi
1.5
Al
0.1
) via Si alloying. Interdendritic eutectics composed of body-center cubic (bcc) matrix and 
hexagonal silicides are formed after solidification. The alloying of Si facilitates substantial grain refinement and 
formation of intergranular silicides upon annealing at 1300 
◦
C for 48 h. Owing to such microstructure modifi -
cations, the compressive strengths of the Si-containing alloy at room and elevated (1200 
◦
C) temperatures are 
effectively improved. The alloying of Si also promotes transgranular fracture upon compressive testing at room 
temperature. Dynamic recrystallization (DRX) is evidently accelerated in the Si-containing alloy during 
compression at 1200 
◦
C, and necklace-like microstructures are generated with the formation of small-sized grains 
along original grain boundaries. The work suggests that Si-alloying can be an effective approach for significantly 
enhancing strength and deformation compatibility of RHEAs at room and elevated temperatures by enabling the 
formation of fine silicides and the additional solute effects. 
1. Introduction 
One effective way to improve the Carnot efficiency of jet turbine 
engines is to increase the operating temperature of hot-section compo -
nents such as turbine blades [ 1 ]. Current nickel-based superalloys used 
for turbine blades are reaching limits with service temperatures up to 
1150

fore, developing new high temperature materials beyond nickel-based 
superalloys remains a pressing issue for academic and industrial 
communities. 
The refractory high entropy alloy (RHEA) shows great potential in 
high temperature applications, which normally consists of refractory 
elements like W, Ta, Mo, Nb, Zr, etc., at equiatomic or near-equiatomic 
concentrations [ 3 – 6 ]. As a member of the high entropy alloy family, 
RHEA is designed according to the high con fi gurational entropy strat -
egy, and contains multiple principal elements in contrast to single 
principal element in conventional alloys [ 7 , 8 ]. The compositional space 
of RHEAs is thus extensively expanded. Since 2010, various RHEA 
compositions with body-center cubic (bcc) structures have been re -
ported, demonstrating exciting mechanical properties [ 9 ]. For example,

4. Discussion 
4.1. Role of Si in microstructural evolution 
Microstructural evolution in different conditions of RHEA and Si- 
RHEA are schematically shown in Fig. 15 . A dendritic microstructure 
with evident compositional segregation is formed in each experimental 
alloy after solidification. The compositional segregation of both RHEA 
and Si-RHEA is eliminated via annealing at 1300 
◦
C for 48 h with the 
elimination of dendritic microstructures. As a result, both RHEA and Si- 
RHEA achieve an increment of compressive strength after annealing 
treatment, while the fracture strain decreases. We find that the addition 
of Si fails to effectively refine the microstructure of the as-cast RHEA, as 
suggested by the slightly higher primary dendrite spacing. This finding 
differs from the observations shown in previous literature, where the 
microstructure is substantially refined by the addition of Si in the as-cast 
state [ 14 , 15 ]. Significant grain refinement, however, is achieved for the 
Si-containing alloy after annealing treatment. It indicates the addition of 
Si has a limited influence on nucleation behavior of refractory HEA 
during solidification in this work. Instead, its grain growth behavior is 
evidently affected by interdendritic silicides that effectively impede the 
migration of grain boundaries during annealing treatment. 
The alloying of Si also shows significant effect on the microstructure 
evolution during high temperature compressive deformation, e.g., pro -
moting the DRX behavior. DRX is not frequently observed in bcc crystals 
with a lower diffusivity than fcc crystals [ 28 , 29 ]. In this work, a few DRX 
grains and bulged grain boundaries are found in the compressed RHEA 
specimen at high temperature (1200 
◦
C), which can be considered as the 
initial process of DRX. In contrast, the Si-RHEA specimen is distin -
guished by the formation of necklace-like microstructures, revealing 
that DRX is substantially promoted by the addition of Si. The DRX 
mechanism of single-phase bcc refractory HEAs has been elaborated in 
previous literature [ 21 , 30 ], and therefore, we focus more on the DRX 
behavior of Si-containing refractory HEA. On one side, the addition of Si 
facilitates significant grain refinement in the annealed alloys. The DRX 
kinetics thus increases as more grain boundaries acting as preferred 
nucleation sites are provided [ 31 , 32 ]. Also, the stored energy during 
compression for the fine-grain Si-RHEA specimen is higher owing to an 
improved compressive strength, which can provide larger driving force 
for DRX [ 33 ]. 
On the other side, the second-phase silicides are introduced to pro -
mote DRX, mainly via a particle-stimulated-nucleation mechanism [ 34 , 
35 ]. It is worth noting that small-sized second phases can sustain grain 
boundary motion and decelerate DRX due to the Zener drag effect [ 36 ]. 
For the relatively coarse and non-deformable silicides with a particle 
width higher than 2 μ m ( Fig. 8 d), they can impede the migration of 
dislocations [ 37 ]. Consequently, local regions with an increased dislo -
cation density and strain accumulation are formed around intergranular 
silicides, together with higher misorientations. The 
particle-stimulated-nucleation mechanism is thus activated with the 
development of recrystallized nuclei [ 34 , 35 ]. 
The process of DRX includes growing grain boundary fl uctuations, 
developing serrations and bulges, and creating new grains along original 
grain boundaries. The initiation of grain boundary bulging can be 
explained by the strain induced grain boundary migration mechanism 
[ 21 , 27 ]. Eventually, necklace-like microstructures are formed in the 
Si-RHEA specimens after high temperature compression ( Fig. 15 ). 
4.2. Strengthening mechanisms mediated by Si alloying 
The alloying of Si introduces hexagonal silicides for Si-containing 
refractory HEA, which effectively enhances the compressive strengths 
at both room and high temperatures (1200 
◦
C), without substantial loss 
of fracture strain. Such a property improvement is closely associated 
with the microstructure modification arising from Si addition, involving 
the following strengthening mechanisms. 
Firstly, solid solution strengthening. HEAs that are composed of 
multi-principal elements with equimolar or near-equimolar ratios are

distinguished by severe lattice distortion with long-range lattice friction, 
due to the differences on atomic radii and related characteristics [ 38 ,

HEAs to interpret substitutional solid solution strengthening over a wide 
range of temperatures, including the dipole dragging model at low 
temperatures and the jog dragging model at high temperatures. In the 
present study, the added Si in the alloy mainly reacts with refractory 
elements such as Zr to form bcc/silicide eutectics, and the residual Si is 
partitioned into the bcc matrix phase. This is revealed by the decrease in 
the bcc crystal lattice parameter ( Fig. 1 ) and the experimental obser -
vations using APT ( Fig. 6 ). The Si atoms are substitutional rather than 
interstitial, as the difference of atomic radius between Si and refractory 
atoms is limited. As a result of the corresponding severe lattice distor -
tion, the Si solutes enhance the frictional resistance to dislocation mo -
tion by anchoring them in their glide, and effective strengthening is thus 
enabled [ 41 , 42 ]. 
Secondly, fine grain strengthening. The addition of Si to RHEA 
contributes to a significant grain refinement upon annealing at 1300 
◦
C, 
with the grain size decreasing from ~236 μ m to ~43 μ m ( Fig. 8 ). The 
amount of grain boundary in the Si-RHEA specimen thus increases, 
acting as obstacles to dislocation motion and forming dislocation pile- 
ups at grain boundaries [ 43 , 44 ]. Higher stress is required for the slip 
of dislocations in the finer grains, as described by the Hall-Petch relation 
[ 45 ]. In addition, grain refinement contributes to the reduction of 
dislocation slip distances, which promotes grain rotation and grain 
boundary movement during high temperature compressive deforma -
tion. This suggests that the deformation compatibility of grains is 
improved for the annealed Si-RHEA, which explains the absence of grain 
boundary cracking after high temperature compression relative to the 
annealed Si-free RHEA ( Fig. 13 ). Besides, the fine grain strengthening 
effect is likely to be weakened at high temperatures, compared with that 
at room temperature, due to the aforementioned mechanisms including 
grain rotation and grain boundary movement under high temperature 
deformation. 
Thirdly, second-phase strengthening. The silicides in Si-RHEA act to 
impede dislocation motion to obtain higher compressive strength. 
Particularly, synergetic strengthening can be enabled by intergranular 
silicides and grain boundaries. The rationale to combine metals with 
intermetallic or ceramic phases has extensively utilized for enhanced 
strength, especially at high temperatures [ 15 , 46 , 47 ]. Owing to the high 
hardness and thermal stability, intermetallic silicides are less softened 
compared with bcc phases at high temperature, and the compressive 
strength is thus improved for the Si-RHEA specimen [ 15 ]. 
In addition to the strengthening effects, transgranular fracture is also 
mediated by Si addition, rather than intergranular fracture for Si-free 
RHEA ( Figs. 12 and 15 ). It thus indicates the improvement of grain 
boundary strength by intergranular silicides.
