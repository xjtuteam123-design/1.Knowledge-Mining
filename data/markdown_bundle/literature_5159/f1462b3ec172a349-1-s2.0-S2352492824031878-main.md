ABSTRACT
The thermal tensile behavior of the annealed Mg-3Gd-0.5Zn alloy, containing lamellar (long-period stacking 
ordered, LPSO) phases, was investigated at the same strain rate (2.2 × 10 ⁻⁴ s ⁻ ¹) under different temperatures 
(100
◦
C – 200
◦
C). The results showed that the ultimate tensile strength decreased with increasing temperature, 
while the fracture elongation increased. At all three tensile temperatures, the alloy exhibit no dynamic recrys -
tallization behavior.Before deformation, the sample displayed no obvious texture. After hot tensile deformation, 
the alloy exhibited fiber texture characteristics. The relatively weak [0001]//ND texture transformed into a 
stronger [10]//ED fiber texture. Although clear kinking and bending of lamellar LPSO phases were observed 
within the non-dynamic recrystallized grains, the dense arrangement of the LPSO phases limited space for 
dislocation movement, suppressing dynamic recrystallization. The lamellar LPSO phases parallel to the (0001) 
plane inhibited basal slip.Although numerous low angle grain boundaries are formed within the microstructure, 
the lower temperature prevents the occurrence of continuous dynamic recrystallization behavior, resulting in the 
accumulation of a large amount of stress. The high-temperature deformation mechanism of the alloy is mainly 
based on prismatic and pyramidal slip, synergistically coupled with LPSO kinks and {11 20} tensile twinning.
1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys offer significant potential across diverse domains, 
including biomedicine, aerospace, and military applications, owing to 
their remarkable attributes of superior specific strength, reduced den -
sity, and exceptional fatigue resistance [1 – 3] . Limited room-temperature 
formability restricts their application range. This is because the hcp 
crystal structure of magnesium with close-packed planes provides very 
limited slip systems that activate during room-temperature deformation, 
mainly relying on {0001} < 11 – 20 > basal slip and 
{10 12} < -1011 > twinning [4 – 6] . Generally, adding rare earth ele -
ments serves as an effective way to improve the formability of magne -
sium alloys. Studies show that the addition of rare earth elements 
modifies the texture structure of magnesium alloys, reduces the critical 
resolved shear stress (CRSS) of non-basal slip, and thereby enhances 
their formability [7] . The researchers found that Mg-Gd alloy experi -
enced excellent plasticity after extrusion deformation, which can be 
attributed to the formation of rare earth texture extrusion deformation 
[8,9] . The addition of RE-elements can form a < 11 – 21 > parallel to the 
extrusion direction (ED) rare-earth texture, which makes the orientation 
of a large number of grains more susceptible to activating basal slip, thus 
improving the plasticity of the alloy.
Mg-Gd based alloys demonstrate the ability to precipitate the 
β -Mg5Gd phase, showcasing remarkable strength and outstanding 
thermal stability. The β phase can impede the movement of dislocations 
and strengthen grain boundaries at high temperatures, thereby 
improving the high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy
[10 – 12] .The precipitation sequence of phases in Mg-Gd based alloys is

The addition of Zn can induce the formation of long-period stacking 
ordered (LPSO) phases in Mg-Gd alloys. The morphology of the LPSO 
phase (block or lamellar) is determined by different cooling rates and 
alloy compositions [19] . Some studies have indicated that LPSO phases 
significantly affect recrystallization behavior: block LPSO phases pro -
mote dynamic recrystallization (DRX), while lamellar LPSO phases 
inhibit DRX [19 – 21] .
Extensive research focuses on the deformation behavior of Mg-Gd-Zn 
alloys that incorporate LPSO at ambient temperature, but their defor -
mation mechanism at high temperatures receives less investigation, and 
the role of LPSO phases in the hot stretching process remains unclear.In 
this paper, the thermal tensile behavior of Mg-3Gd-0.5Zn (wt%) alloy 
containing LPSO phases was studied by Electron Back Sctarrer Diffrac -
tion (EBSD) technique. In addition, the addition of Gd to the alloy is 
aimed at refining the grain size and delaying the growth of DRX grains 
during deformation, thereby refining the grain size of the deformed state 
alloy [22] . Previous studies have focused on increasing the content of Gd 
and improving the high-temperature properties of alloys by precipita -
tion strengthening, which greatly increases the cost and loses its light -
weight advantages. This study aims to improve the high temperature 
performance of LPSO-reinforced magnesium alloys under the condition 
of low alloying, and point out the direction for expanding the applica -
tion of magnesium alloys.

abundant inside the deformed grains, and their arrangement aligns with

3.2. High temperature mechanical properties of the alloys
Fig. 4 (a) depicts the true stress-strain curves of the alloy at different 
temperatures. It is apparent that the curves exhibit a significant negative 
temperature effect, where the peak stress decreases with increasing 
temperature. The deformation behavior of the alloy at all four temper -
atures can be classified into three stages: 1. Elastic deformation stage, 
where the applied force causes an increase in lattice spacing within the 
alloy, resulting in a volume increase without any change in the unit 
cell ’ s shape, ultimately leading to a rapid rise in stress up to the elastic 
limit. 2. Work hardening stage, where dislocations rapidly proliferate 
after the elastic deformation stage, resulting in a continuous increase in 
stress. As the strain further increases, the softening effect due to dislo -
cation annihilation gradually balances with the work hardening effect, 
leading to the peak stress. 3. Necking deformation stage, where some 
defects within the sample act as crack initiation points, and as the strain 
increases, cracks continuously propagate, resulting in necking and 
eventual fracture. [25,26] . The yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, 
and elongation of the alloy at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 4
(b). It can be seen that the peak stress of the alloy decreases with 
increasing temperature, while the fracture elongation increases with 
increasing temperature. This is because higher temperatures provide 
more energy to reduce the interatomic bonding force and increase the 
atomic vibration amplitude, resulting in a decrease in deformation 
resistance and making dislocations more likely to occur. The disloca -
tions accumulate near the grain boundary, and the higher temperature 
makes the dislocations induce the dynamic recovery, which is consistent 
with the curve [27,28] .

Fig. 8 (a)-(f) show the pole figures (PFs) and inverse pole figures

that the texture strength of the deformed microstructure is higher than

pole density points arranged along the TD direction, whereas for the 
(11 20) and (10 10) pole figures, the pole density points are endpoints

maximum pole density point of the (0001) PF being only 2.84 mrd. This 
indicates that the basal fiber texture of the alloy formed when the grains 
rotated under tensile stress parallel to the ED direction during the hot 
stretching deformation process. The activation of the non-basal slip

reason for texture strengthening.In addition, the IPFs of the deformed 
samples exhibited a significant fiber texture in the // [10] direction. The 
lower deformation temperature prevented the sample from undergoing 
dynamic recovery (DRV) during the stretching process, which further 
contributed to texture strengthening [37] .

Fig. 12 illustrates a schematic diagram of the LPSO kink strength -
ening mechanism in the alloy. The dense intragranular lamellar LPSO 
generates a large number of kink bands as deformation occurs, as shown 
in the upper right of the figure. The presence of these kink bands divides 
a grain into multiple substructures. The existence of these substructures 
effectively divides the grain, and dislocations accumulate significantly 
at the boundaries of the kink bands, hindering the movement of dislo -
cations and effectively strengthening the alloy. Additionally, the 
deformation of LPSO causes significant stress concentration at the 
boundary with the a-Mg matrix, promoting the activation of slip sys -
tems. The resulting dislocations are then obstructed by the boundaries of 
the kink bands, making it difficult for them to move, further strength -
ening the alloy.
4. Conclusion
The extruded and annealed Mg-3Gd-0.5Zn alloy with a lamellar 
LPSO phase exhibited a decrease in peak stress and an increase in 
fracture elongation as temperature increased, but no dynamic recrys -
tallization (DRX) occurred during tensile testing at elevated tempera -
ture. Initially, the alloy display limit texture, but during hot 
deformation, it developed a basal fiber texture, with a transformation 
from a weaker [0001]//ND texture to [10] //ED fiber texture. The pri -
mary deformation mechanisms at high temperatures involved slip on 
prism and pyramidal planes, LPSO kinking, and {11 20} tensile twin -
ning. Despite the formation of low-angle grain boundaries, stress accu -
mulation prevented continuous dynamic recovery at lower 
temperatures. The dense packing of lamellar LPSO phase further limited 
dislocation rearrangement, preventing DRX.
