ABSTRACT
Mo has received much attention in Ni-based single crystal superalloys as a main strengthening element at 
elevated temperatures above 1000 
◦
C. However, the effects of Mo addition in new generation single crystal 
superalloys at medium temperature conditions are still not clear. This paper investigated the creep property at 
900 
◦
C and 392 MPa in a 4th generation Ni-based single crystal superalloy with different Mo contents, indicating 
that Mo addition decreased the creep life and induced large amounts of microcracks. Higher Mo content could 
weaken the interface strength by inducing more stacking faults, causing crack initiation along the interface. The 
elemental segregation along the stacking faults triggered the nucleation of other precipitates, which could further 
lead to cracks. The precipitation of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases introduced by more Mo addition 
caused crack initiation along the interfaces between the TCP phases and γ ′ phase. By investigating the microcrack 
formation induced by Mo during creep at 900 
◦
C, we provided a new perspective for the alloy design.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys (Ni-based SCs) are essential ma -
terials for gas turbine blades since they possess outstanding mechanical 
performance at elevated temperatures [ 1 ]. The mechanical properties 
could be improved by solution strengthening in the matrix and precip -
itation strengthening. The widely used strengthening elements include 
Re, Mo, W and Ta [ 2 – 5 ]. Mo is one of the main solution-strengthening 
elements that mainly partitions in the γ phase [ 6 , 7 ]. The Mo addition 
significantly increases the magnitude of lattice misfit between γ phase 
and γ ′ phase [ 8 ]. Large lattice misfit leads to a denser interfacial dislo -
cation network and promotes the rafting process during creep defor -
mation at high temperatures such as 1100 
◦
C, enhancing the creep 
resistance [ 9 , 10 ]. However, Mo significantly promotes the formation of 
topologically close-packed (TCP) phases at elevated temperatures, 
which consumes massive solution-strengthening elements and causes 
local stress concentration [ 11 – 13 ]. Therefore, controlling the content of 
Mo is vital to balance the positive and negative effects.
The temperature range for the turbine blade service roughly lies 
700 – 1100 
◦
C, and the stress range is approximately 100~700 MPa 
[ 14 – 17 ], which requires excellent mechanical properties under different 
conditions. It seems that the creep at mediate temperatures receives less 
attention compared to the creep at elevated temperatures above 
1000 
◦
C. The research on the influence of Mo content on the creep at 
mediate temperatures of new generations Ni-based SCs is rarely re -
ported. It is not clear whether the strengthening effects of Mo at elevated 
temperatures stated above still work effectively at mediate 
temperatures.
This paper investigated the effect of Mo on the creep performance of 
a 4th generation Ni-based SC at 900 
◦
C and 392 MPa, emphasizing the 
mechanisms of microcrack formation induced by Mo addition. Mo 
addition strikingly decreased the creep life monotonically from 2 wt% to 
4 wt%, which was inconsistent with the role of Mo as a strengthening 
element at elevated temperatures. Mo promoted microcracks in the

2.2. Creep tests and microstructural observation
Creep rupture tests were carried out at 900 
◦
C and 392 MPa. The 
creep rupture test samples were mechanically machined to dumbbell 
shape with a gauge length of 25 mm and a diameter of 4 mm or 5 mm. 
Each investigated alloy with different Mo contents was tested to rupture 
at least twice at 900 
◦
C and 392 MPa to verify the experimental pattern. 
For a certain investigated alloy, the samples for repeated rupture tests 
were cut from different single crystal rods with slightly different devi -
ation degrees from [001] to ensure the reliability of the results. The 
interrupted creep tests were conducted under the same temperature and 
stress. When the interrupted tests stopped, the samples were cooled to 
room temperature under loading. After rupture, all the samples showed 
a typical necking phenomenon, indicating that the microstructure near 
the fracture might be different from that away from the fracture due to 
the stress concentration. The observation area for the microcracks 
formation was selected near the fracture surface.
Specimens for SEM observation were mechanically polished and 
etched chemically with a solution consisting of HF, HNO
3

grinding and polishing. Then the electron transparent foils were etched 
by the electrochemical double jet thinning method at a voltage of about 
15 V and temperature of about 30 
◦
C. The corresponding electrolyte 
was an ethanol solution containing 5 – 7 vol% HClO
4

high resolution observation were cut through focused ion beam (FIB) by 
Thermo Scientific Helios 5 CX. The TEM and scanning transmission 
electron microscope (STEM) pictures were mainly obtained by FEI 
Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN. The atomic configuration was obtained by 
Thermo Scientific Spectra 300 with high-resolution energy dispersive 
spectrometer.
2.3. Theoretical calculation methods
The theoretical calculations were performed in the framework of the 
density functional theory (DFT) with the projector augmented plane- 
wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation 
package [ 18 ]. The generalized gradient approximation proposed by 
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) was selected for the 
exchange-correlation potential [ 19 ]. The cut-off energy for plane wave 
was set to 480 eV and the Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid of 5 × 3 × 1 was 
adopted. The energy criterion is set to 10
 5 
eV in the iterative solution of 
the Kohn-Sham equation. All the structures were relaxed until the re -
sidual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.02 eV/Å. 
Moreover, the thermodynamic calculation of TCP phase precipitation in 
Section 4.4 was carried out on Thermo-Calc, using the database of 
TCNi9: Ni-Alloys v9.1.

The strain-time and the strain rate-time curve were plotted in Fig. 1 a 
and b, respectively. The creep process included a typical short primary 
stage, long steady stage and tertiary stage. At the end of the tertiary 
stage, the strain rates rapidly increased until rupture. 2Mo alloy 
exhibited the most extended creep life, which reached 1228 h. The creep 
life of 3Mo and 4Mo were 985 h and 903 h, respectively, indicating that 
increased Mo content decreased creep life at 900 
◦
C and 392 MPa. The 
result was strikingly inconsistent with the traditional characteristics of 
Mo as a strengthening element.

3.3. Microcrack initiation during loading
From the above results, one can judge that Mo addition led to the 
weakening of dendrite cores. However, it was hard to tell exactly how 
Mo addition degraded the creep property merely based on the above 
results. Therefore, different interrupt experiments were carried out to 
investigate how Mo influenced the microcracks formation.
3.3.1. The γ ′ / γ interface structure after loading
To further investigate the origin of these microcracks in the dendrite 
cores, the 3Mo sample was interrupted at the middle of the tertiary stage 
(800 h). Pores with spherical morphology appeared near the rafted γ ′ 
and γ phase interface in 3Mo alloy ( Fig. 3 a and b), which could act as 
crack initiation. The microcracks have not propagated significantly yet. 
The crack initiation location hinted that the crack initiation might be 
closely related to the γ ′ / γ interface structure evolution during external 
loading.
Fig. 4 displayed the distribution of refractory elements near the γ ′ / γ 
interface, pointing out that Re and Cr apparently segregated near the 
interfaces in the γ phase. Our previous study also indicated that re -
fractory elements, such as Re and Cr, segregated at the γ ′ / γ interface 
during the γ ′ coarsening without external loading [ 21 ]. Since Mo addi -
tion enhanced the partitioning behavior of Re and Cr, the amount of Re 
and Cr atoms that needed to diffuse away was relatively more in the high 
Mo content alloys. Hence, severe elemental segregation at the interface 
of the rafted structure was more likely to occur when Mo content 
increased [ 21 , 22 ].
The fine structure of γ ′ / γ interface was further characterized in Fig. 5 . 
Fig. 5 highlighted the stacking faults with a width of around 10 nm that 
emerged at the interface between γ ′ phase and both sphered and rafted γ 
phase, which were hard to discover by regular TEM methods. The 
stacking faults might be formed by the dissociation of < 110 > {111} 
dislocations in the γ phase [ 23 ]. After the stacking faults formed, the 
elemental segregation along the stacking faults indicated by the bright 
contrast ( Fig. 5 ) could prevent the interface from moving [ 24 , 25 ]. The 
high-resolution mapping of elemental distribution near the stacking 
fault in 3Mo alloy was exhibited in Fig. 6 , confirming the segregation of 
refractory elements. Therefore, the stacking faults pinned the interface 
after long-term microstructural evolution.
Moreover, when observing under the BSE mode of SEM, bright 
contrast near the pores in the interrupted 3Mo alloy indicated that the 
precipitates containing refractory elements also led to the formation of 
micropores ( Fig. 7 a). Fig. 7 b revealed the significant Re segregation of 
the precipitates near the pores, suggesting that the particles might be 
TCP phases or Re-rich δ phases reported by our previous study [ 26 ]. The 
precipitation of such phases might be related to the structural evolution 
and elemental segregation behavior at the γ ′ / γ interface during loading, 
which was further analyzed in Section 4 .
3.3.2. Precipitation of TCP phases
In Fig. 2 c, considerable microcracks existed near the TCP phases in 
4Mo alloy, indicating the detrimental effects of TCP phases. The EDS 
mapping results in Fig. 8 a indicated the segregation of Re, Mo and Cr in 
TCP phases. To explore the initiation of microcracks near the TCP 
phases, the 4Mo alloy was interrupted at 750 h (middle of the tertiary 
stage). The SEM image of microcrack initiation in the interrupted sam -
ples ( Fig. 8 b and c) showed that the crack formed and propagated along 
the interface of TCP phases (marked by green arrows), confirming that 
the TCP particles promoted the final failure. Moreover, in Fig. 8 d, no 
dislocation pile-up occurred near the TCP particle and few short dislo -
cations were discovered, indicating that TCP phases did not cause sig -
nificant stress concentration in 4Mo alloy. Furthermore, the TCP particle

Fig. 7 indicated that small-size precipitates might also lure the for -

4.2. Microcracks caused by massive TCP phases
Figs. 2 and 8 indicated that TCP phases were responsible for the large 
amount of microcracks in the dendrite cores of 4Mo alloy. Fig. 8 d sug -
gested that the microcracks near TCP particles were not attributed to the 
stress concentration. Furthermore, the corresponding lattice misfit be -
tween the TCP particle and γ ′ phase was calculated in Table 3 according 
to the orientation relationship between the P phase and γ ′ phase in 
Fig. 8 e. The magnitude of lattice misfit was large, hinting that the 
interface between the blocky TCP phase and γ ′ phase was not well 
coherent. The microcrack propagating along the interface between the 
TCP phase and γ ′ phase in Fig. 8 c confirmed that the crack is caused by 
the weak coherency of the interface rather than stress concentration. 
Recently, some researchers have pointed out that the phase trans -
formation (such as TCP phase formation) induced by Mo addition could 
effectively enhance the mechanical properties in some alloys [ 45 , 46 ]. 
However, in single crystal superalloys without grain boundaries, the 
formation of TCP phases acts differently. TCP precipitation consumes 
massive strengthening elements, and causes stress concentration or in -
duces weak interfaces in typical single crystal superalloys [ 47 – 49 ]. 
Therefore, TCP phases mainly play harmful roles in the mechanical 
properties of single crystal superalloys. Though dense dislocations 
appeared in γ phase of 4Mo, fewer superdislocations cut into γ ′ phase 
than 3Mo alloy and cracks along the γ / γ ′ interface were barely observed. 
The microcrack initiation might be mainly attributed to the interface 
between the TCP particles and γ ′ phase, probably weaker than the γ / γ ′ 
interfaces.
It was noteworthy that TCP phases only emerged in 4Mo alloy, rather 
than 2Mo and 3Mo alloy with longer creep lives. Such an interesting 
phenomenon raised the question of the Mo content threshold that trig -
gered the TCP precipitation at 900 
◦
C. Therefore, the thermodynamic 
calculation was conducted by Thermo-Calc to investigate the relation -
ship between the volume fraction of the TCP phase and Mo content at 
900 
◦
C. The results in Fig. 11 indicated that no TCP phases existed when 
Mo content was under 3.23 wt%, which was consistent with the 
microstructural observation. Hence, the Mo content should be 
controlled below ~3.23 wt% to avoid the precipitation of TCP phases, 
providing a reference for the alloy design. Besides the fact that Mo is one

[ 6 , 21 ]. Such enhancement in partitioning behavior by Mo addition 
benefits the formation of TCP phases. Furthermore, Mo promotes the 
precipitation of μ phase and P phase in particular, especially P phase at 
relatively high temperatures [ 3,4,50 ], which could be supported by the 
SAED result in Fig. 8 e.
4.3. Degradation effects of the microcracks at mediate temperature
From the above discussion, the mechanisms of the microcrack for -
mation by Mo addition were attributed to the weakening of γ ′ / γ interface 
and the TCP phase precipitation, as depicted in Fig. 12 . In the investi -
gated alloys with Mo contents between 2 and 3 wt%, where no TCP 
emerged, the nano-size stacking faults played important roles in the 
formation of microcracks. The effects of stacking fault energy at 900 
◦
C 
seemed different from those at lower temperatures and higher stresses. 
The low stacking fault energy in the γ or γ ′ phase promotes the formation 
of stacking faults in the γ phase, which hinder the movements of dislo -
cation sliding and increase the creep resistance at low temperatures and 
high stresses [ 5 ]. The difference in operating mechanisms of stacking 
faults is because stacking fault energy increases as the temperature rises 
[ 51 , 52 ], leading to fewer stacking faults with narrow widths when 
deforming at relatively high temperatures. The narrow stacking faults 
could not effectively hint the movement of dislocations like the situation 
at low temperatures.
Similar to the deformation at elevated temperatures, the TCP parti -
cles promoted microcrack formation at 900 
◦
C. Compared to the typical 
needle-like TCP phases, the block or rod particles possess less coherency 
with the matrix [ 49 ]. Therefore, the crack tended to expand along the 
interfaces of TCP particles as shown in Fig. 8 c. Moreover, it was note -
worthy that the morphology of TCP phases in 4Mo alloy after loading 
was mainly blocky or rod-like, different from the samples without 
external stress in our previous study [ 21 ]. Some previous studies indeed 
pointed out that the applied stresses could affect the precipitation 
behavior of TCP phases [ 48 , 53 ]. However, the exact mechanisms 
require further investigation.
In Figs. 3 and 8 , the micropores or microcracks have not significantly 
propagated yet at the middle of the tertiary stage, which hinted that the 
microcracks formed after the middle stages of creep. Since the stacking 
fault energy might be high at 900 
◦
C, the formation of stacking faults in 
Fig. 5 required sufficient time, promoted by the elemental re- 
distribution between γ ′ and γ phase in the long-term heating process 
[ 21 ]. Moreover, the elemental segregation at stacking faults, which 
triggered the new phase precipitation in Fig. 7 , might need sufficient 
time due to the low elemental diffusion rates at 900 
◦
C. The volume 
fraction of TCP phases needed to reach a certain value after long-term 
exposure to influence the mechanical property [ 54 ]. Therefore, it was 
quite reasonable to deduce that the microcracks led by Mo addition 
initiated at the late stages of creep, mainly played the role of acceler -
ating the final failure.
5. Summary
The creep property of a 4th generation Ni-based SC with different Mo 
content was investigated. When the Mo content lay within 2 wt% to 4 wt 
%, Mo addition decreased the creep life at 900 
◦
C and 392 MPa of the 4th 
generation Ni-based SC, promoting large amounts of microcracks in the 
dendritic cores. The conclusions are as follows.
(1) Mo addition promoted the formation of stacking faults at the γ ′ / γ 
interface by decreasing the stacking fault energy in γ phase, 
which weakened the interfacial strength and caused the micro -
pores at the γ ′ / γ interface in 3Mo alloy. The elemental segregation 
at the interfacial stacking faults could promote the formation of 
other phases, which also accelerated the emerge of micropores 
and crack initiation.
(2) Mo addition caused the precipitation of TCP phases in the 
dendrite cores in 4Mo alloy. The weak interface strength between 
the TCP particles and the matrix led to crack initiation during 
service. To suppress the formation of TCP phases, the Mo content 
should be lower than 3.23 wt%.
Based on the above results, it could be concluded that the main 
reasons that initiated the crack were micropores and TCP phases, which 
provided a novel perspective for alloy design. It is crucial to control the 
SFE and the tendency of TCP precipitation when adjusting the content of 
Mo or other strengthening elements.
