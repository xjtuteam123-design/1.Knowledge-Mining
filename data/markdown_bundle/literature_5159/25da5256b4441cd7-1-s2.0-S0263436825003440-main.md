ABSTRACT
The global scientific and engineering communities have continuously pursued novel high-temperature alloys 
capable of performing under increasingly severe thermal conditions. Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs), 
which consist of elements with high melting points, exhibit outstanding mechanical properties at both ambient 
and elevated temperatures, making them promising candidates for aerospace applications requiring high- 
temperature resistance. However, their practical engineering applications are hindered by several limitations, 
such as relatively high density and limited room-temperature ductility. This study incorporated aluminum into a 
TiZrHfNb refractory high entropy alloy matrix, fabricating TiZrHfNbAl
x 
(x = 5, 10, 15, 20 at.%) refractory high 
entropy alloys via vacuum arc melting. The research systematically examined the microstructural characteristics, 
mechanical properties at both room and elevated temperatures, and tribological behavior under ambient con -
ditions. Experimental results demonstrate that all the alloys possess a BCC solid solution phase. However, in the 
Al-20 alloy, an ordered BCC phase (B2) forms due to the limited solubility of aluminum. With increasing Al 
content, the hardness and room-temperature compressive yield strength of the alloys are enhanced, although the 
compressive ductility of the Al-20 alloy is somewhat reduced. At 1000 
◦
C, the high-temperature compressive 
yield strength of the alloys first decreases and subsequently increases with further addition of Al. Under room- 
temperature friction conditions, all tested alloys undergo both abrasive and oxidative wear, with higher Al 
content contributing to improved wear resistance.
1. Introduction
With the continuous increase in thrust-to-weight ratio requirements 
for aero engines, the operating environment of turbine blades has 
become progressively more complex and severe, thereby imposing 
stricter performance demands on high-temperature alloy materials [ 1 ]. 
Currently, nickel-based and cobalt-based superalloys are widely used; 
however, their applications are limited by the melting point of the ma -
trix, the extent of alloying, and design constraints related to precipitate 
phase ( γ ´ ) content, which make them insufficient to meet the perfor -
mance requirements of turbine blades. High entropy alloys (HEAs) are a 
novel class of multi-component alloys defined by their high thermody -
namic entropy, multi-principal element composition, lattice distortion 
effects, and slow diffusion kinetics [ 2 , 3 ]. These unique characteristics 
endow high entropy alloys with exceptional mechanical and functional 
properties, such as high strength, superior hardness, radiation resis -
tance, excellent toughness, and fatigue resistance, making them prom -
ising candidates for a wide range of engineering applications. Moreover, 
high entropy alloys exhibit a superior combination of yield strength and 
fracture toughness compared to conventional nickel-based superalloys 
and titanium alloys, highlighting their potential as high-performance 
structural materials for load-bearing applications [ 4 ].
Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) were initially introduced by 
Senkov et al. at the U.S. air force research laboratory in 2010 [ 5 ]. They 
are mainly composed of refractory transition metals such as Mo, Nb, Ta, 
and W. These alloys exhibit outstanding high-temperature performance, 
making them promising candidates for use in extreme environments, 
including aerospace propulsion systems, deep petroleum exploration, 
and nuclear energy applications. Consequently, they hold significant 
potential for advancing the development of turbine blade materials in

2. Experimental materials and methods
2.1. Material preparation
The raw materials Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Al, with purities exceeding 99.9 
wt%, were provided by Beijing Yanbang Advanced Materials Technol -
ogy Co., Ltd. Refractory high-entropy alloy ingots with different Al 
contents, labeled as TiZrHfNbAl
x 
(x = 5, 10, 15, 20 at.%, corresponding 
to Al-5, Al-10, Al-15, and Al-20, respectively), were synthesized using 
vacuum arc melting. The smelting procedure is carried out as follows: 
First, the smelting furnace is evacuated to establish a vacuum environ -
ment. Next, high-purity argon gas (99.99 % purity) is introduced into 
the furnace. The smelting process then begins. To ensure uniform 
composition, the ingot is remelted ten times with inversion, with each 
cycle lasting approximately three minutes under a current of approxi -
mately 550 A. After smelting is complete, the material is cooled to room 
temperature, and samples are taken, resulting in a 100 g button-shaped 
alloy ingot.

3.1. Microstructure
The XRD patterns of the TiZrHfNbAl
x

are shown in Fig. 1 (a), indicating that the Al-5 to Al-20 alloys all exhibit 
BCC solid solution structures. As illustrated in Figs. 1 (b) and 1 (c), the 
diffraction peaks corresponding to the BCC crystal planes gradually shift 
toward higher diffraction angles, suggesting a progressive decrease in 
the lattice parameters of the alloys. This trend can be attributed to the 
fact that the atomic radius of TiZrHfNb (1.518 Å) is larger than that of Al 
(1.432 Å), leading to lattice distortion. This observation confirms that 
the addition of Al has a significant effect on the lattice parameters [ 16 ]. 
Comparative analysis shows that the (110) diffraction peak width of the 
Al-20 alloy undergoes a significant change, suggesting a tendency to -
ward phase separation. Moreover, the appearance of a (100) charac -
teristic peak at approximately 26
◦
, as shown in Fig. 1 (d), indicates the 
formation of an ordered BCC phase in this alloy composition. This 
behavior can mainly be attributed to the limited solubility of aluminum 
in the BCC phase. According to the extinction rules for diffraction of 
disordered BCC phase diffraction, only crystallographic planes with 
even sums of Miller indices produce diffraction peaks in the X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) pattern. The lattice parameters of the disordered and 
ordered BCC phases are highly similar, as indicated by the overlapping 
diffraction peaks observed in XRD. The ordered solid solution phase can 
be regarded as an intermediate state between conventional solid solu -
tions and intermetallic compounds. It resembles solid solutions in terms 
of lattice type and parameters, yet displays a mixture of metallic, co -
valent, and ionic bonding features [ 17 ]. Research findings indicate that 
aluminum (Al) acts as a BCC phase stabilizing element and promotes 
ordered structural transformations in alloys [ 18 – 20 ]. The p-orbitals of 
Al atoms are fully occupied, which results in a high concentration of 
outermost electrons and an elevated Fermi level [ 21 ]. In contrast, 
transition metals such as Ti, Zr, Hf, and Nb have partially filled d-or -
bitals. As a consequence, Al atoms tend to donate electrons to these 
transition metals, leading to the formation of covalent bonds through 
pd. hybridization. This interaction facilitates the formation of ordered 
solid solutions or intermetallic compounds [ 22 ].

the Al-20 alloy, as shown in Fig. 2 . Fig. 2 (a) displays a bright-field image 
of the alloy, indicating the presence of stress fringes near the grain

and B2 phases arises from the ordered substitution of atoms at the body- 
centered positions, which leads to the formation of an ordered structure.

to solidify first, forming the dendritic cores, whereas elements with 
lower melting points crystallize later within the interdendritic regions.

As shown in Fig. 4 (a), there is a strong correlation between the 
aluminum (Al) content and the hardness of the alloy. The hardness in -
creases from 290 HV for the Al-5 alloy to 434 HV for the Al-20 alloy, 
indicating a considerable improvement of 49.66 %. Both the Al-5 and Al- 
20 alloys consist of a BCC solid solution phase. The observed increase in 
hardness with higher Al content can primarily be attributed to the solid 
solution strengthening mechanism, which causes significant lattice 
distortion and enhances dislocation pinning [ 29 ]. Furthermore, the shift 
of diffraction peaks to higher angles suggests a decrease in lattice pa -
rameters, which in turn modifies the overall bonding characteristics 
among atoms. According to density functional theory (DFT)-based 
research, the pd. hybridization effect strengthens the bonds around Al 
atoms, thereby contributing to enhanced hardness [ 30 ]. Moreover, the 
ordered BCC phase in Al-20 alloy significantly enhances hardness 
through precipitation strengthening mechanisms. Fig. 4 (b) shows the 
compressive engineering stress-strain curves obtained at room temper -
ature. Owing to the solid solution strengthening effect, the compressive 
yield strength ( σ
y
) increases from 1073 MPa for the Al-5 alloy to 1773 
MPa for the Al-20 alloy, representing a 65.24 % improvement. The 
significant enhancement in the compressive yield strength of the Al-20 
alloy can be attributed to second-phase strengthening. Furthermore, 
work hardening during compressive deformation leads to a relatively 
high ultimate compressive strength ( σ
b
) of approximately 1881 MPa.
The Al-5 to Al-15 alloys demonstrate superior compressive plasticity, 
exhibiting macroscopic deformation without fracture and achieving 
fracture plasticity ( ε ) values exceeding 50 %. However, the Al-20 alloy 
shows a noticeable decrease in plasticity, with a fracture strain of 
approximately 28 %, due to the presence of the ordered BCC phase. This 
phase, while contributing to the alloy ’ s strength, is inherently hard and 
brittle, thereby introducing a degree of brittleness that limits plastic 
deformation. The fracture morphology shown in Fig. 5 indicates that the 
Al-20 alloy undergoes macroscopic fracture at a 45
◦
angle relative to the 
axial direction under compressive loading. At the microscopic level, the 
fracture surface exhibits typical river patterns, distinct steps, small 
facets, and the presence of dimples, collectively suggesting a quasi- 
cleavage fracture mechanism. Quasi-cleavage fracture, a type of brittle 
transgranular fracture, is commonly observed in materials with high 
strength and low ductility [ 31 , 32 ]. Considering its strength, ductility, 
and microhardness, the Al-20 alloy demonstrates superior mechanical 
performance.

4. Conclusions
The as-cast TiZrHfNbAl ₓ (x = 5, 10, 15, 20 at.%) alloys were fabri -
cated using the vacuum arc melting technique. A comprehensive 
investigation was carried out on the microstructure, mechanical prop -
erties at both room and elevated temperatures, and the tribological 
behavior at ambient temperature of the as-cast alloys. The main findings 
are summarized as follows: 
(1) The as-cast TiZrHfNbAl
x 
alloys exhibit a BCC solid solution 
structure. However, in the case of the Al-20 alloy, an ordered BCC 
phase is formed, which can be attributed to its highly negative 
mixing enthalpy and the attainment of the solubility limit of Al 
content.
(2) The increase in aluminum content significantly improves both the 
room-temperature and high-temperature mechanical properties 
of as-cast TiZrHfNbAl
x 
alloys. Alloy hardness increases from 290 
HV for the Al-5 alloy to 434 HV for the Al-20 alloy, reflecting a 
49.66 % enhancement. At room temperature, the compressive 
yield strength increases from 1073 MPa for the Al-5 alloy to 1773 
MPa for the Al-20 alloy, representing a 65.24 % improvement. 
However, the fracture plasticity of the Al-20 alloy decreases to 28 
%, which can be attributed to the presence of hard and brittle 
ordered BCC phases. Moreover, the high-temperature compres -
sive yield strength at 1000 
◦
C reaches a maximum of 80 MPa for 
the Al-20 alloy. The main strengthening mechanisms are identi -
fied as solid solution strengthening and second-phase 
strengthening.
(3) The increase in aluminum content significantly improves the 
room-temperature tribological properties of as-cast TiZrHfNbAl ₓ 
alloys. The wear rate decreases from 9.45 × 10
 5

3
/(N ⋅ m) for 
the Al-5 alloy to 6.45 × 10
 5

indicating a 31.75 % improvement in wear resistance. The pri -
mary wear mechanisms are determined to be abrasive wear and 
oxidative wear.
