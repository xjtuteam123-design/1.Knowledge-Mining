ABSTRACT 
High-strength materials usually exhibit poor hydrogen embrittlement resistance, and thus, there are demands for 
materials with high strength and good ductility under hydrogen. Here, gradient structures containing surface 
nanotwins are introduced in a CrMnFeCoNi high-entropy alloy by surface mechanical attrition treatment, and 
hydrogen embrittlement resistance is compared with coarse- and nano-structured alloys produced by high- 
temperature homogenization and high-pressure torsion, respectively. The coarse-grained alloy shows high 
ductility, but its yield stress is low. The nanostructured alloy shows ultrahigh yield stress, but with poor hydrogen 
embrittlement resistance. The gradient-structured alloys have both high yield stress (500 – 700 MPa) and good 
ductility (15 – 33%) under hydrogen. 
1. Introduction 
Decades of metallurgical progress have led to the development of 
high-strength alloys that can contribute to applications where light 
weight is desirable. This success is held back in the presence of hydrogen 
by the phenomenon of hydrogen embrittlement, as the main obstacle 
preventing a wider uptake of high-strength alloys [1] . In the hydrogen 
embrittlement phenomenon, the ductility and fracture resistance of 
metals and alloys are drastically reduced in the presence of hydrogen 
and such a reduction is proportional to the material strength [1,2] . 
Although practically all metals and alloys suffer from hydrogen 
embrittlement, it is generally accepted that materials with the 
face-centered cubic (FCC) structure such as austenitic steels are more 
resistant to hydrogen diffusion and hydrogen damages [1,2] . Therefore, 
an important research trend in the field of hydrogen embrittlement is 
finding FCC alloys that can exhibit high strength and good ductility in 
the presence of hydrogen [1] . Recently, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with 
the FCC structure, such as equiatomic CrMnFeCoNi (also known as 
Cantor alloy [3] ), were introduced as new candidates with good resis -
tance to hydrogen embrittlement [4] . 
HEAs are composed of five or more principal elements in equiatomic 
or near-equiatomic ratios with high configurational entropy [5,6] . HEAs 
are usually synthesized by melting methods in coarse-grained forms, 
although there are some trends to produce nanograined HEAs particu -
larly using severe plastic deformation (SPD) [7] . The concept of HEAs 
offers a new path for developing advanced materials with high ther -
modynamic stability and some unique mechanical and hydrogen-related 
functional properties such as superior strength-ductility balance [8] , 
cryogenic toughness [9] , corrosion resistance [10] , hydrogen storage 
performance [11] , photocatalytic hydrogen production [12] , hydrogen 
embrittlement resistance [4] , etc. Despite reported high resistance of 
FCC HEAs to hydrogen embrittlement, their main drawback is their low 
yield strength [3] . Attempts to enhance the yield stress usually lower the 
ductility, but several strategies were developed for improving the 
strength-ductility combination: bimodal microstructure formation [13] , 
grain boundary engineering by SPD processing [14] , nanotwinning 
[15] , lattice softening [16] , heterostructure formation [17] and 
gradient-structure generation [18] . While these strategies have been 
proven to be successful in developing alloys with remarkable combi -
nations of high tensile strength and high ductility in the air atmosphere,

99.5 wt%. The vacuum induction melting was used in this study because 
of the capability of method in producing homogenous ingots with 
minimum contamination and oxidation. The cast ingot was homoge -

3.2. Tensile properties 
Fig. 6 a shows the engineering stress-strain curves of HEA with and 
without hydrogen charging achieved for four different samples: initial 
coarse-grained sample processed by high-temperature homogenization; 
gradient-structured samples processed under soft and mild SMAT con -
ditions, and nanograined sample processed by HPT. The results illustrate 
that the coarse-grained sample has good hydrogen embrittlement 
resistance, but its yield strength is as low as 220 MPa. The large elon -
gation of 65% achieved for the homogenized sample after hydrogen 
charging confirms that the presence of micro-pores in the samples does 
not have a significant effect on hydrogen embrittlement. The HPT- 
processed nanograined sample exhibits a very high yield strength of 
1850 MPa, but its ductility is quite low, and it breaks in the elastic re -
gion after hydrogen charging. In the gradient-structured samples, the 
yield strength increases and reaches 500 and 700 MPa for the soft and 
mild SMAT conditions, respectively, while elongation to failure remains 
reasonably high in the range 15 – 33% even after hydrogen charging. The 
sample processed under soft SMAT condition shows a good combination 
of high yield stress of 500 MPa and high uniform ductility of 22% under 
hydrogen, suggesting its high potential for applications in the hydrogen 
atmosphere. Such a promising combination of mechanical properties in 
the presence of hydrogen are even comparable to those achieved by 
various strategies in the absence of hydrogen [12 – 16] . 
All four samples show a reduction in ductility by hydrogen charging. 
Such a reduction is not only due to direct effect of defects on crack 
initiation and propagation but also due to the role of defects as pathways 
for diffusion and trapping of hydrogen. The TDS analyses, conducted to 
measure the total amount of hydrogen in each sample, are shown in 
Fig. 6 b. Here, it should be noted that since the hydrogen diffusion starts 
from the surface, the concentration of hydrogen is maximum on the 
surface and decreases with increasing the distance from the surface. By 
considering the slow diffusion of hydrogen in FCC alloys, the hydrogen 
concentration in the inner parts of tensile specimens should be negli -
gible after 48 h electrochemical charging. Therefore, the hydrogen 
contents given in Fig. 6 b, as average contents from the surface to the 
center of samples, mainly present in a surface layer. The amount of 
hydrogen is 3.4 wt. ppm for the coarse-grained sample processed by 
homogenization, 5.3 wt. ppm for the gradient-structured sample pro -
cessed by soft SMAT, 6.1 wt. ppm for gradient-structured sample pro -
cessed by mild SMAT and 19.5 wt. ppm for nanograined sample 
processed by HPT. Therefore, the hydrogen content increases with 
increasing the severity of deformation and enhancing the concentration 
of lattice defects. The current results together with a few earlier publi -
cations [29 – 31] confirm the potential of gradient structures in control -
ling hydrogen content and hydrogen embrittlement, suggesting an 
approach for extending the application range of HEAs in the structural 
and hydrogen-related fields [8 – 12] .

3.3. Fracture surface 
Fracture surfaces of tensile specimens without and with hydrogen 
charging are shown in different magnifications in Fig. 7 for the coarse- 
grained sample processed by homogenization, in Fig. 8 for the 
gradient-structured sample processed under soft SMAT condition, in 
Fig. 9 for the gradient-structured sample processed under mild SMAT 
condition and in Fig. 10 for the nanograined sample processed by HPT. 
The four samples show different fracture and hydrogen embrittlement 
behaviors, as described below. 
In the initial homogenized specimen, overall view of fracture surface 
( Fig. 7 a) and high-magnification images of the inner ( Fig. 7 b) and edge 
( Fig. 7 c) regions of the uncharged specimen show completely ductile 
feature, containing many dimples. These dimples are generated by 
nucleation of microvoids and their coalescence during deformation. In 
contrast, two distinct fracture morphologies of ductile and brittle fea -
tures are observed for the hydrogen-charged specimen. The inner region 
shows ductile features ( Fig. 7 d) containing considerable dimples 
( Fig. 7 e). At the edge, a layer of intergranular fracture surface presents, 
as shown more clearly in a high-magnification image in Fig. 7 f. Fig. 7 f 
shows a considerable amount of slip traces on the cracking region. Such 
a slip localization is a common feature of hydrogen-induced intergran -
ular cracking which is usually caused by hydrogen-enhanced localized 
plasticity (HELP) mechanism in FCC alloys [2] , although other 
dislocation-based mechanisms such as adsorption-induced dislocation 
emission (AIDE) can basically produce slip traces [40] . Moreover, 
similar slip traces are observed in transgranular cracking of some FCC 
alloys [1] . 
Fracture surface of uncharged specimens processed by SMAT under 
soft condition shows ductile features ( Fig. 8 a) with many dimples which 
are visible at the edge and center of fracture surface in Figs. 8 b and 8 c, 
respectively. In contrast, the fracture surface of hydrogen-charged 
specimen shows ductile features at the center ( Fig. 8 d) and brittle 
ones at the edge of the specimen, where the hydrogen concentration is 
high ( Figs. 8 e and 8 f). These brittle features include both intergranular 
and transgranular cracking with multiple slip planes and flat surfaces as 
shown in Fig. 8 f. The combination of intergranular and transgranular 
cracking in hydrogen-charged sample should be due to the contribution 
of surface nanotwins on transgranular cracking [41] . 
The fracture surface of specimen processed by SMAT under mild 
condition and pulled without hydrogen charging shows ductile feature 
with the formation of dimples ( Fig. 9 a-c). For the hydrogen-charged 
specimen, despite ductile fracture features with many dimples at the 
center of the specimen ( Fig. 9 d), intergranular and transgranular 
cracking appears at the edge where the hydrogen content and twin 
density are high ( Figs. 9 e and 9 f). The transgranular cracking in Fig. 9 f 
shows a step-like ridge similar to the twin pattern observed in Fig. 4 , 
suggesting that twin boundary cracking contributes to the fracture of 
this material, as reported earlier for some other nanotwinned alloys 
[38] . 
Fracture surface of nanograined sample processed by HPT without 
hydrogen charging shows localized ductility features with small dimples 
in both center ( Fig. 10 a) and edge ( Fig. 10 b). After hydrogen charging, 
fracture surface in edge ( Fig. 10 c) and center ( Fig. 10 d) have features of 
intergranular cracking, and grain boundaries are visible on the surface. 
Such an intergranular cracking and the activation of hydrogen-enhanced 
decohesion (HEDE) mechanism [42] should be the reason for the failure 
of this nanograined material in the elastic region, as shown in Fig. 6 . 
4. Discussion 
The current results show that gradient-structured HEAs exhibit a 
good combination of strength and ductility even in the presence of 
hydrogen ( Fig. 6 a). These promising properties can be discussed in terms 
of (i) effect of gradient structures on high strength and high ductility, (ii) 
significance of FCC HEAs as alloys with good hydrogen compatibility, 
and (iii) significance of gradient structures on controlling the hydrogen 
embrittlement of FCC HEAs. 
Regarding the first issue, in gradient-structured materials, the 
gradient of microstructure from the surface to interior leads to the 
gradient of yield stress due to the effect of grain and twin size on yield 
stress [20] . The plastic deformation propagates from the coarse-grained 
interior with lower yield stress to the refined layer on the surface. In 
addition to yield stress, plastic strain also has a gradual change from the 
coarse-grained interior, where plasticity is maximum, to the outer layer 
on the surface, where the plasticity is minimum [20] . These gradients of 
microstructure, stress and strain give unique mechanical properties to 
the materials including high strength and high ductility [18 – 20] . High 
strength is a natural consequence of refined microstructure on the sur -
face and high ductility is due to the capability of the coarse-grained 
interior to accumulate the dislocations. When the microstructure on 
the surface contains nanotwins, as the one observed in the current study 
( Fig. 4 ), the capability of material to accommodate dislocations further

Regarding the second issue, it was shown that HEAs with the FCC

hydrogen and results in a macroscopic brittle behavior [2,42] , is sup -

Such a justification seems quite reasonable, since HELP is considered as 
a major hydrogen embrittlement mechanism in the FCC alloys [1,2,40] .

Fig. 9 f, twin boundaries on the surface may act as crack initiation and

5. Conclusions 
To achieve high yield strength and high ductility in the presence of 
hydrogen, gradient nanostructures, containing nanotwins on the sur -
face, are introduced in a high-entropy alloy CrMnFeCoNi by surface 
mechanical attrition treatment. The materials in the presence of 
hydrogen show a good combination of high yield stress of 500 – 700 MPa, 
which is 2 – 3 times higher than the yield stress of coarse-grained mate -
rial, and high plasticity of 15 – 33%. The hydrogen diffusion is enhanced, 
and the hydrogen embrittlement resistance is weakened with increasing 
the intensity of deformation and a nanograined sample produced by 
high-pressure torsion experiences severe intergranular cracking with a 
catastrophic failure in the elastic region. This study introduces gradient-

hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity mechanism (e.g., below
