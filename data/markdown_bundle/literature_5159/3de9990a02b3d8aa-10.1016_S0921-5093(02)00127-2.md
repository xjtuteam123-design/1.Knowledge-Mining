Abstract
The superplastic characteristic and diffusion bonding beha vior were in vestigated in a commercial AZ31 magnesium alloy sheet
having equiaxed grain size with an average size of 16.8 mm. The alloy behaved in a superplastic manner from 623 to 723 K, and, the
present material was successfully diffusion bonded at the superplastic temperature by selecting appropriate pressure and time. The
maximum of lap shear strength was 0.85 at a bonding pressure of 3 MPa and a bonding temperature of 673 K, with a bonding time
of 3 h. In the specimen with high ratio of lap shear strength, the bond line was not identified by optical microscopy.

1. Introduction
Recently, the use of magnesium as a structural
material has increased significantly. This is a conse-
quence not only of magnesium’s relati vely low density
but also of its good damping characteristics, dimen-
sional stability, machinability and low casting costs.
Despite these ad vantages, howe ver, magnesium alloys
normally exhibit low ductility near room temperature
because of their h.c.p. structure. It is, therefore,
necessary to impro ve these alloys ductility in order to
enable their use as structural components. Superplastic
forming is a viable technique to form hard materials into
complex shapes. It is generally established that fine grain
size is necessarily required to attain superplasticity [1,2].
It is reported that the superplasticity is obtained in Mg /C1
/
Al /C1/Zn system alloys with fine grain sizes of less than 20
mm and at temperatures between 0.59 and 0.70 Tm,
where Tm is the absolute melting point of the material
[3 /C1/7]. In manufacturing, superplastic forming is often
combined with diffusion bonding, in a process known as
concurrent SPF/DB. Concurrent SPF/DB has been
recognized as a no vel manufacturing technology that
can result in both cost and weight sa vings compared
with conventional manufacturing methods [8,9].
Diffusion bonding is one of the joining techniques,
and it is a solid state joining process in which two clean
metallic surfaces are brought into contact at ele vated
temperatures of B/0.7 Tm under low pressure. The
diffusion bonding process also permits the production
of high-quality joints with little or no need for post-weld
machining [10,11]. To enhance the quality of joints, it is
important to consider the optimal conditions for diffu-
sion bonding. Many factors affect the quality of
diffusion bonds, including temperature, pressure, time
and so on [12]. The optimal conditions to produce high-
quality diffusion bonds ha ve already been reported for
aluminum alloys, titanium alloys and steels [13 /C1
/22].
Titanium and aluminum superplastic materials ha ve
been also studied to apply to SPF/DB technology [19 /C1/
22]. As there is the possibility of combining SPF/DB to
manufacture complex sheet structures with reduced
weight and fabrication costs compared with mechani-
cally fastened structure [23,24], titanium superplastic
materials have been widely used to SPF/DB in aerospace
industry [19,20,24]. However, the possibility of diffusion
bonding using magnesium superplastic materials has not
been examined.

3.3. Comparison with diffusion bonding models
Many factors affect diffusion bonding quality, includ-
ing temperature, pressure, time and so on. It is necessary
to de velop diffusion bonding models to predict the
conditions necessary for high-quality joints. To date,
several models have been proposed: for example, Chen /C1
/
Argon [27] and Pilling /C1/Ridely [28] proposed diffusion
bonding models based on the rate of void growth by the
diffusional flow process. These models were de veloped
by considering void shrinkage as negati ve void growth,
and they were applied to the diffusion bonding of fine
grained superplasticity materials.
The experimental bonding times to obtain high value
in lap shear strength in our study were compared with
those of prediction analysis by Chen /C1
/Argon and
Pilling /C1/Ridely models. The comparison between experi-
mental bonding time and those predicted values in using
models is shown in Fig. 6. The symbols of k, ^ and /C29/
in Fig. 6 show tb/tp ]/0.80, 0.80 /C21/tb/tp ]/0.60 and tb/
tp B/0.60, respectively. From Fig. 6, it is easy to predict
the bonding time to obtain high quality joints, and it is
apparent that the time depends on bonding pressure.
However, experimental bonding time was not relati vet o
bonding pressure. It is suggested that grain growth
influenced the diffusion bonding. From Fig. 2, the grain
growth occurred with increasing time in present used
material. In addition, by comparing Fig. 1 and Fig. 3,i t
is apparent that grain growth occurred during diffusion
bonding. Therefore, the grain coarsening obstructed the
bonding time reduction.
A comparison between our experimental results and
Chen /C1

models also shows that the experimental bonding times
