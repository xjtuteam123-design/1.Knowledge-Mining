a b s t r a c t 
Structural stability of high entropy alloys (HEAs) at intermediate temperatures during which the conﬁg- 
urational entropy loses its dominance is crucial. In this work, we have scrutinized the effects of cooling 
rates on the phase stability of the HfTaTiZr(Ta1) and HfNbTiZr (Nb1) refractory HEAs (RHEAs). Ta1 ex- 
hibited strong microstructure and tensile property dependence on the cooling route, but not Nb1. Ta1 
retained the BCC lattice under water quenching but experienced complex phase decomposition upon air 
and furnace cooling. The different dependence on cooling rates in these RHEAs arises from three main 
reasons: (i) the wider metastable temperature range in Ta1, (ii) the larger mobility difference between 
Ta and other constituents, and (iii) the larger electronegativity difference of Nb1. The current work not 
only sheds new insights into the understanding of the thermal and mechanical stability of HEAs but also 
provides a new paradigm for the optimization of their overall properties.

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are initially proposed to form 
single-phase solid solutions due to the entropy maximization [ 1 , 2 ]. 
Nevertheless, it was found that the high conﬁgurational entropy 
is not suﬃcient to retain the single-phase at intermediate tem- 
peratures because of the increasing of Gibbs free energy [ 3 , 4 ]. 
Several studies have conﬁrmed the microstructural instability in 
HEAs under certain thermal treatments such as prolonged dura- 
tions at intermediate temperatures and slow cooling [5–17] . The 
microstructural instability arises from the intrinsic metastability of 
the single phase in HEAs obtained at room temperature (RT). Phase 
transformation into a thermodynamically more stable state oc- 
curs spontaneously in HEAs when being subjected to temperatures 
where considerable diffusion is facilitated. Such phase decompo- 
sition would undoubtedly affect the mechanical performance of 
HEAs [ 7–9 , 16 , 17 ]. BCC refractory HEAs (RHEAs) are promising for 
aerospace and related industries usage due to their excellent me- 
chanical performance and superior resistance to softening at high 
temperatures [18–21] . Retention of their structural stability and 
outstanding mechanical properties should be seriously considered 
so that they can sustain long-time service in thermally harsh envi- 
ronments.

[7] . Analogous phase decomposition has also been observed in 
TaTiZr during slow cooling from 1400 °C [9] . However, after slowly 
cooling from the single-phase region, NbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr can 
maintain the single BCC phase with very few HCP precipitates at 
grain boundaries (GBs) of the latter [ 12 , 22 ]. These results indi- 
cate that the microstructure stability of RHEAs after slow cooling 
is heavily chemical compositional sensitive. Chou et al. proposed 
the metastable temperature range for secondary phase appearance 
in HEAs, i.e., /Delta1T = T s − T c [23] . The Gibbs free energy of the mul- 
tiphase state is lower than that of the single phase below T s while 
elemental diffusion is suﬃciently quick to form new phases above 
T c . Note that formation of secondary phase is a kinetically driven 
process which demands increasing time at lower temperatures. 
Clearly, /Delta1T is composition speciﬁc and strongly rate-dependent. 
Apropriate compositional design and heat treatment selection are 
necessary to eliminate the dangerous temperature region /Delta1T by 
suppressing phase decomposition during heating and/or cooling 
from the operating temperature. 
To further understand such microstructural instability during 
slow cooling, an in-depth understanding of compositional and 
cooling-rate effects on phase stability and mechanical properties 
are prerequisites for the practical application of RHEAs. In this 
study, we have selected two RHEAs HfTaTiZr (Ta1) and HfNbTiZr 
(Nb1) and scrutinized the evolution of microstructure and tensile 
properties under different cooling rates. The underlying mecha- 
nism responsible for different responses of both RHEAs to cooling 
conditions will also be discussed.

Ta1 and Nb1 ingots were synthesized by arc melting a mixture 
of raw metals with the purity of 99.9 at.% and drop-cast into a 
copper mold with a dimension of 10 × 10 × 60 mm 3 . The aver- 
age alloy compositions listed in Table 1 indicate that the as-cast 
RHEAs are near-equimolar. Specimens with a thickness of 1.5 mm 
were sliced from the as-cast RHEA slabs and then encapsulated in 
vacuum-quartz tubes. The slices were solution-treated at 10 0 0 °C 
for 1 h and subsequently cooled under furnace cooling (FC), air 
cooling (AC), and water quenching (WQ). For simplicity, the sam- 
ples prepared with different processes will be hereafter denoted 
as Ta1-FC, Ta1-AC, Ta1-WQ, Nb1-FC, Nb1-AC, and Nb1-WQ, respec- 
tively. 
Phase constitutions of all the as-prepared samples were exam- 
ined by X-ray diffractometer with Cu-K α radiation and the scan- 
ning rate of 8 °/min in a 2 θ range from 20 ° to 100 °. Microstructures 
were characterized by a Zeiss Supra 55 scanning electron micro- 
scope (SEM) and an FEI Tecnai G2 F30 transmission electron micro- 
scope (TEM) equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). 
Room-temperature tensile tests were performed on a CMT4105 
universal electronic testing machine at a strain rate of 1 × 10 −3 
s −1 . The dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with gauge geometry 
of 15 × 5 × 1.5 mm 3 were prepared by electric discharging ma- 
chining. Calphad modeling was performed using the Thermo-Calc 
with the TCHEA3 database. 
Fig. 1 a shows XRD patterns of all the specimens, along with the 
derived lattice constants for each phase. A single BCC phase was 
observed in all the Nb1 and Ta1-WQ samples, while three phases 
were identiﬁed in the slowly cooled Ta1-AC and Ta1-FC. The pri- 
mary diffraction peaks presented in Ta1-AC were indexed as BCC1 
and BCC2 phase with lattice constant of 3.476 ˚A and 3.367 ˚A, re- 
spectively. Besides, weak diffraction peaks of a primitive hexagonal 
omega ( ω) phase were identiﬁed and further conﬁrmed by TEM 
characterization. ω phase often accompanies metastable BCC ma- 
trix in some Group IV (e.g. Hf, Zr, Ti) alloys [ 24 , 25 ]. SEM back- 
scattered image of Ta1-AC shows circular white particles with a 
diameter of 0.2 - 2 μm dispersing inside grains ( Fig. 1 b), besides 
ﬁner white pellets forming along GBs (not shown). The precipi- 
tates are enriched in Ta and poor in the other three constituents, 
as evidenced by the elemental EDS maps in Fig. 1 c. Further reduc- 
tion of cooling rates (FC) of Ta1 has facilitated a more thorough 
phase decomposition into mainly an HCP (a = 3.174 ˚A, c = 5.037 ˚A) 
and a BCC (3.322 ˚A) phase. The weak diffraction peaks of another 
BCC phase (i.e., BCC1, 3.412 ˚A) indicate a small volume fraction. 
As shown in Fig. 1 d, white precipitates are homogeneously dis- 
tributed inside grains and become slightly pronounced along GBs. 
The zoom-in image ( Fig. 1 e) around a GB indicates two types of 
precipitates with near-round and lamellae shapes. 
The microstructures of all the samples have been further char- 
acterized by TEM. Among the three differently cooled Nb1 sam- 
ples, decomposition is most likely to occur in Nb1-FC. Nonetheless, 
no trace of any secondary precipitates was found in the bright- 
ﬁeld (BF) image and the [ 011 ] BCC selected area electron diffrac- 
tion (SAED) pattern of Nb1-FC ( Fig. 2 a). These results indicate 
that Nb1 alloy has retained the single BCC phase under the three 
cooling conditions investigated. Similarly, The BF image and the 
[ 113 ] BCC SAED pattern of Ta1-WQ veriﬁes the single BCC structure 
( Fig. 2 b). In addition to the Ta-rich precipitates, a spinodal-like mi- 
crostructure with periodic compositional ﬂuctuation was observed 
in Ta1-AC by the high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld scanning electron 
microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image ( Fig. 2 c). Table 1 lists the vol- 
ume fraction and composition of the phases in Ta1-AC and Ta1-FC. 
The dark matrix BCC1 with a volume fraction of 41% contains less 
small atom Ta, and thus its lattice parameter is larger than that 
of the bright BCC2 phase [26] . Adjacent BCC2 nano-lamellas show 
identical orientation within each grain and align on the [ 100 ] BC C 1 
direction, as characterized by the dark-ﬁeld (DF) image ( Fig. 2 d). 
The [ 011 ] BC C 1 SAED pattern clearly shows extra ω reﬂections con- 
sisting of two nanosized variants.

Three distinct regions consisting of the gray matrix, white ir- 
regular particles, and the black phase are visible in the HAADF- 
STEM image of Ta1-FC ( Fig. 2 e). The elemental line scanning across 
the three phases quantiﬁes the striking compositional variations 
among them ( Fig. 2 f). The black Ti-rich phase mostly distributes 
in the vicinity of the ﬁne white Ta-rich phase and has a quite 
small volume fraction ( ∼6%), corresponding exactly to the BCC1 
phase with a larger lattice as identiﬁed by the XRD result. Com- 
positional statistics ( Table 1 ) reveal that the round white phase 
(BCC2-R) with a volume fraction of 12% contains more Ta than 
the lamellar one (BCC2-L), suggesting that the two Ta-rich phases 
formed at different stages. Despite the compositional variations be- 
tween BCC2-R and BCC2-L, the lattice parameters of them are close 
so that their XRD diffraction peaks merge on the XRD pattern of 
Ta1-FC. 
The general features of the phase constitution of the slowly 
cooled Ta1 specimens have certain resemblances with those gen- 
erated through isothermal annealing [ 6 , 10 , 17 ]. Calphad modeling 
was conducted to provide a close view of the phase transforma- 
tion events evolving with temperature in Ta1 and Nb1. As shown in 
Fig. 3 a, an analogous decomposition pathway is predicted for both 
RHEAs below their solvus temperature of secondary phases. The 
relatively lower solvus of Nb1 implies a lowered T s and a dimin- 
ished /Delta1T , given that the two RHEAs subjected to the same cooling 
procedure have similar T c . Particularly, the single BCC phase of Nb1 
is stable above 778 °C, and below which the high-temperature sta- 
ble phase is no longer thermodynamically favorable and decom- 
poses into BCC and HCP phases. The case is slightly different in 
Ta1. In combination with the simulated elemental evolution in dif- 
ferent phases of Ta 1 shown in Fig. 3 b, it is clear that the high- 
temperature BCC phase ﬁrst splits into Ta-poor BCC1 and Ta-rich 
BCC2 phases below 1030 °C, then the BCC1 phase gets particu- 
larly abundant Ti and transforms into Ta-rich BCC2 (i.e., lamellar 
BCC2-L in Ta1-FC) and HfZr-rich HCP phases among 730 - 830 °C 
through a eutectoid reaction. Below 730 °C, the equilibrium state 
is a mixture of Ta-rich BCC and HfZr-rich HCP phases. Although 
the solution temperature conducted in this work is 30 °C lower 
than the calculated solvus of Ta1, single phase microstructure was 
still obtained in Ta1-WC specimen experimentally. Calphad results 
for HEAs could be not so accurate due to the limitation of the 
database, and thus tens of degrees ﬂuctuation on the speciﬁc tem- 
perature boundaries are possible [ 6 , 16 , 27 ]. Nevertheless, the ob- 
tained phase constitution of Ta1-AC and Ta1-FC corresponds well 
to the calculated equilibrium phase diagram. 
The temperature range from which the slowly cooled Ta1 sam- 
ples were quenched can be simply estimated by comparing the ob- 
tained BCC1 phase with the phase diagram. For Ta1-AC, the volume

and 820 °C. Below 760 °C, the equilibrium state cannot be reached

ﬁcient diffusion time. Note that ω phase precipitated in the Ta- 
depleted metastable BCC1 phase below the estimated quenching 
temperature, which is similar to the phenomenon observed in the 
hot isostatically pressed and slowly cooled TaTiZr [ 9 , 25 , 28 ]. Longer

Evidently, phase decomposition is more prone to occur in Ta1 
during slow cooling. It is known that the alloys containing Ta(Nb) 
and Zr usually experience BCC phase separation in a speciﬁc tem- 
perature and composition range because of the miscibility gap 
that arose from the repulsive interaction between Ta (Nb) and Zr 
[ 7 , 9 , 29 , 30 ]. Particularly, the substitution of Nb with Ta can broaden 
the miscibility gap to a higher temperature range, which is also 
the case shown in Fig. 3 a [31] . Therefore, although cooling from 
the same temperature, Ta1 underwent a wider metastable temper- 
ature range compared to Nb1, leading to different microstructural 
dependence on cooling rates. 
Generally, great mobility difference among the constituents is 
more likely to cause precipitation. Fig. 3 c depicts the diffusivity of 
Ta, Nb, and Ti in several alloys based on previous studies [32–35] . 
The interdiffusion of Nb and Ta in Ti alloys is close and much faster 
than their self-diffusion, but slower than that of the solvent ele- 
ment Ti. The diffusivity ratio D T i / D Ta in Ti-12.5 at.% Ta between 
10 0 0 °C and 160 0 °C is around 4.9, more than twice of D T i / D Nb 
in Ti-19.6 at.% Nb [ 33 , 34 ]. Such diffusivity discrepancy multiplies 
considerably as the temperature decreases ( Fig. 3 c). Therefore, the 
mobility difference between Ta and the other constituents in Ta1 
is more pronounced than that in Nb1 at any given temperature 
during cooling, resulting in a stronger decomposition tendency 
of Ta1. 
Ta and Nb are alike elements having an approximate atomic ra- 
dius and valance electron concentration, but different electroneg- 
ativity and mixing enthalpy /Delta1H mix with Hf, Zr, and Ti [ 26 , 36 ]. 
Table 2 lists the electronegativity difference /Delta1χ and /Delta1H mix of Ta1, 
Nb1 and some typical RHEAs, along with their microstructures af- 
ter slow cooling. /Delta1H mix fails to quickly reﬂect the phase decom- 
position tendency among the RHEAs, but /Delta1χ can well reﬂect the 
microstructural stability. The RHEAs with a larger /Delta1χ like Nb1 
and NbTiZr have a greater tendency to maintain single phase dur- 
ing cooling. Large /Delta1χ is an indicator of strong short-range atomic 
bonding, which retards the long-range movement of atoms dur- 
ing rapid cooling [37] . In this case, /Delta1χ also offers a qualitative 
yet quick description of the propensity of phase decomposition of 
RHEAs. 
Fig. 3 d sketches a hypothesized continuous cooling transforma- 
tion (CCT) curve for Ta1 showing the obtained RT microstructure 
associated with each cooling route. It is foreseeable that the BCC1 
phase observed at RT would disappear after an even slower cool- 
ing. According to the CCT curve, cooling rates faster than WQ are 
expected to yield a single BCC phase in Ta1. Note that the critical 
cooling rate for the occurrence of phase decomposition in Nb1 is 
much slower than FC. Therefore, all the transformation curves for 
Nb1 should move to the low-temperature and long-time regime, 
as compared with those for Ta1. Our results indicate that other ho- 
mologous RHEAs would also confront phase instability under their 
relevant critical cooling rates.

In conclusion, microstructure and mechanical properties of Ta1 
and Nb1 RHEAs subjected to solution treatment and followed by 
three cooling methods were systematically investigated. Ta1 ex- 
hibits strong dependence of microstructure and mechanical prop- 
erties on cooling rates while Nb1 does not. Based on the Cal- 
phad approach, the microstructure of Ta1-AC and Ta1-FC was es- 
timated to be quenched from the temperature range of 760 - 
820 °C and 650 - 740 °C, respectively. Different microstructural de- 
pendence on cooling rates of Ta1 and Nb1 originates from three 
reasons. First, the broadening of the miscibility gap in Ta1 makes 
it confront a wider metastable temperature range. Second, the rel- 
atively signiﬁcant diffusivity difference between Ta and the other 
constituents in Ta1 leads to a strong precipitation tendency. Last, 
the large electronegativity difference /Delta1χ of Nb1 indicates stronger 
atomic bonding which can retard long-range atomic mobility. Our 
work highlights the importance of processing conditions on the 
microstructural stability of RHEAs and is useful for controlling mi- 
crostructures and the related properties.
