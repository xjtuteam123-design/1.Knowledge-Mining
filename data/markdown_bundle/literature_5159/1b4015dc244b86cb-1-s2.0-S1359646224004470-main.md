ABSTRACT
Near- α high-temperature titanium alloys suffer from low ductility due to silicide and α
2
(Ti
3
Al) precipitates. This
study explores how adjusting the Al/Zr ratio affects these precipitates and their impact on ductility. Dispersed
silicide precipitates activate the pyramidal 〈 c + a 〉 slip systems, enhancing ductility by limiting basal 〈 a 〉 slip.
Conversely, α
2
nanoparticles promote basal 〈 a 〉 slip, resulting in planar dislocations and reduced ductility. These
findings offer a new strategy for balancing strength and ductility in near- α high-temperature titanium alloys
through controlled precipitation.
High-temperature titanium (Ti) alloys are essential for
manufacturing engine blades, discs, and fan casings in aircraft due to
their high specific strength, fatigue resistance, and corrosion resistance
[ 1 , 2 ]. These applications require alloys with both high strength and
ductility for reliable performance. Silicon (Si) is commonly added to
near- α high-temperature Ti alloys, such as IMI834, Ti-65, and Ti-1100
alloys, to raise service temperatures [ 3 , 4 ], while aluminum (Al) serves
as an α -stabilizer to enhance high-temperature strength [ 5 , 6 ]. However,
the addition of Si and Al inevitably leads to the precipitation of silicides
and α
2
phase, which reduce ductility [ 7 – 10 ]. This strength-ductility
trade-off has been a longstanding challenge in near- α
high-temperature Ti alloys [ 11 ].
Recent investigations have increased focus on nanoscale precipitates
in these alloys [ 12 , 13 ], showing that Si can either form solid solutions or
precipitate as silicide (S
2
-type (Ti, Zr)
6
Si
3
), typically at α / β interfaces
[ 14 , 15 ]. While silicides are known to enhance strength [ 16 , 17 ], their
effect on ductility remains debated. Dispersed nanoscale silicides
maintain ductility by distributing tensile loads [ 18 , 19 ], whereas segre-
gated and grown silicides significantly impair ductility [ 20 , 21 ]. This
study delves deeper into the interaction between dislocation slip and
silicide precipitation to better understand its impact on ductility.
The precipitation of the α
2
phase is highly dependent on the Al
content and heat treatment, as indicated by the Ti-Al phase diagram
[ 22 ]. Typically forming in alloys with an Al content greater than 6 wt.%,
α
2
precipitates significantly impact the mechanical properties [ 23 ].
While α
2
phase provides precipitation strengthening, it also induces
planar slip, inhibits cross-slip, and reduces ductility and fracture
toughness [ 24 , 25 ]. Ductility is fundamentally tied to dislocation slip
under external loads [ 26 ], with different precipitates resisting disloca-
tion motion based on their structure, size, and morphology. Dislocation
slip plays a crucial role in Ti alloy deformation behavior [ 27 – 30 ].
However, the roles of silicide and α
2
phase remain unresolved. In this
study, two high-temperature Ti alloys were designed: one primarily
containing silicide and the other containing the α
2
phase. The impact of
these precipitates on ductility were investigated, providing insights into
achieving a superior strength-ductility synergy in high-temperature Ti
alloys.

Fig. 3 a shows the room temperature stress-strain curves for Ti-69 and
Ti-74 alloys after IMDF. The tensile strengths measure 1268.4 MPa for
Ti-69 and 1310.8 MPa for Ti-74, with elongations of 12.2 % and 3.8 %,
respectively. Compared with samples after blooming forging, IMDF
enhances ultimate tensile strengths by 144 MPa and 225 MPa for Ti-69
and Ti-74 alloys. This strength improvement is attributed to grain
boundary strengthening and precipitation hardening. The disparity in
elongation suggests different plastic deformation behaviors influenced
by dislocation-precipitate interactions. Interestingly, both IMDF-
processed alloys exhibit higher strengths than most near- α Ti alloys
and Ti matrix composites, with Ti-69 showing a superior strength and
ductility synergy [ 7 , 18 , 21 , [25] , 35 – 43 ].

Specifically, 〈 a 〉 , 〈 c 〉 , and 〈 c + a 〉 dislocations were analyzed using
[0002], [ 01 10 ] , [ 01 11 ] diffraction vectors, respectively ( Fig. 4 (g – i)). The
results reveal prominent 〈 a 〉 and 〈 c 〉 dislocations in Ti-74 alloy, while 〈 c

for Ti-74 alloy. Therefore, basal slip is the primary mechanism in Ti-74
alloy.
Mechanistic modeling, shown in Fig. 5 , clarifies the plastic slip
mechanisms. In Ti-69 alloy, nanoscale silicides are present at the α / β
interface and α grain boundaries, with residual β phase clearly delin-
eated. The presence of Si and Zr facilitates the precipitation of larger
silicides [ 14 ]. The dispersed nanoscale silicides at grain boundaries
reduce the prevalence of basal slips and enhance pyramidal 〈 c + a 〉 as the
primary slip mechanism ( Fig. 5 a). The increased participation of
non-basal slip systems alleviates localized stress and reduces the likeli-
hood of premature fracture, thereby enhancing ductility [ 54 ].
Compared to Ti-69 alloy, Ti-74, with its lower Zr and higher Al
content, has fewer silicides and primarily α
2
precipitates. Fig. 2 shows
that nanoscale α
2
precipitates are dispersed within the α matrix, acting
as obstacles to dislocation glide. These nano- α
2
precipitates can induce
planar slip at room temperature ( Fig. 5 b). Analysis from Fig. 4 g to i
indicates that basal slip is activated, with 〈 a 〉 type dislocations accu-
mulating on the basal plane, while 〈 c + a 〉 type dislocations are less
common during tension. The interaction between mobile dislocation
and α
2
precipitates obstructs dislocation movement and hinders cross
slip. Concurrently, dislocation accumulation within the α matrix in Ti-74
forms dislocation walls, promoting localized deformation and micro-
crack initiation, leading to premature fracture [ 55 ].
In summary, the Ti-69 alloy demonstrates a superior strength-
ductility synergy, with an ultimate tensile strength of 1268.4 MPa and
an elongation of 12.2 %, outperforming the Ti-74 alloy. The presence of
silicides in Ti-69 enhances non-basal slip activation and facilitates
deformation delocalization during tensile loading, resulting in excellent
ductility. Conversely, the nano- α
2
precipitates in the Ti-74 alloy impede
dislocation movement, induce planar slip, and promote basal slip,
thereby reducing ductility. These findings highlight the significant
impact of silicide and α
2
precipitates on ductility and offer valuable
insights for designing high-temperature Ti alloys with improved
strength-ductility balance through controlled precipitate modulation.
