1. Introduction
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), consisting of refractory el-
ements (W, Mo, Ta, Nb, Cr, Hf, V, Zr, Ti) with high melting temper-
taures, have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional high-
temperature strength, thermal stability and softening resistance even up
to 1600
◦
C [ 1 , 2 ]. These attributes make RHEAs ideal candidate alloys for
next-generation engines [ 3 ]. However, traditional casting processes
suffer from several drawbacks in RHEA preparation. For instance,
arc-melted RHEAs often experience severe grain coarsening and
elemental segregation, significantly compromising their mechanical
properties [ 4 ], while cast-formed RHEAs are constrained by limited size
and geometry due to poor melt fluidity [ 3 ]. Consequently, there is an
urgent need for innovative preparation technologies for RHEAs. Laser
additive manufacturing (AM), renowned for its high melt cooling rate
( > 10
3
K/s) and ability to form complex-shaped components, has been
extensively explored for metallic parts fabrication [ 5 ]. Recently, a few
attempts have been devoted to prepare RHEAs using laser-based AM
techniques, such as directed energy deposition (DED) and laser powder
bed fusion (LPBF). For example, Gou et al. [ 6 ] utilized the DED process
to fabricate TiZrHfNb RHEA, which exhibited remakable
room-temperature yield strength (~1034 MPa) and ductility (~18.5 %),
attributed to solid-solution strengthening and local chemical fluctua-
tions, respectively. However, this kind of RHEAs containing Hf – Ta,
Nb – Ti, Hf – Zr and Ta – Zr coordination tend to exhibit poor
high-temperature strength due to structural instability at intermediate
temperatures (~500
◦
C) and decomposition into multiple phases,
thereby compromising their high-temperature mechanical properties
[ 7 ]. Zhang et al. [ 8 , 9 ] demonstrated the synthesis of WTaMoNb RHEA
via LPBF using mixed elemental powders, resulting in improved

Here, we selected a CrMoNbV RHEA alloy system known for its excep-

AM-processed parts in-situ using the DED technique with blended
elemental powders. Unfortunately, mass of micro-cracks occurred in the
AM-processed parts. Inspired by the solving oxygen embrittlement of
refractory high-entropy alloy via grain boundary engineering reported
before [ 14 ], microalloying of B or C atoms which preferentially segre-
gate to GB can suppresses the O segregation at grain boundaries and
alleviate grain boundary brittleness. We added minor C into the blended
elemental powders and found that microcracks in the as-built AM-pro-
cessed parts were successfully inhibited, resulting in excellent me-
chanical properties at both room and elevated temperatures.

Fig. 1 b, the SEM image of + 0C DEDed RHEA in the backscattered
electron (BSE) model reveals abundant microcracks and some unmelted
Mo particles within the as-built RHEA. Despite optimization attempts
( Fig. S1 ), these microcracks persist, a common issue in laser AM-
processed RHEAs reported in previous literature. These cracks typi-
cally arise from thermal stress induced by the rapid cooling of molten
pools and phase transformation stress during in-situ alloying [ 15 ].
Fig. 1 c presents the EBSD morphology of the + 0C DEDed RHEA sur-
rounding a microcrack, showing an equiaxed-grain structure with an
average grain size of 42 μ m. It is worth noting that the microcrack
predominantly propagates along the grain boundaries, indicating their
weakness. The brittleness of the grain boundaries in RHEAs is attributed
to grain-boundary segregation of oxygen contaminants which weakens
cohesion [ 14 ]. The original elemental powders have large specific sur-
face area, and easily adsorb oxygen in their surfaces during the process
of production and utilization, thereby increasing the oxygen content of
as-built DEDed RHEA and embrittling its grain boundaries. Thermal
stress tends to tear these weak grain boundaries, resulting in the for-
mation of intergranular cracking. Inspired by the grain boundary engi-
neering in as-cast RHEAs [ 14 ], we added 1 at.% metalloid C into the
CrMoNbV blended powders (named + 1C mixed powders) and con-
ducted the DED process to obtain the + 1C DEDed RHEA via in-site
alloying. Encouragingly, microcracks in the + 1C DEDed RHEA were
effectively suppressed ( Fig. 1 d). The as-built + 1C DEDed RHEA also
exhibit a single BCC and equiaxed-grain morphology with average grain
size of 46 μ m ( Fig. 1 e). Inside the grains, a dendritic and interdendritic
microstructure was observed owing to compositional fluctuation
( Fig. 1 f), reminiscent of the microstructure observed in the as-cast state
[ 13 ]. The elemental distribution within dendrites and interdendrites
was analyzed by an energy dispersive system (EDS), as illustrated in
Fig. 2 b – e. The dendrites are enriched with Nb and Mo, while the
interdendrites are enriched with V and Cr, indicating that, Nb and Mo
with high melting points solidify first during solidification, while Cr and
V with low-melting points crystalize later [ 13 ]. Quantitative composi-
tions of these regions in + 1C DEDed RHEA are present in Table 1 .
Additionally, 2 at.% metalloid C was added into the CrMoNbV blended
powders to fabricate the + 2C DEDed RHEA ( Fig. S2 ). While the as-built
sample exhibits a single BCC structure, it contains more microcracks
propagating across grains, indicating that excessive C addition (2 at.%)
exacerbates thermal cracking in the DEDed RHEA due to the strong
carbide-forming nature of Cr, Mo, Nb, and V, leading to brittle carbide
precipitation [ 16 ]. We also compared the Vickers hardness of the + 0C,
+ 1C, + 2C DEDed RHEAs ( Fig. S3 ), and the + 2C DEDed RHEA (845.25
± 16.33 Hv) exhibited significantly higher hardness than that of + 0C
DEDed RHEA (604.43 ± 18.31 Hv) and + 1C DEDed RHEA (633.03 ±
9.31 Hv), which implied that more intense interstitial strengthening
effect formed there with the minor addition of C. These results manifest
that a 1 at.% addition of C effectively suppresses microcracks in DEDed
CrMoNbV RHEA. For the crack inhibition mechanism of C addition, as
reported before, the weak electronic interaction between O and the
metallic atoms in RHEA weakens the strength of grain boundaries,
resulting in intergranular cracking. Microalloying of C atoms which

performed to the FIB samples. As shown in Fig. S5 , the grain boundary in
the + 0C sample is more distinct than that of + 1C sample, and the bright
feature there commonly corresponds to the light element (only O in this
study). After 1 % C addition, the bright feature in the grain boundary is
vague, indicates that the enrichment of O can be alleviated by the minor
addition of C. Unfortunately, restricted by the resolution of TEM and
tiny content of O, C, we cannot further detect the distribution of O, C and
the atomic difference around the grain boundary. In the following, we
deeply analyzed the microstructure and mechanical properties of + 1C
DEDed RHEA.
To elucidate the intricate microstructure of the + 1C DEDed RHEA, a
thin foil positioned at the interface between the dendritic and inter-
dendritic regions was meticulously extracted via FIB technique and
subsequently subjected to TEM analysis ( Fig. 2 f and g). Based on the
selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern, a disordered BCC
structure was formed in both dendrite and interdendrite. The dendrite
kept a fully coherent orientation relationship with the interdendrite, and
no discernible presence of precipitates or ordered phases were detected.
Further examination revealed the presence of high-density dislocations
within the interdendritic region (dislocation density was estimated to be
6.07 × 10
12
m
 2
), characterized by the occurrence of long, straight
screw dislocations extending from the interdendritic to the dendritic
phase ( Fig. 2 g). During the laser scanning process inherent to DED, the
rapid thermal cycles experienced by the molten pools generate steep
temperature gradients, inducing substantial thermal stresses [ 18 ]. Once
the local stress exceeds the fractural strength of materials, micro-
cracking occurs. The nucleation and motion of dislocations can effec-
tively mitigate the stress concentration and release the strain energy,
consequently prevent the microcrack initiation [ 19 ]. Notably, the
dislocation density at the interdendrite in the + 1C DEDed RHEA is
significantly higher than that of dendrite ( Fig. 2 f and g), suggesting that,
the interdendrite possesses lower yield strength and local plastic
deformation caused by thermal stress mainly takes place there.

deformed, to geometrically accommodate the deformed continuity of
dendrite and interdendrite, the adjacent part of dendrite would proceed
cooperative deformation, mass of dislocations was produced in the
transitional region, close to the boundary of dendrite and interdendrite

phase heterostructure alloys [ 22 ]. An enlarged view of region B is pre-
sented in Fig. 4 c, revealing significant curved dislocations in it (pinning
points on the dislocations are indicated by the purple arrows). Precipi-
tation hardening is negligible in this alloy due to the absence of pre-
cipitates during high-temperature deformation. Thus, the pronounced
pinning effect exerted on the dislocations is attributed to severe local
lattice distortion, local chemical fluctuations (LCFs) and solute atoms,
similar to the casting CrMoNbV alloy reported before [ 13 ]. The
atomic-size mismatches among Cr (0.128 nm), Mo (0.140 nm), Nb
(0.147 nm), V (0.135 nm) induce substantial local lattice distortions,
leading to obvious hindering effect for the dislocation motion [ 23 ].
Additionally, for a HEA phase, its chemical distribution can hardly
achieve fully homogeneous, LCFs commonly exist on the atomic scale
[ 24 ]. Local lattice distortion induced by LCFs can act like the secondary
particles to pin the dislocations and produce dislocation loops [ 25 ]. Such
LCFs-induced pinning effect is similar to the detrapping events of
nano-scale segments, which have been reported in BCC HEAs [ 26 ].
Moreover, it should also be mentioned here that the potential influence
of interstitial carbon on the pinning effect of the + 1C DEDed RHEA. The
addition of carbon, except for the quantity preferentially segregated to
grain boundaries and suppressed the oxygen segregation, others of
carbon may act as interstitial element in the RHEA and affect the
dislocation motion [ 14 ]. The deformation mechanism of interdendrite
(region C) is similar to the border of dendrite (region B) ( Fig. 4 d), but the
severe deformation of interdendrite facilitates its dislocation multipli-
cation and interaction, producing abundant dislocation forests and
dislocation tangles [ 27 ], which could become barriers to hinder the
dislocation movement and contribute to the observed superior
high-temperature strength. The continuous piling up of dislocations
makes the formation of dislocation walls and have the possibility to
cause stress concentration ( Fig. 4 d and f), which will further cause the
crack nucleation and propagation [ 25 ].

from blended elemental powders via DED technique. Our findings
