ABSTRACT
Ti60 alloy with excellent high-temperature strength (820 MPa at 600 
◦
C) was fabricated via Directed Energy 
Deposition. However, silicide and Ti
3
Al precipitation are suppressed due to ultrahigh cooling rate, leading to its 
creep life being less than half that of forgings. Compositions of conventional titanium alloys need re-engineered 
for additive manufacturing.
1. Introduction
High-temperature titanium alloys, characterized by low density, 
high specific strength, and exceptional high-temperature performance, 
have become crucial engineering materials in aerospace industry to 
produce high-pressure compressors of jet engines and intermediate- 
pressure compressors in three-spool turbofan engines [ 1 – 3 ]. 
State-of-the-art high-temperature titanium alloys based on the 
Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Si system [ 4 ], including IMI834 [ 5 ], Ti1100 [ 6 ], and 
Ti60 [ 7 ], are designed to operate effectively at maximum service tem -
perature of 600 
◦
C. The development of these alloys is based on the 
near-equilibrium phase transformation process of casting and forging 
technologies, where excellent high-temperature properties are achieved 
through solid solution strengthening and dispersion strengthening 
mechanisms [ 8 , 9 ].
The advent of additive manufacturing (AM) technologies, including 
directed energy deposition (DED), has revolutionized the production of 
critical high-temperature components in aerospace engines, such as 
turbine blades and combustors [ 10 – 12 ]. In contrast to conventional 
methods, DED builds components layer by layer, enabling the produc -
tion of complex geometries [ 13 ]. However, the DED process introduces 
unique conditions such as high cooling rate [ 14 ] and repeated thermal 
cycling [ 15 ], which differ significantly from conventional metallurgical 
processes. The applicability of Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Si high-temperature ti -
tanium alloys, originally developed for conventional techniques, in DED 
processes and their ability to meet the intended performance standards 
have not yet been sufficiently explored.
Current studies on DED-fabricated high-temperature titanium alloys 
have mainly focused on printability and room-temperature properties. 
To data, DED can produce titanium alloys with a relative density of over 
99.9 % with optimized processing parameters [ 16 ]. However, the 
distinctive metallurgical behaviors inherent to DED result in significant 
differences in the microstructure compared to conventional methods. 
For instance, Tan et al. [ 17 ] reported that Ti-6Al-4V alloys fabricated via 
DED predominantly comprises metastable α ′ martensite instead of the 
stable α phase observed in conventionally processed counterparts. 
Similar results have been found in other high-temperature titanium 
alloy such as Ti6242 [ 18 ], TA19 [ 19 ], and TA15 [ 20 ], where the unique 
microstructural features significantly improve the room temperature 
tensile strength to 1224 MPa [ 21 ], far exceeding the levels achieved in 
forged counterparts. Nevertheless, systematic evaluation of the 
high-temperature performance of DED-fabricated titanium alloys re -
mains limited.
This work focuses on Ti60 alloy, which demonstrates excellent high- 
temperature properties when fabricated through conventional methods 
[ 22 ]. The primary objective is to investigate the high-temperature

3. Results
Fig. 1 (a) illustrates the engineering stress-strain curves of DED- 
fabricated Ti60 alloys at 25

printed alloys exhibit a remarkable UTS of up to 1.4 GPa at room tem -

This high-strength characteristic is maintained at elevated temperatures, 
achieving a UTS of 820 MPa, which corresponds to 58.2 % of the UTS at 
room temperature UTS and exceeds the forged standard (680 MPa [ 24 ]) 
by 20.6 %. Even under high-temperature conditions, the DED-fabricated 
Ti60 alloy maintains exceptional strength, which is critical for 
high-temperature applications. However, the room temperature elon -
gation ( δ ) of the Ti60 alloy is only 5.13 %, which is lower than the forged 
standard. At elevated temperatures, the elongation significantly in -
creases to 21.8 %, surpassing the forged standard (18 %). This demon -
strates that the high-temperature tensile properties of the 
DED-fabricated Ti60 alloy achieve a simultaneous enhancement in 
both strength and elongation compared with the forged Ti60 alloy [ 24 ].

tested at 600

600

DED-fabricated Ti60 alloy exhibits a much shorter creep rupture life of 
113 h. In contrast, the forged Ti60 alloys endure for 240 h and 133 h

These results clearly indicate that, although DED processing enhances 
the tensile strength of Ti60 alloy, it does not deliver comparable im -
provements in high-temperature creep resistance. In fact, the inferior 
creep performance of the DED alloy - even under the same or milder 
stress conditions-highlights the critical role of microstructural stability 
in determining creep resistance.
To investigate the correlation between the comprehensive mechan -
ical properties and the microstructure of the DED-fabricated Ti60 alloy, 
its microstructural characteristics were examined, as shown in Fig. 2 . 
The inverse pole figures (IPFs) in Fig. 2 (a) and (b) indicate that the grain 
morphology in the Ti60 sample primarily exhibits equiaxed grains, 
while the intragranular phase displays elongated laths. Quantitative 
analysis reveals an average grain size of approximately 108 μ m and an 
average intragranular phase width of 0.96 μ m for the DED-fabricated 
Ti60 sample. SEM image of the etched Ti60 sample is provided in 
Fig. S2 for further illustration. It can be seen that the microstructure of 
the as-DED Ti60 alloy consists of coarse prior β -grains, with fine, needle- 
like α ’ martensitic transformation products formed due to the ultrahigh 
cooling rates during the DED process. Additionally, TEM analysis reveals 
nanoscale twins within the DED-fabricated Ti60 alloy, as depicted in 
Fig. 2 (c). The diffraction spots in the inset further confirm the presence 
of twinning features. Such twin structures are uncommon in near- α ti -
tanium alloys, particularly those with high aluminum content, as 
aluminum typically suppresses twin formation and growth [ 25 ]. How -
ever, while mechanical twinning is rarely observed in near- α titanium 
alloys under plastic deformation, it has been reported in certain condi -
tions, such as in shot-peened IMI834 [ 26 ].

4. Discussion
The DED-fabricated Ti60 alloys exhibit a limited creep rupture life 
during high-temperature creep, primarily due to the absence of precip -
itated phases such as Ti
3
Al and silicides, as evidenced by the XRD results 
in Fig. 2 . This phenomenon can be explained using thermodynamic and 
kinetic theories, which highlight the influence of the unique metallur -
gical conditions in DED. The precipitation of Ti
3
Al and silicides is a 
diffusion-driven phase transformation process, governed by the rela -
tionship between the diffusion coefficient (D) and temperature (T), as 
described by the following equation [ 34 ]:

Based on the above analysis, the diffusion coefficient ( D ) increases

critical cooling rate ( R

alloy processing, where cooling rates typically range from 0.1 K/s to 10

temperatures for longer durations. This extended time promotes the 
diffusion of Al and Si atoms in the solid solution, enabling atomic

However, in this work, the Ti60 alloys were fabricated using directed 
energy deposition (DED) technology, characterized by higher cooling 
rates of approximately 10

mobility of Al and Si atoms. Consequently, the precipitation of Ti

behavior of the Ti

to that of silicides. This will not be discussed further here.
Regarding the formation of twin structures in the DED-fabricated 
Ti60 alloys, Lavogiez et al. [ 38 ] demonstrated that local twin struc -
tures originate from stress concentrations caused by dislocation pile-up. 
The high cooling rates intrinsic to the DED process facilitate dislocation 
accumulation within the Ti60 alloy, as depicted in Fig. S4(a) . Further -
more, the rapid solidification characteristic of DED induces the forma -
tion of α ′ martensite, which undergoes partial or complete 
decomposition during thermal cycling associated with successive layer 
deposition [ 17 ]. The decomposition of α ’ martensite primarily occurs 
through slip or twinning mechanisms [ 39 , 40 ]. However, numerous 
microstructural defects, such as stacking faults induced by high cooling 
rate, as shown in Fig. S4(b) , inhibit the shearing motion of slip. As a 
result, the twinning mechanism prevails, leading to the formation of 
twin structures within the Ti60 alloys.
This work underscores the intrinsic challenges faced by Ti-Al-Sn-Zr- 
Mo-Si high-temperature titanium alloys under DED conditions, partic -
ularly the lack of dispersed strengthening phases, such as Ti
3
Al and 
silicides, which undermines long-term creep performance. To address 
this, future alloy development for additive manufacturing should focus 
on re-engineering compositions, such as adjusting Al and Si contents, to 
promote the precipitation of strengthening phases even under rapid 
solidification conditions.
However, these adjustments must be carefully balanced, as they may 
lead to trade-offs, such as reduced ductility or increased solidification 
cracking sensitivity. To mitigate these risks, alloy composition modifi -
cations must be coordinated with optimized AM process parameters- 
such as laser power, scan speed, and layer thickness-which influence 
the thermal gradient and cooling rate. This coordinated approach en -
sures a balanced thermal input and solidification environment, enabling 
the formation of desired microstructures while minimizing negative side 
effects such as cracking or poor ductility.
In addition to composition and process design, the application of a 
tailored heat treatment regimen - such as solution treatment followed by 
aging - could further promote the formation of strengthening phases. 
Alternatively, introducing nanoscale reinforcing particles (e.g., ceramics 
or intermetallics) represents another promising route to strengthen AM- 
fabricated titanium alloys. Collectively, these strategies could advance 
the development of AM-specific titanium alloys with improved high- 
temperature performance, thereby expanding the applicability of addi -
tive manufacturing in structural applications.
5. Conclusions
This work evaluates the high-temperature tensile and creep proper -
ties of Ti60 alloys fabricated via directed energy deposition (DED) and 
reveals the underlying mechanisms through microstructural analysis. 
The high cooling rates led to the formation of ultrafine α ′ martensite and 
nanoscale twins, resulting in exceptional tensile properties, with a room 
temperature tensile strength of 1.4 GPa and a 600 
◦
C tensile strength of 
820 MPa. These values represent improvements of 27.3 % and 20.6 %, 
respectively, compared to forged alloys. However, the DED Ti60 alloy 
showed limited creep performance, with a rupture life of only 113 h 
under 600 
◦
C/150 MPa, less than half that of the forged counterparts. 
This behavior was attributed to the suppression of dispersed strength -
ening phases of Ti
3
Al and silicides due to the high cooling rates. This 
work provides valuable insights into how rapid solidification during 
DED affects microstructure and performance, offering valuable guidance 
for the compositional and microstructural design of AM-specific high- 
temperature titanium alloys.
