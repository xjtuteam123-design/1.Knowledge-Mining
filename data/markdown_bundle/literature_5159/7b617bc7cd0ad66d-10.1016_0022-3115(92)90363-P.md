Optimum alloy compositions in reduced-activation martensitic 9Cr

F. Abe, T. Noda and M. Okada 
National Research Institute for Metals, Sengen l-2-1, Tsukuba 305, Japan 
Received 18 March 1992; accepted 20 April 1992 
In order to obtain potential reduced-activation ferritic steels suitable for fusion reactor structures, the effect of alloying 
elements W and V on the microstructural evolution, toughness, high-temperature creep and irradiation hardening behavior 
was investigated for simple 9Cr-W and 9Cr-V steels. The creep strength of the 9Cr-W steels increased but their toughness 
decreased with increasing W concentration. The 9Cr-V steels exhibited poor creep rupture strength, far below that of a 
conventional 9Cr-IMoVNb steel and poor toughness after aging at 873 K. It was also found that the A-ferrite should be 
avoided, because it degraded both the roughness and high-temperature creep strength. Based on the results on the simple 
steels, optimized martensitic 9Cr steels were alloy-designed from a standpoint of enough thoughness and high-temperature 
creep strength. Two kinds of optimized 90 steels with low and high levels of W were obtained; 9Cr-1WVTa and 
9Cr-3WVTa. These steels indeed exhibited excellent toughness and creep strength, respectively. The 9Cr-1WVTa steel 
exhibiting an excellent roughness was shown to be the most promising for relatively low-temperature application below 
5oo”C, where irradiation embrittlement is significant. The 9Cr-3WVTa steel was the most promising for high temperature 
application above 500°C from the standpoint of enough high-temperature strength. 
1. Introduction 
Ferritic steels are being considered as alternate 
candidate structural materials to austenitic Type 316 
stainless steel (ss) for application to first wall and 
blanket structures of demonstration (DEMO) and com- 
mercial fusion reactors [1,2]. A primary reason for use 
of ferritic steels is a higher resistance to swelling com- 
pared to Type 316 ss [1,3]. Ferritic steels also have 
advantages of higher thermal conductivity and lower 
thermal expansion than Type 316 ss. Cr-Mo steels 
such as 2.25Cr-lMo, 9Cr-1MoVNb and 12Cr- 
1MoVW have been considered as leading candidates 
for fusion reactor applications. 
Since guidelines for the classification and disposal 
of low-level nuclear wastes were issued by the United 
States Nuclear Regulatory Commission (referred as 10 
CFR Part 61) [4] in the early 198Os, the development of 
reduced activation ferritic steels has been promoted in 
the US, Japan and European Community [5-71. In 
reduced-activation ferritic steels, W, V and Ta can be 
used to substitute for less desirable elements MO and 
Nb, because they do not transmute to long-lived ra- 
dioactive nuclides and behave like MO and Nb in 
ferritic steels [8,9]. Other alloying elements that must 
be minimized include Ni, Cu and N. Various reduced- 
activation ferritic steels such as 2.25Cr-2WV, 9Cr- 
2WVTa, 12Cr-2WV and so on have been proposed. 
However, a systematic variation of alloying elements 
Cr, W and V on the materials properties of this class of 
steel has not been conducted. Such a systematic study 
would be necessary for a detailed understanding of the 
behavior of Cr-WVTa steels as well as in the develop- 
ment of an optimized steel. 
We have investigated systematically the effect of 
alloying elements Cr, W and V on the microstructure 
and mechanical properties of reduced-activation Cr-W 
base ferritic steels [lo-191. In previous reports 
[14,18,19], we revealed that the ductile-to-brittle transi- 
tion temperature (DBlT) increased only slightly but 
the creep strength increased significantly with increas- 
ing Cr concentration from 2 to about 10% in Cr-2W- 
O.lC steels, where the steels consisted of the bainite or 
martensite phase. However, the DBTT increased 
abruptly and the creep strength decreased at high Cr

3. Experimental results 
3.1. Phase stability of simple 9Cr- W and 9Cr-V steels 
As shown in table 2, only the 9Cr-4W, 9Cr-1V and 
9Cr-2Mo steels consisted of the dual phases of 
martensite and S-ferrite and other steels were observed 
to be fully martensitic, suggesting that the S-ferrite 
forms at high concentrations of ferrite forming ele- 
ments such as W, V and MO. Taking into account that 
the atomic concentration of W, V and MO is 1.21, 1.13 
and 1.16% in the 9Cr-4W, 9Cr-1V and 9Cr-2Mo 
steels, respectively, it appears that the critical concen- 
tration of W, V and MO for the formation of h-ferrite 
is near 1 at%, corresponding to about 3.3 wt% W, 0.9 
wt% V and 1.7 wt% MO, in the 9Cr steel with 0.1 
wt% C. The critical concentration of W at austenite/ 
(austenite + ferrite) phase boundary in Fe-Cr-W 
ternary system without C is reported to be only about 1 
wt%, corresponding to about 0.3 at% at 1473 K when 
the concentration of Cr is 9 wt% [22]. Therefore, the 
addition of 0.1 wt% C to the 9Cr-W steels increases 
the critical W concentration for the formation of 6-fcr- 
rite from 1 wt% (0.3 at%) to about 3 wt% (1 at%).

3.2. Toughness of simple 9Cr- W and 9Cr-V steels 
Fig. 4 shows the DBTT of the 9Cr-W and 9Cr-V 
steels in the as-tempered conditions and after aging at 
873 K for 3600 ks (1000 h). In the as-tempered condi- 
tions, the 9Cr-V steels exhibited an excellent tough- 
ness, as shown by the lowest DBTT, 175-205 K, of the 
steels examined. With increasing W or V concentra- 
tion, the DBTT first decreased and then increased 
after reaching a minimum at about 1% W or 0.25% V. 
The decrease in DBIT by the small addition of W or V 
was due to a refinement of carbides formed during 
tempering compared to those in the 9Cr steel without 
W or V. The higher DBIT at the highest concentra- 
tion of W or V, the 9Cr-4W and 9Cr-IV steels was 
mainly due to the presence of the S-ferrite. In the 
previous report [11,18], the DB’IT was measured as a 
function of volume fraction of the h-ferrite for Cr- 
2W-O.lC steels with various Cr concentration and the 
increase in DBTT per unit volume percent of the 
S-ferrite was evaluated to be about 2 K/vol% of &fcr- 
rite. Using this value, the increase in DBTT due to 
S-ferrite is estimated to be 20 and 14 K for the 9Cr-4W 
and 9Cr-IV steels, respectively. Therefore, the differ- 
ence in DBTT, 33 K, between the dual phase 9Cr-4W 
and martensitic 9Cr-2W steel and that, 17 K, between 
the dual phase 9Cr-1V and martensitic 9Cr-0.5V steels

s-ferrite.

toughness 
The 9Cr-V steels exhibited poor creep rupture 
strength far below that of 9Cr-1MoVNb steel and 
poor toughness as shown by a large increase in DB’IT 
due to aging at 873 K (figs. 3 and 8). The large addition 
of V higher than 0.25% resulted in a decrease in creep 
rupture strength, suggesting that V appears to be effec- 
tive as a precipitation strengthening element due to 
V,C, rather than as a solid solution strengthening 
element in the martcnsitic 9Cr steels. Therefore, the 
combination of V with solid solution strengthening 
elements such as W is better to improve strength and 
toughness rather than the addition of V alone. 
The creep rupture strength of the 9Cr-W steels 
increased but their toughness decreased with increas- 
ing W concentration. The highest W steel 9Cr-4W 
exhibited enough creep rupture strength, as high as the 
9Cr-lMoVNb, but poor toughness of the highest 
DBTT, as high as near room temperature after aging 
at 873 K for 3600 ks (1000 h). On the other hand, the 
lowest W steel 9Cr-1W exhibited an excellent tough- 
ness but poor creep rupture strength, far below that of 
the 9Cr-1MoVNb. Thus the simultaneous optimization 
of creep rupture strength and toughness is very diffi- 
cult by varying the concentration of W alone in the 
framework of the simple 9Cr-W steels. In the present 
research, the improvement of strength was attempted 
for the 9Cr-1W steel which exhibited an excellent 
roughness and the improvement of toughness was at- 
tempted for the 9Cr-4W steel which exhibited an 
excellent strength. 
The creep rupture strength of the 9Cr-1W steel 
was due to solid solution strengthening by W and Cr 
and precipitation-strengthening due to M-&,. The 
creep rates and creep strength of the present marten- 
sitic steels correlated with the microstructural evolu- 
tion. For reducing the microstructural evolution, a de- 
crease in self-diffusion rates or pinning of lath bound- 
aries by fine carbides are required. MC type carbides 
are promising for pinning lath boundaries effectively 
compared to M,,C,, because the agglomeration rates 
of MC are very low. Interfacial segregating impurities 
such as B are also effective for pinning or locking lath 
boundaries. For reducing self-diffusion rates in the 
martensitic 9Cr steels, the addition of W and or MO 
are effective. However, the addition of W is not appro- 
priate because it causes an increase in DBTT as de- 
scribed above and MO is not acceptable from a view- 
point of its high induced activity. Therefore, only the 
addition of precipitation strengthening elements and 
interfacial segregating elements is effective for the 
improvement of creep strength of the 9Cr-IW steel 
without any degradation in toughness. In conventional 
Cr-Mo steels, the Va group eiements, V, Nb and Ta, 
in the periodic table can form fine MC type carbides. 
Fujita and Takahashi [X] reported that V,C, and NbC 
were very effective to improve long period creep rup- 
ture strength without any degradation in total elonga- 
tion for 12% Cr heat-resisting steel and that the opti- 
mum amounts of V and Nb to obtain a maximum creep 
rupture strength at 873 K were about 0.2% V and 
0.1% Nb. In the present steel, Nb is not acceptable 
from a viewpoint of its high induced-activity, but Ta 
can be used instead of Nb. The optimum concentration 
of Ta in the present YCr-1W steel may be about 0.1% 
by analogy for Nb described above and that of V is 
about 0.2% as determined in fig. 8. It is also well 
known that a small addition of boron, about 50 ppm, 
improves creep strength as well as ductility of Ni-base 
superalloys through a segregation along grain bound- 
aries f32]. Therefore, boron was also added in the 
present steels by analogy for Ni-base superalloys.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Optimum alloy compositions in reduced activation 
martensitic 9Cr steels for application to fusion devices 
Fig. 21 shows the change in yield stress AuY due to 
irradiation for the various 9Cr martensitic steels in- 
cluding the present steels, as a function of irradiation 
and test temperature. The results on the present 9Cr- 
1WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa steels were obtained after 
light damage below 1 dpa, while those on other steels 
[42-441 were for heavy damage above 7 dpa. The AuY 
for heavy damage shown by the hatched zone exhibits 
that the irradiation hardening becomes more signifi- 
cant with decreasing irradiation temperature below 
723-773 K (450-5OO”C), however irradiation enhances 
softening above 773 K (500°C). This suggests that irra- 
diation response to hardening changes at around 773 K 
from hardening to softening. The recovery of irradia- 
tion hardening correlated with the annealing out of 
irradiation produced defects or defect dusters. Klueh 
et al. [43] reported on 9Cr-1MoVNb steel that irradia- 
tion-enhanced recovery of excess dislocations and car- 
bide agglomeration caused softening at irradiation

temperatures above 773 K. The similar results were 
reported by Gelles et al. [45,46]. As described before, 
softening also occurred signi~cantly in the martensitic 
9Cr steels during thermal creep by stress-assisted mi- 
crostructural evolution. Therefore, creep-enhanced 
softening would be superimposed on irradiation-en- 
hanced softening in the martensitic 9Cr steels at high 
temperatures above 773 K. 
The change in irradiation response to hardening at 
around 773 K suggests that the alloy designing concept 
should be different depending on whether the operat- 
ing temperature of the first wall and blanket structures 
in fusion reactors will be lower or higher than 773 K. 
The steels, the operating temperature of which will be 
lower than 773 K, should have higher resistance to 
irradiation hardening. Because irradiation hardening 
causes an increase in DBTT, enough toughness will be 
the primary factor for the development of the steels 
operating at low temperatures below 773 K, rather 
than high-temperature strength. On the other hand,the 
steels, the operating temperature of which will be 
higher than 773 K, should have higher resistance 
to softening. Therefore, enough high-temperature 
strength will be the primary factor for the steels oper- 
ating at high temperatures above 773 K. 
According to the design concept of the Steady State 
Tokamak Reactor (SSTR) [47], which is a demonstra- 
tion-type fusion power reactor designed at Japan 
Atomic Energy Research Institute, the blanket struc- 
ture is composed of a reduced-activation martensitic 
steel 8Cr-2WVTa which is cooled by pressurized wa- 
ter. The temperature of the water at the outlet side in 
the blanket is designed at 598 K (325°C) and a part of 
the blanket structure reaches at 723-773 K (45O-S~O~) 
at maximum, indicating that the operating temperature 
is below 500°C. Thus, the present design concept of a 
fusion power reactor using the martensitic steel sug- 
gests that the blanket structure will operate normally 
at temperatures below 500°C. Therefore, the primary 
factor for the development of redu~d-activation fer- 
ritic/martensitic steels for the first wall and blanket 
structures should be fracture toughness. 
The martensitic 9Cr-1WVTa steel showed an excel- 
lent toughness in the as-tempered condition and even 
after long-term aging as well as after neutron irradia- 
tion. This steel showed much lower DBTT than the 
9Cr-1MoVNb in the as-tempered condition and a 
enough high-temperature strength as high as the 9Cr- 
1MoVNb. Therefore, it is concluded that the 9Cr- 
1WVTa steel is the most promising from the stand- 
point of enough toughness for the first wall and blan- 
ket structures, which will be operated at relatively low 
temperatures below 500°C. The other martensitic 9Cr- 
3WVTa steel is the most promising from the stand- 
point of enough high-temperature creep strength for 
future power reactor structures which will be operated 
at high temperatures above 500°C. 
5. Conclusions 
(I) The maximum concentration of W in the 
martensite phase of 9Cr-W-O.lC steel was 3%, while 
that of V in 9Cr-V-O.lC steel was 0.9%. The Laves 
phase Fe,W precipitated in the S-ferrite of the dual 
phase 9Cr-4W steel during aging at around 873 K but 
not in the as-tempered condition. The fine V,C, car- 
bides precipitated densely in the s-ferrite of the dual 
phase 9Cr-IV steel already in the as-tempered condi- 
tion. 
(2) The creep rupture strength of the 9Cr-W steels 
increased but their toughness decreased with increas- 
ing W concentration. The 9Cr-V steels exhibited poor 
creep rupture strength far below that of 9Cr-1MoVNb 
steel and poor toughness after aging at 873 K. For the 
improvement of toughness, the S-ferrite should be 
eliminated. For the improvement of creep strength, the 
microstructural evolution during creep should be re- 
duced. 
(3) Two kinds of martensitic 9Cr-WVTa steels with 
low and high levels of W were alloy-designed by taking 
into account higher toughness and creep strength; 
9Cr-1WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa steels. The low-W steel 
9Cr-1WVTa exhibited an excellent toughness, as 
shown by much lower DB’IT than 9Cr-1MoVNb and 
high creep strength as high as 9Cr-1MoVNb. The 
high-W steel 9Cr-3WVTa exhibited an excellent creep 
strength much higher than 9Cr-1MoVNb and good 
toughness as high as 9Cr-1MoVNb. 
(4) The 9Cr-1WVTa steel was the most promising 
for relatively low-temperature application below 500°C 
from the standpoint of enough toughness. The 9Cr- 
3WVTa steel was the most promising for high tempera- 
ture application above 500°C from the standpoint of 
enough creep strength.
