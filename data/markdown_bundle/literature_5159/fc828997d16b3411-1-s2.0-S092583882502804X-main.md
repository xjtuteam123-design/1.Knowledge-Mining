ABSTRACT
Cu-Cr-Nb is a high-strength, high-conductivity copper alloy that outperforms most copper alloys, particularly at 
high temperatures. Due to limited powder acquisition channels, most additive manufacturing research on Cu-Cr- 
Nb alloys focuses on systems with a Cr/Nb ratio of 2:1, particular on Cu-4Cr-2Nb (at%). However, there are few 
studies on the microstructure and properties of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with different Cr/Nb ratios prepared by additive 
manufacturing. This study utilizes ball-milled elemental powders of Cu, Cr, and Nb as raw materials for laser 
directed energy deposition (L-DED) to fabricate five different Cu-Cr-Nb alloys, maintaining a total alloy content 
of approximately 6 at%. At the micron and submicron scales, Nb-rich alloys consisted primarily of Nb particles 
and Cr
2
Nb, while Cr-rich alloys mainly comprised Cr particles and Cr
2
Nb. Notably, nano-scaled Cr precipitates 
and dense dislocations were observed in all as-built alloys. Alloys with different Cr/Nb ratios did not exhibit 
significant differences in mechanical and electrical properties in the as-built state. During the direct aging 
process, the residual supersaturated solution continued to decompose and coarsen the Cr precipitates formed 
during intrinsic heat treatment. As a result, hardness remained largely unchanged, while electrical conductivity 
increased to above 70 % IACS. The hardness of Cu-1Cr-5Nb, Cu-2Cr-4Nb, and Cu-3Cr-3Nb showed only slight 
changes, while both the hardness and conductivity of Cu-4Cr-2Nb and Cu-5Cr-1Nb alloys were notably enhanced 
after aging at 400 
◦
C for 24 hours followed by solid solution treatment. Specifically, a hardness of 117.4 HV and a 
conductivity of 86 % IACS were obtained in Cu-4Cr-2Nb, while 124.8 HV and 84 % IACS were achieved in Cu- 
5Cr-1Nb. This work provides valuable insights into the microstructure and properties of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with 
varying Cr/Nb ratios, fabricated using L-DED with elemental powder mixtures. The findings also offer practical 
guidance for optimizing composition and heat treatment in the production of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys.
1. Introduction
Cu-Cr-Nb is a high-strength, high-conductivity copper alloy designed 
for high-temperature applications. It features a low thermal expansion 
coefficient, excellent high-temperature strength and creep resistance, 
making it the preferred material for the main combustion chamber of the 
new-generation of rocket engines. The superior performance of Cu-Cr- 
Nb is primarily attributed to the thermally stable Laves phase Cr
2
Nb 
(melting point 2043 K [1] ) dispersed within the matrix. However, if the 
Cr/Nb ratio cannot be precisely controlled to 2:1, excess Cr-rich or 
Nb-rich phases may form. Due to the low room temperature solubility of 
Cr and Nb in Cu (0.03 wt% for Cr, and Nb being nearly insoluble), and 
their high mutual affinity, the second phase in Cu-Cr-Nb alloys can 
become very coarse during preparation [2] . If uncontrolled, this coars -
ening can significantly degrade the alloy ’ s mechanical properties.
Two primary factors governed second-phase size: composition and 
manufacturing methods. First, different compositions significantly affect 
the size of second phase [3 – 5] . Zhong et al. [4] found that reducing Cr 
and Nb content could delay precipitation, shortening the solidification 
process. Our previous work demonstrated that increasing Cr and Nb 
content causes Cr
2
Nb to transition from dispersed granules to contin -
uous rod-shaped structures, accompanied by a significant increase in 
number and size [5] . Second, manufacturing methods also impact the 
size of second-phase due to variations in solidification rates. For 
instance, Ellis et al. [1,6] observed severe Cr
2
Nb aggregation in vacuum 
induction-melted Cu-Cr-Nb master alloys, with sizes exceeding 1 mm

Moreover, the limited solubility of Cr and Nb in Cu poses significant 
challenges in producing Cu-Cr-Nb alloy powders with uniform compo -

additive manufacturing using Cu-Cr-Nb alloy powder has focused on a 
fixed Cr/Nb ratio of 2:1 [7 – 11,14 – 18] , particularly focusing on 
Cu-4Cr-2Nb with a total alloy content of about 6 at%. Investigations into 
non-stoichiometric Cu-Cr-Nb alloys remain scarce.
Given the in-situ alloying capability of laser additive manufacturing 
[19,20] , it is feasible to prepare high-density Cu-Cr-Nb alloys using 
mechanically mixing or ball milling elemental powders as raw materials 
[5] . This approach reduces dependence on pre-alloyed powders and 
allows for convenient adjustment of alloy proportions. In this study, we 
investigated the microstructure and properties of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with 
different Cr/Nb ratios fabricated by laser directed energy deposition 
(L-DED). Elemental powders of Cu, Cr, and Nb were ball-milled and used 
to produce five different Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with Cr/Nb ratios of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 
2, and 5, while maintaining a total alloy content of approximately 6 at%. 
The microstructure of the printed alloys with varying Cr/Nb ratios were 
observed and characterized, with size distribution and changes in the 
second phase illustrated through schematic diagrams. The mechanical 
and electrical properties after direct aging or solid solution treatment 
followed by aging were tested. The study explains the performance 
variation trends, expands the foundational database for laser-printed 
Cu-Cr-Nb alloy powders, and clarifies the mechanisms and advantages 
underlying the performance of L-DED-fabricated compositions.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Powder preparation
The Cu powder used for ball milling was sourced from Asian New 
Materials (Beijing) Co., Ltd. and is spherical with a size distribution of 
53 – 150 μ m. The Cr and Nb powders, obtained from Shandong Sitaili 
Metal Materials Co., Ltd., are mechanically crushed into irregular shapes 
with sizes ranging from 5 to 10 μ m. These powders were weighed and 
proportioned according to the desired ratios. The ball milling process 
was conducted using a QM-3SP4-CL planetary gear ball mill from 
Nanjing Nanda Instrument Co., Ltd., with a maximum capacity of 4 li -
ters. Pure Cu balls were chosen as the milling media to minimize im -
purities. To prevent overheating and oxidation, the milling was 
periodically paused for 10 minutes every 30 minutes under an Ar at -
mosphere. Following milling, the ground powder was sieved through an 
80-mesh sieve to ensure uniformity. After milling at 250 rpm for 4 hours, 
pre-treated powders with different proportions of Cu-Cr-Nb were ob -
tained and used as raw materials for laser additive manufacturing.
2.2. L-DED process and heat treatment
The prepared powder was loaded into a feeder for L-DED equipment 
(LDM6000 – 2, Xi ’ an YDLaser Co., Ltd.) equipped with a 1060 nm 
infrared laser and operating under argon with O
2 
levels kept below 
200 ppm. Based on prior investigations, the manufacturing parameters 
were optimized to a laser power of 900 W, a scanning speed of 10 mm/s, 
and a preheating temperature of 100 
◦

ponents with five different compositions and varying Cr/Nb ratios were 
produced in batches, each reaching ~15 mm in height — sufficient for 
tensile specimen extraction. The as-built Cu-Cr-Nb alloys (AB state) 
produced via L-DED were labeled AB15, AB24, AB33, AB42, and AB51 
according to their target Cr/Nb ratios. Actual compositions were 
measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), 
as detailed in Table 1 , yielding Cr/Nb atomic ratios of approximately 
0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5.( Fig. 1 )

Instrument and Equipment Co., Ltd., China) with a 200 g load and a 10 s

2.4. Microstructure characterization
The powder samples, obtained after ball milling, were examined 
using various microscopy techniques to assess their microstructural 
characteristics. Characterization was conducted using a combination of 
metallographic microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM- 
IT800, JEOL Ltd.), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD; NordlysMax 
2, Oxford Instruments), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, 
JEM-F200 multi-purpose electron microscope from JEOL Ltd.) with 
energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). For EBSD analysis, samples were 
first polished with 5000-grit sandpaper, followed by fine polishing with 
a 0.05 μ m silica suspension, and then vibratory polished for 3 hours. 
EBSD data were processed using Aztec software, with a step size of 2.5 
μ m at 100x magnification. For TEM sample preparation, specimens were 
initially mechanically ground to a thickness of 50 μ m and then thinned 
using an automatic twin-jet electro-polisher. The electrolyte for twin-jet 
polishing was a nitric acid-methanol mixture (1:3), with the process 
conducted at 30 
◦
C. High-resolution TEM imaging and EDS mapping 
were performed on the same JEM-F200 instrument equipped with two 
JEOL spectrometers.

3.2. Laser forming and post-heat treatment
Fig. 3 a presents macroscopic images of the walls printed using Cu-Cr- 
Nb powders with varying Cr/Nb ratios, along with illustrations indi -
cating the building direction (BD), scanning direction (SD), and trans -
verse direction (TD). Each Cu-Cr-Nb wall is approximately 15 mm in 
height, indicating stable process parameters and consistent molding. 
The walls for each Cr/Nb ratio were sectioned and prepared for cross- 
sectional metallographic observation. The analysis revealed a dense 
structure with minimal porosity. Image-based statistical analysis 
confirmed that the densities of the five samples exceeded 99 %. The 
actual densities-measured by the Archimedes method-were presented in 
Figure S1 . In samples with high Nb content, AB15 ( Fig. 3 b) and AB24 
( Fig. 3 c), numerous coarse second-phase particles were observed within 
the blocks. As the Cr content increased and Nb content decreased, the 
prevalence of coarse second-phase particles gradually diminished, 
leading to improved structural uniformity ( Fig. 3 d-f).
The hardness and electrical conductivity of the printed Cu-Cr-Nb 
alloys, measured in both the AB state and AB + SS state, were initially 
recorded ( Fig. 4 ). In the AB state, the hardness values of the alloys were 
generally around 100 HV, regardless of the Cr/Nb ratio. However, Cr- 
rich alloys (AB42, AB51) exhibited higher conductivity compared to 
Nb-rich alloys (AB15, AB24, AB33), with conductivity generally 
increasing as the Cr/Nb ratio increased. Following solid solution treat -
ment, hardness did not decrease significantly for most samples, with the 
largest reduction — 6.4 % — observed in AB51; the reduction in hardness 
for the other alloys was within the margin of error. In contrast, electrical 
conductivity showed a more noticeable decline after solid solution 
treatment: Nb-rich alloys experienced a slight decrease, whereas Cr-rich 
alloys underwent a more substantial reduction. These results suggested 
that the Cr/Nb ratios and solid solution treatment affected the properties 
of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys, with Cr-rich alloys showing higher initial conduc -
tivity and greater sensitivity to solid solution treatment.
Direct aging of the AB samples resulted in minimal changes in 
hardness. However, aging of the AB + SS samples produced notable dif -
ferences in the hardness of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with different Cr/Nb ratios 
( Fig. 5 a1-a5). Initially, all the AB + SS + AG alloys had lower hardness 
compared to the corresponding AB + DA alloys. This trend reversed as 
the aging time was extended. AB + SS + AG42 and AB + SS + AG51 
exhibited obvious aging hardening. After aging at 400 
◦

hardness of AB + SS + AG42 and AB + SS + AG51 reached 115.5 HV and 
121.2 HV, reflecting the increments of 15.3 % and 28.0 % compared to 
AB + SS42 and AB + SS51, respectively. Notably, after aging for 12 h, 
AB + SS + AG51 reached the peak hardness of 125 HV.
All the Cu-Cr-Nb alloys in AB state exhibited an increase in electrical 
conductivity ranging from 10 % to 15 % IACS after direct aging at 400 
◦
C for 24 h, with a continuing upward trend (Figure 5b1-b5). All the Cu- 
Cr-Nb alloys in AB + SS + AG state showed a more pronounced increase in 
conductivity compared to their AB + DA state. Although the initial con -
ductivity of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys after solid solution treatment was relatively 
low, it surpassed the levels of the corresponding direct aging state after 
aging for 24 h. The conductivity improvements in the AB + SS + AG 
samples followed distinct trends dependent on the Cr/Nb ratios. Spe -
cifically, the conductivity of the five AB + SS + AG alloys increased by 
27.6 %, 26.4 %, 28.1 %, 33.1 %, and 40.2 % IACS as the Cr/Nb ratio 
increased, when aged for 24 h. Notably, Cr-rich alloys demonstrated 
more substantial conductivity improvements following solid solution 
and aging treatment, indicating a more pronounced effect of Cr content 
on elevating electrical conductivity.
The tensile properties of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with various Cr/Nb ratios in 
AB state were generally similar with each other except AB51 ( Fig. 6 a). 
Among the five alloys, AB51 exhibited the highest ultimate strength and 
AB42 demonstrated the most balanced mechanical properties with a 
tensile strength of 312.2 MPa and an elongation of 44.1 %. In the 
AB + DA state, the tensile strength of the alloys remains almost un -
changed compared to their AB state. After solid solution and aging 
treatments, the tensile strength of all alloys improved. The 
AB + SS + AG51 alloy exhibited the most significant increase in ultimate 
tensile strength (UTS), surpassing 400 MPa, with a slight reduction in 
elongation.

In addition, high-density dislocations were observed in all AB alloys. 
Fig. 11 showed the complex dislocation network in AB15 and AB51. 
More dislocations were observed at the interface between the second

Fig. 12 a1-a4 showed the EBSD observations of AB15. The second 
phases were identified as Nb-rich phase through EBSD. Due to the dif -
ferences in thermal expansion coefficients and the relatively soft Cu 
matrix, repeated thermal cycling during L-DED produced incompatible 
strains, driving a marked increase in dislocation density on the Cu side of 
the interface. The kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps in 
Fig. 12 a3 were used to quantify the average local misorientation around 
a specific grain, as it is related to the density of geometrically necessary 
dislocations (GND) [26] . In Fig. 12 a4, KAM angles plotted across two 
arrows at the Nb-rich boundary showed an abrupt rise at the interface, 
confirming severe lattice distortion there. Most GNDs accumulated at 
the grain boundaries of the soft phase, resulting in a higher KAM value 
on the adjacent Cu matrix side, while the Nb-rich phase interior 
remained relatively dislocation-free [27] .
Fig. 12 c1-c4 showed the EBSD results for AB51. Although there was 
also a higher KAM value at the Cr/Cu interface (yellow dashed line), it 
did not significantly affect the average KAM value in the Cu-Cr-Nb alloy 
(Figure 12c3).
After direct aging, the dislocation network was retained under the 
pinning effect of the second phase. As shown in Fig. 13 a-b, in AB + DA15,

4.1. Beneficial effects of L-DED and the evolution of microstructure

ball-milled powder in this study offers several advantages. First, L-DED 
provides an in-situ alloying method, allowing for flexible adjustment of 
the alloy composition. This not only enables the printing of Cu-Cr-Nb 
alloys using a mixture of Cu, Cr, and Nb elemental powders, but also 
allows for the flexible adjustment of the Cr/Nb ratio. This approach 
facilitates the production of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys and the formation of Cr

In summary, L-DED offers three key advantages: (1) it provides in- 
situ alloying of elemental powders with high fabrication flexibility, 
eliminating the need for pre-alloyed Cu-Cr-Nb powders; (2) its high 
cooling rate and non-equilibrium solidification reduce segregation and 
facilitate the production of Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with dense dislocations; (3) 
its IHT effect promotes the decomposition of the supersaturated solu -
tion, producing nano-sized Cr particles, and reduces or eliminates the 
need for post-heat treatment processes in additive manufacturing, thus 
shortening production cycles and lowering costs.
Fig. 14 illustrated the microstructural evolution during L-DED pro -
cessing. In the topmost deposition layer, Cu, with its low melting tem -
perature, melted first, forming a melt pool and constituting the matrix of 
the alloy. Irregularly shaped Cr and Nb then melt at higher temperatures 
and enter the metastable miscibility gap [34] , causing liquid-phase 
separation into Cr-rich and Nb-rich droplets. Vigorous Marangoni flow 
in the molten pool facilitated the interaction of Cr and Nb droplets [35] , 
leading to the formation of Cr
2
Nb. While the unreacted liquid phases 
spheroidized under the action of surface tension and remain during 
solidification. At the same time, the rapid non-equilibrium solidification 
not only retained a supersaturated solution with a large amount of Cr 
dissolved in it [34] , but also induced strong internal stress and lattice 
distortion, which promoted the generation of dislocations [35] . The 
subsequent deposition layers and the IHT effect caused the supersatu -
rated solution to decompose, leading to the formation of Cr 
nano-precipitates in both Cr-rich and Nb-rich systems. Direct aging or 
aging after solid solution treatment did not affect the distribution of 
micron-sized second phases.
4.2. Variation of properties before and after aging
Based on the TEM observation results and the analysis of micro -
structural changes, it is evident that Cr exhibited a more pronounced 
change in solubility compared to Nb, leading to its dominance in the 
precipitation process during IHT. This explained the presence of Cr 
nano-precipitates in all as-built alloys, even those with Cr/Nb ratios 
below 2:1 (Nb-rich alloys). In the high-temperature L-DED environment, 
Cr attained high solubility, forming a supersaturated Cr solution that 
both enabled synchronous decomposition during IHT and greatly 
increased the driving force for precipitation. As a result, abundant nano- 
Cr precipitates formed ( Fig. 9 c), allowing the as-built alloys to achieve 
conductivities exceeding 60 % IACS in the AB state. In contrast, alloys 
such as Cu-0.9Cr [36] , Cu-1.3Cr [29] , Cu-0.95Cr-0.06Y [36] , 
Cu-0.26Cr-0.15Zr [37] and Cu-1.65Cr-1.35Nb-0.12Ti [38] exhibited 
conductivity below 50 % IACS in the AB state before aging, despite their 
total alloying element contents being significantly lower than the total 
Cr and Nb content in the alloys studied in this work.
In Nb-rich alloys with a Cr/Nb ratio below 2, Cr reacted predomi -
nantly with Nb, leaving only a minor amount of Cr in solid solution. As a 
result, more nano-Cr precipitates formed in AB24 ( Fig. 9 e) than in AB15. 
This also explained why AB15 and AB + SS15 exhibited slightly higher 
hardness compared to AB24 and AB + SS24.
In Cr-rich alloys, Cr cannot fully react with Nb to form Cr
2
Nb under 
equilibrium conditions theoretically. The unreacted Cr increased su -
persaturation and accelerated Cr nanoprecipitation during IHT, sub -
stantially boosting both strength and conductivity.
Consequently, Cr-rich alloys initially showed higher conductivity 
and a more significant conductivity increase during direct aging 
compared to Nb-rich alloys.
During the later stage of direct aging, since IHT had caused most of 
the Cr precipitates to nucleate during the previous printing process, the 
density of Cr precipitates increased slightly, and coarsening occurred 
[39] ( Fig. 13 a-b). However, dislocations were still present and pinned 
by second-phase particles (as shown in Fig. 13 a-d). These combined 
effects explained why hardness did not change substantially. Never -
theless, conductivity increased slightly as coarsening consumed 
remaining Cr solute atoms. The hardness of AB + DA51 decreased after 
direct aging for 24 hours.
In AB + SS + AG alloys, the amount of Cr in Nb-rich alloys was limited, 
and thus, there was no strong strengthening effect after solid solution 
and aging treatment. Consequently, hardness and conductivity 
improved only marginally compared to AB + DA alloys. For Cr-rich al -
loys, solid solution treatment dissolved significant Cr, forming a Cr su -
persaturated solution, which reduced both strength and conductivity 
compared to the AB state. Upon aging, the Cr nano-precipitates gener -
ated, leading to substantial increases in both strength and conductivity. 
After 24 h aging, the coarsening of Cr precipitates caused a slight 
hardness decrease, but conductivity surpassed that of corresponding 
AB + DA alloys.
Given the critical role of mechanical properties and conductivity in 
Cu-Cr-Nb alloy applications, hardness and conductivity for all tested 
states in our study ( Fig. 15 a), and a comparison with alloys produced by 
other fabrication methods ( Fig. 15 b), were analyzed and plotted. The 
analysis indicated that Cu-Cr-Nb alloys prepared by L-DED, particularly 
the Cr-rich alloys with higher Cr/Nb ratios compared to others, 
exhibited excellent characteristics (highlighted in the oval in Fig. 15 ). 
However, AB + SS + AG51 showed significant performance fluctuations; 
unoptimized aging parameters (e.g., duration) could lead to suboptimal 
outcomes (green box, Fig. 15 ). In contrast, AB + SS + AG42 demonstrated 
consistent performance, achieving a better balance than alloys with Cr/ 
Nb < 2, albeit with slightly lower hardness than AB + SS + AG51. When 
ideal solid solution treatment is impractical, a slight Cr excess (Cr/Nb >
2) is advantageous, as AB + DA51 fully utilizes IHT effects to achieve 
higher overall performance.
Compared to other fabrication methods ( Fig. 15 b and Table S2 ), such 
as vacuum induction melting and spark plasma sintering, L-DED with 
post-heat treatment offered higher electrical conductivity and tunable 
property combinations. Although a performance gap existed between L- 
DED alloys using ball-milled elemental powder and SLM/EBM alloys 
using pre-alloyed powder, this method eliminated reliance on alloy 
powders, reduced raw material costs, and enabled flexible composition 
adjustment. Moreover, it achieved excellent strength-conductivity syn -
ergy in the as-built state.
For engineering applications, stronger IHT effects in large L-DED 
components can enhance as-built performance. For smaller components, 
post-heat treatment (even brief low-temperature aging) is essential to 
optimize the strength-conductivity balance, ensuring adaptability to 
diverse requirements.
5. Conclusions
In this study, Cu-Cr-Nb alloys with varying Cr/Nb ratios were pro -
duced using L-DED with ball-milled elemental powders. The micro -
structure and properties of the printed alloys were analyzed, and 
performance variations following direct aging and solid solution with 
aging treatments were compared. Key conclusions from the study 
include: 
1. All alloys fabricated via L-DED achieved > 99 % density. As-built 
samples exhibited abundant dislocations. Nb-rich alloys contained 
micron-sized Nb particles and Cr
2
Nb, while Cr-rich alloys predomi -
nantly featured Cr particles and Cr
2
Nb. Higher Cr/Nb ratios reduced 
second-phase particle size and narrowed their distribution. Cr

2. Direct aging minimally affected microstructure and hardness but

minor hardness losses. Subsequent aging further boosted conduc -
