ABSTRACT
The role of coherency loss on rafting of superalloys under high temperature low stress creep conditions is in-
vestigated by phase- ﬁeld crystal plasticity simulations. It is demonstrated that coalescence, critically depending
on the state of coherency between precipitate and matrix is crucial to understand the rafting behavior of su-
peralloys. An explicit mechanisms is developed predicting coherency loss based on the plastic activity in the
matrix. The simulations are veri ﬁed using experimental creep test results.
1. Introduction
Single crystal Ni-based superalloy blades are key components in
aero engines and stationary turbines because of their exceptional me-
chanical properties and extraordinary corrosion resistance at high
temperature. The microstructure of Ni-based superalloys is the main
rationale for exhibiting superior creep and fatigue properties. This
microstructure is composed of a high volume fraction of ordered L 12-
precipitates ′γ , dispersed in the FCC-matrix γ [1]. ′γ in the as heat
treated material is coherent with γ phase across the interface with
negative lattice mis ﬁt which leads to alignment along 〈100 〉 direction
by minimizing the elastic energy [2]. The volume fraction of the ′γ in
technical superalloys is as high as 70% or above with an edge length of
the cubic precipitates of typically
450nm , separated by the γ channels
of width 20nm to 70nm [3]. The ample volume fraction of the ′γ
precipitates leads to the superior mechanical properties and high tem-
perature strength of Ni-based superalloys [4]. The morphology of the ′γ
precipitates is known to be mainly controlled by the lattice mis ﬁt δ
between the ′γ and γ phase, given by

few nano-meters [3]. The plastic strain generated during creep de-
formation decreases the mis ﬁt stresses and the disjoining potential and
allows precipitates to close the channel between each other if there is a
positive driving force for γ to ′γ transformation.

A signiﬁcant evolution of the microstructure is observed under high
temperature and low stress creep condition. The reason behind this is
the diﬀerence in elastic strain between vertical and horizontal channels
under uniaxial tensile load, as shown in Fig. 1. This leads to contraction
of vertical and expansion of horizontal channels. Under a purely elastic
loading, this contraction and expansion is due to the di ﬀusion con-
trolled transformation between the precipitates and the matrix, driven
by the elastic energy di ﬀerence at the interfaces respectively. The
phenomenon, called ‘rafting’, is additionally be supported by plastic
ﬂow of material. Kamaraj [11] concluded that the driving force for
rafting is controlled by the heterogeneity of the chemical potential
under the application of external stress. Additionally, plastic deforma-
tion in the channels causes coherency loss and accommodates the
misﬁt, which favors rafting [15]. Nabarro [16,17] explained compre-
hensively the driving force of rafting in Ni-based superalloys and stated
that the cuboidal precipitate will evolve into a morphology that exhibits
a lower energy. As the anisotropy in the interfacial energy of the co-
herent precipitate is signi ﬁcantly low, strain energy minimization is
primarily responsible for the resulting microstructure. Bu ﬃere and
Ignat [18] explained the rafting phenomenon based on the chemical
gradient generated by the interaction of alloying elements with the
interfacial dislocations produced during creep. Matan et al. [19] con-
ﬁrmed that the reduction of the mis ﬁt by the creep strain and loss of
coherency is responsible for the rafting in superalloys. Tanimoto et al.
[20] explained that the transition of interface from coherent to semi-
coherent is decisive for superalloys morphology during creep. Tinga
et al. [21] reported through numerical analysis that rafted micro-
structure produces signi ﬁcant decreasing in strain energy density as
compared to non rafted microstructure. By using phase- ﬁeld simula-
tions, Zhou et al. [22] explained the preferred location of dislocations
under the application of external stress and sign of lattice mis ﬁt, which
also explains the direction of the rafting. The dislocations are found in
the normal channels to the direction of applied stress for a negative
misﬁt under tension. Simulation studies using the phase- ﬁeld method
also indicate that the
γ channel plasticity results in a driving force for
rafting [23,22,24,25]. It is still unclear, however, if rafting of the mi-
crostructure is bene ﬁcial for the creep properties or decrease the creep
performance by softening mechanism [2,11].

Plastic deformation in the γ channels is one of the main driving
forces for the rafting of the ′γ precipitates [56]. This observation is
conﬁrmed in a study by Matan et. al. [19] according to which they have
found the threshold strain for the rafting i.e. the limit beyond which the
precipitates start to raft even in the absence of external load. Moreover,
phase-ﬁeld simulation of single precipitate is done by Tsukada et al.
[57], where he removed the external load after the threshold value of
plastic strain 0.12% to 0.16% and observed the rafting of the single
precipitate in prestrained matrix. If the material is not prestrained then
the ′γ precipitates and matrix are coherent because of which they ex-
perience a mutual repulsion which restrict the merging of ′γ pre-
cipitates. Plastically deformed matrix renders the cuboidal ′γ micro-
structure unstable due to the loss of coherency at the ′γγ/ interface. To
observe the e ﬀect of plastic strain on the microstructure more clearly,
re-precipitation is carried out in the 1.0% plastically strained γ matrix
after dissolving the ′γ particles completely by holding the material at a
temperature above the solvus to get a homogeneous solid solution while
keeping the local plastic strain in the matrix intact. Then the
′γ particles
are nucleated randomly in the γ matrix as represented in the Fig. 9a and
allowed to grow in the presence of inherited plastically strained solid
solution without the application of external loads. The plastic strain
accommodates the lattice mis ﬁt in the horizontal channels and alters
the driving force for ′γ precipitate evolution. It favors the directional
coarsening of the precipitates along the horizontal direction, where
plastic strain is at a minimum level. At this point
′γ precipitates start
coalescence which meets the criteria of the interface overlapping, ex-
istence of same anti-phase domains and presence of a magnitude of
critical resolved shear stress more than the threshold value. Further
coarsening of the
′γ precipitates is assisted by Ostwald ripening process
[58], which transforms the cuboidal ′γ particles into completely rafted
microstructure as displayed in Fig. 9b.
4. Summary and conclusion
The present work elucidates creep deformation of Ni-based super-
alloys in the framework of multi phase- ﬁeld method by employing a
phenomenological crystal plasticity model. The model calculates plastic
strain on the activated slip systems considering the internal mis ﬁt
stresses, as well as external stresses acting on the material. Moreover, a
model that controls the coalescence of the particles based upon the
critical resolved shear stress, anti-phase domain, driving force and
overlapping of the neighboring particles is introduced. The results leads
to the following conclusion:
 Creep deformation up to 1% strain has been simulated.
Microstructure evolution and creep kinetics have been evaluated
and veri ﬁed with experiments.
 Correctly modeling of repulsion and coalescence of ′γ precipitates
has been found to be crucial for obtaining a realistic rafted micro-
structure in creep.
 The mechanisms of rafting are investigated by separating the
”elastic” and ”elastic and plastic ” contribution and it is concluded
that the plastic deformation signi ﬁcantly accelerated the conversion
of cuboidal morphology to a rafted one.
