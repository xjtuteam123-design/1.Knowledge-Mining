High Temperature Strength and Ductility 
of Recrystallized Ni3AI-Ni3Mn Alloys 
N. MASAHASHI, T. TAKASUGI, and O. IZUMI 
The tensile properties of pseudobinary L12-type intermetallic compounds based on Ni3A1 and Ni3Mn 
were examined on recrystallized specimens. The alloys with manganese composition lower than 
15 at. pct were tested from room temperature to 1073 K (1273 K for some alloys). The yield stress 
appears to be controlled by solid solution hardening (athermal component) at room temperature 
and by the so-called Kear-Wilsdorf mechanism (thermal component) at elevated temperatures. The 
activation constant for the latter mechanism, i.e., for the anomalous positive temperature depen- 
dence of the yield stress, increases with increasing the manganese composition. The tensile elongation 
shows a maximum at 9 at. pct Mn and at intermediate temperatures (~700 K). Also, at sufficient 
higher temperatures (1273 K), an increase of elongation due to dynamic recrystallization was ob- 
served. The temperature and compositional dependence of the ultimate tensile strength are similar to 
that of the elongation. The fracture mode was closely correlated with the elongation behavior: the more 
the transgranular fracture, the higher the ductility. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
THE Ll2-type intermetallic compound Ni3AI has unique 
mechanical properties at high temperatures. It has been 
shown that the yield stress increases with increasing tem- 
perature up to about 1000 K and this behavior can be 
explained using the Kear-Wilsdorf mechanism. 1 The in- 
creasing strength with increasing temperature is caused by 
the immobilization of dislocations when (ll0) screw dis- 
locations undergo thermally activated cross slip from { 111} 
planes to {100} planes, a process driven by the APB energy 
anisotropy on the two planes. In addition, Ni3A1 shows high 
oxidation resistance and distinctive creep properties. 2 In 
spite of these superior properties, its low ductility at low 
temperatures caused by the brittleness of the grain bound- 
aries has prevented it from practical use as an engineering 
material. To improve the ductility, Aoki and Izumi added a 
small amount of boron 3 and increased the deformability 
at room temperature without lowering of the yield stress 
(indeed, B increased the strength). It was shown 4 that boron 
has a strong tendency to segregate to grain boundaries and 
thereby to enhance the grain boundary cohesion. Other 
methods have been used to ductilize polycrystalline Ni3AI: 
rapid solidification, 5 dynamic recrystallization, 6 and re- 
crystallization of a single crystal. 7 Although these methods 
succeeded in improving the room temperature ductility, the 
ductility at elevated temperatures (where Ni3A1 is particu- 
larly strong) remains low. 
The present authors proposed, based on an atomistic anal- 
ysis of the grain boundary structure, that the substitution for 
aluminum atoms by atoms located near nickel in the periodic 
table should greatly improve the grain boundary strength. 
This was based on the idea that such a substitution creates 
a homogeneous bonding environment in the grain boundary 
region of the Ni3A1. As a consequence, the substitution of 
manganese atoms (the VIIa group in a periodic table) was

In this paper, the Ni3A1-Ni3Mn pseudobinary alloys were 
prepared using recrystallized materials, their mechanical 
properties were measured in tensile tests, and the resulting 
properties correlated with fractography. The main purpose 
is to see if the changes of these properties with mangan- 
ese concentration are due to changes of microstructures or 
due to 'alloying' effects which are therefore intrinsic to the 
material.

C. Fracture Behavior 
The effects of testing temperature and chemical com- 
position on fracture behavior are shown in Figures 10 and 
11, respectively. The fracture surface of the 9 at. pet Mn 
alloy tested at room temperature was almost totally inter- 
granular with smooth grain boundary facets (Figure 10(a)). 
At intermediate temperature (673 K) transgranular frac- 
ture was observed, indicating a ductile fracture mode 
(Figure 10(b)). At 873 K the intergranular fracture mode 
again appeared (Figure 10(c)). At sufficiently high tem- 
perature (1173 K) evidence of grain boundary migration was 
clearly visible (Figure 10(d)). The temperature dependence 
of the fracture mode in the 15 at. pet Mn alloy is basically 
the same as that observed in the 9 at. pet Mn alloy (Fig- 
ure 11), but there is more transgranular fracture in the 15 at. 
pet Mn alloy at both room temperature and at 873 K. In 
addition, no evidence of grain boundary migration was seen 
in the 15 at. pet Mn alloy at 1173 K. 
Thus, it is seen that there is a strong correlation be- 
tween the fracture mode and the ductility. With increasing 
ductility, the fracture mode becomes increasingly trans- 
granular. Both at room temperature and at high temperature, 
where the ductility is low, intergranular fracture is dominant 
whereas at intermediate temperatures, where the ductility is 
high, transgranular fracture is dominant except for the 
6 at. pet Mn alloy.

IV. DISCUSSION 
The mechanical properties of the Ni3(A1, Mn) have been 
shown by this study to vary continuously with composition. 
At the same time, these alloys retained some of the charac- 
teristics of pure Ni3A1, for example, the positive tem- 
perature dependence of the yield stress. In addition, since 
the microstructures of these alloys are almost independent of 
composition and since all the alloys are single phase L12, the 
changes in the resultant mechanical properties are not at- 
tributable to a "microstructural" effect. This means that the 
changes must be due to an "alloying" effect. 
First, we discuss the yield stress of the alloys. We found 
a maximum at 9 at. pct Mn when the yield stress at room 
temperature is plotted vs manganese concentration (Fig- 
ure 5). This result suggests that the yield stress of N% 
(A1, Mn) at low temperatures is controlled by solid solu- 
tion hardening due to mixing of aluminum and manganese 
atoms on the aluminum sites. Considering now the positive 
temperature dependence of the yield stress (Figure 4), the 
slopes of stress increment vs temperature plots decrease 
as the manganese concentration increases. To evaluate this 
result quantitatively, the apparent activation constant 
U, which can be related to dislocation behavior, was calcu- 
lated according to the following method proposed by Liang 
and Pope: ~7

Next, we discuss the ductility of these alloys. At almost 
every testing temperature the elongation increases with Mn 
additions above 6 at. pct (Figure 7). This ductilization is 
attributed to the substitution for aluminum atoms by man- 
ganese atoms, thereby producing a homogeneous chemical 
bonding environment in the grain boundary region, and 
thereby enhancing the grain boundary cohesive strength. 9 
This result suggests that substituting Mn atoms for one-fifth 
of the A1 atoms is sufficient to bring about a measurable 
improvement in the grain boundary strength. 
In the plots of elongation vs manganese concentration, 
the elongation is maximum at 9 at. pct Mn and then a shal- 
low, rather indistinct minimum appears at 12 at. pct Mn. 
The low ductility observed at 12 at. pct Mn may be due to 
the fact that at this composition there are roughly equal 
amounts of A1 and Mn in the alloy and this could possibly 
lead to a highly ordered grain boundary structure, whereas 
in the 9 at. pct Mn alloys this is less likely. If it is also 
assumed that the ductility drops with increasing grain 
boundary order, then the minimum is rationalized, although 
more work is clearly necessary to check these speculations. 
In the plots of the elongation vs testing temperature a 
maximum appeared at intermediate temperatures. Here it 
must be remembered that the tensile tests at room tem- 
perature were conducted in air but the tests at elevated 
temperatures were conducted in a vacuum. Recent work by 
the present authors has shown that the low ductility of 
Ni3(A1, Mn) is due to hydrogen-assisted intergranular em- 
brittlement. 23 The tensile elongations of the 12 at. pct Mn 
and the 15 at. pct Mn alloys at ambient temperatures are 
as high as 40 pct when tested in vacuum, as compared to 
a few pct when tested in air; see Figure 7. Thus the duc- 
tility at room temperature is almost the same as that at 
intermediate temperatures, if both tests are performed in 
vacuum. This result demonstrates that the present alloy 
is intrinsically ductile even at room temperature but suf- 
fered from embrittlement in air. In fact, it has also been 
shown in the Co3Ti 24 and NiaA1 doped with boron 25 that the 
hydrogen greatly reduces the ductilities of Co3Ti 24 and 
NiaA125 by promoting intergranular fracture. The decreasing 
elongation at temperatures above the peak temperature is 
probably attributable to cavitation damage of the grain 
boundaries which increases with increasing temperature. 
The improved ductility at temperatures above the mini- 
mum at 1073 K found in the 9 at. pct Mn alloy (Figure 6) 
is due to the dynamic recrystallization (Figure 10(d)). A 
large number of small newly-created grains were found in

the prior grain boundary regions. Such an improved ductility 
at sufficiently high temperature has also been reported for 
Co3Yi. 26 Dynamic recrystallization suppresses cracking be- 
cause the stress concentrations at the original grain boundary 
plane are relaxed. This phenomenon is commonly observed 
in materials with low stacking fault energy at high tem- 
peratures, high strain rates, and large deformations. 
It was found in this work that high strength and high 
ductility are observed simultaneously in Ni3(A1, Mn). Such 
a correlation implies that the two properties are controlled 
by the same mechanism. When the grain boundaries are 
strengthened by optimizing the manganese concentration 
and testing temperature, intergranular fracture is suppressed, 
and thus the grain interiors are deformed, resulting in high 
elongations and high UTS. On the other hand, when the 
grain boundaries are weakened, the intergranular fracture 
occurs before the stresses are very high and before the grain 
interiors can deform. 
Finally, we describe the effect of microstructure (such as 
grain morphology) on the present properties. When we com- 
pare the present results with those obtained on as-cast mate- 
rials, ~5 the variation of mechanical properties with both 
composition and testing temperature were almost identi- 
cal in spite of the considerably different microstructures. 
Microstructure simply affects the levels of the mechani- 
cal properties but not the trends; for example, for the 
9 at. pct Mn alloys tested at 673 K the UTS and elonga- 
tion found for the recrystallized samples are about 15 pet 
higher than those of cast samples. Thus microstructure has 
a smaller effect on strength and ductility than do composi- 
tion and temperature, and therefore we conclude that the 
compositional dependence shown in this work is intrinsic 
and can be attributed to an "alloying" effect. However, from 
a practical point of view, microstructure is still one of the 
important factors affecting the mechanical properties of or- 
dered alloys. We are now investigating the effect of grain 
size on the mechanical properties of the Ni3(A1, Mn) alloys, 
and these results will be described in a subsequent paper. 
V. CONCLUSION 
Polycrystalline Llz-type Ni3(AI, xMn) compounds were 
tensile-tested at temperatures from room temperature to 
1173 K as a function of composition (X = 6 at. pet Mn to 
15 at. pet Mn). The yield stress, elongation, and ultimate 
tensile stress (UTS) were evaluated and then correlated with 
the fracture behavior. The results obtained in this work are 
described as follows: 
1. The yield stresses at room temperature were controlled 
predominantly by solid solution hardening of the alloy 
due to the mixing of aluminum and manganese atoms on 
A1 sites. The maximum yield stress occurs at about 
10 at. pet Mn. The yield stresses at elevated tempera- 
tures are controlled by thermally activated cross slip of 
screw dislocations, the so-called Kear-Wilsdorf process, 
and therefore increased with increasing temperature. 
This positive temperature dependence of the yield stress 
becomes less pronounced as the manganese concentra- 
tion increases. 
2. The tensile elongation is a maximum at 9 at. pct Mn 
over a wide range of testing temperatures. Also, the 
. 
. 
tensile elongation is a maximum at intermediate tem- 
peratures (673 K to 773 K) over a wide range of chemi- 
cal compositions. The former variation is attributed to a 
change of the grain boundary cohesive strength with 
composition while the latter variation is attributed to the 
introductions of the hydrogen embrittlement at ambient 
temperature and of grain boundary cavitation at high 
temperatures. 
The variations in both UTS and ductility brought about 
by changes in testing temperature are similar. The same 
is true for changes in chemical composition. This implies 
that the UTS and ductility are controlled by the same 
mechanism. 
The fracture mode is intimately related to the ductility. 
The greater the fraction of transgranular fracture the 
higher the ductility, and contrarily, the greater the frac- 
tion of intergranular fracture the lower the ductility. This 
trend was basically independent of alloy composition and 
testing temperature.
