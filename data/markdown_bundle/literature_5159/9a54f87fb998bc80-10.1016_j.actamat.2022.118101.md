The coarsening of γ’ precipitates in superalloys involves multiple factors and impacts the performance of 
mechanical properties, such as strength and creep resistance. Classical kinetic theory describes the coars- 
ening of γ’ under limited conditions, as it shows deﬁciencies in γ’ precipitation with excessive coars- 
ening. Finding effective factors underlying the kinetic behavior signiﬁcantly affecting precipitate coars- 
ening remains largely unsolved. We address this by using machine learning models to identify materials 
descriptors describing γ’ precipitate coarsening in Co-based superalloys. Using descriptors that include 
Young’s modulus difference and valence electron number, we obtain an explicit relation assisted by sym- 
bolic regression that ﬁts the experimental γ’ size data far better than classical kinetic theory in describing 
γ’ coarsening. We infer that the Young’s modulus difference dominates γ’ coarsening mechanism in dif- 
ferent Co-based superalloys. Moreover, alloys containing additional elements with large Young’s modulus 
should favor smaller γ’ precipitates size, thus providing guidance for designing advanced multicompo- 
nent Co-based superalloys with high γ’ coarsening resistance.

1. Introduction 
Superalloys provide excellent temperature capacity, microstruc- 
ture stability and high-temperature mechanical properties. They 
are therefore preferred in the manufacturing of high-pressure tur- 
bine blades and vanes for aero-engines and gas turbines [1] . Tra- 
ditional Co-based superalloys strengthened by carbides have been 
used in industry. However, their low temperature capacities ( < 
1050 °C) limit applications at higher temperatures [2] . Ni-based su- 
peralloys, depending on the strengthening of coherent and ordered 
γ’ precipitates, provide signiﬁcantly improved temperature capac- 
ities and mechanical properties, and are widely used in a broad 
range of applications [3] . Thus, to expand the applications and uti- 
lize their performance advantages [4] (e.g., high-temperature cor- 
rosion resistance and thermal fatigue), it is desirable to introduce 
γ’ strengthening in Co-based superalloys. Sato et al. [5] discovered

the signs of material degradation. Earlier, Meher et al. [12] studied

activation energies for the diffusion of Al, W and Ni in Co, sug- 
gesting that coarsening of γ’ precipitates is rate-limited by bulk

indicates that γ’ coarsening takes place in the low-component al- 
loy system below 10 0 0 °C and interactions among elements are 
ignored in the analysis [15–18] . Given that superalloys with excel- 
lent multiple properties usually contain several elements, there is 
a need to understand how the mechanical properties in multicom- 
ponent Co-based superalloys above 10 0 0 °C can be improved by 
kinetic analysis of γ’ microstructure evolution. 
The Ostwald ripening process in materials kinetics is a clas- 
sical description of precipitate evolution. As such, it is appropri- 
ate for understanding γ’ precipitate coarsening in superalloys. This 
phenomenon is generally regarded as a slow, diffusion-controlled 
process occurring by the growth and coarsening of larger particles 
at the expense of smaller ones. The classical Lifshitz, Slyozov and 
Wagner (LSW) [ 19 , 20 ] description of coarsening assumes a very di- 
lute system in which the volume fraction of the second phase ap- 
proaches zero with particle-particle interactions neglected. It pre- 
dicts self-similar ripening behavior at long times with a power law 
growth exponent of 1/3, typical of Brownian diffusion, and dynam- 
ical scaling. The predicted particle size distribution is however nar- 
rower and sharper than seen experimentally. Although more real- 
istic models of the ripening process at ﬁnite volume fractions of 
coarsening particles have been proposed [ 21 , 22 ], a fully satisfac- 
tory solution is still elusive. There are subtle differences among the 
various models and the complexity of various inﬂuencing factors 
hinders an understanding and control of γ’ precipitate coarsening. 
A method to identify inﬂuencing factors needs to be developed, 
and machine learning (ML) with feature engineering on available 
data is one approach to this problem. 
The use and application of methods from ML has attracted con- 
siderable interest from material scientists [23–29] . In some areas, 
the prediction accuracy of ML model has been far better than that 
of human experts [ 27 , 30 ]. In this work, we utilize a computer 
vision framework incorporating the deep learning-based convolu- 
tional neural networks model to extract accurate microstructural 
information from γ/ γ’ microstructure images. This then serves as 
input to the γ’ coarsening analysis in conjunction with classical ki- 
netic theories and to feature engineering and symbolic regression. 
The workﬂow is shown in Fig. 1 . 
We analyze the effects of alloying elements on the microstruc- 
ture evolution based on 600 γ/ γ’ microstructure images of eight 
and nine-component CoNiAl-based medium-entropy superalloys 
after aging at 1050 °C and 1100 °C. Coupled with classical kinetics, 
we can ﬁt well the early time coarsening of regular γ’ precipitates 
in certain alloys using LSW. However, classical kinetic theory does 
not adequately ﬁt the rapid coarsening of irregular γ’ precipitate. 
The feature selection and symbolic regression strategies we adopt 
provide a means of ﬁtting experimental γ’ size data to describe 
γ’ precipitate coarsening using descriptors such as Young’s modu- 
lus difference ( σ E) and valence electron number ( V EN), which we 
identify from ML models. The relation we propose for γ’ size pre- 
diction in terms of aging temperature, aging time, σ E and V EN is 
superior in ﬁtting to the data than classical kinetic theory, and Co- 
based superalloys with large σ E value tend to follow the γ’ coars- 
ening predicted by LSW theory. For strengthening materials, this 
model for γ’ size, which inversely depends on σ E, predicts that 
the addition of elements with large Young’s modulus, such as Re, 
Ru, W and Mo will favor smaller γ’ precipitates in Co-based su- 
peralloys. This potentially can be a means to design multicompo- 
nent CoNiAl-based medium/high-entropy superalloys with high γ’ 
coarsening resistance. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
In order to consider the microstructure evolution of multicom- 
ponent CoNiAl-based medium-entropy superalloys at 1050 °C and 
1100 °C, the alloys investigated in this work were designed ac- 
cording to the following requirements: (1) The alloys should con- 
tain multiple elements and we chose those with more than eight 
elements; (2) The γ’ solvus temperature of the alloys should be 
higher than 1100 °C; (3) The microstructure consists only of γ/ γ’ 
microstructure without detrimental phases after aging at 1050 °C 
and 1100 °C. Accordingly, we chose the alloy Co-29.7Ni-10.8Al-2Ti- 
1.8W-2Ta-1Mo-3Cr (at.%) as the base alloy to study the addition of 
Ti, Ta, Mo, Cr, Nb, Re, Ni, Al and W on γ’ precipitate evolution. 
The alloys with corresponding γ’ solvus temperatures are shown 
in Table 1 , and the representative experimental γ/ γ’ microstruc- 
ture images are shown in Fig. 2 . 
2.2. Synthesis and characterization 
We chose raw metals with high purity (above 99.95%) to syn- 
thesize the alloys. The alloys were melted eight times by vacuum 
arc melting to yield a 40 g ingot. In order to ensure the homo- 
geneity of the elemental distribution in each alloy, the ingots were 
sealed in a quartz tube ﬁlled with high purity argon, and then sub- 
jected to solution heat treatment at 1225 °C for 12 h followed by 
air cooling. The samples were cut and subsequently aged at 10 0 0 
°C for 24 h followed by water cooling. The γ’ solvus was detected 
by DTA (NETZSCH STA 449F3) with high purity argon ﬂow, and the 
samples with a size of ϕ3mm × 1 mm were tested at a heating 
rate of 10 °C min −1 . To further study the microstructure evolution 
of the alloys, long-term aging was monitored at 1050 °C for 24, 72, 
168, 300, 500, 750, 1000 h and 1100 °C for 24, 72, 168, 300, 500 
h. All samples were prepared using standard metallographic proce- 
dures, and etched for a few seconds using a solution of HNO 3 : HCl 
: H 2 O = 1 : 1 : 1. The microstructure images were collected from a 
Zeiss GeminiSEM 300 ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) in backscattered electron imaging mode, and equipped with 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy detector, used to measure the 
alloy composition. To mitigate the impact of anisotropy in poly- 
crystal samples, ﬁve images at different regions of each sample 
were used to represent its microstructure under the correspond- 
ing aging conditions. 
The bulk samples of alloys MGE-W and MGE-Mo after aging 
at 1050 °C for 10 0 0 h were selected for nanoindentation. The mi- 
crostructure of these alloys consisted of a separate γ matrix and γ’ 
precipitate, enabling individual indentation on a single phase. Af- 
ter mechanical polishing, the samples were further polished with 
an electrolytic solution of 80% CH 3 OH and 20% H 2 SO 4 (by volume) 
at 20 V for 20 s to completely remove the residual plastically de- 
formed layers. Nanoindentation was performed with Nano Indenter 
XP using the continuous stiffness method [31] . The strain rate and 
Poisson’s ratio were 0.05 s −1 and 0.3, respectively, and the maxi- 
mum load displacement was set to 100 nm. The hardness of the γ
matrix and γ’ precipitate was determined by analyzing the load- 
displacement curves with the Oliver-Pharr method [32] . The mean 
value of 10 indentation data was used to represent the hardness of 
each phase.

2.3. Computer vision framework using deep learning-based 
convolutional neural networks 
The microstructure of materials is commonly represented by 
image data in real space or reciprocal space captured by a varying 
electromagnetic wave detector, such as an optical microscope, elec- 
tron microscope or X-ray diffractometer. The image data is complex 
unstructured data, and a large number of experimental images are 
expected to provide insight into the mechanism of γ’ precipitate 
coarsening. An eﬃcient high-throughput method is necessary to 
extract accurate microstructural information. Image segmentation 
provides the means to divide an image into speciﬁc objects (e.g. γ
matrix and γ’ precipitate), and this can be followed by statistical 
analysis on the segmented images. Traditional image segmentation 
methods are dependent on using the contrast in image pixels with- 
out learning image features, which will cause large deviations with 
complex images. However, deep learning can produce accurate re- 
sults by learning texture, geometry and other complex features. 
For comparison, we used traditional image segmentation methods 
as well as deep learning models to segment the γ/ γ’ phases in 
our superalloys. The OTSU, adaptive threshold and K-means were 
used as typical, traditional image segmentation methods [ 33 , 34 ], 
and the fully convolutional networks (FCN), Unet, U ++ and ResNet 
were adopted as representative deep learning models [35–38] to 
perform the segmentation. 
We used 14 SEM images containing γ/ γ’ microstructure with 
a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels, and the microstructure image 
has 2 semantic classes ( γ matrix and γ’ precipitate) labeled us- 
ing expert knowledge. To reduce the burden of computation cost 
and improve the eﬃciency of model training, a large image in 
which edges were ﬁlled with mirror padding was divided into 24 
smaller images by an overlap-tile strategy with size 256 × 256 pix- 
els. Therefore, the image data set consisted of 336 images. To build 
a model, 294 images were used for training and 42 for validation. 
To evaluate the accuracy of these image segmentation models, 15 
ground truth images were used as testing data, which were man- 
ufactured by superalloy scientists according to the original corre- 
sponding SEM images. In order to demonstrate the effectiveness 
of the overlap-tile strategy, simple processing was used, namely, 
a large SEM image composed of 1024 × 768 pixels was directly

3. Results and Discussion 
3.1. Extracted γ / γ ’ microstructural information 
The typical γ/ γ’ microstructure images after segmentation and 
quantiﬁcation are shown in Fig. 3 . The basic microstructural fea- 
tures in γ’-strengthened superalloys included the number, volume 
fraction, size and morphology fraction of γ’ precipitate. In our 
statistics, there are more than 270,0 0 0 γ’ precipitates in the 60 0 
experimental SEM images. As shown in Fig. 4 a, all the alloys have 
a large N A in the early aging stage. With increase in aging time, the 
γ’ precipitates tend to coarsen, and N A becomes smaller. Compared 
with alloy MGE-Base after aging at 1050 °C, most alloys, such as 
MGE-W, MGE-Mo and MGE-Re, have higher N A values. However, al- 
loy MGE-Al has the lowest N A value, which implies a faster coars- 
ening of γ’ precipitates. The decrease of γ’ number is correlated 
with γ’ coarsening. 
The γ’ volume fraction is an important microstructural feature, 
which can signiﬁcantly affect mechanical properties. In advanced 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys, the γ’ volume fraction is usu- 
ally expected to be higher than 60%, which makes for long creep 
life [59] . As shown in Fig. 4 b, the addition of elements Ti, Ta, Nb, 
Mo, W and Al can increase γ’ volume fraction, and alloying with 
Ni, Cr and Re reduce it. Notably, the γ’ volume fraction of the alloy 
with low γ’ volume fraction ( < 30%, such as the alloys MGE-Base, 
MGE-Re and MGE-Cr aging at 1100 °C) slightly decreases with time 
at a high aging temperature close to the γ’ solvus temperature, 
and the higher γ’ volume fraction in the alloys MGE-Ti, MGE-Ta, 
and MGE-Nb changes little. Alloy with high γ’ volume fraction ( > 
60%) often has high γ’ solvus temperature to keep the microstruc- 
ture stable. Therefore, it is important to increase γ’ solvus temper- 
ature in designing advanced single crystal superalloys with higher 
γ’ volume fraction. 
The γ’ size is another critical microstructural feature directly 
reﬂects the coarsening of γ’ precipitates, and affects macroscopic 
properties at service temperature. Nathal et al. [60] reported that 
the Ni-based single crystal superalloys with an optimal initial γ’ 
size can have low steady-state creep rates and long creep life at 
10 0 0 °C. As shown in Fig. 4 c, γ’ precipitates of all alloys coarsen 
to varying degrees. As for alloy MGE-Base after aging at 1050 °C, 
it has a γ’ equivalent radius of 255.7 nm at 24 h and coarsens up 
to 1334.2 nm at 10 0 0 h. The alloy MGE-Base has coarsened more 
than 5 times in γ’ size at 1050 °C. The γ’ precipitates of alloys 
MGE-Ta, MGE-Nb and MGE-Al coarsen, elongate and coalesce, in- 
dicating the weak resistance on γ’ microstructure degradation. In 
addition, some alloys show small γ’ size, such as alloys MGE-W, 
MGE-Mo and MGE-Re. With increase of aging temperature from 
1050 °C to 1100 °C, most alloys have a larger γ’ size. These results 
also demonstrate that the aging temperature and time are key pa- 
rameters, which deserve attention in the ML model. 
The γ’ morphology reﬂects the γ’ shape of the alloy with dif- 
ferent lattice misﬁts, which is a noteworthy microstructural feature 
in superalloys. Sluytman et al. [61] found that the “optimal” shape 
is that of γ’ precipitates with cuboidal morphology, and the cor- 
responding value of lattice misﬁt is about 0.25% ∼ 0.4%. Further- 
more, certain industrial Ni-based single crystal alloys with favor- 
able creep resistance, such as CMSX-4 and CMSX-10, have a large 
number of cuboidal γ’ precipitates, suggesting that the good high- 
temperature properties are correlated with γ’ precipitate shape. 
The cuboidal γ’, circular γ’ and coarsened γ’ (including irregu- 
lar γ’, oversized circular and cuboidal γ’, etc) are the major mor- 
phologies present [ 2 , 61 ]. Therefore, we consider the ratio of three 
approximate morphologies according to the rules we deﬁned in the 
program, and the seriously irregular reticulate γ’ precipitates (e.g., 
the γ’ precipitates in alloys MGE-Ta and MGE-Nb aging at 1050 °C 
for 10 0 0 h in Fig. 2 b) are not included in our statistics. The mor- 
phology fraction is shown in Fig. 4 d. With increasing aging time, 
the fraction of cuboidal morphology in some alloys gradually de- 
creases, and the coarsened morphology fraction increases. 
3.2. Analysis of γ ’ coarsening based on kinetic theories 
3.2.1. Coarsening laws 
The microstructural features γ’ number, γ’ size and γ’ mor- 
phology fraction may be utilized in the kinetic analysis of γ’ 
coarsening. The γ/ γ’ microstructure can be divided into two cate- 
gories: regular γ’ precipitates which coarsen slowly (such as the 
microstructure of alloys MGE-W, MGE-Mo and MGE-Re), and ir-

3.3. Descriptor analysis via machine learning 
Machine learning methods provide a means of identifying po- 
tential factors, descriptors or features in the data collected inﬂu- 
encing γ’ coarsening. Fig. 7 a shows that the model for γ’ size 
with the lowest MSE is the RF model. According to the voting re- 
sult by expert committee in Fig. 7 b, σ E is recommended as the 
most important descriptor, and the optimal descriptor combina- 
tion containing σ E is σ E and V EN with the lowest MSE from the 
RF model. Fig. 7 c indicates that the RF model with descriptors in- 
cluding T and t as input has lower MSE values than the bench- 
mark model with T, t and chemical compositions as input. The 
two descriptors σ E and V E N are consequences of the atomic in- 
teraction in metals and alloys. In the metallic bond, valence elec- 
trons are shared by neighboring atoms and are the main carriers 
of the atomic interaction. When the alloys contain different types 
of atoms, these electrons inﬂuence the atomic interaction, which 
eventually inﬂuences the driving force and evolution of γ’ coars- 
ening. It is known that electronic behavior controls part of super- 
alloy properties, such as the number of electron vacancies or holes 
above the Fermi energy level in the d band, as proposed by Boesch 
et al. [65] , and the average energy level of d orbitals as proposed 
by Morinaga et al. [53] . These methods, also referred to as PHA- 
COMP and new PHACOMP, have proved effective in studies to con- 
trol the precipitation of harmful phases [66–68] . In addition, the 
inﬂuence of modulus on the evolution of γ’ precipitates has been 
used in phase-ﬁeld simulation studies to reproduce γ’ evolution of 
low-component Co-based superalloys [69–71] .

4. Conclusion 
We have provided an analysis of the evolution of γ’ precipi- 
tate coarsening in Co-based superalloy using classical kinetic the- 
ory and machine learning methods. The γ’ microstructural features 
including γ’ number, γ’ volume fraction, γ’ size and γ’ morphol- 
ogy fraction can be obtained eﬃciently within 3 seconds for each 
SEM image, which helps superalloy scholars and manufacturers 
to quickly and accurately quantity the microstructure information. 
The kinetic aspects such as temporal evolution of γ’ size, particle 
number density, coarsening rate and particle size distribution can 
be calculated based on extracted γ’ microstructural information to 
test LSW theory. Furthermore, machine learning models and de- 
scriptor selection strategy help us determine effective factors, in- 
cluding σ E and V EN, with σ E impacting the γ’ coarsening mech- 
anism that can follow or not LSW in different Co-based superal- 
loys. The use of an explicit relation provides a means to control γ’ 
size and hence coarsening and creep resistance in designing γ/ γ’ 
strengthened CoNiAl-based medium/high-entropy superalloy con- 
taining large-moduli elements with stable γ/ γ’ microstructure and 
coarsening resistance.
