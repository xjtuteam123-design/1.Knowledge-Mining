Mechanical properties and oxidation behaviour of two-phase iron
aluminium alloys with Zr(Fe,Al) 2 Laves phase or Zr(Fe,Al) 12 t1 phase
F. Stein*, M. Palm, G. Sauthoff
Max-Planck-Institut fu¨r Eisenforschung GmbH, Postfach 140444, D-40074 Du¨sseldorf, Germany
Received 8 June 2004; received in revised form 1 August 2004; accepted 18 August 2004

Abstract
Two-phase Fe-rich Fe–Al–Zr alloys have been prepared consisting of binary Fe–Al with a very low solubility for Zr and the ternary Laves
phase Zr(Fe,Al)2 or t1 phase Zr(Fe,Al)12. Yield stress, ﬂexural fracture strain, and oxidation behaviour of these alloys have been studied in
the temperature range between room temperature and 1200 8C. Both the Laves phase and thet1 phase act as strengthening phases increasing
signiﬁcantly the yield stress as well as the brittle-to-ductile transition temperature. Alloys containing disordered A2 C ordered D03 Fe–Al
show strongly increased yield stresses compared to alloys with only A2 or D0 3 Fe–Al. The binary and ternary alloys with about 40at.% Al
and 0 or 0.8at.% Zr show the effect of vacancy hardening at low temperatures which can be eliminated by heat treatments at 4008C. At higher
Zr contents this effect is lost and instead an increase of low-temperature strength is observed after the heat treatment. The increase of the
high-temperature yield strength of Fe-40at.% Al by adding Zr is much stronger than by other ternary additions such as Ti, Nb, or Mo. Tests on
the oxidation resistance at temperatures up to 1200 8C indicate a detrimental effect of Zr already for additions of 0.1at.%.

1. Introduction
Fe–Al alloys have been studied widely as candidates for
high-temperature structural applications [1–8]. Their main
advantages compared to other Fe-base materials are their
excellent high-temperature corrosion resistance in oxidizing
and sulﬁdizing environments [9–12] and their low density.
However, high-temperature strength and creep properties of
binary Fe–Al alloys are not sufﬁcient for structural
applications. Various strategies for improving the strength
at high temperatures were already discussed [7,13–16].A
promising possibility is the precipitation of a second phase,
e.g. a Laves phase, as strengthening particles [3,15,17].I n
this context, Zr is a noticeable alloying addition for Fe–Al.
Even very small amounts of Zr result in the precipitation of
Laves phase Zr(Fe,Al)
2 or t1 phase Zr(Fe,Al) 12 particles
depending on the Al content [18].
The effect of Zr additions on the mechanical properties of
Fe–Al alloys has not yet been studied in detail. Some
information exists on the effect of small amounts (!1at.%)
of Zr. From Charpy impact tests, Sainfort et al. [19]
concluded that very small additions of 0.05at.% Zr result in
an increase of the toughness of Fe–40Al (always at.% if not
stated otherwise), but a Zr content of 0.1 or 0.5at.% again
decreased the toughness. A combined addition of 0.1at.% Zr
and 0.4at.% B to an Fe–40Al alloy was observed to increase
the strength up to about 900 8C as a result of simultaneous
grain reﬁnement and precipitation hardening [20,21]. In Fe–
45Al–5X (XZTi to Cu) alloys, 0.1at.% Zr and 0.2at.% B
were added as impurity scavenger and in order to improve
the grain boundary strength [22–24]. Small amounts of Zr
were also found to reduce the moisture-induced hydrogen
embrittlement in Fe–28Al–5Cr [25,26].I na d d i t i o n ,a
beneﬁcial effect on the ductility and fatigue crack growth
resistance was found in the same investigation for additions
of 0.1 and 0.5at.% Zr, but both properties deteriorated again
in an alloy with 1at.% Zr.

The mechanical behaviour of Fe–Al alloys with higher
Zr contents (O1at.%) was studied by compression tests on
alloys of composition Fe–39Al–5Zr, Fe–40Al–2Zr[27], and
Fe–47Al–5Zr [28]. The ﬂow stresses at about 1000 8C were
found to be signiﬁcantly increased compared to binary
alloys. The room-temperature ductility of an Fe
3Al alloy
was found to be decreased by alloying with 5at.% Zr [29].
Systematic investigations of the dependence on the Zr and
Al content have not been performed.
Some information is available on the oxidation
behaviour of Fe–Al alloys with small amounts of Zr.
The scale adherence of alumina scales formed on iron–
aluminides during oxidation at temperatures above
900 8C was found to be improved by the addition of
small amounts ( !0.1at.%) of Zr [12,30–34]. For an Fe–
11.7Al alloy with 0.03at.% Zr, the formation of ZrO
2
was found to improve the adherence of Al 2O3 layers and
to catalyze the transformation from metastable Q-Al2O3
to stable, protective a-Al2O3 scales on the alloy surface
[30]. On the other hand, the oxidation rate of an Fe–37Al
alloy at 1000–1200 8C in air was observed to increase by
adding 0.3 or 0.8at.% Zr [35]. In thermal cycling tests,
5at.% Zr added to FeAl were found to have a detrimental
effect on the oxidation behaviour [36].
The phase diagram of the ternary Fe–Al–Zr system
between 800 and 1150 8C has recently been established
in a detailed investigation [18]. A major ﬁnding was that
the solubility of Zr in Fe–Al is below 0.1at.% Zr for all
Al contents and for the complete investigated tempera-
ture range. Fig. 1 shows a sketch of the Fe-rich corner of
the Fe–Al–Zr phase diagram. The position of the Zr-poor
boundary of the Laves phase ﬁeld and the solubility of
Zr in the binary Fe–Al hardly change with temperature in
the investigated range between 800 and 1150 8C.
The low solubility of Zr in the binary Fe–Al phases
and the possibility of substituting large amounts of Fe by
Al in the Fe(Al) solid solution, the Laves phase
Zr(Fe,Al)
2, and the t1 phase (Zr(Fe,Al) 12) result in the
formation of extended two-phase ﬁelds. This allows for
varying the Al content and amount of both phases
independently over a wide range. Alloys of respective
composition combine the comparably ductile Fe(Al) solid
solution with the hard Laves phase or the t
1 phase. In
the present paper, results of systematic investigations on
the strengthening effect of the ternary Laves phase and
the t
1 phase in Fe-rich Fe–Al–Zr alloys as a function of
Al and Zr content are presented.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure
The liquidus surface near the Fe-rich corner of the
ternary Fe–Al–Zr system shows a eutectic trough parallel to
the Fe–Al axis at Zr contents of 9–10at.% extending from 0
to about 35at.% Al between the Laves phase and Fe(Al)
solid solution (see Fig. 1) [18]. At higher Al contents, the
eutectic trough continues at lower Zr contents between the
phase ﬁelds of the t
1 phase and FeAl. Since during cooling
from the liquidus to room temperature no phase transform-
ations occur besides the well-known ordering reactions of
the disordered Fe(Al) phase, the as-cast microstructure of all
investigated two-phase alloys consists of a primary
solidiﬁed phase plus a eutectic mixture of the two phases.
In case of the large two-phase ﬁeld between the binary
Fe–Al and the Laves phase, the primary phase is the A2-
ordered a-Fe(Al) solid solution (or D0
3-ordered Fe 3Al
above about 20at.% Al) for Zr contents below 9–10at.%. For
higher Zr contents, the primary phase is the Laves phase. In
case of the Al-rich FeAl Ct
1 two-phase ﬁeld, B2-ordered
FeAl is the primary phase in all investigated alloys. (None
of the prepared alloys had a Zr content above that of
the eutectic which is 5–6at.% depending on Al content). The
microstructures of the investigated as-cast alloys and the
approximate volume fractions of the Laves or t
1 phase are
listed in the last two columns of Table 1.
Fig. 2 shows two examples of typical microstructures
of as-cast two-phase alloys. The volume fraction of the
eutectic is about 20% in the alloy with 2.5at.% Zr and
about 50% in the one with 5at.% Zr. In alloys with 2.5
or less at.% Zr, the eutectic is distributed along the
boundaries of the Fe–Al grains as a hard skeleton. TEM
investigations of the ﬁne-scaled eutectic in the two alloys
Fe–10Al–2.5Zr and Fe–20Al–2.5Zr show that the eutectic
consists of a lamellar structure of Laves phase and Fe–Al
[37,38]. The width of these lamellae is in the range of
0.2 mm in both alloys. The thickness of the frequently
interrupted grain boundary layers varies in a wide range
(cf. Fig. 2 (a)).

3.2. 0.2% Yield stress
The large two-phase ﬁelds allow studying the effect of
variations of the Al content and volume fraction of the
phases independently from each other over a wide range of
alloy compositions. Therefore, two types of alloy series
were produced for the investigations of the mechanical
behaviour. The dash-dotted lines in Fig. 1, which connect
the binary Fe–Al axis with the Laves phase or with the t
1
phase, are nearly parallel to tie-lines which had been
determined in [18]. In alloys with compositions along these
lines, the compositions of the phases remain almost
unchanged and the volume fraction of e.g. the Laves
phase varies from 0 to 100%. Along the lines parallel to the
Fe–Al axes, the volume fractions of the two phases are
almost constant, but the Al content of both phases varies.
3.2.1. Effect of volume fraction of second phases
Fig. 3(a) shows the compressive 0.2% yield stresses as a
function of temperature for the series of alloys consisting of
binary Fe–10Al with varying volume fractions of Laves
phase. With the exception of one heat-treated sample, which
is discussed in Section 3.2.3, all tests were performed on as-
cast samples. The yield stresses increase with increasing
amount of Laves phase showing clearly the strengthening
effect of the hard Laves phase. This can also been seen in
Fig. 3(b), where the data for the same alloys are shown as a
function of the volume fraction of Laves phase. The alloys
with Laves phase contents of 80 and 100 vol% were so
brittle in the lower temperature range that they broke before
reaching the 0.2% yield stress level.
For the series of alloys with 20 instead of 10at.% Al, a
qualitatively very similar behaviour was observed. The
0.2% yield stresses continuously decrease with increasing
temperature for each composition and again there is a
continuous increase of the 0.2% yield stresses with
increasing amount of Laves phase at all temperatures.

Besides strengthening the alloys by increasing the
volume fraction of the hard phase, contributions to
strengthening in particle-reinforced materials may come
from the grain size-related Hall–Petch mechanism and other
effects as Orowan strengthening or load transfer from the
ductile phase to the reinforcing particles. However, the
present Fe–Al–Zr composite material has a comparably
complex microstructure with a ﬁne-scaled eutectic com-
posed of a hard and a ductile phase and primary crystallites
which are the ductile Fe–Al phase or the hard Laves (or t
1)
phase (as described in Section 3.1). In addition, coarsening
of the eutectic and the primary crystallites will occur during
testing at high temperatures. As microstructures of the
alloys after testing have not been studied, a meaningful
discussion of the inﬂuence of the separate contributions is
not possible.

3.2.2. Effect of Al content and atomic ordering
On increasing the Al content in the alloys, the type of
atomic ordering in the Fe–Al phase changes from the
disordered a-Fe(Al) solid solution (A2) to D0
3-ordered
Fe3Al and, on further adding Al, to B2-ordered FeAl. The
critical Al content required for the change of the ordering
state is a function of temperature (see e.g. [39]). The D0
3-
ordered Fe 3Al phase is only stable up to a maximum
temperature of about 550 8C. Besides the effect on the
ordering state of the Fe–Al phase, the increase in Al content
also results in a change of the second phase from the Laves
phase to the t
1 phase at about 28at.%. Both, the effect of the
change of atomic ordering in the Fe–Al phase and the
change from the Laves to thet
1 phase on the measured yield
stresses of the ternary Fe–Al–Zr alloys are discussed below.
Fig. 5 shows the 0.2% yield stresses of three two-phase
alloys with an approximate volume fraction of Laves phase
of 20% and Al contents of 0, 10, and 20at.%. The Fe–Al
phase in the alloys with 0 and 10at.% is the disordered
Fe(Al) solid solution for all temperatures. In addition, the
alloy with 20at.% Al contains the D0
3-ordered Fe3Al phase
up to a temperature of 4278C [18]. It can clearly be seen that
the yield stresses are strongly increased by the co-existence
of A2 CD03 order. At and above 600 8C, all three alloys
contain the disordered A2 phase and only a small difference
between the yield stresses remains reﬂecting the compar-
ably weak effect of solid solution hardening due to the
increasing Al content.

3.2.3. Effect of heat treatment
All compression tests discussed above were performed
with as-cast samples. For two alloys (Fe–10Al–12.5Zr and
Fe–20Al) the effect of a heat treatment at the testing
temperature prior to the experiment was checked. Both
alloys were annealed for 100 h at 1000 8C under Ar and
quenched to room temperature in iced brine. The subsequent
compression tests gave practically the same 0.2% yield
stresses as measured on the as-cast material (for the alloy
with 10at.% Al, see the black triangle in Fig. 3; for the Fe-
20alloy, values of 7.7 and 7.3 MPa were measured with and
without prior heat treatment respectively).
It is well-known that B2-ordered FeAl alloys in the as-
cast state or cooled from high temperatures contain high
concentrations of thermal vacancies which lead to
signiﬁcant hardening [43–46]. Long-time anneals at
400 8C for removing the excess vacancies were found to
reduce the low-temperature yield stresses signiﬁcantly.
The resulting temperature-vs.-yield strength curves
revealed the existence of a strength anomaly (see e.g.
[47,48]), which is essentially based on hardening by
thermal vacancies at intermediate temperatures according
to the George–Baker model [49–51].
In order to check the effect of vacancy hardening on the
present Fe–Al–Zr alloys containing B2-FeAl, the three
alloys of the series Fe–40Al Ct

At testing temperatures below 500 8C, the yield stresses of
the binary Fe–40Al alloy are drastically decreased after the
annealing treatment. The room temperature values for the
yield stress before and after annealing differ by a factor of
more than three. At higher temperatures the difference of the
yield stresses between the as-cast and annealed state
disappears. As expected, the effect is also observed for the
alloy containing 10 vol% t
1 phase. Due to the presence of
the second phase, the reduction of the yield stress is less
strong than in the binary alloy.
In the alloy with 50 vol% t
1 phase, the softening effect
due to the annealing treatment was no longer observed. The
room temperature yield stress and the yield stresses above
500 8C are about the same in the as-cast and annealed state,
whereas at 400 and 500 8C there is even an increase instead
of a decrease of strength after annealing. As the alloy still
contains about 50 vol% of binary Fe–40Al phase with a Zr
content below 0.1at.%, this behaviour is difﬁcult to under-
stand. The compositions of both phases present, i.e. B2-
FeAl and t
1 phase, are the same as in the alloy with 10 vol%
t1 phase and only the amounts of both phases and the
microstructures are different. It is well-known that—besides
vacancies—interstitial impurities as carbon, oxygen or
nitrogen may result in strengthening effects by precipitation,
isolated interstitials, interstitial agglomerates or formation
of interstitial-vacancy complexes [52–54]. The formation of
e.g. precipitates during the 400 8C anneal could explain the
increased strength at this temperature. However, the room
temperature strength should also be increased in this case in
contrast to the present experimental observations. In order
to understand the effect of strengthening by the 400 8C
anneal, further more detailed studies are needed.
3.2.4. Comparison with other ternary Fe–Al–X alloys
In Fig. 8, the strengthening effect of 4at.% Zr added to an
Fe–40Al alloy is compared to strengthening by about equal
(or higher) amounts of Ti [55],N b [56],o rM o [57].

Nb(Fe,Al)2, the Mo-rich phase Mo 3Al, and the t1 phase
Zr(Fe,Al)12. Due to the very different contents of X in these
second phases, the volume fractions of the respective phases
differ signiﬁcantly from each other. Therefore, the volume
fraction of second phase is the highest in case of the Zr alloy
although the added amount of third element is the smallest.
As Fig. 8 shows, the addition of Zr is much more effective in
increasing the strength than Ti, Nb, or Mo giving e.g. at
900 8C a 0.2% yield stress of 329 MPa compared to 58, 70,
and 50 MPa for Ti, Nb, and Mo, respectively.
3.3. Brittle-to-ductile transition (BDT)
In order to study the BDT, the ﬂexural fracture strains of
as-cast samples were measured as a function of temperature
in four-point bending tests. In Fig. 9(a) the results for the
series of two-phase alloys with 50 vol% second phase and
varying Al contents are shown. The BDT is indicated by the
steep increase of the ﬂexural fracture strains from values of
less than 1% to more than 3%. For alloys with 0–20at.% Al,
the temperature range of the BDT only weakly depends on
the Al content, but for the Al-rich alloy with 42.5at.% Al,
the BDT occurs at signiﬁcantly higher temperatures.
In the case of binary Fe–Al, where the BDT was
observed at much lower temperatures than for the series of
ternary alloys shown here, a qualitatively very similar
dependence on the Al content was found [58]. The BDT
temperatures only weakly increase at low Al contents but
show a sudden increase between 40 and 45at.% Al. This at
least qualitatively very similar behaviour is remarkable as
the Fe–Al phase obviously plays the major role for the
occurrence of the sudden shift of the BDT to higher
temperatures notwithstanding the presence of about
50 vol% of second phase in the ternary alloys.

Fig. 11 shows the mass gains of ternary Fe–Al–Zr alloys
with 4–5at.% Zr as a function of the Al content for

The above results on the short-term oxidation behaviour
do not allow conclusions on the kinetics and mechanism of
oxidation. Therefore, thermogravimetric long-time oxi-
dation tests at 900 8C were performed on four selected
alloys (Fig. 12). After an initial transition period, a more or
less parabolic growth law was observed and the apparent
parabolic growth constants k
p were determined for the four
alloys (Table 4). A drastic increase of kp by two orders of
magnitude was found as a result of increasing the Zr content
from 0.1 to 2.5at.%. The two alloys with 0.8at.% Zr but
higher Al contents give k
p values in between.
The obtained values are somewhat higher than those
reported for ternary Fe–Al–Ti alloys wherekp is generally in
the range 10 K14–10K13 g2 cmK4 sK1 [55]. Compared to
what is expected for Al 2O3-forming alloys, the values are
too high [59]. For binary Fe–Al, the formation of protective
a-Al2O3 scales is known to occur only at sufﬁciently high Al
contents and temperatures [9,60–63]. Below a critical
temperature which depends on Al content, Q-Al2O3 with
a much higher growth rate, i.e. higher kp values forms [63].
In the present case of the ternary Fe–Al–Zr alloys, it may be
suspected that the added Zr will have a strong tendency to
form non-protective ZrO
2 which would explain the
experimentally observed detrimental effect of Zr. However,
from the present data no precise information on the possible
concurrent and competing oxidation processes can be
obtained. For an understanding of the oxidation mechanism
of the ternary Fe–Al–Zr alloys, further studies are
necessary.
4. Conclusions
Fe-rich two-phase Fe–Al–Zr alloys have been investi-
gated with respect to their microstructure, mechanical
properties and oxidation behaviour. From the results the
following conclusions are drawn:
– All investigated two-phase alloys consist of the compar-
ably ductile Fe–Al phase and a hard second phase which
is the Laves phase Zr(Fe,Al)
2 for the alloys with 0, 10,
and 20at.% Al and the t1 phase for 32 and 40at.% Al.
Due to the extended eutectic trough parallel to the Fe–Al
axis, the microstructure of the as-cast alloys consists of a
ﬁne-scaled eutectic Cprimary nearly Zr-free Fe–Al or
Laves phase or t
1 phase.
– The results from the tests on the 0.2% compressive yield
stress clearly show that both the Laves phase and the t1
phase act as strengthening phases. The strengthening
effect of equal volume fractions of Laves phase or t1
phase is about the same at high temperatures. However,
for the formation of the t1 phase lower Zr contents are
needed, i.e. for the same amount of added Zr the
strengthening effect is much stronger if the t
1 phase is
used as second phase.
– Alloys containing A2 CD03 Fe–Al show signiﬁcantly
increased 0.2% yield stresses compared to alloys with
only A2 or D0
3 Fe–Al.
– Thermal vacancies signiﬁcantly increase the low-tem-
perature yield stresses of Al-rich binary Fe–40Al and
