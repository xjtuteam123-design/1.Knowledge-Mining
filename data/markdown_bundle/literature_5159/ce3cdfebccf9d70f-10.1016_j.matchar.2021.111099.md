ABSTRACT 
A novel Cu-Co-Ti alloy with high strength of 480 MPa, high conductivity of 81% IACS and excellent softening 
temperature up to 600 
◦
C was fabricated via solid solution, cold rolling, and aging treatment. Various means 
were used to study the strengthening, conductive and anti-softening mechanisms of the alloys. It was detected 
that Co and Ti reacted and precipitated during the aging process. Both theoretical calculations and experimental 
results revealed that the solid solution scattering and precipitation strengthening were the dominant mechanisms 
that affected the electrical and mechanical properties of the alloy, respectively. While the high softening tem -
perature of the alloy was mainly due to the coarsening of the precipitates was restricted by the diffusion rate of 
Co in the Cu matrix. 
1. Introduction 
High strength and high conductivity Cu alloys are desired in many 
conductive devices, such as lead frames and high-speed railway contact 
wires [ 1 – 3 ]. Pure Cu enjoys a high electrical conductivity but low 
strength, and there exists a trade-off relationship between the strength 
and electrical conductivity of the Cu alloys. Sustained efforts have been 
devoted to study how to increase the strength with an insignificant 
reduction in the electrical conductivity of Cu [ 4 , 5 ]. Among the 
strengthening methods, precipitation strengthening is the most effective 
because the precipitation of the second phases can strengthen the alloy 
while purifying the Cu matrix, leading to both high strength and rela -
tively high conductivity of the Cu alloys [ 3 , 6 – 8 ]. Besides strength and 
conductivity, softening resistance is also a key performance of advanced 
Cu materials, for example, lead frame Cu materials require a softening 
temperature > 400 
◦
C [ 9 ]. 
Two different precipitation strengthening mechanisms exist: the so -
lute elements may directly precipitate, or they may react with others and 
precipitate compounds. Cu
–
Cr alloy is a representative Cu alloy of the 
former and has gained special attention for its excellent conductivity 
and strength due to the precipitation of Cr particles [ 10 ]. However, Cr 
precipitates are easy to coarsen at high temperatures so that the binary 
Cu
–
Cr exhibits poor softening resistance [ 11 , 12 ]. Restricting the 
coarsening of precipitates in the direct precipitation strengthening Cu 
alloy is mainly by the addition of a third element to form physical hin -
drances. For example, Zr is usually added to the alloy because it can 
distribute around Cr particles to restrain their coarsening [ 13 , 14 ]. 
Recently, more attention has been focused on adding Mg [ 15 ], Ti [ 16 ], 
and Nb [ 6 ] to hinder the growth of Cr and increasing the softening 
temperature of Cu
–
Cr alloys. Comparatively, the growth of precipitates 
in the reaction precipitation strengthening Cu alloy is itself restricted by 
the diffusion rate of the solute elements in the Cu matrix. For example, in 
the Cu-Ni-Si alloys, the process of Ni and Si react and co-precipitate 
δ -Ni
2
Si phases is restricted by the diffusion rate of Ni and Si in Cu 
[ 17 , 18 ]. 
A reaction precipitation strengthening Cu-Co-Ti alloy was synthe -
sized to develop high strength, high conductivity and high soften tem -
perature Cu alloys in this work. Due to the Co has a limited solubility in 
the Cu matrix, and has a relatively small mismatch with matrix ( < 2%) 
[ 19 ]. Co phase can precipitate from supersaturated solution and main -
tain a coherent face-centered cubic (FCC) structure until it transform 
into equilibrium hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure as growing up 
to a larger size [ 20 , 21 ]. However, the Co element is regarded to have a 
deteriorating effect on the conductivity of Cu [ 22 ]. Ti has a similar effect

2. Experimental 
The Cu-Co-Ti alloy was melted and cast in a medium-frequency in -
duction furnace under an argon atmosphere with the raw materials of 
pure copper (99.9%), cobalt (99.9%) and titanium (99.9%). The corre -
sponding composition of the alloy was Cu-0.61Co-0.43Ti (wt%), 
measured by an inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spec -
trometer (ICP-AES). After removing the surface oxides, the ingot (with a 
size of 20 × 50 × 100 mm
3
) was solid solution treated at 1050 
◦
C for 8 h 
and quenched with water. Subsequently, the alloy was rolled at room 
temperature to a total reduction of 90% in thickness and then aged at 
400, 500, 600 
◦
C for various times. 
The samples used for resistivity testing are discs with a diameter of 
20 mm and a thickness of 1.5 mm. The electrical resistivities of the 
samples were measured by using the RTS-11 four-probe resistivity tester 
at 20 
◦
C. Then the resistivities were transformed into electrical con -
ductivities according to the international annealed copper standard 
(IACS). The hardness was measured on the HDX-1000 micro Vickers 
hardness tester with an indentation load of 100 g and a holding time of 
10 s. Tensile specimens were measured on the Zwick/Roell Z020 tensile 
machine with a constant strain rate of 2.8 × 10
 4

ture. The samples for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) obser -
vations were prepared by electro-polishing by the Struers Tenpol-5 twin- 
jet unit with a methanol solution with 30 vol% nitric acid at 30 
◦
C. 
TEM observations were conducted on a JEM-2100 TEM with an accel -
eration voltage of 200 kV. The scanning transmission electron 
microscopy-high angle annular dark field (STEM-HAADF) observations 
were performed on an aberration-corrected FEI Themis Z STEM equip -
ped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) is tested on an X-Pert Powder model X-Ray diffractometer. A 
three-dimensional atom probe (3DAP) was detected to analyze the dis -
tribution of Co and Ti elements using the LEAP 4000 HP electron 
spectrometer (CAMECA Co., Gennevilliers, France) with a pulse rate of 
200 kHz at 50 K.
