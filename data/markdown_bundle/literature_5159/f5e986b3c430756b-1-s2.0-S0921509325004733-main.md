ABSTRACT
There is a revival of interest in niobium (Nb) based alloys for high-temperature structural applications, due to 
their relatively higher melting point, lower density, as well as more tolerance to interstitials compared to other 
refractory metals. Although some wrought Nb alloys were made by ingot metallurgy in the past, powder met -
allurgy processing of Nb alloys has not been well developed. In particular, optimum sintering conditions, alloy 
homogenization and densification behavior in powder metallurgy processing have not been well understood. In 
this study, alloying and sintering behavior of C103 niobium alloy (Nb-10Hf-1Ti; composition in wt.%), during 
spark plasma sintering (SPS), have been investigated. The objective is to study the densification, homogeniza -
tion, and microstructure evolution during spark plasma sintering at temperatures significantly below the solidus 
temperature. The alloy densified almost fully with very little residual porosity (~1.3 vol. %) and exhibited 394 
MPa yield strength, 458 MPa tensile strength, 23 % elongation and a plane stress fracture toughness (K
Q
) value of 
~25 MPa √ m. The strength levels are significantly higher than that of the wrought C103 alloy. The ductility was 
found to be only slightly reduced (from 26 to 23 %) relative to the wrought version, even though the oxygen 
levels were relatively higher ( > 2000 ppm) in the present alloy. The strength levels are also significantly higher 
than those found in additive-manufactured (EB-PBF and LB-PBF) C103 niobium alloys and without any loss in 
ductility. The strengthening mechanisms as well as the microstructural factors that lead to the ductility and high 
fracture toughness levels are discussed.
1. Introduction
An increase in the operational efficiencies of the gas turbine engines, 
beyond the state-of-the-art, requires relatively higher engine operating 
temperatures. For long-term endurance at such high temperatures, new 
structural alloys with mechanical properties that are substantially 
greater than that of nickel-based superalloys will be required. At oper -
ating temperatures higher than 1200
◦
C, good high temperature strength 
and adequate creep resistance are very important for adequate structural 
integrity in application. Nickel-base superalloys are limited to operating 
temperatures of about 900 – 1100
◦
C, and this is largely due to incipient 
melting that occurs around 1300
◦
C [ 1 ]. Therefore, the only option is to 
consider alloys based on refractory metals such as niobium (Nb), 
tantalum (Ta), tungsten (W), and molybdenum (Mo) [ 2 ]. Due to rela -
tively high homologues temperatures, alloys based on refractory metals 
generally have excellent mechanical properties at temperatures above 
1200
◦
C [ 3 , 4 ].
In recent years, Nb-based alloys have been in focus for very-high- 
temperature applications such as gas turbine blades, hypersonic re- 
entry vehicles, and rocket propulsion systems due to higher melting 
point of Nb (2468
◦
C), lower density (8.57 g/cm
3
) and excellent room 
temperature ductility. This research work is a part of the ARPA-E Ulti -
mate program that has been recently initiated for developing novel re -
fractory alloys for high-temperature applications. Additionally, Nb has 
relatively higher solubility levels for alloying elements in groups IV-VI, 
providing a wide scope for alloying. Nb forms extended solid solutions 
with tungsten (W), molybdenum (Mo), tantalum (Ta), vanadium (V), 
hafnium (Hf) and zirconium (Zr) [ 5 ], providing a greater flexibility for 
compositional variations in alloy development. In the past, some Nb 
alloys were developed by alloying with some refractory elements to 
increase strength and creep resistance strength through solid solutions 
[ 6 , 7 ], precipitation, and dispersion strengthening mechanisms [ 8 , 9 ]. 
When the degree of alloying is ensured to maintain ductility, good 
workability and weldability can be achieved in Nb alloys compared to 
other refractory alloys [ 10 ], which are significant advantages in 
manufacturing.

The C103 niobium alloy (Nb-10Hf-1Ti; composition in wt.%) is a 
well-known niobium alloy, commercially made as wrought alloy by 
vacuum arc melting followed by forging and rolling. In this alloy, Hf is 
added due to its effectiveness in the solid solution strengthening of 
niobium [ 11 ]. Alloying with titanium is known to increase the ductility 
and the fracture toughness [ 12 ]. Additionally, most niobium alloys 
contain significant oxygen due to contamination during alloy process -
ing. The C103 alloy specification limits the oxygen level to about 250 wt. 
ppm [ 13 ], because at levels higher than this, oxygen can lead to a sig -
nificant reduction in ductility [ 14 ]. Alloying with Hf, besides strength -
ening, also leads to the formation of hafnium oxide (HfO
2
) when excess 
oxygen is present, thus limiting the oxygen in solid solution. The Gibbs 
free energy of formation of HfO
2 
is more negative [ 15 ] relative to many 
transition metal oxides. Thus, Hf in C103 alloy helps to balance strength 
and ductility, by removing oxygen from Nb solid solution. Due to all 
these factors a good balance of properties exists in C103 alloy. Hence, 
the alloy is popular in applications such as thrust chambers, nozzles, 
extensions, and radiation shields in rocket engines and heat pipes in 
hypersonic vehicle [ 16 , 17 ].
Recently, there have been attempts to make C103 Nb alloy by ad -
ditive manufacturing techniques such laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 
[ 18 ], laser directed energy deposition (L-DED) [ 19 ], and electron beam 
powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) [ 20 ]. Some Nb alloys have also been made 
by powder metallurgy and hot isostatic pressing [ 21 , 22 ] techniques. 
However, the powder metallurgical (PM) processing of Nb alloys has not 
been well developed as a mainstream method, especially for the C103 
alloy. Naturally, the powder metallurgical (PM) processing using 
elemental powders will be challenging due to the relatively higher ox -
ygen levels in these powders, which lead to higher levels of oxygen in 
PM alloys. If the negative effects of oxygen on ductility are controlled, 
then the PM approach will be a promising approach for Nb alloys.
Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS) is a relatively new PM processing 
technique in which accelerated sintering and densification can be ach -
ieved by direct Joule heating of powders under pressure. Hence, it has 
some inherent advantages over hot pressing or hot-isostatic pressing 
(HIP) techniques [ 23 ]. Trapp et al. [ 24 – 26 ] have reviewed the details of 
the spark plasma sintering (SPS) method and identified the alloys that 
could be made by this technique. However, the SPS process has not yet 
been adapted for the fabrication of niobium alloys.
The novelty of this work is that for the first time SPS has been used 
here to make C103 alloy using commercially available elemental pow -
ders. SPS processed alloys do not need any post-processing treatments to 
obtain optimum microstructure and grain size, compared to other 
techniques, such as wrought processing or additive manufacturing. Also, 
when fully developed, the SPS process can produce near-net shaped 
components such as turbine blades, which require minimal fabrication 
steps for applications. However, the literature contains very little in -
formation about refractory alloys processed through spark plasma sin -
tering. It is shown here that in the SPS process yields alloys that are 
equivalent or better than the wrought alloy even with sintering tem -
peratures well below the solidus line.
The specific objective of this work is to show that C103 Nb alloys can 
be made by spark plasma sintering of elemental powders (despite having 
relatively higher oxygen levels), to yield alloys that are nearly fully 
dense and that can compete with wrought alloys in terms of strength and 
ductility. The alloying, homogenization and densification behavior 
during SPS processing was investigated and the mechanical properties 
were determined. The SPS processed alloy has a significantly higher 
strength compared to the wrought alloy. Surprisingly, the ductility is 
only slightly less than that of the wrought alloy, despite the high oxygen 
levels ( > 2000 wt. ppm) in the alloy.
2. Experimental procedure
The source materials are elemental powders of Nb ( 325 mesh, 99.9 
% pure), Hf ( 325 mesh, 99.6 % pure) and Ti ( 325 mesh, 99.5 % pure), 
all of which were obtained from Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Waltham, 
MA, USA. All of the elemental particles are of 325 mesh, which means 
that the maximum particle size is about 44 μ m. Hence, the distribution 
of particle sizes in the powders is expected to be below this value, likely 
following Gaussian distribution below this value. The oxygen content of 
the Nb, Hf and Ti powders were 2433, 4420 and 6380 wt. ppm, 
respectively.
For SPS processing, powder mixtures of nominal composition: Nb- 
10Hf-1Ti (wt.%), were prepared by blending the elemental powders 
for 24 h in a jar mill. Nb slugs were added to the powder mixture to 
break up agglomerates during milling. The jar milling was intended to 
achieve a good blending of powder particles without reducing the sizes. 
The blended powder was then sintered in the SPS unit (Model 10 – 4, 
Thermal Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA) using graphite dies. The sin -
tering temperatures and times were varied to determine the optimum 
solid-state sintering conditions at a temperature that is significantly 
below the solidus line (~2350
◦
C). The optimum sintering temperature 
was determined to be 1700
◦
C based on multiple trials in the temperature 
range of 1500 – 1800
◦
C. At this sintering temperature, dense samples of 
size: 20 mm in diameter and 7.84 mm in thickness can be sintered and 
densified almost to full density in 2 h under a pressure of 10 MPa. The 
samples were heated at a rate of 50
◦
C/min to the sintering temperature. 
The temperature was controlled using an optical pyrometer throughout 
the sintering process. The power, pressure, temperature, and ram 
displacement were recorded throughout the process.
The sintered samples were sectioned using a diamond saw and 
metallographically polished following the standard preparation tech -
nique, with the final polishing completed using 0.3 μ m alumina sus -
pension. The polished samples were etched using a solution of 
composition: 30 ml hydrofluoric acid (HF), 15 ml nitric acid (HNO
3
) and 
30 ml hydrochloric acid (HCl). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
imaging and X-ray elemental mapping of the polished samples were 
performed using FEI TENEO FEG scanning electron microscope. X-ray 
diffraction was performed using Miniflex 600 (Rigaku, Japan) diffrac -
tometer (scanning at 5 deg./min with a step size of 0.05 deg.) to 
determine lattice parameters and the phases. Porosity (vol. %) levels 
were determined by the standard point count technique [ 27 ]. Oxygen 
levels were determined by Leco analyzer (model ON 736).
For mechanical testing, a relatively large C103 alloy disc (76 mm in 
diameter and 15 mm in thickness) was made using a SPS sintering unit 
with a higher capacity. The larger disc was also sintered at 1700
◦
C for 2 
h and with similar heating and cooling conditions of the smaller sam -
ples. Density and microstructural studies confirmed that the larger 
samples sintered very similar to the smaller samples. The average grain 
size was determined by the linear intercept method. The size, spacing 
and volume fraction of HfO
2 
particles were determined by the standard 
grid line intercept technique [ 27 ]. Micrographs from multiple regions 
were used to determine the average values of the size, spacing and 
volume fraction of particles.
Tensile testing was conducted to determine the mechanical proper -
ties of SPS processed alloy. Specimens for tensile tests and fracture 
toughness tests were made by a combination of electrical discharge 
machining (EDM) and milling. The tensile samples had a gage section 
with 2 × 2 mm
2 
in cross-section and 12.5 mm in length. Prior to tensile 
testing, the samples were polished to remove the surfaces layers affected 
by machining. Room temperature tensile tests were performed at the 
engineering strain rate of 0.0005 s
 1
. The strain was measured using an 
extensometer (Epsilon Model 3442). Fracture toughness testing was 
conducted to determine the critical stress intensity factor for plane stress 
fracture. These tests employed single-edge-notched four-point-bend 
specimens (3 × 4 × 50 mm
3
) per ASTM C1421-10 standard [ 28 ]. An 
automated MTS 810 mechanical testing system was used to perform the 
tensile and fracture toughness tests.

◦

same cooling rate as the first cycle. The ram displacement that occurred 
during the second cycle is largely due to the expansion of the sample and 
the tools. The displacement data from the second cycle is then sub -

subtraction (or correction) are shown in Fig. 1 d for all sintering times. 
The corrected displacement data for all the five samples are in good 
agreement, reflecting the true densification behavior. That is, each of the 
expansion-corrected displacement profiles followed the same densifi -

3.6. Mechanical properties
3.6.1. Tensile properties
The tensile stress-strain curves of test specimens taken from different 
regions of the SPS-processed C103 disc are shown in Fig. 7 a. For com -
parison, the stress-strain curves of AM-processed C103 alloys, reported 
in literature, are also included. The SPS processed alloy shows signifi -
cantly higher yield and tensile strength levels and a slightly lower 
elongation, compared to the alloys processed by electron beam powder 
bed fusion (EB-PBF) and laser beam powder beam fusion (LB-PBF) 
techniques.
The true stress-strain curves are presented in Fig. 7 b, including the 
work hardening rate calculations. The work-hardening rate (dS/de) 
steadily decreases with increasing strain and is consistent with the 
dislocation-induced hardening behavior. Another noteworthy observa -
tion is that the strains at the ultimate tensile strengths of the three 
samples are nearly equal to the strain hardening exponent (n), which are 
0.12, 0.11, and 0.13, respectively for the three samples tested, which is 
consistent with the beginning of tensile instability at ultimate tensile 
strength.
3.6.2. Fractography
Fractography of tensile samples ( Fig. 8 a,b) showed the presence of 
micro-voids on the fracture surface, indicating fracture by void nucle -
ation and coalescence, which supports the observed ductility in the 
alloy. The EDS spot analysis ( Fig. 8 e,f) reveals that the particles present 
in the large voids are HfO
2
, suggesting that void growth and coalescence 
occurred from these particles during fracture, in addition to the void 
growth and coalescence from the matrix. A relatively small fraction of 
Nb grains fractured by cleavage ( Fig. 8 d). The slightly lower elongation 
of the SPS-processed alloy, relative to the wrought version, is likely due 
to these cleavage fracture areas.

4. Discussion
4.1. Sintering and densification behavior
The ram displacement data ( Fig. 1 b) indicates that significant 
consolidation occurred during the heating period, even with a relatively 
low pressure of 5 MPa. The beginning of consolidation after reaching the 
sintering temperature and the pressure (10 MPa), is marked by an 
inflexion point in the displacement profile ( Fig. 1 b). The rapid decrease 
in displacement after the inflexion point ( Fig. 1 c and d) indicates that 
significant further consolidation occurred during the holding period. 
Beyond about 40 min of holding, the displacement profiles tapered out. 
From metallography ( Fig. 3 d) and density data, it was determined that 
nearly full densification was achieved in 2 h at 1700
◦
C. This sintering 
temperature is ~650

10Hf (wt.%) alloy [ 32 ].

4.4. Mechanical properties
A summary of tensile properties of SPS processed C103 alloy, along 
with that of alloys processed by different methods are presented in 
Table II . Although the C103 alloy specification is fixed, subtle variations 
in composition can occur due to different processing histories of the 
alloys. The additively manufactured versions of C103 alloy (EB-PBF and 
LB-PBF) show relatively lower strength levels compared to the SPS 
processed alloy. It was noted in a study [ 20 ] that the EB-PBF C103 alloy 
had some evaporation of Ti, leading to a final Ti content of about 0.75 
wt.% dissolved in the matrix. The lower level of oxygen and titanium in 
niobium matrix of the EB-PBF-processed alloy is likely the reason for its 
lower strength relative to the SPS-processed C103 alloy. The LB-PBF 
processed C103 alloy [ 40 ] had an overall oxygen level of 370 wt. 
ppm. After forming some HfO
2
, the final level of oxygen in Nb matrix is 
likely to be lower than that in the matrix of the SPS processed C103 
alloy. This can explain the relatively lower strength and the higher 
ductility of the LB-PBF alloy, relative to the SPS processed C103 alloy.
In this work, although the elemental powders had high oxygen 
levels, most of the oxygen is captured in the form of HfO
2 
particles. 
However, the HfO
2 
particles do not significantly affect ductility. Rather 
a small decrease in ductility (from about 26 % in wrought alloy to about 
23 % in the SPS processed alloy) is observed indicating that the HfO
2

amount of oxygen (346 wt. ppm) remains in Nb matrix, contributing 
significantly to the strength of the alloy. Therefore, the SPS process has 
the advantage of a significant increase in strength, without severely 
degrading the ductility, relative to the wrought alloy.
The only processing induced defect that is applicable here is the 
porosity. The porosity after sintering at 1700

inverse relationship between strength and ductility.

5. Conclusions
The study demonstrated that the spark plasma sintering (SPS) based 
powder metallurgical processing approach is successful in making bulk 
C103 niobium alloy with good mechanical properties. The specific 
conclusions are: 
1. The spark plasma sintering process resulted in a homogeneous and 
nearly fully dense C103 alloy during solid state sintering at tem -
peratures far below (~650
◦
C) the solidus temperature. Sintering at 
1700
◦
C took only 2 h for alloying, sintering and densification.
2. A major part of alloying and densification occurs during the heat-up 
period to the sintering temperature. The remaining alloying and 
densification was complete at the sintering temperature in 2 h.
3. The SPS-processed C103 alloy has significantly higher yield (394 
MPa), and tensile strength (458 MPa) levels compared to wrought, 
EB-PBF and LB-PBF processed versions. Solid solution strengthening 
by hafnium, titanium, and oxygen were mainly found to be respon -
sible for the improvement in tensile properties. The strengthening 
contributions due to grain boundaries, and dispersion strengthening 
due to oxide particles, were found to be quite small.
4. Fractography indicated that tensile fracture occurred by the nucle -
ation and growth of large voids from the hafnium oxide (HfO
2
) 
particles, as well as the nucleation and growth of smaller voids from 
Nb alloy matrix, supporting well the good ductility (23 %) levels 
observed in the alloy.
5. The SPS-processed niobium alloy also exhibited good plane stress 
fracture toughness (K
Q
~25 MPa √ m), arising from the extensive 
plastic deformation of the crack tip during crack propagation, which 
is consistent with the significant ductility in the alloy.
