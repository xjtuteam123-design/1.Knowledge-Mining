ABSTRACT
Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) of Ni-based superalloys is critical for aerospace applications, yet cracking re -
mains a significant challenge. This review examines composition strategies, focusing on the solid solution, 
precipitation, and grain boundary strengthening elements in crack susceptibility. Alloying elements are cate -
gorized into three groups: (i) Segregating elements (e.g., B, C, Hf, Zr), which widen the solidification temperature 
range (STR), increasing hot cracking risk but mitigating other elemental segregation; (ii) High-melting-point, 
non-segregating elements (e.g., Co, Mo, W, Cr), which enhance solid solution strengthening, refine primary γ ′ 
morphology, and reduce solid-state cracking; and (iii) Long-term stability elements, which suppress TCP phase 
precipitation and control mixed-mode cracking. These findings guide the design of crack-resistant Ni-based su -
peralloys for LPBF.

Recent studies demonstrate that modifying alloy composition with 
solute elements like Cr, which have large atomic sizes and low diffusion 
rates [ 22 ], reduces cracking sensitivity by slowing elemental diffusion 
and suppressing γ ′ coarsening [ 23 ]. Similarly, Harrison et al. [ 24 ] 
reduced cracking density in LPBFed Hastelloy X by 65 % through W and 
Mo adjustments, while Murray et al. [ 25 ] developed a crack-free Co-Ni- 
based superalloy, leveraging Co's ability to reduce stacking fault energy 
and promote recrystallization. These studies highlight that solid- 
solution-strengthening elements effectively suppress crack initiation 
and growth, also enhancing the strengthening effects of precipitation- 
hardening and grain boundary-strengthening elements.
Recent studies have extensively investigated cracking in LPBFed 
nickel-based superalloys, emphasizing the role of alloying elements in 
reducing crack sensitivity [ 26 – 29 ]. However, a systematic analysis of 
the individual and synergistic effects of solid-solution-strengthening, 
precipitation-hardening, and grain boundary (GB) strengthening ele -
ments on cracking behavior remains limited. This review analyzes the 
microstructural characteristics of LPBFed nickel-based superalloys, 
identifies crack formation mechanisms, and examines the role of alloy -
ing elements in mitigating crack initiation and propagation. It proposes 
compositional design strategies to suppress cracking, aiming to provide 
a comprehensive reference for developing nearly crack-free superalloys 
for LPBF. Studies on process parameter optimization, printing strategies, 
and heat treatment conditions are excluded, as they have been exten -
sively addressed in prior reviews [ 8 , 13 , 19 , 30 – 32 ].
2. Mechanism of crack formation
The cracking mechanism associated with the additive elements in Ni- 
based superalloys for LPBF is briefly covered below.
2.1. Types of cracks in LPBFed Ni-based superalloys
2.1.1. Solidification cracking
Two temperature ranges during solidification exhibit significant 
ductility reduction, corresponding to crack initiation: the first, between 
the solidus (T
S
) and liquidus (T
L
), is associated with hot cracking. So -
lidification cracking (SC) occurs due to element segregation in inter -
dendritic regions (IRs) [ 33 ] during LPBF thermal cycling. This 
segregation lowers the IR solidus [ 34 ], inducing non-equilibrium so -
lidification. As dendrite arms connect, insufficient liquid replenishment 
traps solute-rich molten metal between semisolid surfaces [ 35 ], forming 
a low-strength semisolid zone prone to thermal stress, resulting in holes, 
cracks, and pores.
2.1.2. Liquation cracking
Similar to solidification cracking (SC), liquation cracking (LC) also 
initiates during the early stages [ 33 ], driven by intergranular liquid film 
fracture ( Fig. 1 b) [ 36 ]. LC involves elemental segregation, lowering the 
solidus temperature in interdendritic regions (IR) and forming low- 
melting-point phases via eutectic transformations [ 37 ]. During LPBF 
thermal cycles, these phases repeatedly remelt, sustaining metastable 
liquid films at the precipitate – matrix interface [ 38 ], which fracture 
under solidification stress, causing cracks [ 39 ]. Fig. 2 a illustrates the 
microstructural morphology of SC and LC in LPBFed nickel-based 
superalloys.
2.1.3. Other types of cracks
As shown in Fig. 1 (a), below the dynamic recrystallization threshold 
but within the grain boundary sliding range (0.5 – 0.7 of the solidus 
temperature) [ 40 ], grain boundary sliding induces DDC [ 41 ], as shown 
in Fig. 1 (c). While γ ′ precipitates and carbides hinder dislocation slip, 
causing stress concentration that exceeds stress relaxation and drives 
SAC initiation [ 42 ]. SAC and DDC interact synergistically, influencing 
crack propagation, as shown in Fig. 2 .
Additionally, cold cracking could occur post-solidification ( Fig. 2 b), 
induced by inconsistent shrinkage from incompatible cooling rates, but 
it is rare in LPBFed nickel-based superalloys and can be mitigated by 
post-heat treatment [ 43 , 44 ].
Based on the description of the crack formation mechanism 
above, the following can be summarized. 
(i) Thermal cracking results from liquid film rupture due to solidus 
mismatch between DC and IR under solidification stress, while 
solid-state cracking occurs when solidification stress exceeds the 
yield strength (YS) [ 26 , 46 ].
(ii) Cracks propagate along high-angle columnar GBs [ 37 ], and 
controlling the volume fraction and coarsening behavior of pre -
cipitates can mitigate solid-state cracking [ 8 ]. However, their 
high volume fraction and non-uniform coarsening may exacer -
bate cracking [ 47 ]. Understanding columnar grain evolution and 
precipitation coarsening kinetics is essential for reducing LPBF- 
induced cracking.

2.2.3. Effect of γ ′ phase on cracking
The γ ′ phase, the primary strengthening phase in Ni-based superal -
loys, enhances strength via precipitation hardening by impeding dislo -
cation motion in the γ matrix [ 19 , 61 , 62 ]. With a Burgers vector twice 
that of the γ matrix, the γ ′ phase exhibits fourfold lattice distortion en -
ergy [ 63 ], significantly increasing the difficulty of dislocation nucle -
ation, storage, and movement within the γ channel ( Fig. 6 b3, b4). At 
this stage, dislocations from the γ matrix correspond to partial disloca -
tions in the γ ′ phase [ 64 ], requiring energy exceeding the antiphase 
boundary (APB) formation energy to avoid being trapped by the γ / γ ′ 
interfacial dislocation network, which relaxes misfit strain ( Fig. 6 c) 
[ 65 ]. Although the γ matrix tolerates dislocations [ 66 ], excessive density 
can trigger SAC at the γ / γ ′ interface [ 67 ].
In addition, elemental diffusion promotes the coarsening, volume 
fraction increase, and cubic of the γ ′ phase [ 65 ], thereby facilitating SAC 
cracking. The diffusion of γ ′ -consist elements into the γ ′ phase enhances 
its growth and volume fraction, while elemental additions alter the 
lattice parameter, increasing γ / γ ′ misfit and inducing cubic trans -
formation ( Fig. 6 d – e): Ni-site substitutions increase it, while Al-site 
substitutions decrease it. However, excessive lattice mismatch induces 
γ ' rafting ( Fig. 6 c), which accelerates dislocation accumulation at the 
raft tips and leads to premature alloy failure [ 68 ]. Concurrently, stress 
concentration from rafting is accommodated by GB sliding in the γ 
matrix, further promoting DDC crack initiation [ 2 , 6 ].
2.2.4. Effect of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases and borides/ 
carbides on cracking
As shown in Fig. 7 a, TCP phases act as brittle phases and serve as 
crack nucleation sites during the LPBF deposition process. TCP phases 
accelerate alloy failure under high temperature and stress [ 69 – 71 ] 
through several mechanisms: (1) TCP precipitation depletes strength -
ening elements (e.g., Re, Cr, Co), forming precipitation-free zones (PFZs) 
that expand above 1100 
◦
C, creating stress concentration sites and 
promoting cracking [ 70 , 72 ]; (2) large solute atoms induce local stress/ 
strain fields due to γ -matrix lattice distortion, facilitating heterogeneous 
TCP precipitation at defects [ 73 , 74 ]; (3) TCP phases nucleate in solute- 
supersaturated regions, while dislocation glide/climb drives element 
segregation along γ / γ ′ interfaces, triggering γ ′ rafting and partial γ ′ 
dissolution, which releases TCP-forming elements and further promotes 
TCP precipitation [ 69 , 75 ]. Dislocation accumulation in γ / γ ′ channels 
and their invasion into the γ ′ phase destabilize γ ′ , promoting its trans -
formation into TCP phases during LPBF aging ( Fig. 7 b – c).
In addition, carbides and borides in LPBFed Ni-based superalloys 
also act as brittle phases that can induce SAC-like TCP phases [ 76 ].

3.2. Effect of solid solution-strengthening elements on cracking
Fig. 9 a illustrates the diffusion coefficient K of alloying elements in 
Ni-based superalloys. Elements with K ≥ 1 (type I, e.g., Al, Fe, W, Co, Cr) 
exhibit positive or neutral segregation, concentrating near dendrite 
cores and increasing solidus and liquidus temperatures. Conversely, el -
ements with K < 1 (type II, e.g., Zr, B, C, Hf, Ti) display negative 
segregation, accumulating in interdendritic regions and reducing the 
solidus and liquidus temperatures [ 43 ]. Although Re and Ru are also 
classified as type I elements that elevate the solidus and liquidus tem -
peratures, they are typically not considered solid-solution strengthening 
elements [ 93 ]. The combined addition of type I and type II elements 
broadens the solidification range, raising hot cracking susceptibility. 
Solid-solution strengthening elements, as outlined in Section 3.1 , exhibit 
low segregation tendencies or remain within dendrite cores, restricting 
matrix mobility and effectively hindering dislocation diffusion. Their 
larger atomic diameters compared to Ni induce substantial lattice 
distortion ( Fig. 9 b), elevate the shear modulus, and enhance alloy 
strength [ 94 – 96 ].
Solid-solution elements play a critical role in yield strength, influ -
encing solid-state cracking behavior [ 89 ]. Such cracks predominantly 
form in interdendritic regions when local yield strength falls below re -
sidual solidification stress and may propagate along hot cracks, resulting 
in mixed hot-solid-state cracks [ 26 ]. The alloy's resistance to residual 
stress-induced cracking is termed Tearing Stress Resistance (TSR). Fig. 9
c demonstrates that increased Co content enhances the yield strength of 
U720LI Ni-based superalloy [ 99 ]. Additionally, a slight increase in Mo 
and W content in Ni-based superalloys could reduce crack density by 
nearly 65 % by inducing lattice distortion to improve TSR [ 24 ]. Notice 
that the strengthening effect of solid-solution elements depends on their 
matrix solubility, as per the Hume-Rothery rule, which states that ele -
ments with atomic size deviations within ± 15 % exhibit high solubility. 
Fig. 9 b summarizes their solubility limits in the γ matrix [ 102 , 103 ].

exhibit a similar effect. W and Re exhibit low diffusion rates in the γ

However, Nb, Ta, Ti, Al, and Ni are also constituent elements of low-

high-temperature processing, driven by dislocation slip and nucleation, 
leads to coarse, irregular eutectic and TCP phase precipitation,

composition design for LPBFed Ni-based superalloys should prioritize 
component correlation analysis over trial-and-error approaches.
Ru, a long-term stabilizing element, effectively inhibits TCP phase 
formation through its “ reverse partitioning effect, ” altering the segre -
gation behavior of γ and γ ′ phase-forming elements [ 142 – 144 ]. Adding 
3 % Ru reduces the distribution of Cr, Co, Mo, and Re in the γ matrix and 
Al and Ta in the γ ′ phase ( Fig. 10 c). Although the exact mechanism is 
unclear, this segregation attenuation suppresses γ ′ rafting, mitigating 
cracking and TCP precipitation [ 145 ]. Fig. 10 d shows that Ru signifi -
cantly reduces TCP formation, while Ru-free alloys exhibit increased 
TCP volume fractions with higher temperatures and aging time.

influencing γ ′ phase characteristics: Al > Ti > Nb > Ta > Mo for 
increasing γ ′ volume fraction, and Al > Ti > Mo > Nb > Ta for enhancing 
γ ′ size. Mo, Cr, and Ni significantly affect γ ′ morphology, with Mo 
showing the highest potency. Al, Ti, and Mo notably increase γ ′ size,

Ta, Al, and Nb critically impact γ ′ morphology, potentially leading to

and precise component content to avoid cracking.
4.1. Regulating the inherent composition
Traditional composition design for LPBFed Ni-based superalloys is 
limited by lengthy and costly procedures. Modern methods enable the 
development of complex alloys suitable for additive manufacturing. 
Tang et al. [ 54 ] designed two weldable, higher crack-resistant Ni-based 
superalloys, ABD-850 AM and ABD-900 AM, using a modified Scheil 
model ( Al þ 0.15 Ta þ 0.5 Ti þ 0.3 Nb < 4 wt% ) to resist SAC cracking 
during LPBF processing. In contrast, IN939 and CM247LC, with γ ′ phase 
content, exhibit more cracks under identical conditions ( Fig. 13 a). 
Preliminary results ( Fig. 13 b) show that ABD-850 AM offers superior 
elevated-temperature ductility due to its crack-free surface, though 
strength properties need enhancement. The modified Scheil model 
provides a compositional guideline for developing crack-resistant 
LPBFed Ni-based superalloys.
In brief, hot cracking originates from elemental segregation-induced 
STR widening, while solid-state cracking stems from strain accumula -
tion or residual stresses surpassing the alloy's YS. Notably, sufficient 
inherent YS can prevent cracking despite internal strain accumulation 
[ 168 ]. To address these challenges, Xu et al. [ 43 ] introduced the HR-DR 
(Hot resistance-deformation resistance) model, comprehensively eval -
uating component contributions to STR and YS.

5. Summary and outlook
Cracking in LPBFed Ni-based superalloys is primarily driven by 
element segregation at IR and GB, leading to low-melting-point phase 
formation. External factors, such as rapid laser-induced melting and 
solidification, generate excessive thermal stresses. Additionally, STR, GB 
angles, stress states, and their interaction with temperature distribution 
significantly influence crack initiation and propagation. The suscepti -
bility to cracking during LPBF can be reduced by following several 
strategies: 
1) Dendrite morphology significantly influences cracking susceptibility. 
Coarse, elongated dendrites enhance segregation and liquation 
cracking, while refined dendrite structures hinder crack propagation. 
Higher cooling rates from increased scanning speeds and reduced 
hatch spacing promote refined dendrite formation.
2) The improved Scheil model guides in regulating the inherent 
composition, while the HR-DR model aids LPBF alloy composition 
design. However, elements that markedly widen the STR, particu -
larly B, Hf, and Zr, should be carefully controlled, as they exacerbate 
hot cracking more severely than solid-state cracking.
3) A cuboidal γ ′ morphology enhances mechanical properties when the 
γ / γ ′ lattice misfit remains ≤ 0.4 %; higher values promote rafting and 
cracking. Ti offers superior γ ' strengthening compared to Al, while Ta

This work proposes the following strategies from the perspec -

process: 
1) For SSS elements, prioritize those with weak diffusion ability or 
those that hinder solute atom diffusion, such as large atomic ele -
ments (e.g., Re) with high vacancy-binding energy. Slow diffusion 
mitigates hot cracking by reducing element segregation and sup -
pressing γ ′ phase coarsening. Additionally, the refractory nature of 
these atoms ensures dislocation motion restraint at elevated tem -
peratures, enhancing high-temperature creep resistance, γ ′ phase 
stability, and reducing SAC and DDC cracking risks.
2) For precipitation-strengthening elements, prioritize those with high 
atomic radii and strong Al/Ni bonds to enhance matrix YS and 
reduce solid-state cracks. When precipitated, these elements increase 
dislocation-cutting energy in the γ ′ phase and strengthen its cubic 
morphology through lattice mismatch. Additionally, super -
computing approaches, such as machine learning, investigate 
synergistic effects between PS elements, minimizing TCP phase 
formation.
3) Al and Ti are primary precipitation-strengthening elements but can 
accelerate γ ′ phase coarsening and cracking. Modest substitution 
with Nb and Ta is recommended. However, excessive PS elements 
may form TCP phases at elevated temperatures, degrading alloy 
performance and promoting cracking. The phase transformation 
conditions must be predicted, and the precipitation-cracking sensi -
tivity index can be estimated using the formula 0.5 wTi + 0.3 wNb +
0.15 wTa + wAl < 4 wt%.
4) GB strengthening elements, essential for enhancing high- 
temperature creep resistance, are prone to segregation at dendrite 
boundaries, increasing hot cracking susceptibility. Minor elements, 
including GB strengtheners and impurities, should form fine pre -
cipitates with other constituents. The crack sensitivity influence is Zr 
> B > Hf > C > Si > Mn.
