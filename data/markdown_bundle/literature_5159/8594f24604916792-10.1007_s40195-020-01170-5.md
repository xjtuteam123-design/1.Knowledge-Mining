Abstract
The process of preparing SiC fiber-reinforced γ-TiAl composites by the conventional methods is difficult and complicated due 
to the high reactivity, high melting point and poor deformability of γ-TiAl alloys. In this work, suction casting, a promising 
method for preparing SiCf/TiAl composite, had been attempted. In the process, γ-TiAl alloy melt was introduced rapidly into 
a mold within pre-arranged fibers that were coated with additional layer of titanium alloy. This simple method successfully 
prevented serious reactions between the alloy melt and the fibers which remained intact during the solidification process. The 
interfacial reaction layer was observed by optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The interfacial reac-
tion products were identified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Tensile tests of the matrix alloy and composites 
were performed at room temperature and 800 °C. The results exhibited that the tensile strength of SiC
f/γ-TiAl composite 
was higher than that of the matrix alloy at both room temperature and 800 °C. At room temperature, tensile strength of 
 SiCf/γ-TiAl composite was increased by about 7% (50 MPa), whereas a double increase in tensile strength 14% (100 MPa) 
was obtained at 800 °C. The titanium alloy coating on the fiber not only prevented the serious interfacial reaction between 
the γ-TiAl alloy melt and the SiC fiber, but also played a role in delaying the propagation of cracks in the matrix to the fiber 
at 800 °C. The fracture mechanism of the composite was analyzed by fracture metallographic analysis.

1 Introduction
Continuous SiC fiber-reinforced titanium matrix alloy com-
posites (TMC) are promising materials for high-tempera-
ture structural applications due to their excellent mechani-
cal properties such as high specific stiffness and specific 
strength [1]. Matrix alloys that can be used for TMC mate-
rials include (α + β) type [2 –4], metastable β type [5 ] and 
near α type [6], as well as γ-TiAl [7–9] as α2-Ti
3Al [10, 11]. 
Among these matrix alloys, γ-TiAl possesses the best com-
prehensive mechanical properties at elevated temperature 
[12, 13]; therefore, SiC fiber-reinforced γ-TiAl composite 
materials can be used at higher temperature compared to 
the other TMCs, and they were considered to be one of the 
most promising aerospace materials [7, 14]. Since the 1990s, 
several methods have been used to prepare SiC
f/γ-TiAl com-
posite, including powder metallurgy [ 8, 15], powder cloth 
[16], plasma spray [9 ], foil–fiber–foil [17 , 18] and matrix-
coated fiber [19]. However, these methods are complicated 
and expensive. Hence, it is difficult to manufacture parts 
that meet engineering requirements by utilization of those 
methods. Undoubtedly, casting is a simple and cost-effective 
preparation process. However, it has been reported that a 
serious reaction occurred between the TiAl liquid alloy and 
the SiC fibers in direct contact [20]. If the problem of the 
reaction between the SiC fiber and the TiAl liquid alloy can 
be solved, the casting method will be a more advantageous 
method to prepare SiC
f/γ-TiAl composite. In this study, a 
SiC fiber coated with titanium alloy was used to prepare 
composite materials. This titanium alloy is expected to

strength of composite materials and their fracture mecha-

analysis of tensile test results and fractographs.
2 Experimental
2.1 Materials and Fabrication Process
Ti–48Al–2Cr–2Nb master alloy ingots were prepared by 
vacuum arc melting. Each ingot was remelted 5–6 times 
to obtain a homogeneous chemical composition. The SiC 
fibers used in this study were prepared by chemical vapor 
deposition. The fiber has a tungsten core with a diameter of 
about 13 μm, and outside of the SiC was a layer of carbon 
coating about 2 μm in thickness. A layer of titanium alloy 
(Ti–5Al–2Sn–2Zr–4Cr–4Mo, at.%) was sprayed on the sur-
face of the fiber by magnetron sputtering. The fiber with 
additional surface coatings used in this work is shown in 
Fig. 1.
The composite was prepared by a vacuum suction casting 
equipment. Figure 2a shows a schematic of the suction cast-
ing process. Before suction casting, the fibers were arranged 
inside the mold as shown in Fig. 2b. The fibers were passed 
through holes A and B at both ends of the mold, and then 
the two ends were fixed by a special ceramic binder that 
was completely separated from the inner runner to prevent 
contamination. In this study, 20 fibers were added to each 
composite. After that, the vacuum system was opened, and 
during the vacuuming process, the melting chamber and the 
suction chamber were connected. The vacuum system was 
closed when the vacuum was below 10
–1 Pa. At the same 
time, the connection valve between the two chambers was 
also closed and the melting chamber was filled with 50 MPa 
high-purity argon gas. Finally, the master alloy was remelted 
for casting suction. After the alloy melted, the connection 
valve between the two chambers was opened quickly, and 
then the melt filled the mold under its gravity and the dif -
ferential pressure between the two chambers immediately. 
The SiC fibers were enclosed by the melt, and the compos-
ite was obtained after solidification process. The prepared 
composite rod was 10 mm in diameter and 75 mm in length; 
reference samples of the γ -TiAl matrix alloy were also cast 
in the same manner.

3 Results
3.1 Microstructure and the Interfacial Reaction 
of the Composite
Figure 3a shows the fiber distribution on the cross section of 
a composite bar. Overall, the fibers are concentrated at the 
center of the cross section, but the individual fiber was dis-
tributed randomly and some of the fibers were not separated 
from each other. The bright gray band around the fibers 
indicates the titanium alloy coating. The microstructure of 
the matrix is composed of equiaxed dendrites. In addition, 
a small amount of γ phase segregation is generally visible 
between the dendrites. The microstructure of the matrix 
alloy observed under the scanning electron microscope is 
fully lamellar, as shown in Fig. 3b, with the white lines rep-
resenting the orientations of the lamellae.
The morphology of the interfacial reaction can be seen 
in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig.  4a, the fiber was tightly bound 
to the matrix alloy and it remained intact even after solidi-
fication. Areas I, III, II indicate the titanium alloy coating, 
the γ-TiAl matrix and the transition layer between them, 
respectively. Figure 4b shows the interfacial reaction layer 
formed between the carbon coating and the titanium alloy 
coating. The average thickness of the carbon coating and 
reaction zone after casting is 1.06 μm and 1.39 μm, respec-
tively. Compared with its original thickness of 2 μm, the 
carbon layer is almost halved in its final thickness due to 
reactions with the titanium alloys at the high-temperature 
period experienced during casting.
The interfacial reaction products of the as-cast SiC
f/
TiAl composite were identified by TEM. Figure  5 shows 
the microstructure of the interfacial reaction layer and the 
selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of the 
interfacial products. The reaction layer consists of layer I 
with fine equiaxed grain about 50–100 nm and layer II with 
coarse equiaxed grain about 800–1000 nm. According to

layer I; as a result, the growth between grains was no longer 
constrained by each other.
3.2 Tensile Properties
Tensile test results of the matrix alloy and composite are 
given in Table 1, and the corresponding stress–strain curves 
are shown in Fig. 6. As expected, the tensile strength of the 
composites was higher than that of the matrix alloy at both 
room temperature and 800 °C. The tensile strength at room 
temperature was increased by about 7% (50 MPa), whereas 
a double increase in tensile strength 14% (100 MPa) was 
obtained at 800 °C. As for yield strength, no values were 
obtained at room temperature (seen in Table 1), which might 
be attributed to the low ductility of the as-cast alloy and 
composite material. The existence of defects is the main 
reason behind the low ductility of as-cast alloy. Among these 
defects can be cited shrinkage cavities and segregation that

when subjected to tensile loading, so it was easy for the 
crack to initiate, thus affecting the mechanical properties of 
the material.

equiaxed crystal is mainly composed of C and Ti, and their 
atomic ratio was almost 1:1, which indicated the product of 
the interfacial reaction layer (TiC). Obviously, interfacial 
debonding occurred between the interfacial reaction layer 
and the carbon layer.
The interfacial bonding strength is an important key fac-
tor in reinforcing the composite. For instance, if the interfa-
cial bonding strength is too weak, the load cannot be effec-
tively transferred to the fiber, while if it is strong enough, 
the crack expands easily into the fiber. Only the moderate 
interface bonding strength will be suitable to enhance the 
strength of the composite material [ 30]. At room tempera -
ture, most fibers had moderate interfacial bonding strength, 
as shown in Fig. 9d. When the crack propagated to this fiber, 
the stress concentration at the crack tip forced the interfacial 
debonding. Then, the crack continued to propagate along 
the interface and deflected to the matrix until the energy of 
the crack tip was completely released. This process played 
a role in preventing and delaying the crack growth, thus 
increasing the strength of the material. However, a few fibers 
had a strong interfacial bonding strength with the matrix at 
room temperature; when the crack propagated to such fiber, 
it directly grew into the fiber; as a result, this may lead to 
catastrophic failure of the fiber, as seen in Fig. 9c. Therefore, 
this part of the fibers did not have the effect of increasing the 
strength of the material. According to the above analysis, it 
can be concluded that at room temperature, those fibers with 
moderate interface bonding strength had the reinforcement 
effect. The main fracture mechanisms of the composite at 
room temperature consisted of cleavage fracture and trans-
lamellar fracture of the matrix alloy, interface debonding of 
the fiber, crack deflection and fiber pullout.
Figure  10 shows the fractographs of tensile tests at 
800 °C. Compared to the case at room temperature (Fig. 9a), 
the matrix alloy underwent larger deformation. Interface 
delamination occurred between the lamellas. Additionally, 
the cleavage “tongues” and river pattern were almost invis-
ible. Translamellar fracture was recognized to be the main 
failure mode (Fig.  10a). As shown in Fig.  10b, there was a 
great degree of fiber pullout as compared with the fracture 
morphology at room temperature. On some of the fracture 
surfaces, few broken fibers can also be seen in Fig. 10c. EDS 
analysis results showed that these fibers had a carbon layer 
attached to the surface. Interfacial debonding occurred in 
all the fibers. Meanwhile, all the fiber holes have undergone 
large deformation as shown in Fig. 10d. Moreover, the car-
bon layer remained well bonded with the fiber. The appear-
ance of dimples in Fig.  10e indicates that obvious plastic 
deformation occurred at the titanium alloy coating. In addi-
tion, the most obvious characteristic of tensile fracture at 
800 °C was the pullout and plastic deformation of tungsten 
core, suggesting that the interfacial debonding also occurred 
between SiC and tungsten core as can be seen in Fig. 10f.
There are several reasons behind the significant rein-
forcement at 800 °C which can be discussed as follows. 
The first reason is large plastic deformation occurred in 
the titanium alloy coating. This would result in stress con -
centration on both the matrix alloy side and the fiber side 
connected to the titanium alloy, which would impede the 
crack propagation. The second reason is the propagation 
of the crack from the matrix alloy to the fiber was inhib-
ited due to the microyielding of the titanium alloy. So 
more tensile load was needed for the continuous propaga-
tion of the crack. In other words, the addition of titanium 
alloy coating played a role in delaying crack propaga-
tion. Another reason is no cracks were found to propa-
gate directly into the fibers, because the strength of the 
interfacial bonding decreases as the temperature increases 
[29]. On the other side, all cracks propagated toward the 
fiber causing an interface debonding and contributing to 
the strengthening of the materials. When the crack propa -
gated toward the fiber, the tip of crack was blunt leading 
the crack to be deflected along the interface; this would 
prolong the growth of the crack. The fourth reason is the 
large deformation of both the matrix alloy and titanium 
alloy at 800 °C causes a radial compressive stress on the 
fiber, so much higher tensile stress was required to pull 
out the fiber. This resulted in a greater degree of fiber 
pullout as compared with the fracture surface at room 
temperature. The fifth reason is interfacial debonding 
was also found between the tungsten and SiC as shown 
in Fig.  10f. Obviously, when the crack propagated to 
the tungsten core, it also deflected along the interface 
instead of propagating directly into the tungsten core. So 
cracks in the fiber were not in the same plane. The fiber 
would fracture until the cracks from different propaga-
tion planes meet together. As a result, the fracture surface 
of the fiber was rough at 800 °C as shown in Fig.  8d. 
So the process of the fiber fracture at 800 °C was more 
complicated than that at room temperature, which also 
contributed to the increase in tensile strength. Finally,
