1. Introduction 
The importance of refractory metals like tungsten, molybdenum, • 
tantalum and niobium has increased continuously in recent years. 
I Aside from their classical application in electronics and vacuum-
tube technology they are also of increasing interest in nuclear 
reactor construction, for the propulsion of spacecraft and in 
general machine construction. Due to their high melting points 
and good mechanical properties at high temperatures they can still 
be considered promising materials when other service metals have 
already failed. A certain limitation, however, is their brittle-
ness at room temperature and their lack of oxidation resistance. 
This transition from ductile to brittle behavior Is well known .. 
in steels. In these it occurs in the range of room temperature 
when notched specimens are stressed by impact but shifts to lower 
temperatures under monoaxial states of stress and at normal test 
speeds. However, this "low-temperature brittleness" sometimes 
occurs even far above room temperature in some refractory metals 
such as vanadium, chromium, molybdenum, and particularly in tung-
sten and beryllium. 
An increase in ductility by structural modification produced 
by treatments—and these refer to changes in structure develop-
ment visible under the optical microscope as well as to submicro-
scopic lattice defects—cannot be guaranteed sufficiently for

Sometimes it is possible to prevent brittle fracture at room 
temperature by alloying. Alloying with rhenium developed only in 
recent years led to decisive progress. But since the cost of 
rhenium is very high its use remains limited to special applications. 
2. Test Methods 
The "brittle-ductile transition" is a change in mechanical 
properties taking place at a certain temperature expressed mainly 
^in the toughness data. Below the "transition temperature" the 
material is brittle, and above it plastic deformation is possible. 
The mechanical test methods listed in Table 1 can be used to 
determine the transition temperature. The test methods are listed 
according to increasing stress conditions. The tensile and 
bending tests at low loading speeds give the lowest transition 
temperatures, while impact tests give the highest values.

this position.

It is conceivable that such high strains can occur in the outer-
most sheet fiber at too small a "T" r&tio that the deformability of 
the material is exceeded. In this case, it will never be possible 
to reach the maximum bending angle (for example, 180°) although the 
test is conducted already above the transition temperature. The 
rise of the transition curve suddenly stops and the specimen tears 
in a ductile process. 
Once the transition temperature has been exceeded in annealed 
and recrystallized bending specimens the deformability probably is 
generally so high that the specimen can be bent around the sharpest 
punch. In the work-hardened rolled state with its small strain 
values a sharp punch, i.e. testing with a very small T-value, can 
already cause such a stress in the outermost fiber that a surface 
crack is produced. 
Such effects actually could be seen in a study of molybdenum. 
In the 2-T test work-hardened molybdenum is actually inferior in 
ductility to recrystallized molybdenum: its "transition temperature" 
is higher (Fig. 5b). The 4.5-T test again furnishes the customary 
pictures The ductility of the work-hardened material is better than 
that of the recrystallized one.

transition. The experiences collected led to suggestions for stan-

An improvement of the surface condition by mechanical or electro-

Metallographic investigations of recrystallized specimen^ show

test is conducted in the brittle range. In a fiber structure the 
crack would have to move a greater distance if it were to take place

this case, the energy to be expended seems to be lowest in trans-
crystalline fracture.

no longer be expected from the grain shape alone.

impairs the ductility while a grain grown by critical deformation
