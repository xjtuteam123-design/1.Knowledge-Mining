1. Introduction
There have been a number of review articles over the years that have 
highlighted the properties of refractory metal alloys in general, and 
those of Nb-based ones in particular. The timing of these articles has 
been prompted by technological or political and economic factors, such 
as advances in the production and processing of powder metallurgical 
forms of the alloys [ 1 ] and the more recent, revised interest in advanced 
(Gen IV) nuclear reactor technologies [ 2 ]. The present review is simi -
larly spurred on by two present factors, first is the advent of additive 
manufacturing (AM) technologies and their recent application to re -
fractory metals e.g., [ 3 – 5 ]. Advanced Nb-alloys were previously brought 
to the brink of commercialization but failed to be adopted due to the 
scale of investment that was required in the context of conventional cast 
and wrought metallurgical processing. It is of interest to reconsider their 
possible role in the context of AM. The second factor is the widespread 
interest in refractory alloys as possible means to break the temperature 
barriers above which Ni-based alloys cannot perform, for example in 
high-temperature power generation and aerospace applications [ 6,7 ].
Distinct from the review of space nuclear reactor materials by Leo -
nard [ 2 ], the present review includes more focused consideration of 
alloys high in Hf, such as C-129(Y) and WC-3009, which would be un -
tenable in the context of nuclear applications due to the very high 
neutron absorption cross-section of Hf. That said, many of the alloys 
considered here do not contain Hf or other strong neutron absorbers 
( Table 1 ), so the results are of interest in the context of some Gen IV 
nuclear reactors as well as aerospace applications. A high premium is 
placed on the potential to design lightweight, high-temperature struc -
tures, therefore, space reactor designers, as well as rocket and turbine 
engine designers, will benefit from the present findings. Recent alloy 
development studies (summarized nicely in [ 8 ]) have focused on 
increasing strength, through additions of Re and Al to Nb-Ti-W alloys 
[ 9 ], or the density reducing effects of replacing W and Hf with Mo and 
Ti, respectively [ 10 ]. Although these alloys are promising, their specific

and the analytical methods employed. The main analytical results 
involve the temperature and strain rate dependencies of the steady-state 
flow stress, which are then related to alloy melting points, as melting 
points are often correlated with creep resistance.

2.2. Sellars-Tegart model

power to describe the stress dependence of steady-state (or minimum) 
creep rate ( Eq. (8) ) [ 30 ]. This function asymptotes to an exponential

power function at low stresses (typical of climb- and glide- controlled

Also shown in Fig. 2 , the slope of the Cb-752 data increases with LMP*. 
This behavior can be well modeled by a quadratic dependence of the

described via two (linear) fits of Eq. (7) , as shown in Table 2 . The change 
in slope suggests a possible change in the underlying mechanism con -

relatively little primary creep, as described by Eq. (3) and discussed 
further in Supplement A, a steeper slope (higher b parameter in Eq. (7) ) 
suggests a lower power-law stress exponent. A decrease in the 
power-law stress exponent can correlate with a transition from 
low-temperature to high-temperature power-law creep and ultimately to 
grain boundary sliding or diffusional creep. However, these alloys 
largely exhibit power-law stress exponents indicative of solute drag, and 
the authors can think of no reason why such a transition would occur at a 
significantly lower temperature (or higher strain rate) for alloy Cb-752, 
as compared to the other alloys. Notice the increased scatter in the data 
of most of the alloys at high LMP* levels. It is proposed that additional 
experimentation would reveal alloy Cb-752 to exhibit very similar 
behavior to the others, but the possibility of an increase in slope is 
presented in the interest of conservatism, on behalf of design engineers 
contemplating the use of these alloys at very high temperatures and/or 
low strain rates.

steady-state flow stress and the ST model predictions reveal the veracity

effects of temperature and strain rate ( Fig. 4 ). Parametrically, the 
transition from power-law creep to thermally activated plasticity

plot is controlled by the parameter α in the ST expression and is delin -
eated by the dashed black line (where ασ = 1) in Fig. 4 [ 75 ]. Being 
aware that this transition to higher strain rate dependence exists can be 
insightful for materials selection and for developing proper expectations 
for component performance at a given rate and temperature.

Breakaway stresses associated with the individual solutes within the 
alloys were compared with the stresses at which creep tests were 
performed. For alloys C103, WC-3009, and C-129(Y), the breakaway 
stresses associated with Hf were above the creep flow stresses, which 
supports the conclusions of Wadsworth and Nieh [ 33 ]. For alloys Cb-752 
and FS-85 the breakaway stresses associated with Zr are also higher than 
all the reported creep flow stresses (within the power-law regime) 
( Fig. 5 ), prompting the conclusion that Cb-752 and FS-85 are also Class 1 
(Class A) materials. Altogether, the fact that most of the alloys exhibit 
Class 1 (solute drag) high-temperature behavior means they are rela -
tively insensitive to the details of their microstructure. This suggests that 
additively manufactured versions of these alloys will exhibit similar 
responses to traditionally cast and wrought or powder metallurgically 
processed versions, despite the fact that additively manufactured metals 
and alloys exhibit distinct microstructures.
On the other hand, most of the creep stresses sustained by alloy D-43 
are larger than the breakaway stress required to separate dislocations 
from both the W and Zr solute within it. Hence, solute drag is not ex -
pected to be rate controlling for D-43. This conclusion is corroborated by 
the ST fit of n = 5.5 for D-43, where a value of n ≈ 5 indicates that a 
dislocation climb and glide mechanism controls the creep, rather than 
solute drag. Alloy D-43 has slightly lower Zr content than Cb-752 (1 wt% 
vs. 2.5 wt%), and also has C additions, which getter-Zr atoms from the 
lattice form ZrC precipitates, further reducing the dissolved Zr within 
the matrix. In fact, D-43 is the only intentionally precipitate strength -
ened Nb-alloy investigated in this study, and these precipitates have a 
significant, positive impact upon the creep resistance. However, the 
long-term stability of this carbide has not been studied. Precipitate 
coarsening (increases in particle size at constant volume fraction [ 83 ]), 
including coarsening of grain boundary precipitates [ 76 ], which is 
common in refractory metal alloys, may deleteriously impact the 
long-term creep performance [ 31 ]. It is important to note that although 
D-43 is shown to exhibit a power-law exponent typical of a Class 2 alloy, 
the data employed to develop the D-43 LMP* trend did not include a 
significant amount of primary creep, allowing the modified LMP* 
expression to be used.

4.3. Rationale for a minimum flow stress model
The LMP* and ST model predictions are compared in Fig. 6 . Across 
all alloys except FS-85, LMP* predicts a lower, more conservative 
strength at the highest temperatures probed; whereas the ST strength 
prediction becomes the more conservative option at the lowest tem -
peratures examined. The LMP* prediction becomes nonconservative 
when it is extrapolated to lower temperatures and higher strain rates 
(outside of the range experimentally assessed) because the underlying 
mechanism has changed and is no longer properly described by the 
LMP* model. The mechanism transitions from power-law creep to 
thermally activated plasticity, also known as power-law breakdown, and 
is well described by the ST model ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). Conversely, the failure 
of the ST model to account for transient deformation renders it

nonconservative, especially at temperatures in which the LMP* model 
predicts the minimum flow stress, as the source of transient creep strain 
for Nb-alloys is via tertiary (accelerating) creep associated with strain 
softening. The LMP* model is immune from this type of non -

that it is denser than some competing alloys (Supplemental E). To ac -

employ the minimum flow stress from a combination of both models, 
where ST is used at lower temperatures and higher rates and LMP* is 
used for higher temperatures and lower rates. Caution must still be 
exercised when employing the ST model, as its assumption of 
steady-state deformation always leads to a level of nonconservatism.

5. Conclusions
1. Review of historical data augmented with new data for Hf-containing 
alloys, C103 and WC-3009, emphasizes that Nb-alloys are highly 
strain rate sensitive in the temperature range of interest ( > 1100 
◦
C) 
relative to other materials. The viscoplastic nature of these materials 
must be taken into account, rather than assuming the material re -
mains elastic up to a specific stress level.
2. All the Nb-alloys investigated, except D-43, exhibit Class 1 (Class A) 
creep behavior, indicating creep resistance is controlled by solute 
drag; Hf and Zr are key additions leading to solute drag. Alloy D-43 
has low amounts of Zr, and carbon additions further deplete the 
matrix of Zr due to the formation of zirconium carbides. Importantly, 
these carbides significantly enhance the creep strength of alloy D-43 
up to temperatures where dissolution and coarsening occur.
3. Thermomechanical processing histories of Nb-alloys affect their 
creep strength. Generally, the higher the final annealing tempera -
ture, the more creep resistant the material. In some cases, second 
phase precipitate microstructures (including carbides and oxides) 
are responsible, but in other cases, the reason for these trends is 
unknown. Interestingly, there is no obvious composition dependence 
in the creep behavior of C103 or WC-3009 amongst samples con -
taining 170 – 1500 ppm of oxygen.
4. A unified understanding of both creep and tensile testing modalities 
is provided by the classical Sellars-Tegart (ST) model, which bridges 
low stress, power-law behavior (creep) and higher stress power-law 
breakdown (thermally activated plasticity).
5. One of the characteristics of the Class 1 (Class A) type creep behavior

or rupture.
