abstract
In this study, the compositional dependence of the age hardening response and high temperature tensile
properties of the Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼3, 6, 10, and 12 wt%) alloys are investigated. The amount of
cuboid-shaped phases and β′ precipitates increased signi ﬁcantly with increasing the Gd content. The
Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy exhibited the maximum ultimate tensile strength at room temperature, while
at higher temperatures the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy exhibited the maximum yield strength and ulti-
mate tensile strength. The yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy
increased with the test temperature and showed its maximum at 150 °C, and then decreased as the
temperature increased further. The Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy maintained a high ultimate tensile strength
of more than 300 MPa up to 250 °C. The superior high temperature tensile strength of the tested alloy is
mainly associated with solution strengthening and precipitation hardening of the cuboid-shaped phases
and β′ precipitates in Mg matrix. Especially, β′ precipitates can hinder the dislocation movement at high
temperature.

1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys are lightweight structural materials whose
densities are ∼75% lower than steel and ∼35% lower than Al alloys.
As a result, Mg alloys have great potential in structural applica-
tions in which weight reduction is important, including armor,
portable electronic devices, biomedical devices, automobiles, and
aerospace components [1–7]. The powertrain offers an area of the
vehicle structure where signi ﬁcant improvements in energy utili-
zation are achievable. Modern engines require higher operating
temperatures up to 250 °C and pressures up to 180 bar to meet
demands for a lower fuel consumption and higher Greenhouse Gas
emission standards [8]. The existing commercial Mg alloys for
automotive applications are AZ91D (Mg –9Al–0.7Zn), AM50A (Mg –
5Al–0.4Mn), and AM60B (Mg –6Al–0.4Mn). These alloys have an
excellent combination of mechanical properties, corrosion re-
sistance, and die castability [5]. However, they lose strength above
125 °C and are not suitable in these applications [9]. This has been
attributed to the low thermal stability of their microstructural
constituents, which cannot withstand the imposed high tem-
peratures [10,11 ]. For this reason, to date, many studies have fo-
cused on the attempt to improve the high temperature mechanical
properties of the magnesium alloys. In fact, alloying is one of the
most important approaches to improve heat resistance of mag-
nesium alloys [12]. It was reported that addition of RE (rare-earth)
element to magnesium alloys can improve both room and high
temperatures mechanical properties [13,14]. The solubility of the
RE elements exponentially decreases with decreasing the tem-
perature [13–15]. This makes Mg –RE alloy an ideal system for age
hardening. Mg –Gd, Mg –Gd–Nd and Mg –Gd–Y alloys exhibits re-
markable ageing hardening by precipitation of
β″ phase with DO 19
crystal structure and β′ phase with bco crystal structure even at
aging temperature higher than 200 °C [16,17]. HE et al. reported a
four-stage precipitation process in Mg –10Gd–3Y–0.4Zr alloy aged
at 250 °C, i.e.: α-Mg supersaturated solid solution (S.S.S.S.) -me-
tastable β″ (D019)-metastable β′ (cbco)-metastable β1 (fcc)-
stable β (fcc). It was reported that the metastable β′ phase, which
has the shape of a convex lens, is responsible for the peak hard-
ness and most strengthening phase in Mg –Gd–Y alloys [18,19].I na
previous study, the effect of Gd content on room temperature

the results have shown that the age hardening response of the

2. Experimental procedures
The alloy ingots with a nominal compositions of the Mg –xGd–
3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼3, 6, 10 and 12 wt%) are made by melting Mg –90%
Gd, Mg –25% Y, Mg –30% Zr master alloys, and pure Mg in an
electric resistance furnace under a mixed atmosphere of CO
2 and
SF6 with the ratio of 100:1. When the temperature reached 750 °C,
molten metal were poured into a steel mold pre-heated to 200 °C.
Samples cut from the ingot are solution-treated at 500 °C for 6 h
and quenched into cold water. Following by aging treatment, the
Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr and Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr are isothermally
aged at 225 °C for 16 h, Mg –3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr and Mg –6Gd–3Y–0.5Zr
are aged at the same temperature for 80 h [19,20].
The samples for optical microscope (OM) and scanning electron
microscope (SEM) analyses are prepared by the standard
technique of grinding with SiC abrasive paper and polishing with
an MgO suspension solution, followed by etching in a 4 vol% nital
solution. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations
are carried out using a JEOL-2100 at an accelerating voltage of
200 kV. Thin foils for the TEM examinations are prepared by twin
jet electro polishing in a solution of 4% HClO
4 and 96% methanol
cooled down to /C0 40 °C. Further thinning is carried out using low-
energy ion beam milling.
Tensile tests of above mentioned alloys are performed at a
crosshead speed of 1 mm/min, according to the relevant standard
of using cylindrical specimen ( Fig. 1 ) with marked dimension of
33 mm gauge length and 5 mm diameter. All tensile tests are
carried out on a Zwick/Roell Z020 tensile testing machine

3. Results
3.1. Microstructural observation
Fig. 2 shows the microstructures of the investigated alloys in
the peak-aged condition. During solid solution treatment, cuboid-
shaped phases appears as dark spots in the microstructure, as
depicted in Fig. 2 . Based on the results in the Ref. [20], small cu-
boid-shaped phase is a compound with high contents of the RE
elements and its size is about 2 –5 μm. It is face-centered cubic
structure and the lattice constant is 5.6 Å. These cuboid-shaped
phases are unevenly distributed in Mg matrix and boundary areas
and persisted during the subsequent aging in all of the studied
alloys, as it can be seen in Fig. 2. For the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy,
which contains the maximum amount of cuboid-shaped phases, a
few of these cuboid-shaped phases are observed at the grain
boundaries (Fig. 2d). It is noticeable that the volume fraction of the
cuboid-shaped phases increases with RE addition. It is also obvious
that Gd addition has effectively re ﬁned the grain size of the base
alloy. With increasing the Gd content from 3 to 12 wt%, the grains
are re ﬁned and the average grain size determined by linear in-
tercept method is decreased from 85 to 64 mm. Fig. 3 shows the
XRD pattern of the T6 treated Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼3, 6, 10 and
12 wt%) alloys. According to the XRD patterns of the alloys, α-Mg
solid solution, Mg 5(Gd,Y) and Mg 24Y intermetallic compounds are
identiﬁed.

3.2. Tensile properties
The temperature dependence of the ultimate tensile strength
(UTS), yield strength (YS) and elongation of the peak-aged samples
of all studied alloys are shown in Fig. 6 . Fig. 6 a shows ultimate
tensile strength of the Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼3, 6, 10 and 12 wt%)
alloys at different temperatures. The Gd additions (up to 10 wt%)
improved UTS values of the alloys at all test temperatures. However,
further increasing the Gd content to 12 wt% deteriorates the
strength of the alloy at room temperature. But, at higher tempera-
tures, the Mg–12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy showed the highest UTS. At the
room temperature, the UTS of the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy is
slightly lower than that of the Mg –10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy. The tensile
strength of the Mg –3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr, Mg –6Gd–3Y–0.5Zr and Mg –
10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloys decreased monotonically with increasing the
temperature, whereas, for the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr, the UTS in-
creased with the temperature and reached its maximum strength at
150 °C, namely 375 MPa. The UTS of the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy
at 200 °C is slightly higher than that at room temperature. The alloy
maintains high strength of more than 300 MPa up to 250 °C. A si-
milar trend has been observed in the Mg –10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr sand-cast
alloy [25],M g –20Gd and Mg –20Tb [26],M g –10Gd–5Y–0.4Zr [13],
and Mg–12Gd–2Y–0.5Sm–0.5 Sb–0.5Zr [27] alloys.
Fig. 6 b shows the variation of yield strength with test tem-
peratures as a function of Gd content. The yield strength of the
alloys increased with increasing Gd content, the Mg –12Gd–3Y–
0.5Zr alloy shows the highest yield strength at all test tempera-
tures. For Mg –3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr and Mg –6Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloys, the
yield strength decreased with increasing the temperature. How-
ever, for Mg –10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr, the yield strength slightly decreased
with increasing the test temperature to 100 °C, and then increased
with increasing the temperature to 150 °C, further increasing the
test temperature again decreased the yield strength. But for Mg –
12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy, the yield strength improved continuously
with increasing test temperature till 150 °C, and then decreased
with further increasing the test temperature. The effect of Gd on
the elongation of the Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy at different tem-
peratures shown in Fig. 6 c. Elongation decreased with increasing
the Gd content and increased continuously with increasing the
test temperatures. It should be the activation of non-basal slip
systems at elevated temperature that facilitated the grain de-
formability. The Mg –3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy showed the highest
elongation at all test temperatures. The elongation of the Mg –
12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy tested at room temperature was 2% while the
elongation of the Mg –3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr was 14.6%. The elongation of
Mg–3Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy tested at 300 °C was 2.8 times higher
than that of the Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy at the same
temperature.

4. Discussion
In this study, the in ﬂuence of Gd addition on the high tem-
perature mechanical properties of the Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼ 3, 6,
10 and 12 wt%) alloys is investigated. It is well known that binary
Mg–Gd alloy shows little precipitation-hardening response during
isothermal or isochronal aging when the Gd content is less than
10 wt% [16]. The increasing of the volume fraction of the β′ pre-
cipitate with increasing the Gd content from 6% to 12% is in good
agreement with previous reports. It was reported by NIE that the
plate shaped precipitates formed on the prismatic plane of the
magnesium matrix are the most effective for dispersion
strengthening [28]. Improving the YS with increasing the Gd
content is attributed to increasing the volume fraction of the
precipitates with increasing the Gd content, as it can be seen from
Fig. 4 . Whereas, the formation of the cuboid-shaped phases along
the grain boundaries in the alloy containing 12% Gd led to stress
concentration along the grain boundary and act as a crack initia-
tion sites and deteriorate the UTS of the alloy at room
temperature.
The Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy shows an anomalous tempera-
ture tensile behavior, i.e. the strength of the alloy increased with
increasing the test temperature from room temperature to 150 °C
and then decreased with further increasing the test temperature,
as it can be seen from Fig. 9 . While, for the conventional alloys,
strength of the alloys decreases with increasing the test tem-
perature. The alloy shows the highest strength at the high tem-
peratures among all the tested alloys. The superior high tem-
perature strength of the alloy maybe attributed to the following
aspects.
With increasing the test temperature the solubility of the RE
element will increase exponentially. The atomic radii of Gd, and
Mg are 0.180, and 0.160 nm, respectively. NIE et al. reported that
substituting an Mg atom with a Gd atom leads to a large negative
misﬁt( /C0 0.125) and compression strain [29]. This stress will
roughen the slip plane and resist the dislocation movement and
strengthen the material.
The ﬁne precipitates which formed during casting, or by heat
treatment of the alloy is another reason for the outstanding high
temperature tensile properties of Mg –12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy. The
stable cuboid-shaped phase and metastable β′ phase are also
considered as one of the strengthening precipitate, for Mg –12Gd–
2Y–0.5Sm–0.5 Sb–0.5Zr [27] and Mg–10Gd–5Y–0.4Zr [13] alloys. It
was reported that cuboid-shaped phase and β′ phase have high
melting point and thermal stability [13,30]. The precipitates ef-
fectively improve the stability of the alloy at high temperatures.

5. Conclusions
The microstructure and high temperature mechanical proper-
ties of the T6 treated Mg –xGd–3Y–0.5Zr ( x¼ 3, 6, 10 and 12 wt%)
alloys containing different Gd content are investigated. The results
obtained are summarized as following:
(1) The Mg –Gd–Y–Zr alloys indicate remarkable age hardening by
precipitation of the cuboid-shaped phase (fcc) and β′ (bco)
metastable phase.
(2) High temperature tensile strength of the T6 treated alloys
improved with increasing the Gd content up to 12 wt%. For the
Mg–12Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy, with temperature increase from
room temperature to 150 °C, the ultimate tensile strength and
yield strength increased and reached its maximum at 150 °C.
However, with further increasing the temperature, the ulti-
mate tensile strength and yield strength decreased. Com-
paratively, the elongation increased monotonously with the
increasing of tensile temperature.
(3) The improvement of the high temperature mechanical prop-
erties of the tested alloys with Gd addition was attributed to
the solution strengthening of RE element and precipitation of
cuboid-shaped phases and β′ precipitates.
(4) The fracture mode for all of the tested alloys changed from
transgranular fracture at room temperature to intergranular
fracture at high temperature.
