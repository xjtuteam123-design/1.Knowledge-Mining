To examine the inﬂuence of grain boundary engineering (GBE) on the high-temperature mechanical prop- 
erties of face-centered cubic (FCC) metals, the tensile tests were carried out at 723 K on the non-GBE and 
GBE Cu-16at.%Al alloy samples. The GBE treatment increases the deformation uniformity and cracking re- 
sistance, thus effectively improves the high-temperature ductility. Moreover, the dynamic recrystallization 
(DRX) of the GBE sample is signiﬁcantly suppressed due to the reduction of the Gibbs free energy, imped- 
ing the high-temperature softening. Therefore, the high-temperature strength and ductility of FCC metals 
might be synchronously improved by a GBE treatment under the premise that DRX occurs.

It has witnessed a dramatic increase in the number of energy 
facilities due to the increasing demand for energy during the past 
few decades. Accordingly, the safety of some high-risk energy facil- 
ities, such as nuclear power plants and oil reﬁneries, has attracted 
extensive attentions. Thus, a variety of new structural metallic 
materials, e.g., austenitic stainless steels, heat-resistant steels and 
nickel-based alloys, were successfully developed and applied to en- 
hance the safety of these facilities [1–6] . It is worth noting that 
the strength and ductility of these polycrystalline materials were 
frequently harmed by high-temperature softening and intergranu- 
lar cracking (IGC) [7] . The decreases in strength and ductility are 
mainly caused by the following factors: (1) the dislocation den- 
sity is cut down under the inﬂuences of dynamic recovery (DRC) 
and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) [8–10] , and (2) the strength 
of grain boundaries (GBs) would be lowered at high-temperature 
or corrosive environment [ 5 , 11 ]. Certainly, the declines of strength 
and ductility would be a menace for the practical applications of 
these materials. Therefore, solving the problem is of great signiﬁ- 
cance to improve the service life of these materials [ 5 , 12 ]. 
In the previous studies, the differences in the property between 
coincidence site lattice (CSL) special boundaries (SBs, /Sigma1CSL ≤ 29 
[13] ) and random high-angle grain boundaries (RHAGBs) have been

deﬁnitely veriﬁed [14–21] . Speciﬁcally, the SBs were found to ex- 
hibit superior cracking resistance to RHAGBs [ 4 , 19 , 21–26 ]. More- 
over, it has been shown that the SBs of high fraction obtained by 
grain boundary character distribution (GBCD) optimization (gener- 
ally called ‘grain boundary engineering’ (GBE)) can effectively im- 
prove the IGC resistance at high temperature and high pressure en- 
vironment [24] , and optimize the room-temperature ductility to a 
certain extent [ 27 , 28 ]. Furthermore, some studies [29–31] have also 
indicated that the DRX tends to initiate at GBs depending closely 
upon the GB types. Actually, the softening phenomenon caused by 
DRX may be weakened by a GBE treatment, because the increased 
SBs with lower interface energy and more stable crystal structures 
would probably suppress the DRX [ 19 , 32 , 33 ]. Thus, the GBCD opti- 
mization, i.e., GBE treatment, might be a feasible strategy for a syn- 
chronous improvement of strength and ductility in polycrystalline 
materials with low stacking fault energy (SFE) at high tempera- 
tures. 
However, it is well-known that the DRX is strongly inﬂuenced 
by the strain rate and deformation temperature [34] . To ensure the 
stability of the initial microstructures of non-GBE and GBE sam- 
ples but the appearance of DRX during mechanical tests, in the 
present work, we investigated the effects of GBE on the mechani- 
cal property of the face-centered cubic (FCC) Cu-16at.%Al alloy with 
extremely low SFE (6 mJ/m 2 ) at 723 K [28] with different strain 
rates.

The non-GBE and GBE samples of Cu-16at.%Al alloy were ob- 
tained by the thermal-mechanical processes (TMP) reported in our 
previous work [28] . Speciﬁcally, the ingot of Cu-16at.%Al alloy was 
obtained by vacuum induction melting, and then hot rolled at 
973 K into a sheet with a thickness of 11 mm. The sheet was fur- 
ther cold rolled with 50% reduction and annealed at 923 K for 1 h 
followed by water quenching (WQ) to obtain the non-GBE sam- 
ples with moderate grain size and homogeneous microstructure. 
Some non-GBE samples were cold rolled with 7% reduction and 
annealed at 723 K for 72 h followed by WQ to obtain GBE sam- 
ples. The dog-bone shape specimens with a gage dimension of 
30 × 5.5 × 2 mm 3 for high-temperature tensile tests of non-GBE 
and GBE sheets were prepared by wire electrical discharge machin- 
ing (WEDM), wherein the long axis was parallel to the rolling di- 
rection (RD). Before tensile tests, the samples were mechanically 
ground and electro-polished to acquire a smooth and strain-free 
surface. 
The high-temperature tensile tests at 723 K on the non-GBE 
and GBE samples were carried out at the strain rates of 10 −2 s −1 
and 10 −4 s −1 using an AG-X Plus 100 kN microcomputer-controlled 
electron universal testing machine with a heating furnace. The 
samples were kept at 723 K for 10 min in the furnace to reduce 
the uneven distribution of temperature before loading. The defor- 
mation microstructures of the non-GBE and GBE samples tensioned 
to fracture were characterized by electron back scatter diffraction 
(EBSD) in the orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) system at- 
tached to a JEOL JSM 7001F ﬁeld emission scanning electron mi- 
croscope (FESEM), and by an FEI Tecnai G 2 20 transmission elec- 
tron microscope (TEM) operated at 200 kV, respectively. The sam- 
ples for EBSD and TEM observations were WEDMed from the gage 
parts of fractured samples. The experimental data of EBSD were 
analyzed by the postprocessing software HKL Channel 5. 
Fig. 1 shows the reconstruction maps of initial microstruc- 
tures, GBCDs, and corresponding RHAGB networks for the non-GBE 
and GBE Cu-16at.%Al alloy samples. The microstructures of these 
two samples consist of essentially equiaxed and randomly orien- 
tated grains, and the average grain size (twins included) is roughly 
equivalent, i.e., 21.3 μm for non-GBE and 25.7 μm for GBE sam- 
ples, as shown in Fig. 1 (a,d). Therefore, the inﬂuence of grain size 
on the mechanical properties can be ruled out in the present work. 
In addition, some annealing twins (ATs) were observed in both 
non-GBE and GBE samples due to a low SFE of this alloy. It should 
be noted that the GBE-quantifying parameters [ 24 , 35 , 36 ] of GBE 
sample are obviously higher than those of non-GBE sample. Specif- 
ically, the fraction of SBs ( f SBs , mainly AT boundaries) increases 
from 53.0% to 86.2%, the ratio v of twin related domain (TRD) size 
to grain size increases from 2.4 to 3.8, and the parameter J2/(1- 
J3) (J2 and J3 junctions denote the fraction of triple-junctions (TJs) 
with two and three CSL boundaries, respectively [35] ) for quantiz- 
ing the connectivity of RHAGBs increases from 0.128 to 0.502 by a 
GBE treatment. Evidently, the connectivity of RHAGBs is effectively 
weakened in the GBE sample, as shown in Fig. 1 (c,f), since more 
SBs tailor the RHAGB networks, as representatively marked by ar- 
rows in Fig. 1 (b,c,e,f). 
Fig. 2 shows the comparisons of engineering stress-strain curves 
for the non-GBE and GBE samples tensioned at 723 K with dif- 
ferent strain rates. It is obvious that the ductility of GBE samples 
is signiﬁcantly improved compared with that of non-GBE samples 
at high temperature. The total elongation is increased by approx- 
imately 17% and 9% at the higher strain rate of 10 −2 s −1 and the 
lower strain rate of 10 −4 s −1 , respectively. The yield and tensile 
strengths of GBE sample are roughly equal to those of non-GBE 
sample at the high strain rate ( Fig. 2 (a)), while they become sig- 
niﬁcantly increased at the lower strain rate ( Fig. 2 (b)). Therefore, 
the high-temperature tensile strength and ductility of Cu-16at.%Al 
alloy at lower strain rate could be concurrently improved by an 
appropriate GBE treatment. Such an inﬂuence of GBE on the high- 
temperature tensile properties of Cu-16at.%Al alloy will be dis- 
cussed in detail based on microstructure observations and activa- 
tion energy analyses. 
The GBCD optimization mainly gives rise to the increase of f SBs 
in GBE sample ( Fig. 1 ). Shibata et al. [16] reported that the interfa- 
cial energies of AT boundaries and some other SBs are signiﬁcantly 
lower than those of RHAGBs, and most of them have a structure 
with less distortion. The characters of SBs lead generally to the fact 
that some moving dislocations, especially for wavy-slipping dislo- 
cations, can transfer these SBs [ 18 , 37 , 38 ], which would weaken the 
strain concentration at GBs to some extent. Therefore, the GBCD 
optimization could signiﬁcantly improve the deformation unifor- 
mity of polycrystalline materials [ 28 , 39 ] and thus promote their 
ductility [28] . 
To gain an insight into the effects of GBE on the high- 
temperature mechanical behavior, the deformation microstructures 
of Cu-16at.%Al alloy were investigated by EBSD ( Fig. 3 ) and TEM

samples, as shown in Fig. 3 (a ′ –d ′ ). As conﬁrmed by the previous 
studies, the distribution of KAM values highly coincides with the 
distribution of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) [40–
42] , which can effectively represent the deformation uniformity 
of polycrystalline materials [43] . Therefore, it is convincingly indi- 
cated that the deformation uniformity becomes signiﬁcantly opti- 
mized in GBE samples. In short, the high-temperature ductility of 
GBE samples is obviously improved ( Fig. 2 ), owing to the hindering 
of crack propagation by SBs and the improvement of deformation 
uniformity. 
Besides, dislocation cells with a lower dislocation density were 
observed in the deformation microstructures of both non-GBE and 
GBE samples at high and low strain rates ( Fig. 4 (a,d,g,j)), which is a 
typical characteristic of DRC [10] . The occurrence of DRC in the Cu- 
16at.%Al alloy must be attributed to the wavy slip of dislocations 
caused by the increase of SFE with increasing temperature [44–46] . 
In addition, deformation twins (DTs) were observed locally in the 
samples at the high strain rate ( Fig. 4 (b,h)). More importantly, ob- 
vious DRX occurs in both non-GBE and GBE samples at the low 
strain rate ( Figs. 3 (b,d) and 4(e,k)) but rarely at the high strain 
rate ( Fig. 3 (a,c)). TEM observation indicates that the DRX mainly 
occurs at RHAGBs ( Fig. 3 (b,d)), and the recrystallized grains local- 
ized at RHAGBs are larger in size than those localized at AT bound- 
aries, as shown in Fig. 4 (e,k). To show more intuitively the inﬂu- 
ence of GBE on the high-temperature property of Cu-16at.%Al alloy, 
the deformation microstructures of the non-GBE and GBE samples 
tensioned at different strain rates were schematically summarized 
in Fig. 4 (c,f,i,l), In short, the GBE treatment could effectively hin- 
der the DRX by improving the fraction of SBs. Galindo-Nava and 
Rivera-Díaz-del-Castillo [47] reported that DRX is a signiﬁcant fac- 
tor causing high-temperature softening in polycrystalline metals. 
Obviously, such an inﬂuence of DRX only occurs at a lower strain 
rate that is beneﬁcial to DRX ( Fig. 3 (a–d)). Therefore, the high- 
temperature tensile strength of the GBE sample is only improved 
at the low strain rate ( Fig. 2 (b)). 
The increase of f SBs may reduce the Gibbs free energy to some 
extent for GBE samples due to the lower surface energy of SBs. 
Therefore, a higher energy is needed for the high-temperature 
deformation of the GBE sample, which might induce a better 
strength-ductility match in the GBE sample at elevated tempera- 
tures.

In summary, the Gibbs free energy of Cu-16at%Al alloy is, to 
some extent, decreased by improving the fraction of SBs through 
a GBE treatment, which improves the high-temperature deforma- 
tion stability of the GBE sample, and thus effectively suppresses 
the DRX during high-temperature deformation. Moreover, the GBE 
treatment enhances the deformation uniformity and cracking re- 
sistance, and effectively improves the high-temperature ductil- 
ity. Therefore, on the premise of DRX, the GBE treatment might 
synchronously improve the high-temperature tensile strength and 
ductility of FCC metals with low SFE.
