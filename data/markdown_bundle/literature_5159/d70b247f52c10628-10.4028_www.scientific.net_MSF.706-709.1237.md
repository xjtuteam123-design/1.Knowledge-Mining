Effect of Long-Period Stacking Order Phase and α-Mg Phase on Strength 
and Ductility of Mg-Zn-Y Alloy 
Masafumi Noda 1,a and Yoshihito Kawamura 2,b 
1Dept. of Mechanical Science and Engineering, Chiba Institute of Technology, 2-17-1 Tsudanuma 
Narashino Chiba 275-0016, Japan 
2Dept. of Material Science, Kumamoto University, 2-39-1 Kurokami Kumamoto 860-8555, Japan 
amasafumi.noda@it-chiba.ac.jp, brivervill@gpo.kumamoto-u.ac.jp 
Keywords: magnesium alloy, strength, ductility, microstructu re, thermal stability, long-period 
stacking order phase 
Abstract. Mg alloys are lightweight structural alloys that n ormally have a good castability and 
machinability as well as an excellent specific stre ngth and rigidity. However, the mechanical 
properties of Mg alloys are inferior to those of Al alloys, and their range of industrial applications is 
limited. Recently, Mg–Zn–Y alloy has been found to show a high tensile yield strength with a good 
elongation. The alloy has a long-period stacking or der (LPSO) phase as the secondary phase in an 
α-Mg phase. In general, the tensile yield strengths of LPSO-type Mg alloy are known to be markedly 
enhanced by the formation of kink bands in the LPSO phase and by microstructural refinement of the 
α-Mg phase during plastic deformation. The separate roles of the LPSO phase and the α-Mg phase in 
relation to the mechanical properties of high-stren gth LPSO-type Mg alloy were investigated at 
ambient and high temperatures. For high strengths at ambient and high temperatures, it was important 
that the α-Mg phase consisted of a fine-grain region and a nonrecrystallized region, and that the LPSO 
phase remained as a block-type phase. On the other hands, it was necessary to change the LPSO phase 
from a block-type phase into a plate-type phase by heat treatment before tensile testing to improve the 
ductility of the alloy while maintaining its tensile yield strength. Microstructural control of the LPSO 
phase and the α-Mg phase is necessary to obtained M g–Zn–Y alloy with superior mechanical 
properties at ambient-to-high temperatures. 
Introduction 
Lightweight Mg alloys with excellent shock-absorpti on properties are being actively adopted for 
electronic-information devices and automotive parts [1,2]. For use in such structural applications, Mg 
alloys need to have adequate ductility, thermal sta bility, and strength. However, Mg alloys often 
exhibit a low ductility and a low tensile yield strength at ambient and high temperatures owing to a 
lack of slip systems in the hexagonal close-packed structure [3]. Among the well-known and effective 
ways of improving the ductility and tensile yield strength of Mg alloys are grain refinement [4] and 
control of texture [5], because these promote prismatic slips and facilitate the creation of large plastic 
deformations [6]. 
Recently, Mg–Zn–Y alloy has been found to have mechanical properties that are superior to those 
of other Mg alloys [7]. This alloy has a long-period stacking order (LPSO) phase as a secondary phase 
in the main α-Mg phase [7,8]. In general, Mg alloys with LPSO ph ases are known to have greatly 
enhanced mechanical properties, whereas their ducti lity can be maintained only by extrusion or 
plastic deformation of the cast metal. It has been suggested that during plastic deformation a kink 
band is formed in the LPSO phase and a microstructu re refinement of the α-Mg phase occurs. The 
strength and microstructure of extruded Mg–Zn–Y alloy has been reported [8]. On the other hand, the 
effects of the α-Mg phase and the LPSO phase on the thermal stability of the alloy are unclear. In this 
study, the effects of the shape and size of α-Mg phase and the LPSO phase on the mechanical 
properties and microstructure of extruded Mg–Zn–Y a lloy with an LPSO phase produced by 
extrusion at 623 K were investigated at ambient and high temperatures.

Experimental Procedure 
The alloy used in the study had a composition of Mg
96 Zn 2Y2 (atomic %). Ingots were prepared by 
high-frequency induction melting in an Ar atmosphere. A cast sample of alloy with a diameter of 29 
mm and a length of 60 mm was prepared. The Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 ingot was extruded at 623 K at an extrusion 
ratio of 10 and an extrusion speed of 2.5 mm ·s–1. Tensile specimens with gauge sections 2.5 mm in 
diameter and 12.8 mm in length parallel to the dire ction of extrusion were machined from the 
extruded and annealed materials. A tensile test was carried out at an initial strain rate of 5 × 10 –4 s–1. 
Annealing was carried out at 473 to 773 K in an electric furnace for 3.6 ks, and the specimens were 
subsequently cooled in water. The microstructures of the as-extruded and annealed specimens were 
observed by optical microscopy (OM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM), and electron back-scattered diffraction (EBSD). EBSD was carried out in a 250 × 
250 µm region with a measurement step of 0.2 µm. A cross-section of the microstructure was 
observed from the direction of the extrusion axis. 
Figure 1 shows the SEM microstructures of as-cast a nd as-extruded Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy. The alloy 
contains an LPSO phase, an α-Mg phase, and a Mg 3Zn 3Y2 phase. The bright areas in both figures are 
considered to be the LPSO phase. The distribution of the LPSO phase in the extruded alloy appears to 
be finer than that in the as-cast alloy.

Results and Discussions 
Mechanical Properties of Extruded and Annealed Mg 
96 Zn 2Y2 Alloy The results of tensile tests 
performed at ambient and elevated temperatures on e xtruded Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 specimens annealed at 
various temperatures are shown in Fig. 2. The yield stress and tensile strength of the as-extruded 
specimen were 391 and 432 MPa, respectively, and th e elongation was 5.4% at room temperature. 
When annealed at 623 K for 3.6 ks (above which temperature, the strength decreased gradually), the 
yield stress was 380 MPa and the tensile strength was 410 MPa. Even when the annealing time was 
extended to 100 h, the tendency to show a gradual decrease in properties was the same. In other words, 
Fig. 2 shows that the α-Mg phase undergoes a static recrystallization at 623 K. On the other hand, it is 
known that the LPSO phase causes locally recovery a nd/or static recrystallization at an annealing 
temperature of 673 K [9]. At an annealing temperature at 673 K, the strength deteriorated as a result of 
recovery of kink bands in the LPSO phase and grain growth of the α-Mg phase, but the elongation 
improved. It has been reported that annealing of conventional Mg alloys at 523 K for 3.6 ks disrupts 
the crystalline orientation and causes a reduction in strength [10]. Even if its strength did deteriorate 
on annealing, the LPSO-type Mg alloy retained a hig h strength and ductility in comparison with 
conventional Mg alloy. The ductility of the alloy could be improved while maintaining a high strength, 
despite the fact that it is a Mg alloy that has a low ductility at room temperature.

Figure 3 shows the results of the high-temperature tensile test. The test was performed at 423 to 523 K 
at an initial strain rate of 5 × 10 
–4 s–1. The yield stress and tensile strength of the as-extruded specimen 
were 281 and 307 MPa, respectively, and the elongat ion was 24% at 523 K. The yield stress and 
elongation of extruded Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy depended on the testing temperature, and the yield stress 
decreased with increasing testing temperature. However, the dependence on the testing temperature 
was small in comparison with that of conventional M g alloys [11–12]. In addition, the yield stress 
depended on the annealing temperature, but materials annealed at 573 K showed the same ratio as the 
yield stress of the as-extruded materials. The yiel d stresses of materials annealed at a higher 
temperature of 673 K, showed a relative decrease regardless of the testing temperature. In this case, 
static recrystallization of the α-Mg phase and locally recovery and/or recrystallization of the LPSO 
phase of Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy occur at 623 K and 673 K, respectively. It is therefore important to maintain 
the kink band formed in the LPSO phase by extrusion to retain a high strength at high temperatures.

Microstructure of Extruded and Annealed Samples of Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 Alloy Figure 4 shows 
optical micrographs of samples annealed at various temperatures. The average grain size of the α-Mg 
phase is shown in the figure. The region of recryst allization formed about 70% of the extruded 
Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 samples, and the remainder consisted of nonrecryst allized material. We suggest that 
discontinuous dynamic recrystallization occurs during extrusion. The grain size of the α-Mg phase 
grew on increasing the annealing temperature, but t he grain size and the proportion of the 
recrystallization region did not change markedly at temperatures of up to 573 K. When the annealing 
temperature was increased to 673 K, the mean grain size of the α-Mg phase was 4.8 µm and the kink 
band was shown to recover partially, as could be seen in the TEM studies on the microstructure (Fig. 
5). Figs. 1–5 also show that significant changes oc cur in the mechanical properties and the 
microstructure of Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy during annealing at temperatures above 623 K. Here, the LPSO 
phase-recovery temperature was higher by about 50 K than the α-Mg phase-recovery temperature. It is 
important to note that the formation of a kink band in the LPSO phase and the microstructure 
refinement of the α-Mg phase during plastic deformation are responsible for the improvements in the 
mechanical properties of the alloy. In addition, we concluded that this alloy is superior to strength and 
ductility because the LPSO phase constrains the grain growth of the α-Mg phase on annealing. These 
results agree with many reports on LPSO-type Mg alloys [7, 8, 13–14].

Thermal Stability of Extruded Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 Alloy Figure 6 (a) shows the yield strength (Y.S.) and 
elongation (El.) for a sample of extruded Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy for annealing times of up to 1000 h at 573 
K when tested at room temperature and 523 K. Fig. 6(b) shows the TEM micrograph of a sample at 
the end of the annealing period. The yield stress a nd tensile strength gradually deteriorated with 
increasing annealing time, whereas the elongation was markedly improved. The TEM micrographs do 
not show any change, and the kink bands present in LPSO phase before annealing remained there 
afterwards. The α-Mg phase did not show extreme grain growth and the crystal orientation did not 
show any major change. The LPSO phase of the anneal ed materials was bent, and the extruded 
Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy showed superior thermal stability when annealed at 573 K. Annealing results in the 
formation of a kink band in the LPSO phase, and it is necessary for the LPSO phase to constrain grain 
growth in the α-Mg phase to develop thermal stability in the LPSO-type Mg alloy. By the way, it is 
known that the LPSO phase is a harder material than the α-Mg phase. When an LPSO phase is present, 
the Mg alloy has a high strength and the grain grow th of the α-Mg phase is constrained, but the 
microstructure remains unchanged. In the case of Mg alloys, it is important that they retain a high 
strength and a high ductility at high temperatures if they are to be used as industrial materials, and the 
LPSO-type Mg alloy is a material that can solve many such problems.

Summary 
The mechanical properties and microstructures of extruded samples of Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy annealed 
at various temperatures were investigated. Annealing the Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 extruded alloy at 623 K caused 
reductions in yield stress and a gradual decrease i n the tensile strength; however, the ductility 
improved from 5.4 to 9.6%. The α-Mg phase and LPSO phase showed static recrystalliz ation and 
recovery at 623 K and 673 K, respectively; therefore the Mg 96 Zn 2Y2 alloy showed a superior thermal 
stability when it was annealed at 573 K. An effective way of improving the ductility of the alloy while 
maintaining a high strength is to recover the kink band into the LPSO phase, thereby controlling the 
grain size of α-Mg phase.
