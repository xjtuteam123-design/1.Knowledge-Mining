High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) with 
body-centered cubic (BCC) single-phase structure exhibit superior me -
chanical strength as a result of solid solution strengthening, but they also 
suffer from low ductility [ 1 , 2 ]. A key design strategy to enhance elon -
gation aims at a dual-phase heterogeneous structure containing both 
BCC and face-centered cubic (FCC) phases upon thermal reversion 
treatment [ 3 , 4 ]. Accordingly, the applied strain is partitioned between 
the two phases, and the FCC regions accommodate the majority of the 
applied strain through the dislocation activities [5] and transformation 
and/or twining-induced plasticity (TRIP/TWIP) [6] . Unfortunately, this 
increase in material elongation often occurs at the expense of mechan -
ical strength [7] . However, a good combination of strength and ductility 
can be achieved upon aging treatment through the configuration of or -
dered precipitates as dislocation motion obstacles within a disordered 
dual-phase matrix [ 8 , 9 ]. In this regard, maraging steels containing Ni, 
Co, Mo, Ti, Al, Nb, and Si constitute a popular category of 
ultra-high-strength materials with a BCC matrix decorated by interme -
tallic precipitates [ 10 , 11 ]. Depending on the precipitate ’ s characteris -
tics and distribution, the mechanical strength can be increased by 
precipitation strengthening and/or antiphase-boundary strengthening 
[12] . However, the formation of some brittle precipitates, such as σ and 
Laves phases, may deteriorate the mechanical properties and lead to 
catastrophic failure [13] . Therefore, compositional and microstructural 
design must be considered as the first crucial step for load-bearing ap -
plications with a good combination of strength and ductility. 
Design theories based on a heterogeneous microstructure composed 
of regions of differing characteristics, including multi-phase design [14] 
and heterogeneous grain structure [15] , were originally developed to 
strengthen HEAs/MEAs through hetero-deformation-induced (HDI) 
strengthening. These theories have been successfully applied in the

To gain further insight into the metastability-engineering strategy, 
additional emphasis was placed on the martensite/austenite interface of 
the reverted specimen ( Fig. 1 f). As shown in reconstructed APT maps 
( Figs. 1 g and 2 a-c), the chemical composition of the reverted austenite 
grains was apparently not identical to that of the parent martensite. The 
one-dimensional concentration profiles across the martensite/austenite 
interface in Fig. 2 d and Supplementary Fig. 5 show enhanced con -
centrations of FCC stabilizer elements (Ni and Mn) in the austenite grain 
at the expense of Fe and Co ( Fig. 2 d and Supplementary Fig. 5 ). As 
shown in Fig. 2 d, the chemical composition gradually varied not only 
across martensite/austenite interfaces but also within individual 
martensite and austenite grains. Ni, Mn, and Co all had equal values of 
10 at% in the initial state, but Mn, as the strongest austenite stabilizer, 
varied in concentration from ~13 at% in austenite grains to ~5 at% in 
martensite grains. In austenite grains, Ni reached its highest value of 
11.5 at% in the vicinity of the interface, where even Mn was relatively 
depleted compared to other areas within austenite grains. This 
elemental partitioning results from the non-equilibrium condition of the 
reversion treatment followed by diffusion-limited solidification during 
water quenching and the associated suppression of chemical ordering 
and atomic diffusion. Similar marginal chemical segregations between 
dual phases have been reported in compositional analyses of an 
AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 
HEA [20] . Compositional fluctuations in the constructive 
elements are also found within both the martensite and austenite phases 
due to non-equilibrium segregation ( Fig. 2 d and Supplementary 
Fig. 5 ). This substitutional element partitioning during the short 
reversion treatment followed by rapid solidification drove the grains to 
non-equilibrium chemical compositions, producing nanoscale compo -
sitional fluctuations [21] . Such nanoscale compositional heterogeneities 
in the martensite/austenite interfaces, as well as within grains, have not 
been reported in conventional maraging steels after reversion treatment. 
This novelty underscores the ability of HEAs/MEAs to offer highly 
metastable compositional heterogeneities due to their complex micro -
structures. Furthermore, APT analysis revealed the elliptical-shaped

The presence of these softer domains as compositional heterogeneities in 
austenite grains facilitated the TRIP effect, resulting in further increases 
in SHR through interface hardening [ 3 , 14 ] and HDI strengthening [ 8 , 
38 ]. These effects led to a ductility improvement of up to 14%. Together, 
all these effects resulted in an outstanding combination of strength and 
ductility in the resulting TRIP-assisted, heterogeneous, dual-phase 
microstructure. The fractograph morphology of the reverted specimen 
also showed a local fine dimple structure as a sign of ductile fracture. 
These dimples appeared alongside relatively vast cleavage fractures, 
which could be assigned to martensite laths, thus demonstrating the 
pseudo-brittle mode ( Fig. 4 g – i). This fracture behavior was expected due 
to the predomination of the martensite phase and its brittle nature. 
To further elucidate the deformation mechanism, the representative 
microstructural features of the reverted specimen at a strain of 10% were 
explored using TEM analysis. As shown in overall views in Fig. 5 a, b, the 
matrix underwent severe deformation, because most of the microstruc -
ture was transformed into a deformed martensite phase. Furthermore, 
intersecting dislocations appeared at high density in the deformed 
martensite grains. However, some narrow austenite grains remained 
between different martensite grain variants. High – resolution TEM mi -
crographs of the narrow region of the remaining austenite ( Fig. 5 c, d) 
revealed a few straight lines with different contrast streaks due to me -
chanical nano-twinning, as demonstrated by the corresponding fast 
Fourier transformation pattern in Fig. 5 e. The mechanical nano- 
twinning within the remaining austenite could provide a further in -
crease in strain-hardening by hindering a mobile dislocation of the twin 
boundary [ 41 , 42 , 43 ]. Due to the metastability-engineering strategy, 
each reverted austenite grain was compositionally heterogeneous, so the 
stacking fault energy varied locally within each grain. This heteroge -
neity allowed the austenite phase to locally accommodate the applied 
strain through the TWIP effect. In sum, the remarkable LNT tensile 
properties of the TRIP-assisted, heterogeneous, dual-phase MEA 
designed herein were due to the co-activation of solid solution 
strengthening, dislocation-moderated plasticity, compositional hetero -
geneity, precipitation hardening, TRIP and TWIP effects, and HDI 
strengthening inherent to the dual-phase microstructure.
