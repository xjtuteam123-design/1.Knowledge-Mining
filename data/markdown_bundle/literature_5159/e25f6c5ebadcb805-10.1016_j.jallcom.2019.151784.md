abstract
The temporal evolution including size, amount, morphology and distribution of g0 precipitates in g
matrix in conjunction with coarsening kinetics and oxidation behavior of a model Ni e15Al alloy aged at
800 /C14 C for 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 h is studied in detail. The microstructure of the investigated samples
consists of L1 2-type ordered g’ (Ni3Al) precipitates and a disordered Ni-rich g (FCC) matrix phase.
Spheroidal-to-cuboidal-to parallelpipedic morphological transition is observed as the mean precipitate
radius increases with increasing aging time. The temporal exponent p is calculated as 0.35 which agrees
well with the diffusion-controlled LSW theory (p ¼ 0.33) for coarsening of a binary alloy. However, the
power law dependence of the number density (N
V) departs from the theoretical predictions which
suggests a quasi-stationary coarsening. The cyclic oxidation behavior of as-cast and aged (800 /C14 C for 4, 16,
64 and 256 h) Ni e15Al superalloys at 1000 /C14 C in laboratory air were investigated in detail. The results
showed that 4 and 16 h aged samples exhibited considerably better oxidation resistance compared to as-
cast and 64 and 256 h aged samples. The oxide product is identi ﬁed as NiO by X-ray diffraction (XRD)
analyses. Uniform, even and adherent oxide scale formed without any void formation and important
scale spallation.

1. Introduction
Nickel based superalloys are widely used for turbine blades and
nozzle guide vanes in military and commercial aircrafts, land based
power generators, rocket engines, space vehicles, submarines and
chemical processing vessels due to their superior high temperature
strength, creep resistance, toughness, oxidation and corrosion
resistance [ 1e4]. These outstanding properties of Ni-based super-
alloys are attributed to their bimodal microstructure composed of
L12-type ordered g’ (Ni3Al) precipitates and Ni-rich disordered g
(FCC) matrix phase [ 4e8]. Both g matrix phase and g0 precipitates
have cubic crystal structure and small lattice parameter mismatch
between them results in the formation of coherent g/g0 interfaces
[9]. However, g0 precipitates in Ni-based superalloys coarsen
unavoidably after long time aging treatments at high temperatures
[10e12]. Coarsening of the g0 precipitates caused by the balance
between interfacial energy and elastic energy destroys the
coherency between the g matrix phase and g0 precipitates. Recent
studies showed that the high temperature mechanical properties
and creep behavior of these materials may be worsened because of
the change of the morphology and size of the g0 precipitates as a
result of coarsening [ 13,14]. At high temperatures, peak strength,
arising from precipitation hardening, is reduced due to the coars-
ening [ 8,15]. Moreover, Wu et al. [ 14] reported that size of the g0
precipitates increase considerably with increasing aging tempera-
ture leading to a decrease of creep rupture life. Therefore, re-
searchers still concentrate on studying the coarsening behavior and
coarsening kinetics of
g0 precipitates during isothermal aging in Ni-
based single crystal and polycrystalline superalloys [ 10e12]. In this
manner, Li et al. [ 10] demonstrated a new isothermal aging process
under alternating magnetic ﬁeld (AMF) to control the chemical
segregation and coarsening behavior of g’ precipitates in Ni-based
superalloys.
On the other hand, these superalloys should have suf ﬁcient
oxidation resistance in their service conditions [ 16]. At elevated
temperatures and oxygen-containing atmosphere, oxidation
behavior of these materials is one of the service-life limiting factors.
The continuous oxidation of Ni-based superalloys under thermal
cycling conditions can accelerate crack propagation and eventually
cause fracture [ 17]. In this regard; even, dense, compact and

2. Experimental procedure
A model binary Ni-based superalloy was investigated with a
nominal composition of Nie15Al (at.%). The Ni85Al15 superalloy was
produced by arc-melting using a non-consumable tungsten elec-
trode and a water-cooled copper tray under a partial Ar atmosphere
to minimize oxidation. High purity Ni (99.95 wt%) and Al (99.9 wt%)
were used as starting materials. Sample was remelted three times
and reversed between each melting step to achieve greater
composition homogeneity. The weight loss during the arc-melting
process was less than 0.5 wt%.
Samples from the cast ingot underwent a three-step heat-
treatment: (i) samples were homogenized in the g (FCC)-phase
region at 1250 /C14 C for 24 h; (ii) the samples were subjected to va-
cancy annealing in the g (FCC)-phase region at 1 150 /C14 C above the
solvus temperature of g (FCC)/g0(L12) curve for 3 h, followed by a
direct drop-quench into ice water, and (iii) samples were ﬁnally
aged in the [ g (FCC) þ g’ (L12) two phase region] at 800 /C14 C for 1, 4,
16, 64 and 256 h.
2.1. Characterization
For the metallographic investigation, samples were mounted in
bakalite at 180 /C14 C, ground with SiC papers from 180 to 2400 grit,
and then mechanically polished to 1 and 0.3 mm alumina solution.
Finally, samples were etched with a 100 ml HCl/100 ml deionized
H2O/1 g K2S2O5 solution, which is an effective etchant for Ni based
superalloys, for up to 20 s, depending on the aging condition. The
grains of the as-cast Ni 85Al15 sample was examined by Nikon
Eclipse MA100 model inverted optical microscope. The g0 nano-
structured precipitates were imaged with FEI Nova Nano 430 ﬁeld-
emission gun scanning electron microscope (SEM) coupled with
energy dispersive x-ray spectrometry (EDS). The dimensions of the
individual g’ precipitates within the micrographs were estimated
using Image J (NIH), a freeware image-processing program which
ﬁts the precipitate cross-sectional area to a rectangular, ellipse or
another polygon. The standard errors for all quantities were
calculated using standard error propagation methods [ 26].
Vickers microhardness measurements were performed using a
Microbul 1000D model microhardness tester on as-cast and aged
samples polished to 1 mm with an applied load of 200 g sustained
for 10 s. At least ten individual indentation measurements were
averaged for each condition.
2.2. Cyclic oxidation tests
The samples for cyclic oxidation experiments were cut from the
cast ingot to the size of 20x8x2 mm 3 by Metkon Micracut 151
model precision saw. Prior to testing, the samples were ground by
SiC papers up to 1200 grit, polished with 1 mm alumina solution to
obtain mirror like ﬁnish, ultrasonically cleaned in an acetone so-
lution and ﬁnally dried in air. Cyclic oxidation tests were conducted
in a muf ﬂe furnace (Protherm PLF 130/9 model, 1300 /C14 C maximum
operating temperature) at 1000 /C14 C in laboratory air. One cycle
consists of heating the sample to 1000 /C14 C at a heating rate of 10 /C14 C/
min, holding at 1000 /C14 C for 1 h and fast cooling to ambient tem-
perature at a cooling rate of 40 /C14 C/min. After being cooled to
ambient temperature, the mass change was measured utilizing an
electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.01 mg. Phase identi ﬁcation
of the oxidation products were performed by x-ray diffraction
(XRD) analyses using Cu-K a radiation ( l ¼ 1.54060 Å) in a Bruker
D8 diffractometer. The XRD spectrum was obtained in the 2 q range
of 20 e130/C14 with step size, scanning rate, voltage and current of
0.02/C14 ,2 /C14 /min, 40 kV and 40 mA, respectively for the higher accu-
racy of peak position.

The morphology of the g0 precipitates depends on two factors:
elastic strain energy and interfacial free energy [ 21,28]. The balance
between two factors ﬁnally determines the morphology. The elastic
strain energy of a g’ precipitate is proportional to R 3, whereas the
matrix/precipitate interfacial energy is proportional to R 2 [3]. Thus,
interfacial free energy is the dominant factor in precipitate
morphology during growth and coarsening. At short aging times,
the precipitate radius is small and surface area to volume ratio of
precipitate is high so interfacial free energy is dominant, leading to
spheroidal morphology. On the other hand, as the precipitate radius
increases, surface area to volume ratio becomes small and elastic
strain energy due to the lattice parameter mismatch determines
the morphology. In this regard, Thomson et al. [ 22] presented a
model in which the magnitude of elastic strain energy can be
expressed as:

4. Conclusions
The temporal evolution and cyclic oxidation behavior of a model
Nie15Al superalloy isothermally aged at 800 /C14 C were investigated
in detail, and the following conclusions can be drawn:
i. With increasing aging time, the mean precipitate radius <R>
increased and the morphology of the g0 precipitates changed
from spheroidal to cuboidal aligned along <100> direction
and ﬁnally to parallelpipedic.
ii. Microhardness values varies over a narrow range from 1.9 to
2.3 GPa, and peak hardness is observed at 4 h aging time.
iii. Although NiO is the main oxide product for all aging condi-
tions, as-cast and long-time aged (64 and 256 h) samples had
lower oxidation resistance compared to short-time aged (4
and 16 h) samples.
This study presents promising properties of a model binary
Nie15Al alloy for structural applications at high temperature. It is
predicted that coarsening kinetics and hardness of this alloy can be
further improved by Nb or Mo addition which will stabilize g0
precipitates and increase the volume fraction of g’ precipitates.
