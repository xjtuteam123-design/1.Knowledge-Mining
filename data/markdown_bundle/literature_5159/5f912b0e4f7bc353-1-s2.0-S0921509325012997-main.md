1. Introduction
The development of aerospace and nuclear energy industries has put 
forward more stringent requirements for high-temperature materials. 
For example, the operating temperature of hypersonic aircraft nose and 
scramjet engines is close to 2000 
◦
C. However, the limit temperature of 
nickel-based superalloys currently used is only 1200 
◦
C [ 1 – 4 ]. Although 
refractory alloys, ultra-high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs), and 
carbon-carbon (C-C) composites can withstand temperatures exceeding 
1400 
◦
C, these materials still fall short of fully meeting practical appli -
cation requirements. For instance, refractory alloys such as TaW and 
IrRe exhibit moderate plasticity and relatively high damage tolerance at 
room temperature. However, their mechanical properties degrade 
rapidly under elevated temperatures, rendering them inadequate for 
high-temperature strength requirements. Ultra-high-temperature ce -
ramics possess the requisite strength for extreme thermal environments. 
However, these materials exhibit poor room-temperature plasticity, 
limited damage tolerance, and significant fabrication challenges.

Refractory medium/high-entropy alloys (RM/HEAs) demonstrate 
exceptional high-temperature strength, positioning them as promising 
candidate materials for ultra-high-temperature applications. In 2011, 
Senkov et al. [ 5 ] investigated the refractory high-entropy alloys 
NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW. It is found that the compressive strength of 
NbMoTaW refractory high-entropy alloy can reach 400 MPa at 1600 
◦

and the compressive strength of NbMoTaWV refractory alloy at 1600

is 479 MPa, which is significantly higher than that of traditional re -

TaWV refractory high-entropy alloys at room temperature is only about 
2.1 %, which seriously limits their processing capacity and practical 
application range. To enhance the room-temperature ductility of 
NbMoTaW RHEAs, studies have shown that the introduction of light 
metal elements such as Ti, Zr and Hf can effectively improve its room 
temperature plasticity [ 6 – 9 ]. At the same time, these added elements 
may also lead to a significant reduction in the strength of the alloy at 
elevated temperatures. How to improve the room temperature brittle -
ness of refractory medium/high-entropy alloys, further enhance the 
high temperature strength, and solve the problem of room temperature 
plasticity and high temperature strength inversion is the key to the 
practical application of refractory medium/high-entropy alloys [ 10 , 11 ].
Previous studies have shown that introducing an appropriate amount 
of secondary ceramic carbides into ductile refractory medium/high- 
entropy matrices can significantly enhance the high-temperature 
strength of the alloy without markedly compromising its room- 
temperature ductility [ 12 ]. For example, the Re

their high-temperature mechanical properties. Building on these studies, 
our research group further introduced trace amounts of molybdenum 
(Mo): on one hand, solid solution strengthening intensifies lattice 
distortion to enhance creep resistance; on the other hand, the segrega -
tion of Mo at grain boundaries enables impurity purification and grain 
boundary strengthening, thereby mitigating the potential brittleness 
tendency caused by carbide addition. Meanwhile, the added trace 
hafnium (Hf) can form stable carbides, which not only inhibit the pre -
cipitation of harmful phases and refine grains but also improve the 
oxidation resistance of grain boundaries.

In prior research [ 19 ], our group successfully employed spark plasma 
sintering (SPS) to fabricate carbide-reinforced refractory medium-entropy 
alloys. Preliminary findings revealed that the controlled formation of 
oxides and carbides exerts a profound influence on the microstructure and 
mechanical properties of these SPS-processed alloys. In the Re-Hf-Ta-W-C 
refractory alloy system, hafnium serves as the critical determinant of 
carbide microstructure. However, when the alloy is prepared by SPS 
sintering, Hf is easily combined with oxygen to form oxides, resulting in a 
decrease in the effective Hf content of carbides, and hafnium oxide can 
also seriously deteriorate the microstructure and properties. Therefore, 
the content and relative content of Hf and C are the key to the formation 
of carbides and oxides in the alloy, which in turn affects the mechanical 
properties of the alloy.

mechanical properties of the alloy. The first mechanism is through the 
formation of hafnium oxide, which in turn influences the mechanical 
properties of the alloy. For this mechanism, further refining the size of 
hafnium oxide is beneficial to the improvement of mechanical proper -
ties. The second mechanism is through influencing the carbide structure 
to affect the mechanical properties of the alloy. For this mechanism, the 
formation of MC-type carbides is more conducive to the improvement of 
high-temperature strength. The influence mechanism of element C on 
the mechanical properties of alloys is relatively simple. The mechanism

However, it should be noted that the content of hafnium involved in
