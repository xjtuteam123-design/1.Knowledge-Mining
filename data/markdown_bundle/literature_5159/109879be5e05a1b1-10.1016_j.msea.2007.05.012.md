Abstract
Room temperature tensile properties of a superplastic AZ31 magnesium alloy prior to and following high temperature deformation at 673
and 723 K were examined. The material behaved in a superplastic manner at low strain rates, and dislocation creep dominated deformation at
high strain rates. The specimens after high temperature deformation exhibited higher ductility and lower strength as the strain rate during high
temperature deformation decreased. Pre-existing basal texture changed gradually toward random during high temperature deformation at low strain
rates, whereas it was strengthened at high strain rates. Both the basal texture weakening and lower cavity volume fraction brought about the higher
ductility following superplastic deformation at lower strain rates.

1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys have high potential as structural materi-
als since magnesium is the lightest of all structural alloys, but
they normally exhibit low formability near room temperature.
Therefore, most of magnesium products have been fabricated by
casting, in particular, by die casting. In order to exploit the ben-
eﬁts of magnesium alloys, it is important to develop secondary
processing, which can effectively produce complex engineer-
ing components directly from wrought products. Application
of superplasticity is expected for the forming of magnesium
alloys. To date, superplastic behavior of magnesium alloys has
been investigated extensively [1]. However, very limited data
are available for the mechanical properties of superplastically
deformed magnesium alloys[2–5]. It is desirable that superplas-
tically deformed alloys possess high ductility from a viewpoint
of reliability. The ductility enhancement of superplastically
deformed magnesium alloys is of industrial interest as well as
of scientiﬁc interest.
Mechanical properties of superplastically deformed magne-
sium alloys may be inﬂuenced by several microstructural factors,

Primary processing such as hot rolling and hot extrusion,
which are often used to fabricate superplastic alloys [1], gen-
erally gives rise to a strong basal texture in magnesium alloys
[6,7], even if the materials are processed by asymmetric rolling
[8–10]: the basal planes in most grains are distributed parallel to
the rolling or extrusion direction. Although development of the
basal texture enhances the strength of rolled or extruded magne-
sium alloys for deformation in tension near room temperature, it
conversely decreases the ductility. This is because basal planes
in most grains are unfavorably oriented for basal slip, which is
the preferred slip system for magnesium over a wide range of
temperature. In order to enhance the room temperature ductility
after superplastic deformation, it is needed to modify the texture
of the deformed alloys.
There is an extreme example of ductility enhancement by tex-
ture control in a magnesium alloy. Mukai et al. [11] performed
Equal-Channel-Angular Extrusion [12] on an AZ31 magnesium
alloy, and succeeded in fabricating ductile AZ31, which has
basal plane orientation inclined 45
◦ to the extrusion direction.

ited high ductility of 45% even at room temperature. Texture
randomization would also be an alternative way for ductility

The K-values of the AZ31 alloy prior to and following high
temperature deformation are plotted in Fig. 10. The fabrication
process (extrusion) created a moderately strong basal texture.
Normal grain growth during annealing at 673 and 723 K led
to more strong basal texture as has been reported in AZ61
[25].
The pre-existing basal texture evolved into strong texture
after 75% deformation in Region III. The texture strengthen-
ing ought to be caused by basal slip, especially in the early stage
of deformation, because basal slip is the most active slip sys-
tem in AZ31 alloy even at elevated temperatures[26]. However,
as the high temperature deformation proceeds, the geometri-
cal condition for the basal slip becomes more unfavorable, and
other slip systems should play an important role. It has been
reported that both the two primary non-basal slip systems in
hexagonal structures, i.e., the prismatic {100 }⟨110 ⟩and the
pyramidal {101 }⟨110 ⟩slip systems, have high average Schmid
factors in extruded AZ31 alloy with ⟨100 ⟩ﬁber texture [15].
The retention of the strong basal texture after 75% elongation
may suggest the predominance of prismatic slip {100 }⟨110 ⟩,
because the prismatic slip gives rise to rotations around c-axis
[18]. Higher strength and lower elongation following high tem-
perature deformation in Region III obtained in the present study
can be explained from the fact that the specimens were unfavor-
ably oriented for basal slip. It is interesting to note in Fig. 7(a)
that the difference in strength of the specimens after 50% and
75% elongations was negligible when the specimens have been
deformed at 723 K (in Region III). It follows that texture hard-
ening appreciably overwhelms the degradation due to cavity
growth.
As is evident fromFig. 10, the K-value decreased after defor-
mation in Region II. This means that texture changed gradually
toward random during deformation, though basal texture was
retained even after 75% elongation. In addition, the lower the
strain rate during high temperature deformation, the lower the
K-value. It is obvious that the grain boundary sliding, which is
the principal mode of deformation in Region II, brought about
texture weakening. The lower strength and higher elongation
of the specimens deformed in Region II are associated with
the fact that much grains are now favorably oriented for basal
slip. The fact that the strengths after 75% elongation were lower
than those after 50% elongation is attributed not only to the
increase in cavity volume fraction but also to texture weakening.
It was demonstrated that the ductility following high temper-
ature deformation increases when the material was deformed
under superplastic ﬂow at lower strain rates. It is expected that
the decrease in cavity volume fraction and basal texture weak-
ening mutually act favorably to increase the ductility following
high temperature deformation.
Importance of texture is evident in Fig. 11 , in which the
variation of (a) yield strength and (b) elongation-to-failure with
K-value is shown for the specimens after 75% elongation at high
temperatures. As the K-value approaches unity, or the texture
changes toward random, elongation increases, whereas yield
strength decreases.
The room temperature ductility of the specimens following
superplastic deformation was lower than that after annealing in
the present study ( Figs. 6(b) and 7(b) ). In addition, although
the degree of texture weakening should be higher in the spec-
imens deformed up to larger strains in Region II, the ductility
of the specimens deformed up to 75% was lower than those
deformed up to 50%. By contrast, there are some cases that
ductility increased even after superplastic deformation (black
arrows inFig. 1). In order to increase the room temperature duc-
tility following superplastic deformation, it is needed that the
improvement due to texture weakening overwhelms the degra-
dation by cavitation.
The relatively low ductility of the specimens following super-
plastic deformation obtained in the present study would be
associated with the relatively coarse grain size of the alloy used.

basal texture evolves into strong texture during heating prior
to straining. For example, it has been demonstrated that basal
texture is strengthened at the expense of grains with other ori-

high even though the dominant deformation mechanism is grain
boundary sliding [18]. Both effects act negatively against tex-

is effective to use ﬁne-grained alloys. The use of ﬁne-grained
alloys enables (i) to reduce the optimum superplastic tempera-
ture [27] and (ii) to decrease contribution from dislocation creep.
Both effects should effectively contribute to change the texture
toward random. The use of ﬁne-grained alloys is also effective
from a viewpoint of productivity [28], because the optimum
superplastic strain rate moves to higher strain rates as the grain
size decreases [27].
5. Summary
Room temperature tensile properties and texture prior to
and following high temperature deformation were examined
in a superplastic AZ31 magnesium alloy with relatively coarse
grains. The specimens were deformed to nominal strains of 50
and 75% at high temperatures of 673 and 723 K and at various
strain rates. At the high temperatures, the material behaved in
a superplastic manner at low strain rates, and dislocation creep
dominated deformation at high strain rates.
(1) The specimens after deformation in the superplastic region
exhibited higher ductility and lower strength. In these spec-
imens, pre-existing basal texture changed gradually toward
random during high temperature deformation, though the
basal texture was retained even after 75% elongation.
(2) The specimens after deformation in the region where the
dislocation creep dominated deformation exhibited lower
ductility and higher strength. In these specimens, the pre-
existing basal texture developed into strong texture during
high temperature deformation.
(3) The mechanical properties of the specimens following high
temperature deformation depended both on the texture
evolution occurring during heating and high temperature
deformation and on cavity formation. For example, the elon-
gations of the specimens after 75% elongation were lower
than those after 50% elongation even under superplastic
ﬂow. This suggests that the degradation of the ductility
by cavitation overwhelmed the improvement due to texture
weakening in the present alloy.
(4) For obtaining higher room temperature ductility, prior defor-
mation under superplastic ﬂow at relatively low strain rates
would be appropriate. Furthermore, in order to enhance the
ductility of superplastically deformed magnesium alloys,
ﬁne-grained alloys should be deformed superplastically at
relatively low temperatures to promote texture weakening
during processing.
