ABSTRACT 
Alloying elements bring with them a level of undesirable societal impact. The present paper examines this 
impact, in terms of primary production, for 340 newly proposed high entropy compositions. Three areas of 
sustainability are considered: economic viability, environmental impact and human well-being. The high entropy 
alloys are considered in two classes: body centred cubic and related alloys with potential for high temperature 
application and face centred cubic and related Cantor-type alloys. The former are compared to Ni-based su -
peralloys and the latter to advanced steels. It is seen that with improved performance of Ni-based superalloys 
there has been an increase in undesirable impact across many of the indicators we employ. However, some 
recently proposed high temperature high entropy alloys – especially Yeh-type High Entropy Superalloys - display 
notably lower levels of undesirable impact than Ni-based superalloy compositions. Cantor-type face centred 
cubic alloys, however, do not compare so favourably when stacked up against the best steels, for room tem -
perature strength and ductility. We show that, in general, there is good scope for high entropy alloys to be 
designed that target lower levels of undesirable impact. 
1. Introduction 
Alloys with near equi-proportional additions of multiple elements – 
e.g. high entropy alloys – are a relatively new class of alloys that can 
provide new and potentially attractive combinations of performance 
indices [ 1 – 4 ]. If these alloys are to find success in commercial applica -
tion, they must also satisfy requirements of a non-technological nature, 
such as their impact on CO
2 
emissions [ 5 – 8 ]. To quote from a recent 
review [ 6 ]: “ Significant efforts are needed to select economically and 
environmentally viable alloy combinations that meet today ’ s technical 
demands." Ultimately, this can be accomplished via a full economic and 
life cycle assessment. However, such an approach is unable to guide an 
alloy search, which requires fast efficient estimations of sustainability 
(e.g. [ 9 ]). 
Two questions arise in the case of high entropy alloys (HEAs, used in 
the general sense of a compositionally complex alloy, which may or may 
not contain secondary phases): what readily evaluated sustainability 
measures are worthy of consideration and, how do the high entropy 
alloys already proposed to date compare? We propose one answer to the 
first question immediately below and then we dedicate the remainder of 
the present paper to our main focus, which is to address the second 
question. 
The present issue has been addressed in part by governments as they 
have sought to identify materials critical to their economies (e.g. [ 10 ]). 
The European Commission Raw Material Information System, for 
example, identifies two criteria of criticality: Supply Risk and Economic 
Importance [ 11 ]. Ferro et al. [ 12 ] have performed a material selection 
exercise for structural components, taking into account raw material 
criticality via measures of Abundance Risk Level, Sourcing and Geopo -
litical Risk, Environmental Country Risk, Supply Risk, Economic 
Importance and End of Life Recycling Input Rate. To facilitate material 
selection, these were all aggregated into a single Raw Material Criticality 
parameter. While aggregation of measures is essential when so many 
different factors are being taken into account, we feel that an interme -
diate level of aggregation is to be preferred to allow distinct criteria to be 
more clearly visible, and thereby more easily weighed separately ac -
cording to need and purpose. In developing a gauge for metal criticality, 
Graedel et al. [ 13 – 16 ] have proposed three highly developed interme -
diate aggregations of indicators under the headings of Environmental 
Implications, Vulnerability to Supply Restriction and Supply Risk. Such 
measures have potential to direct HEA alloy development.

availability and recyclability. They provide limited assessment of pro -

beyond improvement in technical performance at as early a stage as 
possible, lest the excitement over their properties be dashed against the 
reality of their technological viability ’ . Wang et al. [ 17 ] consider 
availability, prices, and chemical hazards and consider a limited number 
of compositions comprising the refractory elements (Mo, Nb, Ta, W, Zr, 
V, Re, Ti, Hf). We believe there is a need for a more comprehensive 
examination and that, in search for potential alloys, it is desirable to 
delineate considerations of economic viability from environmental im -
pacts and potential human health implications and to examine these 
three factors separately. 
The commercial viability of proto-type HEA compositions is a com -
plex matter subject to the vagary of market forces but we agree with Fu 
et al. [ 9 ] and Wang et al. [ 17 ] that it is desirable to consider both raw 
material cost and any uncertainty in the availability of constituent ele -
ments when comparing compositions. Supply risk and price volatility 
can be considered to a first approximation to go hand-in-hand so 
consideration of one can give a gauge of the other. The cost of raw 
materials is a major determinant of the overall cost of producing an alloy 
and in recognition of this, low cost High Entropy Alloy (HEA) compo -
sitions are understandably receiving increased attention (e.g [ 18 – 21 ]). 
In proposing a new alloy, it is also of interest to weigh the likelihood that 
its commercialization might place additional pressure on a resource 
already considered to be important in the economy. It might be ad -
vantageous for a new alloy composition to avoid creating additional 
demand for elements that are already subject to supply risk and which 
are needed in other vital sectors of the economy. The energy transition is 
placing pressure on the supply of certain critical metals (e.g. [ 22 ]). The 
importance of an element to the economy (at a global level) is captured 
in Graedel et al. ’ s [ 13 – 16 ] measure of Vulnerability to Supply Restric -
tion. This term can also be seen as a factor that impacts upon (macro) 
economic viability. 
In assessing the likely environmental impact incurred in the mining 
and production of a given alloy constituent it is desirable to consider the 
energy required for extraction, which correlates strongly with CO
2 
emissions, the water consumed during primary production and the 
amount of extra material needed to be moved and processed to obtain 
the element from its ore. These factors address three key planetary 
boundaries: climate change, fresh water and land-system change [ 23 ]. 
Although environmental impact translates ultimately to impact on 
human existence, in choosing alloying additions, it is desirous to 
consider other more immediate factors that impact on human health and 
well-being during the extraction and use of metals. This can occur as a 
consequence of the nature of the metal, of the processes and reactants 
used to mine and extract it and of the treatment of workers by man -
agement and government. By seeking to assess adherence to human 
rights and international labour standards in countries of elemental 
origin when searching for new alloy classes and compositions, to the 
extent possible, an ethical sensibility can be introduced while helping to 
ensure social acceptability. Sometimes the need is pressing; the toxicity 
of lead to humans, for example, precipitated the development of lead- 
free solders for the electronics sector [ 24 ]. 
Although recyclability is a significant factor to consider for a new 
alloy, we do not feel existing data are sufficiently well-developed for 
inclusion in a quantitative analysis in HEA development. However, we 
note that Fu et al. [ 9 ] show that many proposed HEAs have potential to 
be incorporated into current Cu recycling flows, though this of course 
depends on volumes. We choose here to focus on the impact of primary 
production. In the first instance, a new alloy is frequently introduced to 
replace an existing alloy. Under such circumstances, impacts relating to 
secondary production are likely to be similar. For example, a new alloy 
introduced for use in an automotive outer body will still, in all likeli -
hood, need to be rolled, annealed, welded and painted, just like the 
previous alloy. A new alloy for a wire product, will still need to be cold 
drawn, and so on. This means that differences in the impact of primary 
production will often dominate those relating to downstream 
manufacturing. It is also the case that the most significant health, safety, 
environmental, and economic impacts, as well as the social acceptance 
challenges, will typically be concentrated in the primary raw material 
extraction and metal production stages of manufacture. 
In what follows, we describe our preferred subset of indicators and 
apply some minor necessary modifications to render them fit for the 
present purpose. Then we assess 340 previously proposed HEA compo -
sitions. We consider separately grades entertained for their prospects in 
high temperature applications and compositions proposed for their 
strength-ductility balance. To provide context, we also assess a series of 
Ni-based super alloys and a number of advanced steels. Finally, we 
highlight a number of HEA composition classes that display lower levels 
of potentially adverse impact than other contenders and commend them 
for further study. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Description of indicators 
To provide a balanced concise assessment of each of the categories of 
economic viability, environmental impact and human well-being, we 
employ indicators that provide three separate facets to each category. 
We describe our nine indicators as follows and then outline the details of 
their evaluation in the subsequent section. In all cases, the larger the 
value of an indicator the more undesirable it is. 
2.1.1. Economic viability 
• Raw Material Price (MP) : The price of an alloying addition in its pure 
form is employed here as a proxy for cost due to its ready availability 
(see also [ 9 ]). It is not uncommon for master alloys to be employed as 
a vector for alloying addition and these, in most cases, come at a 
lower cost than the pure element. Recycled material is also employed 
at as an alloying input where available. The use of raw material price 
therefore provides an upper estimate of the cost of an alloying 
addition. 
• Supply Risk (SR) : We use the aggregated supply risk indicator 
developed by Graedel et al. [ 14 ], which accounts for the likelihood of 
disruptions in the supply of raw materials due to geopolitical issues, 
export quotas, resource nationalism and natural disasters. This ad -
dresses the availability of metal ’ s supply and the degree to which the 
availability of that supply might be constrained. 
• (Normalized) Vulnerability to Supply Restriction (NVSR) : The impor -
tance of a scarce metallic element to the economy is captured here by 
normalizing the vulnerability to supply restriction (the importance) 
defined by Graedel and colleagues [ 14 ] by the logarithmic production 
volume of a given element (the scarcity). We perform this normali -
zation because we believe the impact of an additional demand for a 
given mass of an element created by commercialization of a new 
alloy should be considered in relation to the total mass of the element 
already circulating in the economy. Less impact will derive from a 
smaller fractional increase in demand. Furthermore, the attractive 
likelihood of obtaining an element via a recycling pathway is 
increased when the mass of an element already circulating in the 
economy is higher. By way of example, iron is rated at ~65/100 for 
‘Vulnerability to Supply Restriction ’ (VSR) [ 14 ]. This is amongst the 
higher values assigned, a reflection of the significance of this 
element. Neodymium, on the other hand, is rated at ~35/100, one of 
the lower values out of the elements assessed. Although Fe is deemed 
to be more vulnerable than Neodymium, it would be (immediately) 
less impactful for the economy if a fixed tonnage of new alloy con -
tained (an equivalent amount of) Fe than Nd, which, for example, is 
heavily used in magnets destined for wind power generation.

2.1.2. Environmental impact 
• Embodied Energy (EE): We employ the embodied energy associated 
with primary production, which captures the total energy required to 
produce a metal from its ores and feedstock, encompassing extrac -
tion, production, and transportation processes. It is a metric that 
correlates strongly with greenhouse gas emissions but it also mea -
sures, in an indirect way, the extent of any impact energy production 
has on the earth ’ s surface (e.g. rivers dammed, land area occupied by 
solar installations, etc) and the demand placed on a resource that will 
always be to some extent limited. Our source is reference [ 25 ]. 
• Water Use (WU): This is a value that measures the total water 
consumed in the extraction and production of an alloying addition. 
Our source is reference [ 25 ]. 
• Rock-to-Metal Ratio (RMR): This term provides an indication of the 
intensity of land use associated with mining operations. A high ratio 
signifies greater amounts of waste rock and ore mined to produce a 
unit of refined metal, reflecting greater levels of environmental 
impact due to land disturbance, habitat destruction, emissions and 
waste generation. Data were obtained from reference [ 26 ]. 
2.1.3. Human well-being 
• Human Health Damage (HHD): This is an aggregate indicator evalu -
ated in [ 13 , 14 ] based on the ReCiPe endpoint methodology [ 27 , 28 ] 
using the ‘ecoinvent ’ database [ 29 ] which provides a direct impact of 
constituent metals on human well-being. It encompasses the impacts 
of climate change on human health, ozone depletion potential, 
human toxicity, photochemical oxidant formation, particulate mat -
ter formation, and ionizing radiation. 
• Human Rights Pressure (HRP): This term is derived as the complement 
of the Human Rights Index published by ourworldindata.org , an 
index which in turn is based on the Civil Liberties Index as part of the 
Varieties of Democracy Research Project (V-Dem) [ 30 – 32 ]. It relies 
on assessments from a broad base of country based experts and 
although this invariably introduces an element of subjectivity, 
leading to challenges in correlation with other indicators [ 33 ], it is 
for this reason that the index commends itself to us as a measure of 
the level of likely ‘pressure ’ felt by workers involved in elemental 
extraction due to the level of broader respect for Human Rights in 
their communities, including the extent of respect for physical 
integrity rights, private civil liberties and political civil liberties in 
the countries of elemental extraction. 
• Labor Rights Pressure (LRP): This indicator is a measure of the extent 
of respect for labor rights in the countries of elemental extraction. It 
is taken as the complement of the Labor Rights Index published by the 
WageIndicator Foundation at labourrightsindex.org [ 34 , 35 ], which 
aggregates, for the most populous regions of the country of element 
origin, measures of the following factors for legal contracted em -
ployees in companies employing more than 60 people: fair wages, 
decent working hours, employment security, family responsibilities, 
maternity at work, safe work, social security, fair treatment, child 
and forced labour and trade union representation. It captures the 
level of pressure likely to be felt by workers involved in extraction 
due to the level of broader respect for labor rights.

In some cases, we found that data were missing. For instance, when 
calculating the Labor Rights Pressure using the product of the WGC 
matrices, we encountered missing values for 28% of the countries. To 
overcome this issue, we sought out correlations to use as a basis to 
impute the missing data. We built a dataset comprising 180 countries, 
characterized by 26 complete features related to governance, social 
development, and sustainable competitiveness (Table S1). This set of 
features provides a solid foundation for the imputation of the missing 
values for our two country-dependent indicators. A preliminary corre -
lation heatmap of our dataset (Fig. S2) reveals the correlation amongst 
the selected 26 country features, a scenario amenable for K-Nearest 
Neighbours (KNN) imputation, a technique widely used in machine 
learning for its effectiveness [ 36 ]. 
To impute the missing data, we adapted the KNN algorithm within 
Python ’ s Scikit-Learn library [ 37 ], which is a robust suite of machine 
learning tools. The KNN algorithm functions by identifying the ‘k ’ 
closest neighbouring points in the feature space and imputing missing 
values using the mean or median of these neighbours. This method

assumes that points with similar data features are likely to yield similar

to ensure reproducibility of our results, we have utilized the default 
hyperparameters provided by the Scikit-Learn library ’ s KNN imple -
mentation. This includes the default number of neighbours, which is set 
to 5, a common standard in KNN analysis that balances the trade-off 
between underfitting and overfitting. To ensure a robust imputation 
process, we implemented a two-step normalization strategy in the pre- 
processing phase of our analysis. Given the presence of outliers, 
scaling was first implemented to mitigate their impact and subsequently 
scaling to the [0,100] range was performed (using algorithms in the 
Python framework – ‘RobustScaler ’ and ‘MinMaxScaler ’ ). This approach 
ensures that all features contribute equally to the computation of ‘dis -
tance ’ in the KNN algorithm, enhancing the accuracy of the imputation. 
An illustration of the efficacy of the approach is provided in the sup -
plementary materials. 
The same methodology, utilizing the KNN imputation technique, was 
also applied to impute the missing values for the element indicators 
(6 – 22% of values, depending on the indicator). This approach was 
further substantiated by a correlation analysis encompassing additional 
elemental properties (Fig. S2), ensuring a comprehensive and consistent 
treatment of missing data across all features in our study. 
We acknowledge that there are some differences in the time frames 
over which our selected parameters were collected and present our 
collation as a snapshot that holds more-or-less for the decade and a half 
preceding publication. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. A bird ’ s eye view 
Fig. 2 showcases our collection of nine indicators for our palette of 
elements (with data given in Table S3). As each indicator varies across a 
different range, the radial axes of the radar plots in Fig. 2 display the 
ranking of each element per indicator (in all cases, a larger value in -
dicates a larger potential impost on society). Overall – and one needs to 
be careful not to make too much from such a broad statement - the plot 
casts Fe, Al, Cr, Cu, Mn, Nb, Ni, Si, Ti and Zr in a more favourable light 
than Co, Hf, Mo, Re, Ru, Ta, V and W. Furthermore, we note that the 
shape of the ‘footprints ’ differ considerably across the elements. This 
means the indicators are not dominated by correlation and that a 
particular index is not dominating any overall ranking. That is, elements 
exert their impact on society in different ways. (Note, we assign no value 
of impact in any of our indicators to carbon, due to its natural entry into 
Fe through the iron making process and its presence in small amounts in 
the compositions considered here. Fuller consideration of the role of this 
addition in the future may be warranted, due to changing steel making 
practices and new grades that may demand higher levels of it.) 
We calculated values for each of our nine indicators for 340 high 
entropy alloy (HEA) compositions that have been proposed in the 
literature [ 38 – 40 ]. All our data are fully tabulated in reference [ 41 ]. The 
ranges of obtained values are plotted in Fig. 3 . As one might expect from 
the variety in alloying options provided by the high entropy alloying 
concept, there is a considerable variation in values for each societal 
indicator. 
A significant observation to note is that the HEAs evaluated here 
return higher values of impact parameters than for steels. Compared to 
Ni-based high temperature superalloys, HEAs return higher impacts in 
some alloys and measures but lower in others. There is thus a strong 
prima facie observation that substitution of a high performing HEA alloy 
for a Ni-based superalloy, should that prove possible, may result in a 
reduction in compositionally derived societal impact. 
Fig. 4 quantifies our chosen indicators for a subset of 225 high en -
tropy alloy (HEA) compositions that have appeared in the literature and 
that are potential candidates for applications involving elevated tem -

typically not sufficiently well established to allow a comprehensive 
comparison on performance. In light of this, the HEA points are posi -

blades [ 42 ]. We provide rule of mixture estimations of melting tem -

Plotting in the chosen manner provides us with a preliminary basis for 
considering impact in light of potential (best-case scenario) 
performance.

4. Concluding remarks 
In seeking to generate a rapid assessment of sustainability to help 
guide the search for new alloys, we have employed three categories of 
three indicators each under the headings: economic viability, environ -
mental impact and human well-being. We acknowledge the measures we 
have adopted are not mutually exclusive and there is also some corre -
lation amongst them. There is an element of subjectivity and variability 
in our chosen indicators and a broad-brush is needed, so attention is 
focussed on where there are large differences in indicators. With this in 
mind, we undertook to assess a selection of recently published High 
Entropy Alloys. 
A positive image emerges for the prospect of high temperature HEAs 
and HEAs to combine improved performance and reduced undesirable 
societal impacts. In contrast, Cantor-type FCC HEAs provide a less 
attractive prospect in terms of the broader implications of their com -
positions. This arises chiefly from the long shadow cast by the superi -
ority of steels in terms both of performance and relative smallness of 
their societal footprint, per kilogram. The HEAs with the lowest levels of 
impact are those that are dominated by Al, Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni something 
that can be readily rationalized with reference to Fig. 5 . Many of the 
compositions with lower levels of impact also contain Co. This element 
is seen in Fig. 5 to score highly across a number of impact categories so 
there is evidently scope to further reduce societal impact of such com -
positions by lowering the Co content. 
The tough-strong FCC HEAs we examined in terms of the combina -
tion of room temperature strength and ductility compare less favourably 
in both performance and broad societal impact when stacked up against 
advanced steels. We feel there is potential to explore means of lowering 
the societal impact (e.g. increased Fe levels). 
Our work stresses the need for earlier consideration of the societal 
impact that may arise from a new alloy recipe. However, we acknowl -
edge that in some cases the desirable societal benefits reaped in service 
can outweigh the undesirable impacts incurred during production. Ul -
timately a trade-off will need to be made. However, all else constant, it 
should be always beneficial to seek to lower the level of undesirable 
impact of an alloying choice. The present work provides a tool that 
Roeser ’ s [ 47 ] ‘emotional engineer ’ might employ with the explicit aim 
of lowering the level of societal impact of alloy compositions. 
Finally, we point out that the present indicators present a global 
snapshot and local supply chains will have their own set of indicators, 
and these will change over time with, for example, increased recycling 
rates, newly discovered ore bodies and new technology developments. 
However, our approach can easily be modified for local and temporal 
variation. The snapshot we have created nevertheless paints a picture 
that illustrates how sustainability can readily be introduced when 
searching for new alloys. It also reveals that there is considerable vari -
ation in the social impact associated with high entropy alloy 
compositions. There is good potential to steer alloy development to -
wards more desirable compositions, favouring Fe, Al, Cr, Cu, Mn, Nb, Ni, 
Si, Ti and Zr over Co, Hf, Mo, Re, Ru, Ta, V and W but also to arrive at 
optimal compositional combinations that serve to increase 
sustainability.
