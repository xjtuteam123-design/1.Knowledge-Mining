ABSTRACT 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are considered as new hydrogen compatible materials, but enhancing their yield 
strength without deteriorating their hydrogen embrittlement resistance is challenging. In this study, various 
kinds of defects are introduced into a CrMnFeCoNi Cantor alloy by plastic straining via the high-pressure torsion 
method, and the correlations of applied strain, microstructural features, strength, and hydrogen embrittlement 
are studied. The unstrained coarse-grained alloy shows elongations over 80% under hydrogen, but its yield 
strength is only 220 MPa. Twinning is a major deformation mechanism at the early stages of straining, resulting 
in over 1 GPa yield strength and 9% elongation in the presence of hydrogen. With a further increase in strain, 
dislocation-based defects including Lomer-Cottrell locks and D-Frank partial dislocations with low mobility are 
formed, enhancing the strength further. At large strains, nanograins with high-angle boundaries are generated, 
resulting in over 1900 MPa strength with poor hydrogen embrittlement resistance due to large hydrogen 
diffusion and hydrogen-enhanced decohesion. These results suggest that twins and defects with low mobility 
such as Lomer-Cottrell locks and D-Frank partial dislocations are effective to achieve a combination of high yield 
strength and good hydrogen embrittlement resistance by suppressing the hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity 
in HEAs. 
1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are a recent class of materials which are 
based on the presence of five or more principal elements in the solid 
solution form [ 1 ]. These alloys have drawn attention in recent years due 
to their outstanding mechanical and functional properties, such as a 
good combination of strength and ductility [ 2 ], wear resistance [ 3 ], 
fracture toughness [ 4 ], biocompatibility [ 5 ], corrosion resistance [ 6 ], 
etc. The alloys have also been considered for various applications in the 
field of hydrogen such as hydrogen storage [ 7 ], hydrogen production [ 8 ] 
and hydrogen embrittlement resistance [ 9 ]. For the hydrogen embrit -
tlement resistance of HEAs, it was shown that the Cantor alloy, 
CrMnFeCoNi, with the face-centered cubic (FCC) structure can show 
excellent resistance to hydrogen embrittlement [ 10 ]. However, a main 
drawback of the Cantor alloy is its low yield strength [ 11 ], while a 
combination of high yield strength and high hydrogen embrittlement 
resistance is essential for the application of any structural alloy in the 
presence of hydrogen [ 12 ]. 
Grain refinement strengthening which is expressed by the Hall-Petch 
mechanism is a well-known strategy to improve the yield strength of 
polycrystalline materials [ 13 ]. The severe plastic deformation (SPD) 
techniques such as high-pressure torsion (HPT) and equal-channel 
angular pressing (ECAP) have been successfully used in recent years to 
significantly enhance the yield strength of various materials by gener -
ating ultrafine grains (UFG) with submicrometer or nanometer crystal -
line sizes [ 14 ]. It was shown that the HPT-processed Cantor alloy has 
high hardness and yield strength [ 15 – 22 ], although there have been few 
attempts to examine the hydrogen embrittlement resistance of these 
UFG Cantor alloys. The formation of UFG structures can have different 
effects on hydrogen embrittlement resistance. The grain boundaries may

For example, it was shown that palladium exhibits improved hydrogen

elastic region, despite their effectiveness to enhance the yield strength. 
However, twins and dislocation-based defects with low mobility such as 
Lomer-Cottrell locks and D-Frank partial dislocations are effective to 
achieve a good combination of high yield strengths of over 1 GPa and 
high hydrogen embrittlement resistance with up to 9% elongation. 
2. Experimental procedures 
An equiatomic CrMnFeCoNi alloy was prepared by vacuum induc -
tion melting under an argon atmosphere using pieces of elements with 
purity levels higher than 99.5%. After induction melting, disc-shaped 
samples (10 mm diameter and 1 mm thickness) were cut from the 
ingot using the wire-cut electrical discharge machining (EDM) tech -
nique, homogenized at 1273 K for 1 h and quenched in iced water. 
Examination of homogenized discs by a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM with an acceleration voltage of 15 kV) equipped with electron 
backscattered diffraction (EBSD with a step size of 300 nm) confirmed 
that the discs had coarse grains with an average grain size of ~30 μ m, as 
shown in Fig. 1 (a) and (b). Moreover, energy-dispersive X-ray spec -
troscopy (EDS) confirmed that the distribution of elements was homo -
geneous within the grains with a composition close to the intended 
equiatomic one, as shown in Fig. 1 (c – h).

chanically polished to avoid any possible risk of oxidation caused by the 
EDM and homogenization processes. The HPT process was carried out

( T = 300 K), under an applied pressure of P = 6 GPa for N = 1/16, 1/8, 
1/4, 1/2, 1, 5 and 10 turns with 1 rpm rotation speed. The micro -

3. Results 
3.1. Microhardness evolution with straining 
The results of Vickers microhardness testing, which is used as a 
standard test to monitor the severity of deformation and the occurrence 
of the steady state after HPT processing [ 32 , 33 ], are shown in Fig. 2 . The 
variations of hardness against the distance from the disc center in Fig. 2 
(a) show that the hardness increases with increasing the distance from 
the disc center as well as with increasing the number of HPT turns due to 
an increase in the applied shear strain, as shown in Eq. (1) . Despite these 
variations, the hardness tends to saturate at a level of 520 Hv at a large 
number of turns. The variation of hardness with the applied shear strain 
is shown more clearly in Fig. 2 (b), where hardness increases with 
increasing the shear strain at the early stages of straining but saturates to 
a steady-state level of 520 Hv at large strains. This steady-state level is 
3.5 times higher than the hardness of the initial coarse-grained material 
(150 Hv). The occurrence of the steady state, which is a consequence of 
dynamic recovery, recrystallization and grain-boundary migration [ 33 ], 
has been reported in a wide range of HPT-processed materials [ 30 , 31 ] 
including HEAs [ 34 , 35 ]. 
3.2. Structural evolution with straining 
Fig. 3 shows the XRD patterns of HEA before and after HPT pro -
cessing for various numbers of turns. It is evident that the initial material 
has an FCC structure and its crystal structure does not change by HPT 
processing. The absence of phase transformation in this alloy is consis -
tent with the earlier publications on the application of HPT to the Cantor 
alloy [ 15 – 22 ]. Despite the absence of phase transformations, there is a 
clear peak broadening after HPT processing. The peak broadening, 
which is an indication of lattice strain due to the formation of disloca -
tions or the reduction of crystallite size [ 36 ], is shown more clearly in 
Fig. 4 . The peak broadening is significant at the early stages of straining, 
but it tends to reach a steady state at large strains similar to the 
hardness-strain behavior shown in Fig. 2 (b).

3.4. Hydrogen diffusion with straining 
Fig. 7 shows the TDS spectra and the hydrogen content in three 
representative samples: initial sample and the samples processed by HPT 
for 1/8 and 10 turns. Although the three samples were charged exactly 
under similar electrochemical conditions, the amount of hydrogen is 
different for them. The initial coarse-grained sample absorbs a small 
amount of 3.0 wt.ppm of hydrogen. This is reasonable because the 
diffusion of hydrogen through the lattice of FCC alloys is usually low 
[ 12 ]. After small straining via 1/8 HPT turns, the hydrogen content 
increases to 10.1 wt.ppm. This increase should be due to the formation 
of lattice defects [ 28 ]. With the formation of nanograins after HPT 
processing for 10 turns, the hydrogen content reaches 26.8 wt.ppm 
which is one order of magnitude higher than the hydrogen content in the 
initial coarse-grained materials. Such an increase should be due to the 
contribution of grain boundary diffusion which is faster than lattice 
diffusion and can result in a higher hydrogen content for a given period 
[ 39 ]. A similar effect of grain boundaries was reported in other 
HPT-processed materials [ 24 ]. Although this feature of nanograins is 
considered as a negative effect for hydrogen embrittlement resistance 
[ 26 ], it was positively used to activate hydrogen storage materials for 
easy hydrogen absorption [ 25 ]. Here it should be noted that the increase 
in hydrogen content with increasing strain in Fig. 7 (i.e., increasing N 
from 1/8 to 10) suggests that there should be some hydrogen content 
variations across the samples because strain is not homogenous in 
HPT-processed discs. However, these changes should not be significant 
for tiny tensile specimens with only 0.7 mm width.

4. Discussion 
This study suggests that a good combination of high yield stress and 
hydrogen embrittlement resistance is achievable in HEAs by engineering 
the lattice defects through cold working. However, the significance of 
this strategy depends on the type of lattice defects. While coarse grains 
still show the best resistance to hydrogen embrittlement, their yield 
strength is not high enough for practical applications [ 12 ]. A summary 
of all data presented in the previous section suggests that the 
introduction of twins within coarse grains is an effective strategy to 
increase the yield strength while keeping the hydrogen embrittlement 
low. Twin boundaries are known as effective sites for pinning the 
dislocation mobility to enhance the combination of strength and 
ductility [ 38 , 41 ]. Since in the HELP mechanism, hydrogen-induced 
dislocation motion results in hydrogen embrittlement [ 23 ], twins can 
act effectively to reduce the effect of HELP mechanism. Moreover, it was 
suggested that one reason for the good hydrogen embrittlement resis -
tance of coarse-grained Cantor alloy is the formation of deformation 
twins and their interaction with dislocations during the tensile test [ 10 ]. 
However, twins and their interaction with other lattice defects can 
enhance hydrogen diffusion [ 39 ]. Moreover, twin boundaries can act as 
stress concentration sites for crack initiation and propagation and lead 
to intragranular cracking [ 29 , 40 ]. Despite these negative effects of 
twins, their formation appears to be effective to achieve a good com -
bination of strength and ductility in the presence of hydrogen. 
Dislocation-based defects are effective to enhance the yield stress, 
but it is known that they can limit the work hardening and result in low 
ductility. However, dislocation-based defects with low mobility such as 
Lomer-Cottrell locks and D-Frank partial dislocations appear to be 
effective to pin the hydrogen-induced dislocation motion. Although 
there is limited information about the behavior of these defects in the 
presence of hydrogen, their pinning effect in the absence of hydrogen is 
a well-known fact for several decades [ 37 ]. The current study suggests 
that the introduction of these low-mobility defects is an effective strat -
egy to improve the combination of high yield stress and hydrogen 
embrittlement resistance. The main challenge regarding this strategy is 
finding an appropriate thermomechanical treatment to introduce these 
defects without significant refinement of grain structure.

Introduction of nanograins appears as an inappropriate strategy for 
improving the mechanical properties of the Cantor alloy in the presence 
of hydrogen. The nanograined materials in this study absorb large 
amounts of hydrogen due to fast diffusion through the grain boundaries 
and exhibit brittle fracture in the elastic region after hydrogen charging. 
The absence of any indication of localized plasticity in nanograined 
materials suggests that the large amount of hydrogen absorbed on the 
surface leads to the occurrence of catastrophic hydrogen embrittlement 
due to the HEDE mechanism through weakening the metal-metal 
bonding [ 27 ]. Here, it should be noted that the size of grains plays an 
important role in the behavior of different materials in the presence of 
hydrogen. For example, while the reduction of grain size to the nano -
meter level in this study leads to the fracture in the elastic regions, 
reducing the grain size to the submicrometer level by HPT processing 
does not result in catastrophic failure in many pure metals and con -
ventional alloys [ 24 , 26 , 29 ]. Introduction of a combination of nano -
grains and coarse grains was suggested as an effective strategy for the 
improvement of yield stress of gradient-structured HEAs while main -
taining good ductility in the presence of hydrogen [ 42 ]. As attempted 
earlier for some conventional alloys [ 43 ], future experimental and 
theoretical studies are required to find the optimum grain size and ideal 
distribution of nanograins and coarse grains for the best combination of 
yield stress and hydrogen embrittlement resistance in HEAs. 
5. Conclusions 
To achieve a combination of high yield strength and good hydrogen 
embrittlement resistance, different kinds of lattice defects were intro -
duced in a high entropy alloy CrMnFeCoNi by high-pressure torsion 
processing with different levels of strain. The following conclusions 
were drawn from this study. 
1. The initial coarse-grained material evolved with deformation twin -
ning at the early stages of straining, while dislocation activities 
became dominant with further straining. At very large strains, the 
coarse grains transformed to nanograins with an average steady-state 
crystalline size of 25 nm. 
2. Both hardness and yield strength increased with increasing the strain 
and reached the steady-state levels of 520 Hv and 1900 MPa, 
respectively. However, elongation to failure was reduced with 
straining. 
3. The presence of twins led to high strengths over 1 GPa and elonga -
tions up to 9% in the presence of hydrogen. The introduction of 
dislocation-based defects with low mobility such as Lomer-Cottrell 
locks and D-Frank partial dislocations also increased the strength 
in the presence of hydrogen. The formation of large fractions of 
nanograin boundaries significantly enhanced the yield strength, but 
it led to catastrophic hydrogen embrittlement in the elastic region. 
4. It was suggested that twin boundaries and low-mobility defects such 
as Lomer-Cottrell locks and D-Frank partial dislocations can act as 
pinning sites for hydrogen-induced dislocation motion. This action 
subsequently improves the hydrogen embrittlement resistance by 
suppressing the hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity (HELP) 
mechanism. However, nanograin boundaries act as pathways for 
hydrogen diffusion, resulting in a high hydrogen content and brittle 
intergranular cracking in the material due to the hydrogen-enhanced 
decohesion (HEDE) mechanism.
