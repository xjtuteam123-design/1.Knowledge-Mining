ABSTRACT
Neutron diffraction is a well-established method for the measurement and characterization of internal stresses in
metallic and ceramic materials at room temperature. Some cases have been reported in the literature in which
the method has been extended to higher temperatures. In this paper we report both novel setups for in-situ heat
treatments and for in-situ mechanical testing at high temperature at SALSA in the Institut Laue-Langevin (ILL).
Characterization of internal stresses in Ni-superalloys demonstrated the feasibility, stability and exploitation of
results achieved by a tailored induction heating system. Different coils are adapted to accommodate several
sample geometries for accurate focusing of the magnetic heating induction field on the sample, to achieve high
heating rates up to 50
◦
C/s and therefore limiting the microstructural evolution impact during ramping-up to the
target temperature. In return, this enhances the choice of reference d
0
, by either measurements at room tem-
perature and extrapolated values to high temperature, or by direct measurements also at high temperature. A
detailed strategy of d
0
stress-free reference approaches for such in-situ experiments at high temperature is
discussed.
1. Introduction
Neutron diffraction is a non-destructive method for stress and strain
characterization of metallic and ceramic materials when they have to be
probed in the bulk to depths of up to several milimeters or centimeters,
and under different orientations for a complete strain-stress tensor
analysis [ 1 ]. In addition, neutron diffraction provides unique crystallo-
graphic information, i.e., precise information about phases and their
evolution, which is crucial for the correct interpretation and evaluation
of the material and which cannot be obtained by standard laboratory
methods.
Material certification for industrial applications has to be performed
at the temperature at which the material is intended to be used ( oper-
ando conditions ). The higher the temperature at which the material can
be measured by neutron diffraction with sufficiently short acquisition
times, the larger the applicability of this measurement technique in the
industrial components field. Of course, this comes with the challenge for
the operando characterization of structural materials at high
temperature.
To extend the temperature applications under neutron diffraction
measurements some procedures have been reported. These procedures
are summarized below, classifying them according to whether they refer
to heat treatments, mechanical properties at high temperature, or
fabrication technology.
1.1. Heat treatments
To measure stress relaxation following ageing heat treatment, studies
have mainly been limited to ex situ experiments. This translates into a
sequence of samples measured, each having experienced a different time
of ageing. Two major issues arise with this method. The first is the large
quantity of samples required. The second is sample to sample variability:
lattice spacing is difficult to accurately reproduce in heat treatment
processes such as quenching and ageing with a high degree of accuracy.
The alternative to measuring ex situ is to carry out stress relaxation in
situ. During in situ measurements, time resolution is only limited by the

The accurate determination of the evolution of stress from elastic
strain measurements at different temperatures requires the use of suit-

with a maximum output power of 150 W. With this setup, heating rates
up to 15

C

Wang et al. determined the diffraction and single-crystal elastic

2. Materials and methods
In this paper we report a new setup for mechanical measurements at
the SALSA instrument from RT to about 800
◦
C on materials like those of
the study above. In the procedure, heating is achieved by means of a

The procedure has been used in a series of experiments to measure strain
in AM Waspaloy and IN718 samples during heat treatment and during

4. Discussion
4.1. Residual stress in waspaloy wall in AB condition
For Waspaloy in AB condition ( Fig. 6 ), the centre and front lines have
similar stress distributions, while the side line differs in both, especially
for the normal stress component. This could be explained by the laser
scanning strategy. The laser first deposits the weld beads inside the skin,
then the skin is scanned to create the perimeter of each layer. The
transient thermal history experienced by the perimeter is different from
that experienced by the weld beads inside the skin [ 33 ]. When a depo-
sition track inside the skin is completed, the following track would
partially overlap and reheat the previous track. On the other hand, there
is no reheating effect in the horizontal plane and the heat dissipates
more quickly because the surrounding materials have already cooled.
This directly affects the development and annalealing of residual
stresses, as well as microstructural formation. The side line is located on
the perimeter, whereas the centre and front lines are along the same
laser pass inside the skin, resulting in different stress distributions due to
the thermal responses.
The γ ′ phase precipitated very rapidly in Waspaloy during laser
printing (in the order of seconds). The deposition of the subsequent
upper layers reheated the lower layers, acting as an annealing effect
which induced a considerable amount of γ ′ phase (15 – 20%) [ 34 , 35 ]. The
γ ′ phase has a negative lattice misfit with the matrix in most systems, but
has a positive misfit for Waspaloy [ 36 ], leading to compressive stresses
within the grains when cooled to RT. These compete with the thermal
tensile stresses and result in a compressive residual stress state.
Further efforts shall be done to improve the accuracy of the residual
stress values obtained. One concern relates to the specific DEC. Previous
research has shown that the DEC for correlating lattice strains with
engineering stresses is strongly influenced by the presence of crytallo-
graphic texture [ 9 ]. Due to the lack of DEC data, the values of E and ν
were chosen from cast samples. The other concern is the stress-free
powder reference. The powders are essentially stress-free if the con-
stituent particles are small enough and not constrained to each other. If
the particles were constrained, they could have residual stresses due to
the pressures they would exert on each other, which is not the case in
this study. However, the chemical composition may be different from
that of the component being tested. In the particular case of LMD, the
extremely high temperature in the melt pool could dissolve all the
phases in the original powder, and the subsequent high cooling rates
would result in an out-of-equilibrium microstructure. Therefore, there is
no direct relationship between the phases in the powder and in the
printed sample. Other researchers have used macroscopically stress-free
samples obtained by cutting small cubes from the material of interest or
by cutting the material into a comb-like structure to relieve stress [ 37 ,
38 ]. As the aim was to obtain a complete non-destructive character-
ization and follow-up of the effects of heat treatment on the same
sample, it was not practical to cut small cubes or comb-like patterns from
the actual sample after measurement, as the electrical discharge
machining would sacrifice the sample. So coupons from the same sample

structure via planar dislocation motion due to the plasticity induced
during cyclic loading. This behaviour can also explain the FWHM and
intensity results observed during in-situ tensile loading when the sam-
ples experienced the plastic regime.
