ABSTRACT 
ZAXME11100 (Mg – 1Zn – 1Al-0.5Ca-0.4Mn-0.2Ce, wt.%) is a recently developed sheet alloy with excellent 
formability at room temperature. The yield strength of this alloy is significantly improved by a short 1-h aging 
treatment at 210 
◦
C (T6), from 159 MPa in solution-treated state (T4) to 270 MPa in the T6 condition, with a 
slight reduction in tensile elongation (from 31 % in T4 to 26 % in T6). In this work, precipitation microstructure 
was characterized to explain the aging hardening and high strength-ductility synergy of the new alloy. A high 
density (8 × 10
23
/m
3
) of ordered monolayer Guinier-Preston (GP) zones enriched in Al, Ca and Zn uniformly 
form on the Mg basal planes after T6 treatment. Grain boundary segregation of Al, Ca and Zn is also observed. 
Deformation microstructure of T4 and T6 samples tested to various strain levels was examined. At small strain 
levels, basal and non-basal < a > dislocation slip dominates the deformation process, and cross slip of < a > dis -
locations between basal and non-basal planes is observed in T6 samples. The GP zones are gradually destroyed by 
plastic deformation. Based on those results, the strengthening contribution of ordered GP zones was modeled to 
be 116 MPa. Therefore, formation of GP zones is an effective way to achieve high strength-ductility synergy in 
wrought Mg alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Magnesium (Mg) alloys present a significant opportunity for weight 
savings in transportation applications completing with steel and 
aluminum alloys [ 1 , 2 ]. Most Mg components currently in automotive 
applications are generally produced using casting processes [ 2 , 3 ], while 
wrought Mg alloys are largely hindered by their limited ductility and 
formability at room temperature. The poor ductility of Mg mainly comes 
from the significant differences in critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) 
for various deformation modes. With the lowest CRSS, basal < a > slip is 
the dominant deformation mode of Mg at room temperature [ 4 – 6 ]. 
However, basal slip only has two independent slip systems and cannot 
accommodate deformation along the < c > direction [ 4 ]. Tension twin -
ning with relatively lower CRSS can provide limited plasticity along the 
< c > direction. Since pure Mg has a strong basal texture after plastic 
deformation, one effective method to enhance ductility is to reduce the 
CRSS ratios of basal vs. other slip systems, activating non-basal < a > slip 
and pyramidal < c + a > slip [ 4 , 7 – 9 ]. A recent study showed that acti -
vating pyramidal < c + a > dislocations in later stages of deformation 
( > 10 % strain) can progressively increase local stresses, activating other 
secondary deformation mechanisms and accommodating in -
compatibilities among grains, and enhancing ductility and 
work-hardening [ 10 ]. On the other hand, pure Mg also has low strength. 
Precipitation strengthening via alloying is the most important avenue to 
improve strength [ 11 ]. However, for precipitation-strengthened alloys, 
high strength is generally accompanied by reduced ductility, the 
so-called “ strength-ductility trade-off dilemma ” . 
Recently, extensive research has been reported on improving the 
synergy of strength and ductility/formability in Mg alloys [ 12 , 13 ]. 
Innovative thermo-mechanical processing techniques can produce mi -
crostructures characterized by fine grain sizes and weakened textures, 
enhancing both strength and ductility [ 14 ]. Micro-alloying is effective in 
improving both the strength and ductility of wrought Mg alloys. Small 
additions of rare earth (RE) elements to Mg have proven to weaken its 
texture and, consequently, enhance ductility [ 15 , 16 ]. Furthermore, 
experimental studies indicated that additions of Y can enhance < c + a >
slip [ 17 ], prismatic < a > and pyramidal < a > dislocations [ 18 , 19 ]. Trace 
additions of Ce can effectively improve ductility and stretch formability

Minor additions of non-RE elements such as Ca, Zn, or Al have also 
been shown to improve the ductility of Mg alloys [ 27 – 29 ]. Small addi -

can harden basal slip [ 31 ] and soften prismatic slip [ 32 , 33 ]. Atomistic

basal texture and tilt the peaks of basal texture poles from the normal 
direction (ND) towards the transverse direction (TD), leading to a 
TD-split texture [ 36 , 37 ] which was also observed in Mg – Zn – Ce alloys 
[ 38 ]. Furthermore, Zn and Ca segregations at grain boundaries (GB) will 
reduce their energy and mobility, and thus weaken texture during 
recrystallization [ 39 , 40 ], which benefits ductility. More recent studies 
on Mg-Zn-Ca-Zn-Al [ 41 , 42 ], Mg-Al-Ca-Mn [ 43 ], and Mg – Zn – Al – Ca – Ce 
alloys [ 44 ] showed that short aging treatments can significantly increase 
the yield strength without sacrificing ductility. The enhanced strength 
without an apparent decrease in ductility was attributed to the forma -
tion of chemical clusters or monolayer GP zones [ 41 – 44 ]. Nevertheless, 
a comprehensive understanding of the role of such chemical clusters or 
monolayer GP zones in governing strength and ductility, as well as their 
evolution during plastic deformation, remains elusive. Such under -
standing is crucial for the design for cost-effective and dilute wrought 
Mg alloys to overcome the “ strength-ductility trade-off dilemma ” .

formability for lightweight applications [ 44 ]. From its solution treated

yield strength along with a 15 % reduction in elongation compared to 
the T4 properties [ 44 ]. Atomic resolution STEM imaging coupling with 
APT analysis revealed that the enhanced strength mainly comes from the 
high density of GP zones enriched in Al, Zn, and Ca.

4.2. The effects of GP zones on strength-ductility synergy 
Both T4 and T6 samples of the ZAXME11100 alloy feature fine grain 
structure and weak basal texture, contributing to enhanced ductility. 
The fine grain structure and weak texture are likely because of grain 
boundary segregation of Al, Ca, and Zn. Deformation twinning was not 
seen in the current alloy under tensile loading. Previous study suggested 
that twinning is severely hindered with fine grains in pure Mg [ 51 ]. The 
ratio of average Schmid factors of non-basal vs. basal < a > dislocations is 
about 1.5. Although the theoretical value of CRSS between non-basal vs. 
basal < a > dislocations in the ZAXME11100 alloy is unknown, AZ61 Mg 
alloy showed that the critical ratio which favors the activation of pris -
matic < a > slip is about 1.5 – 2.0 [ 9 ]. Therefore, the texture of the 
ZAXME11100 alloy may also favor non-basal slip, as evidenced in the 
non-basal < a > dislocations in ZAXME11100 alloy, leading to high 
ductility. 
GP zone formation in T6 samples results in increased barriers 
encountered by basal < a > dislocations during slip, compared to T4 
samples. Therefore, the CRSS difference for basal and non-basal slip 
systems in the T6 condition may be reduced, leading to non-basal < a >
dislocations. The GP zone formation may also be contributed to the more 
frequent cross-slip of < a > dislocations in T6 than T4 samples. Moreover, 
recent work suggested that minor additions of Al can increase the CRSS

5. Conclusions 
The microstructures and deformation substructures of a new 
Mg – Zn – Al – Ca – Mn – Ce (ZAXME11100) alloy in the T4 and T6 conditions 
were characterized and quantified to reveal the responsible mechanisms 
for a high strength-ductility synergy, with the following conclusions: 
1. Both the T4 and T6 microstructures feature fine grain sizes and weak 
TD-split basal textures, which enhance ductility. In the T4 condition, 
Al, Ca, and Zn segregations were observed along grain boundaries 
during recrystallization, contributing to fine grain structure and 
weak basal texture. 
2. The T6 aging generates a high number density (8 × 10
23
/m
3
) of 
ordered monolayer GP zones (of Al, Ca, and Zn) with a length of 
about 3.1 nm, formed on the basal planes of Mg matrix. The 
strengthening contribution from GP zones was modeled to be about 
116 MPa, which is close to the experimental result (111 MPa). The 
strengthening modeling also suggests that GP zone strengthening 
due to prismatic < a > slip is higher than that of basal < a > slip. 
3. Dislocation slip is a major deformation mechanism in ZAXME11100 
alloy. Basal and non-basal < a > dislocations dominate early stages of 
deformation process in both T4 and T6 conditions. Additionally, 
more cross-slip of < a > dislocations between basal and non-basal 
planes is observed in T6 than T4 conditions. This difference in 
dislocation slip characteristics may be linked to the GP zones in the 
T6 samples. 
4. The strengthening and work hardening contributions from the or -
dered monolayer GP zones gradually decrease with increasing plastic 
deformation, due to their destruction by plastic deformation. There is 
no obvious difference in deformation behavior between T4 and T6 
samples in late stages of plastic deformation, which explains the 
comparable ductility between T4 and T6 samples.
