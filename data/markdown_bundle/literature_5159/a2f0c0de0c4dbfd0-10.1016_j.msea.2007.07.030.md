Abstract
A single master heat of CMSX-10, a third generation, single crystal Ni-base superalloy was processed with withdrawal rates between 5 and
35 cm/h. The as-cast microstructures and solidiﬁcation partitioning ratios were characterized as a function of the withdrawal rate and used to verify
the thermal gradient during processing. As expected, the primary and secondary dendrite arm spacing decreased with increasing withdrawal rate.
Signiﬁcant differences in the phase transformation temperatures were observed in DTA testing due to the withdrawal rates used to process the
samples. After heat treatment, transformation temperatures, microstructures and tensile and creep properties were determined. Once heat-treated,
the solidus, liquidus and /H9253
′-solvus temperatures did not appear to be a function of withdrawal rate. Similarly, mechanical testing of heat-treated
samples also indicated that the tensile and creep properties were not a function of the withdrawal rate.

1. Introduction
Dramatic improvements in the performance of gas turbines
have resulted from the development of directional solidiﬁca-
tion and the production of single crystal turbine blades and
vanes [1,2]. The single crystal processing technique was origi-
nally developed for the production of smaller aerospace turbine
blades and vanes for increased performance. However, there
is also great interest in producing much larger single crystal
components for industrial gas turbines (IGT). Due to the much
larger size of the IGT components, maintaining the required ther-
mal gradient becomes much more difﬁcult and the incidence of
defects increases signiﬁcantly. In addition, the as-cast structure
can exhibit extensive amounts of segregation, including the pres-
ence of eutectic /H9253//H9253
′, which must be reduced or eliminated by
solution heat treatment to produce a material with the optimal
balance of properties [3,4].
As new alloys have been developed, the amount of refractory
metal alloy additions have continued to rise in the superalloys.
In particular, the W, Re and Ta levels have increased steadily

2. Materials and experimental procedures
2.1. Casting
Single crystals of the third generation alloy, CMSX-10 were
produced at ﬁve withdrawal rates: 5, 15, 20, 25 and 35 cm/h.
Note that the 20 cm/h withdrawal rate was considered the base-
line condition, since previous work had utilized this condition
[8]. All of the single crystal samples examined in this study are
from the same master alloy heat (Table 1) procured from Cannon
Muskegon (Muskegon, MI). Single crystals of the alloys were
produced in an investment casting cluster mold with 20 bars
(12.7 mm in diameter and 200 mm long) at PCC Airfoils (Min-
erva, OH). One of the bars was used for chemical analysis and
the remaining 19 bars were used to examine the microstruc-
ture and properties. The single crystal samples were cast in the
⟨001 ⟩direction using a helical selector in a temperature gra-
dient of approximately 15–20
◦C/cm with the baseline 20 cm/h
withdrawal rate. The withdrawal rate through the helical sin-
gle crystal selector was maintained at 15 cm/h for all samples.
Once past the helical selector, the withdrawal rate was changed
to one of the ﬁve withdrawal rates used for the solidiﬁcation of
the single crystals. The orientation of each bar was determined
by Laue back-reﬂection techniques. Only samples with orien-
tations within 5
◦ of ⟨001 ⟩and without defects, such as slivers,
freckles, low angle boundaries or high angle boundaries, were
used in this investigation.
2.2. Heat treatment
The microstructures of the samples were characterized using
2.5 cm long samples sectioned from the bars by a diamond
cut-off wheel. These samples were utilized to examine the
microstructure of the as-cast and solution heat-treated samples.
A modiﬁed solution heat treatment ( Table 2), that has been
shown to result in improved homogeneity and mechanical prop-
erties in CSMX-10 [9], was used in this study. All solution
heat treatments were performed in a laboratory vacuum furnace
operating at a maximum pressure of 1 × 10
−2 Pa. All samples
were cooled from the last heat treatment step by helium quench,
which produced an initial cooling rate of 250
◦C/min. These
rapid cooling rates were used to prevent the growth of the/H9253′that
forms during cooling. The temperatures were measured by two
Pt/Pt-10Rh thermocouples and were maintained within ±3
◦C
throughout the heat treatments.
All samples were given the same three-step aging heat treat-
ment (1150 ◦C/4 h/AC + 870◦C/24 h/AC + 760◦C/30 h/AC,
where AC: air cool) in air to produce coarse, sometimes
referred to primary,/H9253

secondary, /H9253′(10–100 nm) in the /H9253channels. The temperatures
were measured by two Chromel/Alumel thermocouples and
were maintained within±10

The microstructure of the as-cast and heat-treated samples
were examined after etching with the Pratt and Whitney Etch #17
(100 ml H

The /H9253//H9253

of un-etched metallographic samples. At least ﬁve ﬁelds of view

The creep samples were tested in constant load conditions

Differential thermal analysis was performed in a DuPont

and /H9253

alloys [14], cylindrical samples with a minimum size of 250 mg
(approximately 4 mm diameter × 4 mm) were used. Data was
acquired only on-heating to better characterize the effect of the
thermal processing and to avoid the effects of supercooling. All
samples were heated at a constant 20
◦C/min rate and duplicate
samples were evaluated for each condition.

The compositions of the dendritic and interdendritic regions
were determined in the electron microprobe, as a function of the
withdrawal rate. In comparison to the bulk alloy composition,
the dendrite cores of all samples were signiﬁcantly enriched
in W and Re and depleted in Ta, Al and Ti ( Table 4). Con-
versely, the interdendritic region of the as-cast microstructure
was enriched in Ta, Al and Ti, while lower levels of W and Re
were observed. The eutectic /H9253//H9253
′regions were further enriched
in Ta, Al and Ti, and depleted in W and Re. The partitioning coef-
ﬁcients (k
′) of these elements are also presented inTable 5. Note
that the elements which segregated to the dendrite cores dur-
ing solidiﬁcation have partitioning elements greater than unity.
The greater the partitioning coefﬁcient, the greater the degree
of segregation to the dendrite core. For a partitioning coefﬁ-
cient less than unity, the element segregated to the liquid during
solidiﬁcation ultimately solidifying in the interdendritic region.
The smaller the partitioning coefﬁcient, the greater the degree
of segregation during solidiﬁcation to the interdendritic region.
In general, the degree of segregation decreased with decreasing
withdrawal rates. Elements that segregated to the interdendritic
region (i.e., Ta, Al and Ti) exhibited a greater degree of segre-
gation with increasing withdrawal rate ( Fig. 2a). Similarly, the
elements that segregated to the dendrite core (e.g., Re and W),
also exhibited a greater tendency to partition to the dendrite core
with increased withdrawal rate ( Fig. 2b). Elements that exhib-
ited partitioning coefﬁcients close to unity (for example, Co, Cr
and Mo) did not exhibit any signiﬁcant tendency to segregate
to either the dendrite core or the interdendritic regions during
solidiﬁcation ( Fig. 2c). In the case of Cr and Mo, there was a
switch in partitioning as the withdrawal rate was increased. At
slow withdrawal rates, the Cr and Mo partitioned to the inter-
dendritic regions; whereas, partitioning to the dendrite core was
observed in the more rapid withdrawal rates. However, in all
cases, the partitioning ratios were calculated to be close to unity
so the degree of partitioning of these elements was rather limited.
These results are similar to the partitioning coefﬁcients reported
for the model, third-generation single crystal alloy, SX-1 [15]
and to previous reports on CMSX-10 [8].

The tensile properties (Fig. 3) of the heat-treated samples did
not exhibit any signiﬁcant trends with respect to the withdrawal
rate. Tensile tests were performed on samples from each condi-
tion at 25, 600, 850, 950 and 1100

the differences between the minimum and maximum strengths
were limited.
Averaging across all of the withdrawal rates, yield strength
and ultimate tensile strength increased from 799 and 927 MPa,
respectively, at room temperature to 967 and 1013 MPa at

Although some variations in the strength and ductility were
observed in the tensile results, no consistent trends were
observed. The differences between the maximum and minimum
strengths were generally quite small and could be considered
to be within experimental error. In addition, the minimum
and maximum strengths that were observed were not consis-
tently observed at any one withdrawal rate. The withdrawal
rate that exhibited the maximum/minimum yield stress did not
always coincide with processing conditions that exhibited the
maximum/minimum UTS. Similarly, although higher strengths
usually would be expected to result in lower ductilities, the sam-
ples with the maximum strengths did not always exhibit the
minimum ductilities. Therefore, no signiﬁcant trends regarding
the tensile properties were observed in the samples tested in this
study.

4. Discussion
Defect control is critical during the processing of large single
crystal airfoils for IGT and proven to be quite difﬁcult. With-
drawal rate (R) and thermal gradient ( G) are two solidiﬁcation
parameters that signiﬁcantly affect the as-cast microstructure
and the formation of a variety of solidiﬁcation defects. Varying
the solidiﬁcation rate ( G × R) changes the primary and sec-
ondary dendrite arm spacing (PDAS and SDAS, respectively)
and affects chemical segregation, as well. Higher withdrawal
rates yielded lower PDAS and SDAS values, but increased the
degree of segregation for some dendritic and interdendritic parti-
tioning elements. That is to say values of partitioning coefﬁcient,
k
′, greater than one increased with faster withdrawal rates, while
values ofk′less than one decreased with faster withdrawal rates.
More speciﬁcally, the partitioning ratio of Mo and Re increased
substantially and the partitioning ratio for Ta and Ti decreased
substantially. These changes mark increases in the degree of
segregation during solidiﬁcation. Elements that were not signif-
icantly affected by the change in withdrawal rate were Ni, Cr,
Co, W, and Al.
These changes in partitioning may also affect the heat treat-
ment response of the alloy. Greater enrichment of the dendrite
core of Re and Mo, with increasing withdrawal rate, while
slightly increasing the driving force for diffusion, may increase
the heat treatment time required to achieve complete homoge-
nization. However, the higher withdrawal rates will also shorten
the diffusion distances since both the PDAS/SDAS are decreased
with increasing withdrawal rates. Therefore, the withdrawal
rate would not be expected to have a signiﬁcant effect on the
homogenization behavior of the alloy. This is critical as mod-
ern alloy heat treatment requirements are now controlled more
by a need to eliminate segregation than a need to dissolve the
/H9253//H9253
′eutectic formed during solidiﬁcation. Increased refractory
element content, particularly W and Re, slow the diffusion rates
of the elements in the alloys. This behavior is beneﬁcial for
long-term stability and high-temperature performance; however,
long heat treatments are required to overcome this resistance to
diffusion.
In an effort to reduce costs, homogenization heat treatments
are often shortened to only dissolve the /H9253//H9253
′eutectic with the
elimination/reduction of the remaining solidiﬁcation segrega-
tion given less importance. This approach leads to alloys that
will not perform to their full potential. Previous research into
withdrawal rate effects appears to utilize heat treatments that
may not result in complete homogenization the alloys[7]. In this
condition, withdrawal rate can, and does, affect the mechanical
behavior of the alloys. Higher withdrawal rates, which results
in dendrites that are enriched in the solid solution strength-
ening elements W and Re, could produce ﬁner and stronger
dendritic regions which may exhibit greater strength. There-
fore, in partially homogenized alloys, faster withdrawal rates
might be beneﬁcial. In a fully homogenized alloy, however, this
may not be the case. Such a homogenization heat treatment
should leave the microstructure with uniform composition and
uniform precipitate size and morphology; resulting in more uni-
form properties throughout the sample. Therefore, within a fully
homogenized material, no regions will be stronger or weaker
than any other regions. The effects of the withdrawal rate will
then have been essentially removed.
The elimination of the solidiﬁcation structure by heat treat-
ment can be seen in the mechanical testing data presented in
this study. After the full solution heat treatment, no signiﬁ-
cant trends could be seen in the creep and tensile testing. In
some conditions, the faster withdrawal rates performed worse
than the slower rates. Overall, however, there is not much dif-
ference in the strength of the alloy between the ﬁve different
withdrawal rates. A similar behavior can also be seen in the
DTA data. The as-cast samples exhibited solidus and liquidus
temperatures that were a function of withdrawal rate. For exam-
ple, the highest solidus temperature resulted from the fastest
withdrawal rate. Once the material was heat treated, however,
there was no discernable difference in the samples. This again
supports the idea that a complete homogenization heat treatment
has the effect of signiﬁcantly reducing the effects of withdrawal
rate during solidiﬁcation while producing a better, more uniform
alloy.
It should be noted also that the properties evaluated in this
study are less inﬂuenced by the presence of defects, such as
porosity, than, for example, fatigue. It has been shown in previ-
ous studies that higher withdrawal rates result in a reduction in
the number of solidiﬁcation defects, such as porosity [7].H o w -
ever, higher gradients during processing have also been shown
to decrease the solidiﬁcation defects and increase the tensile
and creep strength of several Ni-base superalloys. In the present
study, higher withdrawal rates were also found to result in a
reduced number of solidiﬁcation defects, particularly the for-
mation of slivers and freckles. When the material was fully heat
treated, though, the withdrawal rate, and therefore, the solidi-
ﬁcation gradient, had no signiﬁcant impact on the mechanical
properties examined in this study.
One ﬁnal consideration involves the microstructural stability
of the alloy regarding TCP phases. Previous work has shown
that enrichment of W, Re, and Cr in the dendrite cores can lead
to a reduction in long-term stability[17,19,20]. Even alloys that
are compositionally balanced to avoid the precipitation of these
deleterious phases can produce localized TCP phases if seg-
regation is not eliminated [15]. It was shown that increasing
withdrawal rate, while decreasing PDAS and SDAS, will actu-
ally increase the degree of partitioning for Re, Mo, and to a lesser
extent Cr. If these alloys are used with partially homogenizing
heat treatments, faster withdrawal rates will reduce the DAS but
may increase the tendency to produce TCP phases during long-
term elevated temperature exposures, such as during creep tests
or during actual service exposures.

5. Summary
Based on the results of this study, varying the withdrawal rate
during solidiﬁcation will not signiﬁcantly affect the mechanical
properties of the fully heat-treated alloy. Increasing the with-
drawal rate does reduce the number of defects in a casting.
This was found to be particularly true for slivers and freck-
les.
The key to achieving the best properties for CMSX-10 lies in
the choice of solution/homogenization heat treatment. A partial
homogenization heat treatment may allow for differences as a
result of varying withdrawal rate. The ﬁner structure produced
by faster withdrawal rates can lead to increased mechanical
properties and higher solidus and liquidus temperatures. The
retained segregation, however, might lead to a greater tendency
to produce TCP phases, even in alloys compositionally balanced
to prevent TCP formation. A complete homogenization, how-
ever, can eliminate these differences and produce an alloy that is
uniform in composition and properties throughout. Therefore,
increasing withdrawal rate does not offer an improvement in
properties of fully heat-treated CMSX-10.
