1. Introduction
Additive manufacturing, in particular pow-
der bed fusion – laser beam (PBF-LB),
creates ﬁne microstructures with segrega-
tions that vary from the dendritic core to
the inter-dendritic region. This nonequilib-
rium microstructure, especially for γ 0-
strengthened Ni-base superalloys, must
undergo solution and aging treatments to
achieve the required grain structure and
desired phases. The heat treatments used
for PBF-LB superalloys are often similar
to their cast counterparts. However,
it has been shown for a number of super-
alloys that tailored heat treatments are
required to achieve good high-temperature
properties, such as creep. [1– 3] The solution
heat treatment for PBF-LB components
is an acceptable way of removing crystallo-
graphic anisotropy through recrystalliza-
tion and grain growth. However, it is
not uncommon that most of the Ni-base
superalloys still have some degree of anisotropy even after solu-
tion heat treatments. This is minimized by performing solution
heat treatments above the γ 0 solvus temperature for γ 0-strength-
ened superalloys. Additionally, performing a combined solution
heat treatment and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) in one cycle is of
high interest to minimize defects (cracks and pores).
Recently, high γ 0-strengthened Ni-base superalloys manufac-
tured through PBF-LB have garnered attention due to their high
temperature capabilities. Therefore, their processability, heat
treatment response, microstructure evolution, and high-temper-
ature properties are of high interest. The evolution of microstruc-
tures for different superalloys has been documented, but
emphasis is placed here on CM247LC ( γ 0 /C25 60% to 70% vol%).
Divya et al. [4] studied the microstructures of as-built and fully
heat-treated CM247LC with emphasis on grain morphology, γ 0
precipitation, and carbides. A follow-up investigation by
Mu˜noz-Moreno[5] studied the impact of different solution heat
treatments (1210 to 1260 °C) on CM247LC, with the higher tem-
peratures leading to better recrystallization and uniform γ 0 pre-
cipitation. Markanday et al. [6] studied the evolution of CM247LC
through the heat treatment with a focus on the carbides, but
showed similar ﬁndings to the previous studies. Xu et al. [7]

Superalloys manufactured through powder bed fusion – laser beam (PBF-LB) are
known to suffer from poor creep performance due to their ﬁne and anisotropic
microstructures and unoptimized heat treatments. This study investigates the
impact of a combined hot isostatic pressing (HIP) and solution heat treatment on
the microstructure and creep behavior of the non-weldable Ni-base superalloy
CM247LC manufactured via PBF-LB. HIP treatments were conducted at 1250 °C
and 1280 °C, and 200 MPa pressure to optimize grain structure and obtain
favorable grain boundary decorations. Microstructural characterization revealed
that HIP at 1280 °C resulted in a more homogenous grain size distribution with a
higher fraction of grains between 100 /C0 200 μm, indicative of enhanced recrys-
tallization and grain growth. Subsequent creep testing demonstrated a 23%
improvement in creep life for the HIP at 1280 °C. This enhanced performance is
attributed to a combination of factors, including improved γ 0 solutioning and
beneﬁcial grain boundary decoration (M 2B borides). These ﬁndings highlight the
critical role of tailored heat treatments, particularly HIP parameters, in optimizing
the microstructure and consequently the high-temperature creep resistance of
PBF-LB-processed superalloys.

2. Experimental Section
Gas atomized CM247LC powder provided by Högan ¨as AB
(Högan¨as, Sweden) was used as the feedstock material. The
composition of the powder is shown in Table 1 .T h es a m p l e s
were printed in an EOS M290 machine (EOS GmbH,
Krailling, Germany). This machine is equipped with a 400 W
ytterbium (Yb) ﬁber laser with a spot size of about 100 μm.
The laser power ( P), speed ( v), hatch distance ( h), and layer
thickness ( t) were 125 W, 1200 mm/s, 0.05 mm, and
0.03 mm, respectively. These are optimized parameters based
on a previous study by the authors. [8] A stripe scan strategy with
a stripe width of 5 mm and a stripe overlap of 0.12 mm with
interlayer scan rotation of 67° was employed. The samples were
removed from the build plate using wire-electric discharge
machining.
The samples underwent a combined HIP and solution heat
treatment (hereafter referred to as HIPed) with a pre-pressuri-
zation pressure of 200 MPa in a Quintus QIH21 URC HIP
vessel with a molybdenum furnace at Quintus AB (V ¨asterås,
Sweden). The reason for choosing 200 MPa was that it was
found to minimize strain-age cracking, as shown by Fardan
et al. [11] Two batches of HIP processing were done, where
the former HIP was done at 1250 °C for 4 h and the latter
HIP was done at 1280 °C for 4 h. The heating and cooling rates
for the HIP were /C25 15 and /C25 250 °C min/C0 1, respectively. The
samples then underwent a single-step aging (hereafter referred
to as HIPed þ aged) in a TAV H4-S type industrial vacuum
furnace (TAV Vacuum Furnaces SPA, Caravaggio BG, Italy)
at 871 °C for 20 h.
The HIPed þ aged samples of dimensions ϕ 15 mm and
length of 83 mm were machined to ﬁt the creep sample
requirements. The creep tests were performed as dead weight
creep tests on a Bofors test frame equipped with a purpose-built
furnace from Entech (Entech Energiteknik AB, Ängelholm,
Sweden). The creep tests were carried out at 871 °C and
380 MPa, and the creep strains were measured manually at ﬁxed
time increments. The testing is carried out in accordance with
International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standard
(ISO 204:2018). The creep results were compared on a
Larson– Miller Parameter (LMP) plot with conventionally cast
MAR-M247 alloy. LMP is given by ( T þ 273)*(20 þ log10(t))/
1000, where T is the temperature in °C and t is the creep life
in hours.
The cross section parallel to the build direction (BD) was used
for microstructural characterization. The cross sections were cut
using an abrasive disc on a Buehler IsoMet High Speed Pro pre-
cision cutter. This was followed by hot mounting cross sections
in a conductive bakelite resin. The mounted samples were then
followed by successive grinding steps using SiC papers with 320,
500, 800, 1200, 2000, and 4000 grit sizes on a Struers Tegrapol.
This was followed by polishing using a diamond suspension of
3 μm on a taffeta woven wool surface (MD-Mol by Struers)
and then a 1 μm polishing. Finally, 0.05 μm colloidal silica polish-
ing was done to remove any deformation caused by previous
grinding/polishing steps.
The crack density measurements were performed only for
the as-built condition, as cracks were not observed in HIPed
samples. A stitched image ( /C25 9.5 mm /C2 9.5 mm) at magni ﬁca-
tion of 100X along the BD was analyzed in the Fiji ImageJ
software.[12] The region of interest was cropped and binarized,
which was followed by using the ‘Analyze Particle ’ function was
applied to exclude border defects and particles smaller than 20
pixels. A circularity between 0 and 0.3 was employed to segment
the cracks. The individual crack length is acquired by obtaining
the length of the major axis of the ﬁtted ellipse. The sum of the
individual crack length (in mm) divided by the analyzed cross-
sectional area (in mm 2) gives the crack density (in mm/mm /C0 2).
The microstructural characterization was performed using a
Zeiss Gemini 450 Scanning Electron miroscopy (SEM). The
imaging of the γ 0 precipitates was performed at an accelerating
voltage of 2.5 to 3 kV with a current of 500 pA and a working
distance of 5 mm on unetched samples. A combination of sec-
ondary electrons (SE), backscattered electrons (BSE), and in-lens
detectors was used for imaging. The reason for acquiring images
of the same location with different detectors was to study the
phases, deformation (slip bands), and creep voids simulta-
neously. Additionally, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) were also per-
formed to obtain chemical and crystallographic information,
respectively.
The analysis of the EBSD data was performed in the open-
source MTEX toolbox (version 5.9.0) [13] in MATLAB. Owing
to the presence of columnar grains, ellipse ﬁtting was per-
formed to perform grain size statistics. The ﬁtted ellipse major
diameter and the aspect ratio (obtained by the ratio of the ellipse
major and minor diameters) were used for comparison
purposes.
Finally, the equilibrium phase diagrams for CM247LC were
obtained in Thermo-Calc 2025a using the TCNI12 database.

3. Results
3.1. As-Built Microstructure
The as-built microstructure, as shown in Figure 1 (a)-1, exhibits
a pronounced columnar grain structure oriented along the crys-
tallographic direction, aligned with the BD. This preferential
orientation is evident in the (100) pole ﬁgure (Figure 1(a)-4),
which displays a strong intensity peak along the BD, character-
istic of fcc-type alloys processed using powder bed fusion – laser
beam (PBF-LB). [4,5]
Grain size analysis, represented by ﬁtted ellipse major
diameters (Figure 1(a)-2), reveals that 62% of the grains fall
within the 5 – 100 μm range, while 18% of the grains are between
100– 150 μm, with some larger grains reaching up to 300 μm. The
aspect ratio, de ﬁned as the ratio of the ﬁtted major to minor
ellipse diameters ( ﬁgure 1(a)-3), indicates that 60% of the grains
exhibit aspect ratios between 2 and 7, while the remaining grains
have aspect ratios up to 18. This data suggests a strong tendency
toward the formation of ﬁne, elongated columnar grains, likely
driven by epitaxial growth facilitated by the steep thermal
gradients inherent to the PBF-LB process. [6,7]
A closer examination of the microstructure (Figure 1(b)-1)
reveals the presence of primary dendrites. The observed contrast
within the grains and dendrites in Figure1(b)-1 arises from
variations in crystallographic orientation. The contrast observed
in the interdendritic regions in Figure 1(b)-2 is primarily
attributed to atomic number contrast (Z-contrast). The
bright particles within the interdendritic regions have been
well-documented as primary MC-type carbides, which form
during the solidi ﬁcation process in PBF-LB. [6,14] These MC car-
bides were found to be enriched in Hf, Ta, W, and Ti, consistent
with previous reports.[15,16] The MC carbide size was measured to
be 28 /C6 18 nm (Table 2). No other phases, such as γ 0 precipitates
or borides, were observed in the as-built condition. It is notewor-
thy that the as-built microstructure exhibited solidi ﬁcation cracks
(crack density of 0.19 mm mm /C0 2), which were closed during the
subsequent HIP process. This phenomenon has been previously
reported for CM247LC [8] and other crack-susceptible alloys. [17,18]
3.2. HIPed Microstructure
Subsequent to HIP, the microstructure underwent signi ﬁcant
modiﬁcations ( Figure 2 a– 1 & (b)-1). Recrystallization and grain
growth resulted in a coarser grain structure, with the average
grain size increasing from /C25 50 to 150 μm. This was accompa-
nied by a reduction in texture intensity, as evidenced by a more
random distribution of orientations in the pole ﬁgures. Pole ﬁg-
ure analysis con ﬁrmed the absence of a strong preferred orien-
tation in the HIPed samples, exhibiting a lower maximum
intensity compared to the as-built microstructure. These micro-
structural changes suggest a more homogeneous and nearly iso-
tropic grain structure after HIP.

Analysis of the ﬁtted ellipse major diameters (Figure 2a – 2&
(b)-2) revealed a shift in the grain size distribution toward higher
values, with a majority of grains now residing within the
100/C0 200 μm range. A notable difference in grain size distribu-
tion was observed between the two HIP conditions. The 1280 °C
HIP condition yielded coarser grains compared to the 1250 °C
condition, with 60% of the grains falling within the
100/C0 200 μm range for 1280 °C compared to 45% for 1250 °C.
This suggests that the higher HIP temperature was more effec-
tive in promoting grain growth, which can be bene ﬁcial for
enhancing high-temperature creep performance.
Regarding grain aspect ratios, the majority of grains in both
HIPed microstructures exhibited aspect ratios between 2 and 4
(compared to 2 to 10 in the as-built condition), indicating a sub-
stantial reduction in anisotropy. No signi ﬁcant difference in
grain aspect ratio distribution was observed between the two
HIP strategies.
Comparison of the microstructural constituents between the
as-built (Figure 1b – 1 & (b)-2) and HIP samples ( Figure 3 )
revealed the presence of MC carbides in both conditions.
However, a signi ﬁcant coarsening of MC carbides was observed
after HIP, with the average value three times that of the as-built
(Table 2). In the as-built microstructure, the MC carbide size dis-
tribution was relatively narrow, with 25% /C0 75% of the particles
falling within the 10 /C0 30 nm range, with some of the carbides up
to 150 nm in size. In contrast, the HIPed samples exhibited a
broader size distribution, with 25% /C0 75% of the MC carbides
ranging from 40 to 125 nm, and some precipitates exceeding
300 nm, as observed within the grain boundary in Figure 3d.
EDS line scans across MC carbides ( Figure 4a,b) conﬁrmed their
enrichment in Hf, Ta, and W, with a minor presence of B. The
presence of B suggests the formation of borocarbides, consistent
with previous studies on as-built materials [15,16] that documented
B enrichment in MC carbides using atom probe tomography
(APT). These carbides were observed to reside within the prior
interdendritic regions, evident in Figure3a,b, where they are
aligned along the BD. There were no signi ﬁcant differences in
the γ 0 precipitate size and morphology ( Table 3), which was mea-
sured to be /C25 270 /C6 60 nm in both conditions.

4. Discussion
This study demonstrates an improvement in the creep perfor-
mance by performing solution heat treatment at higher temper-
atures for CM247LC. The microstructural evolution from the as-
built through solution heat treatment and aging, combined with
post-mortem microstructural evaluation, unravels the reason for
this improvement. This section further discusses the results in
conjunction with the literature.
CM247LC, which is a modi ﬁed version of MAR-M247 alloy, is
well known to be used in the hot sections of the gas turbine and is
manufactured using conventional casting or directional solidi ﬁ-
cation casting.[19] The as-cast samples generally undergo solution
heat treatments to dissolve the γ 0 precipitates in the γ matrix, to
minimize segregation from the non-equilibrium solidi ﬁcation
process and to maximize the incipient melting temperature. [20,21]
The following aging heat treatment allows to have a controlled
precipitation of the γ 0 precipitates and other grain boundary
phases, such as borides, which is crucial for high temperature
performance.[20,22] The solution heat treatment for cast
CM247LC/MAR-M247 alloys in earlier works has generally been
in the range of 1230 to 1250 °C [19,23] with some recent works
reporting up to 1270 °C.[24] The primary limitation for higher solu-
tion temperatures has been incipient melting, which was a prob-
lem for MAR-M247, which has been alleviated with the

PBF-LB process.[2,7,8,25] For this reason, there is a need to perform
solution heat treatments at higher temperatures for these alloys.
A number of studies on PBF-LB superalloys have reported
recrystallization, grain growth, and possibly anisotropy reduction
when solution heat treated at higher temperatures. [2,25– 27]
Additionally, there have also been studies on CM247LC [5– 7] that
have shown recrystallized and co mparatively larger grains when
the solution heat treatment temperature is between 1240 and
1260 °C. This agrees with our ﬁnding where recrystallization has
occurred when samples were solution heat treated (HIP) at
1250 °C, however, a crucialﬁnding is that doing solution heat treat-
ments at higher temperatures leads to more pronounced grain
growth. This shows that CM247LC manufactured through PBF-
LB can be heat treated at higher temperatures than their cast coun-
terparts. This is due to the differences in segregation arising from
the two processes. Casting, when compared to PBF-LB, has slower
cooling rates and hence severe se gregations that cause incipient
melting at lower solution temperatures than PBF-LB. This signiﬁes
the importance of revisiting and tailoring the solution heat treat-
ments of cast superalloys produced through PBF-LB.

Apart from the changes in γ 0 precipitates, the presence and dis-
tribution of grain boundary borides (rich in W, Cr, and Mo) sig-
niﬁcantly in ﬂuence the creep performance of CM247LC. As
observed in this study and supported by high-resolution character-
ization in boron-containing superalloys, [28] borides are predomi-
nantly located at the grain boundaries. The presence of these
boride particles can contribute to enhanced creep resistance
through several mechanisms. Firstly, boron additions are known
to inﬂuence the formation and morphology of other grain bound-
ary phases, potentially reducing the agglomeration of embrittling
phases like M 23C6, thus promoting better grain boundary cohe-
sion under creep stress. [28] Secondly, the borides themselves act
as pinning points, impeding grain boundary sliding, a critical
creep deformation mechanism at elevated temperatures. A ﬁner
and more continuous distribution of these boride particles is gen-
erally considered more effective in hindering grain boundary
movement compared to coarser, discontinuous borides. While
the precise mechanism by which the observed boride distribution
contributed to the improved creep life in this study requires fur-
ther detailed investigation, it is plausible that the morphology and
distribution of these grain boundary borides played a signi ﬁcant
role in retarding creep damage accumulation.
It is clear from the previous studies and from Thermo-Calc
simulations (Figure 9b) that the carbides are of MC-type. The
observed increase in size of the MC carbides after the HIP is
due to the thermodynamic driving force of Ostwald ripening.
The rapid solidi ﬁcation inherent to the PBF-LB process results
in elements like Hf, Ta, W, Ti, and C to segregate to the inter-
dendritic region. [4,6] These ﬁne nano-scale carbides have a
relatively high surface area to volume ratio, resulting in their
lower thermodynamic stability. During subsequent heat
4treatment, the diffusion rates of the atoms are signi ﬁcantly
enhanced. This enhanced atom mobility allows for Ostwald rip-
ening, namely the dissolution of some of the smaller MC carbides
and diffusion of the constituent elements (Hf, Ta, Ti, W, and C) to
the larger carbides. [15] Consequently, the ﬁne and dispersed MC
carbide in as-built condition coarsens after the HIP, leading to a
decrease in number density and an increase in their average size.
It is to be noted that there were still ﬁne carbides in HIPed
samples (/C25 40 nm), suggesting that there is generally coarsening,
but ﬁne carbides were still present. These ﬁne carbides were
often found in the interior of the grains with coarser carbides
near the grain boundaries, suggesting that the grain boundaries
are pathways for faster diffusion. The average size for 1280 °C
HIP was larger than the 1250 °C HIP, which according to the
above reasoning makes it clear that higher HIP temperatures
lead to increased diffusion and hence coarser MC carbides.

5. Conclusions
This work focused on the impact of solution heat treatment com-
bined with HIP on the microstructure and creep performance of
a non-weldable Ni-base superalloy CM247LC. The main ﬁndings
can be summarized as follows: 1) Samples HIPed at 1280 °C had
coarser grains than the 1250 °C sample. There was a higher
fraction of grains between the sizes of 100 to 200 μm for the
1280 °C sample; 2) HIPed samples had MC-type carbides that
had coarsened from the as-built state. These carbides were
located in their prior locations before HIPing. Additionally, there
