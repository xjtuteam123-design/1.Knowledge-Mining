ABSTRACT 
Additive Manufacturing (AM) of high temperature refractory metals, including Nb-based alloys, can overcome 
the challenges associated with their processing using the conventional methods. In this study, pure Nb and a 
blend of Nb with 2.5 wt% WC was processed with Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) starting with a full opti -
misation cycle that explored the impact of the laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, and layer thickness on the 
build integrity. Results show that both materials have good processability, as confirmed by the lack of cracking 
and other defects once the parameters are optimised. Nevertheless, the process parameters affected the materials 
in different ways. Increasing both the scan speed and laser power resulted in hardness increase in pure Nb. 
However, this was not seen in the case of the Nb + 2.5 wt% WC powder mixture. In fact, the hardness was on 
average ~ 15% lower in the Nb + WC builds compared with pure Nb builds. This observation was elucidated 
through studying the effect of the process on the oxygen pick-up in pure Nb and the dissociation of the carbide in 
the Nb + WC mixture. Although the hardening effect is expected due to the full solubility of W in Nb or due to the 
formation of carbides, results indicate that oxygen pick-up has a greater impact on hardness. 
1. Introduction 
The need for structural materials suitable for aerospace applications 
moved the attention to the refractory metals, specially to Nb-based al -
loys, a move that started at the end of 1960s [ 1 , 2 ], encouraged by their 
high-temperature strength, good workability, and lower density when 
compared to Mo, W and Ta. Refractory metals are usually manufactured 
using arc melting, powder metallurgy, spark plasma sintering, chemical 
vapor deposition, and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) [ 3 , 4 ]. However, 
mainly due to the high melting point of refractory metals, 
manufacturing components with complex internal and external struc -
tures using these previously mentioned technologies is challenging. 
Processing refractory metals by AM, particularly using LPBF might be a 
competitive approach to produce components with high design 
complexity in a resource-efficient way [ 5 ]. 
LPBF of pure refractory metals has been explored by many studies in 
recent years. These investigations explored the impact of the intrinsic 
properties of refractory metals, which contributed to the formation of 
defects, particularly cracking that is driven by their high brittle-to- 
ductile transition temperature (DBTT) and high oxygen affinity. This 
is beyond the process-induced defects (e.g. balling, lack of fusion, etc.), 
which may occur due to the high melting point of refractory metals. To 
address this challenge, a careful control of the process parameters is 
mandatory [ 6 ]. Some of the challenging properties include the high 
melting point (T

2. Material and method 
Pure Nb and Nb + WC cubes were built to assess the impact of 
processing parameters starting from elemental Nb powder with irregular 
morphology (supplied by Elite Material), which was blended with fine 
WC particles to form the Nb + 2.5 wt% WC powder mixture, Fig. 1 (a) 
and (b) respectively. Similar amounts have been used in previous ex -
periments. Furthermore, it is also found in relevant literature [ 16 ]. The 
choice of WC also allows to better understand the effect of oxygen pick- 
up in case of carbide dissociation, since Tungsten have complete solu -
bility in Niobium, and it does not induce significant changes in the so -
lidification of the pure metal. 
The Nb powder had an average particle size of 33 μ m ( Fig. 1 (c)), 
while WC particles had an average size of 0.6 μ m. Powder mixture was 
performed in a rotating bottle over rollers to avoid altering the 
morphology or the size of the particles, which may affect the spread -
ability/flowability of the powders in LPBF. As shown in Fig. 1 (b), the WC 
particles uniformly coated the surface of the irregular Nb powder par -
ticles, sticking onto the particle surface. The powder was then processed 
using a Concept Laser M2 system, fitted with a continuous-wave Nd-YAG 
fiber laser with a maximum output of 400 W, with a controlled Argon 
atmosphere down to < 1000 ppm. To reduce the impact of the residual 
stresses that are commonly experienced in LPBF, the island scan strategy 
was used (5 mm × 5 mm islands) [ 17 , 18 ].

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Laser powder bed fusion of pure Nb 
High scan speeds are typically known to create lack of fusion or 
balling defects due to the limited time for melt pools to wet and spread, 
impairing the flowability of the molten material [ 5 ]. These speeds also 
promote spatter formation and significant disturb the powder bed by 
promoting denudation [ 20 ]. Visual inspection of the as built cubes, 
Fig. 2 , revealed a considerable contribution of relatively higher scanning 
speeds ( ≥ 800 mm / s ) to the presence of macro defects, confirming pre -
dictions from literature. While this range of scanning speed is typical for 
common structural materials, it can be considered a high range for high 
melting temperature refractory metals like Nb. However, it is important 
to keep in mind that additional processing parameters can impact the 
presence of macro defects other than the scan speed. Griemsmann et al. 
[ 21 ] were able to achieve high density Nb LPBF specimens processed at 
speeds as high as 1250 mm/s, P = 170 W, h = 65 μ m, t = 20 μ m, yet 
using spherical Nb powder. As such, balancing the process parameters to 
generate a reasonable heat input can also generate highly dense defect- 
free builds, which is usually done using the energy density equation 
given by: 
E = P / ( v . h ) (1)

power (P), scan speed (v) and hatch spacing (h).

For the larger hatch spacing ( Fig. 3 (a) and (b)), increasing laser 
power has a considerable influence on the formation of macrocracks 
(detail in Fig. 3 (b)) due to the thermal stresses of multiple cycles, 
combined with the pores associated with the instabilities in the melt 
pool. Significant porosity is also observed in the conditions that combine 
lower laser power (300 W) and increasing speed ( Fig. 3 (c)), associated 
with lower heat input that was failed to consolidate the material. 
Although the higher porosity levels, balling defects were not identified. 
The hatch spacing is another crucial parameter and affects not only the 
build-up rate but can also influence the interconnection of neighbouring 
tracks [ 23 ], influencing on the formation of metallurgic defects. The 
hatch space effect can be analysed qualitatively accessing the surface 
finishing of samples. Smaller hatch spacing resulted in better surface 
finishing (detail on both Fig. 3 (c) and (d) and also in Fig. 2 on white 
dashed squares). Nonetheless, it is important to conclude that no specific 
trend between the build density and energy density was observed for the 
processing parameters used, unlike what is commonly observed for 
other materials [ 24 ]. Literature report that other alloy systems also does 
not show such a trend [ 25 ]. Low energy densities are known to result 
high porosity levels due to lack of metallurgic consolidation. On the 
other hand, high energy densities can also result in high porosity levels 
due to keyhole porosity. 
To correlate the processing parameters with the hardness, the energy 
density equation can be used. Fig. 4 relates the hardness with the energy 
density and highlight a trend using a second order polynomial trendline. 
The results show a trend of increasing the hardness for intermediate 
energy densities, where it reaches a plateau prior to decreasing for 
higher energy densities. The hardness increase with the energy density 
was also reported by Vandenbroucke and Kruth [ 26 ] for LPBF of gas 
atomised Ti-6Al-4 V powder and is associated to the better densification 
of the material. Thus, we can extrapolate that the decrease in hardness 
for higher energy densities might be associated with an increase in 
porosity due to keyhole porosity.

Yang et al. [ 14 ] correlated oxygen pick-up with the asymmetric 
lattice distortions caused by the oxygen compressed in the octahedral 
interstitial site in body-centered cubic (BCC) Nb crystal structure. This 
creates large shear and dilatational distortions that interact with both 
edge and screw dislocations resulting in a hardening effect. XRD was 
also used to study the influence of the energy density on oxygen pick-up. 
Regardless of the energy density value, results confirm that the pro -
cessed Nb has a single phase with a BCC structure, Fig. 5 . In other words, 
it evidences that after LPBF process the oxygen still in solid solution into 
interstitial sites of the Niobium BCC structure. Furthermore, the 
observed peak shifts appear to show a crystal lattice distortion propor -
tional to the energy density. Peaks are shifted towards higher angles 
when the energy density increases. For the present analysis, the 
measured displacement as the energy density increases can be associ -
ated with the solid solution effects of oxygen pick-up. Nevertheless, 
other phenomena could also induce the measured displacement, namely 
crystalline defects, and residual stresses [ 29 ]. The latter effect is likely to 
be minor since XRD was performed after sectioning the samples, 
relieving most stresses. 
3.2. Laser powder bed fusion of niobium + WC 
The impact of blending Nb with WC fine powder on the final LPBF 
builds was observed from the initial visual analysis. No apparent macro 
defects such as delamination or macro cracks were observed, even at 
high scanning speeds, Fig. 6 . Naked eye analysis reveals that the pro -
cessability of the mixture appeared to be better than that of the pure Nb 
powder. This behaviour can be accounted for through the improvement 
in wetting that is provided by the fine WC powders. It was interesting to 
observe that, for both powder mixtures, the higher densification was 
achieved for the set of parameters that offered an energy density of 11.7 
J/mm
2
, as shown by optical microscopies in Fig. 7 and Fig. 8 . Elimi -
nating porosity in the Nb + WC builds proved to be a challenge as shown 
by the larger porosity. 
The average hardness of Nb + WC cubes was 250 ± 16 HV
0.1
, which 
is 15% lower than that of the average hardness of the pure Nb cubes. The 
hardness deviation for the LPBF Nb + WC range from 3 to 26 HV, like 
pure Niobium. Although the presence of WC in the powder mixture is 
meant to provide an additional strengthening contribution, this would 
require that the WC particles should survive the melting process, 
allowing for second phase hardening. However, evidence corroborate 
the hypothesis that the carbide particles were melted during the process. 
Melting of the WC (T

Furthermore, although a hardness increase might also be expected from 
solid solution strengthening of substitutional W in Nb as reported by 
Chaudhary et al. [ 27 ], this is not as effective as the interstitial solid

4. Conclusions 
Under the conditions tested for the processing and analysis of LPBF 
processing of pure Nb and Nb + WC powder mixture, it is possible to 
conclude that: 
• LPBF can provide pure Nb parts with density exceeding 99% using 
non-spherical Nb powder. Highly dense Nb + WC builds can also be 
achieved. Overall, the relationship between the energy density and 
the build density does not seem to follow clear trend, with some of 
the Nb + WC powder mixture containing large porosity. 
• In the pure Nb builds, high energy density conditions led to higher 
oxygen pick-up, resulting in an increase in hardness. 
• The addition of WC decreased the hardness, a consequence of the less 
significant strengthening effect of substitutional W, instead of 
interstitial oxygen in solid solution. 
• The addition of WC reduces oxygen trapped in builds and, conse -
quently, slows down the oxidation rate of the material.
