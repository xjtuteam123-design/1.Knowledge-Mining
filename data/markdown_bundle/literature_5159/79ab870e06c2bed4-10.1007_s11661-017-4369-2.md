Development of High-Strength High-Temperature
Cast Al-Ni-Cr Alloys Through Evolution of a Novel
Composite Eutectic Structure
P. PANDEY, S. KASHYAP, C.S. TIWARY, and K. CHATTOPADHYAY
Aiming to develop high-strength Al-based alloys with high material index (strength/density) for
structural application, this article reports a new class of multiphase Al alloys in the Al-Ni-Cr
system that possess impressive room temperature and elevated temperature ( ‡ 200 /C176 C)
mechanical properties. The ternary eutectic and near eutectic alloys display a complex
microstructure containing intermetallic phases displaying hierarchically arranged plate and rod
morphologies that exhibit extraordinary mechanical properties. The yield strengths achieved at
room temperatures are in excess of 350 MPa with compressive plastic strains of more than 30
pct (without fracturing) for these alloys. The stability of the complex microstructure also leads
to a yield stress of 191 ± 8 to 232 ± 5 MPa at 250 /C176 C. It is argued that the alloys derive their
high strength and impressive plasticity through synergic eﬀects of reﬁned nanoeutectics of two
diﬀerent morphologies forming a core shell type of architecture.

I. INTRODUCTION
RECENT technological advancements in the aviation
and automobile industries demand high-performance
lightweight alloys with stable microstructure that are
easily castable and exhibit superior room- and high-tem-
perature (> 150 /C176 C) strength and plasticity. The lower
density together with good strength gives high speciﬁc
strength that increases fuel eﬃciency. [1,2] Among all the
light alloys, Al-based alloys have attracted signiﬁcant
attention for structural applications. The densities of
other structural alloys, namely, Ti alloys and Fe alloys,
are 2 and 3 times greater, respectively, than Al alloys.
Thus, the latter exhibits superior materials index
(strength-to-density ratio). Moreover, aluminum alloys
are also known for their good oxidation and corrosion
resistance due to formation of stable, adherent, and
protective a-Al2O3 layer.[3–7] However, most of the
commercially available Al alloys suﬀer from a major
drawback of drop in strength at elevated temperature
(> 150 /C176 C). Some of the aluminum alloys containing Si
have reported adequate strength around 200 /C176 C and a
drop in strength beyond that. [8–10] A possible strategy to
develop Al alloys for service at high temperature is by
in-situ reinforcement of hard intermetallic phases in metal
matrix through eutectic solidiﬁcation, where coupled
growth of two or more phases occurs simultaneously.
Among the binary eutectics, some of the most extensively
studied eutectics are Al-Al 3Fe, Al-Al 2Cu, and
Al-Al3Ni.[11–17] Al-Al3Fe and Al-Al 3Ni eutectics exhibit
rod morphology with ﬁbers of intermetallic phases
embedded in Al matrix. On the other hand, the growth
morphologies for Al-Al 2Cu eutectic are diﬀerent and
consist of an alternate lamella of Al 2Cu in Al matrix.
These eutectics show good mechanical properties due to
the presence of hard intermetallic phases that impede the
dislocation motion and, hence, impart strength. How-
ever, the high volume fraction of intermetallic phases
often compromises the ductility of the alloys. [18–20]
The strength and ductility of the eutectics are depen-
dent on interlayer spacing, phase fraction, and mor-
phology. The deformation behavior of rod eutectic is
well reported by Misra et al.[21,22] They have shown that
the metal rods oriented perpendicular to the crack can
lead to crack bridging and, hence, development of
plasticity.[21,22] Cracks oriented parallel to the rods lead
to smaller bridging of cracks and result in a smaller
increment of improvement in toughness. In recent years,
it has been shown that careful manipulation of
microstructure by rapid solidiﬁcation or by ternary
addition can increase strength and plasticity. [23–31] A
small addition of ternary elements can change the
morphology of the binary eutectic. These changes
include formation of colonies, reﬁnement of eutectic,

II. EXPERIMENTAL
The alloys of diﬀerent compositions (marked in the
liquidus projection as well as presented in Table I) are
prepared using constituent elements of 99.99 pct purity
through arc meting under an argon atmosphere follow-
ing evacuation of the melting chamber under a vacuum
of 10
/C05 mbar. The alloys were remelted at least 8 times
to ensure homogeneity. The arc-melted buttons were
chill cast using a vacuum suction casting setup equipped
with a water-cooled split copper mold to obtain rods of
3-mm diameter and 80-mm length. Slices were cut from
the as-cast buttons and chill-cast rods for characteriza-
tion. Analysis of the microstructure was carried out
using scanning electron microscopes (SEMs) (FEI,
ESEM Quanta and FEI, SIRION XL-30 equipped with
a ﬁeld emission gun). The overall compositions of
well-polished suction-cast alloys were determined by
taking at least ﬁve regions of appropriate spot size in the
WDS mode using an electron-probe microanalyzer
equipped with a ﬁeld emission gun (JEOL*
JXA-8530F). The ﬁnal measured composition, as pre-
sented in Table I, is the average composition of ﬁve
readings. Composition variation across the eutectic
colonies and phase composition of suction-cast samples
were determined in the WDS mode using electron-probe
microanalysis. To reveal the morphological evolution of
the eutectic phases, suction-cast samples were electro-
chemically etched using Struers A2 electrolyte contain-
ing 90-mL water + 730-mL ethanol + 100-mL butyl
cellosolve + 80-mL perchloric acid. Electrochemical
etching was carried out at a voltage of 35 V, and the
temperature of the electrolyte was maintained at 15 /C176 C.
The thermal analyses of the suction-cast samples were
carried out in a diﬀerential scanning calorimeter (Met-
tler DCS-822e). The calibration was checked by melting
pure Al, and its accuracy was found to be ± 2 /C176 C. Fifty
milligrams of weighted samples were heated at a heating
rate of 5 /C176 C/min to a temperature of 690 /C176 C followed by
cooling at a rate of 5 /C176 Ct o5 0 /C176 C. An X-ray
diﬀractometer with a Cu K
a (k = 0.1540 nm) source
(PAN analytical make) was used to generate the X-ray
diﬀraction proﬁles from the suction-cast samples. For
transmission electron microscope (TEM) foil prepara-
tion, 3-mm-diameter discs were sliced from suction-cast
rod and ﬁnally mechanically polished to 40 lm using
silicon carbide grit paper. The electron transparent foils
were prepared using a precision ion polishing system
(PIPS-GATAN make) operating at 5.0 kV. The ﬁnal
polishing, however, was carried out at a voltage of 1.0
kV to eliminate ion-beam-induced damage. The com-
position and structure of the individual phases present
in the suction-cast sample were characterized with the
help of a TEM (FEI Tecnai, F-30) equipped with an
energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector. Initial
evaluations of the mechanical properties of suction-cast
samples were carried out using a CSM make instru-
mented microindentation system equipped with a dia-
mond pyramid indenter. The indents were taken at a
load of 0.5 N, and at least 15 indents were taken for
better sampling. A screw-driven Zwick/Roell UTM was
used for compression testing of suction-cast samples.
Cylindrical samples with height/diameter of 1.5 to 2.0
and a strain rate of 10
/C03 s/C01 were used for tests at
diﬀerent temperatures. For high-temperature tests, each
sample was kept at the test temperature for 15 minutes
to stabilize the testing system (including the sample)
prior to actual testing. Well-polished samples were used
for testing, and the lateral surface of the deformed
samples was further examined in an FEG SEM (FEI,
SIRION XL-30) in secondary electron mode.
III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Cast Microstructure and X-ray Characterization
Using binary Al-Al
3Ni eutectic (Al-3.1Ni) samples for
benchmarking, we have studied three alloys with varying
additions of Cr along the line AD (Figure 1). We shall
designate these as Al-4Ni-0.5Cr, Al-4Ni-0.6Cr, and
Al-5Ni-0.7Cr.
The microstructure of the arc-melted button of
Al-3.1Ni alloy exhibits typical rod eutectic in which

3Ni (orthorhombic, Pnma) phase are
embedded in a continuous matrix of a-Al (face-cen-
tered-cubic (fcc), Fm-3m). Addition of a small amount
of chromium does not change the structure of the
phases present but does change the morphology of the
eutectic. A detailed descript ion of the microstructures
of the as-melted button is given in the supplementary
material (Figures S1(a) through (e)). Hereafter, we will
only present and discuss the microstructural and
mechanical properties of suction-cast samples.

(suction-cast) rod is shown in Figure 2(a). It is similar to
the microstructure of the middle section of the arc-melted

The bright phases near the boundaries of the eutectic

decrease in growth velocity during eutectic growth also
leads to an increase in inter-rod spacing from the center of
the colony toward the boundaries. This can be seen on the
right side of Figure 2(a). The change in eutectic spacing

Figure 2(d). It is clear that rod eutectic spacing increases

IV. CONCLUSIONS
This article reports the morphological transition of
the eutectic cell during the growth in ternary Al-Ni-Cr
alloys with Cr content varying from 0.5 to 0.7 at. pct. At
the initial stage of growth, the eutectic grows as a rod
eutectic similar to the binary Al-Ni eutectic alloys.
However, as the growth proceeds, a morphological
transformation occurs, yielding eutectics with plate
morphology. No structural change occurs during the
process, and the change is associated with an increase in
Cr content, suggesting morphological transition takes
place when growth occurs in Cr-rich liquid. This unique
morphology with nanometer spacings of rods and plates
