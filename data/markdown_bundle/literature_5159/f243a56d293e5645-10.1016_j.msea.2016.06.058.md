Abstract 
Hot hardness, related to the mechanical strength and wear resistance of materials at high temperatures , 
has been measured from room temperature up to 800°C for a comprehensive set of iron -based alloys 
having different microstructures and chemical compositions. The results obtained suggest the 
existence of several softening regimes with increasing temperatures, also with a massive hardness drop 
observed to begin at temperatures close to 0.5 times the melting te mperature for most of the chosen 
alloys. Austenitic steel grades were also observed to show a significant softening behaviour at 
moderate temperatures compared to ferritic and martensitic alloys, attributed to the dislocation 
dynamics of face cubic centred alloys. The exact nature of the temperature dependence shown by 
hardness has been proposed to adopt the form of an exponential Arrhenius equation . Another model 
suggested in the available literature is also discussed within this context . Additionally, the role of 
alloying elements has been correlated to the softening behaviour. Molybdenum or boron were found to 
slow down the softening behaviour, while carbide -forming elements such as vanadium and tungsten 
were found to be beneficial for room temperature hardness. 
 
Keywords: High Temperature, Hot hardness, Iron, Steel, Hardfacing

1. Introduction 
Hardness can be generically defined as the resistance of a material against localised plastic 
deformation, such as inde ntation, erosion or scratching [1]. The first modern attempt at a systematic 
hardness characterisation was developed as late as in the nineteenth century by Friedrich Mohs, 
comparatively determining the hardness of a solid by observing its scratch resistance against different 
reference materials. However, the use of this qualitative hardness scale remained largely confined to 
the field of mineralogy. The first half of the twentieth century, on the other hand, saw the development 
of true quantitative hardness measuring techniques. Chief amon g them, and still widely in use, are 
Rockwell, Vickers, Shore, Brinell and Knoop indentation hardness tests, developed between 1900 and 
1939. However, as in the case of the definition of hardness itself, none of these techniques has become 
an undisputed st andard, as they were designed to fulfil the requirements of industrial applications 
vastly different from each other. In any case, the aforementioned methods are still widely used for the 
characterisation of materials. The hardness values thus obtained have proven to be especially 
important for the quantification of the following material properties: 
 The resistance against wear mechanisms such as abrasion, scratching and erosion [2,3] 
 The strength of materials, in particular the overall resistance against large-scale deformation [4,5] 
 The estimation of the fatigue limit for metallic alloys [6] 
 The onset and extent of phase transformation in solids [7] 
The second case has proven to be especially useful, as hardness testing is a non -destructive technique 
and may be used instead of the more complex uniaxial stress testing. The determination of material 
strength from hardness data can be performed by using the empirical equation (Eq. 1) introduced by 
Tabor [8,9], with σ being the yield stress and C a const ant factor, which has been reported to be close 
to 3 for ductile materials tested under Vickers indentation [10,11], assuming no work hardening on the 
tested material. However, the validity of this parameter for all temperature ranges has been put into 
question. For instance, a mismatch between hot hardness and yield stresses has been reported for 
ferritic steels at temperatures above 400°C [12].

In recent times there has been a growing interest in hardness testing at high temperature (HT). This has 
been especially true in tribology, as hot hardness is a key parameter for the study and mitigation of 
wear processes at HT, giving estimates of material strength and resistance to plastic deformation under 
extreme thermal conditi ons. Metals in particular can show severe softening at temperatures above 
500°C [13], so for many industrial applications hot hardness is a crucial parameter as it is closely 
related to mechanical strength at the operational temperature . Also, the prevalent wear mechanisms 
may change at HT due to the softening process experienced by alloys [14]. However, there is no 
agreement so far for the real dependence of hardness with temperature. 
Two main equations have been postulated in an effort to model the softening behaviour observed for 
metals at high temperatures : Eq. 2 was f ormulated by Ito and Shishokin as early as 1923 [7] and 
reported more recently in [15], [16] and [17] . Eq. 3, on the other hand, dates back to the early 1970s 
and can be found in the works of Sherby, Armstrong and Merchant [18,19].

It is noteworthy that both expressions are based on exponentially decreasing functions of temperature 
although being mutually incompatible. Additionally, in both cases, parameters A and B are postulated 
to have one set of values at low and high temperatures respectively, in order to account for the two 
different softening regimes commonly described in the available literature [20,21]. 
The main goal of the present study is to obtain further insight in the softening behaviour experienced 
by metals at HT. To this end, the hot hardness data measured for iron-based alloys in the framework of 
several research projects has been systematically analysed, and linked to the microstructure and 
chemical composition of the chosen materials . The true nature of the hardness -temperature 
dependence has been examined using logarithmic hardness plots, and possible softening processes 
have also been discussed in the light of the available literature on deformation mechanisms. The broad

2. Experimental 
2.1. High temperature hardness measurements 
The hardness values reported throughout this paper were determined using a test rig developed at the 
Austrian Center of Competence for Tribology (AC2T research GmbH), whose co nfiguration is shown 
in Fig. 1 a. By means of this device, Vickers indentation tests (HV10) were carried out from room 
temperature (RT) up to 800°C going through the intermediate temperatures of 100, 300, 500, 600 and 
700°C. The maximum testing temperature of 800°C correspond ed approximately to 0.5 8 times the 
melting temperature of steel. In order to prevent damage both to the indenter and the sample due to 
oxidation at HT, the testing chamber was kept under low vacuum condi tions (5 mbar) during the 
duration of the test. 
A test load of 10 kg was selected in order to produce indentations large enough to be measured using 
an optical microscope , and also to obtain a reliable average of the hardness regardless of the 
inhomogeneities which may occur in the tested materials due to the presence of hard phases . Three 
indentations at least were performed for every chosen temperature to ensure repeatability of the results 
(see Fig. 1 b for an example) and the procedure was repeated on a second sample for statistical 
evaluation. Additional hardness measurements were performed on several of the chosen alloys at 
intermediate temperature values when further insight into the softening behaviour was required . The 
hardness test applied within this work was designed to have a short duration in order to minimise 
microstructural changes on the samples during the test. A representative temperature curve is shown in 
Fig. 1c, with the indenter’s position also plotted. 
As already mentioned, m ost of the results used within the present work were obtained during the past 
years within the framework of several research projects, as reported in [22-32].

2.2.1. Ferritic/pearlitic alloys 
This set of iron alloys featuring a body centred cubic (BCC) structure was comprised of carbon steel 
grades with low amounts of alloying element s, resulting in ferritic microstructures with different 
amounts of pearlite depending on the carbon content . Carbon steels are widely used due to their 
affordability, excellent formability and acceptable mechanical properties for many non -demanding 
applications.

 Grade 1.5415 was a heat -resistant carbon steel with a small addition of molybdenum

comprised of lamellar ferrite and pearlite, as shown in Fig. 2d. RT hardness was measured as 
153 ± 1 HV10 using a macro-Vickers hardness tester.

2.2.4. Composite materials 
This category comprised several iron-based hardfacings typically used for the localised wear 
protection of components operating under harsh wear environment, usually involving HT abrasion . 
Contrary to the alloys previously described, they feature d a high hardphase content intended to 
decrease wear. All materials were acquired in powder form from the manufacturer Castolin GmbH 
(Austria) and deposited using different welding techniques, as described below. The resulting 
hardfacings had thickness values of at least 2 mm. In particular, hardness was found to be strongly

higher hardness values, as it also did two-layer welding, as the dilution with the substrate was very 
low for the second deposited layer. All the materials within this class were welded on austenitic 
substrates made of grade 1.4301 steel, in order to minimise the softening of the substrate at HT. This 
choice of substrate for hardfacing deposition led to slower cooling rates and slightly lower hardness 
values compared to mild steel substrates.

activation energy for dislocation creep [19,33]. Hot hardness results for the chosen alloy classes are

3.1. Ferritic/pearlitic alloys 
The hardest grades up to 500°C turned out to be 1.0037 and 1.1191, both ferritic/pearlitic and with a 
RT hardness of ~200 HV10. The hardness of both materials decreased slowly up to 600°C, with 
measured values of 110 and 135 HV10, respectively. As seen in the Arrhenius plot, grade 1.0037 was 
less thermally stable than 1.1191, reaching values as low as 20 HV10 at 800°C. 
Another ferritic/pearlitic grade, 1.5415, showed higher thermal stability. From an initial hardness of 
154 HV10 at RT, it remained close to 150 HV10 up to 500°C, although it subsequently dropped to 
~50 HV10 at 800°C. 
The grey cast iron grade 0.6025 was found to be the softest material within this class. RT hardness was 
measured to be 125 HV10, but it also proved to be relatively stable at higher temperatures, still 
remaining close to 120 HV10 at 500°C. 
 
3.2. Martensitic alloys 
Grade 1.3343 was found to be the hardest alloy for this class all over the tested temperature range. Its 
initial hardness was measured as ~850 HV10 at RT, decreasing steadily to ~615 HV10 at 600°C. A 
subsequent hardness drop was found for higher temperatures, with a value of ~175 HV10 at the 
maximum temperature of 800°C, less than one fifth of the initial value. The second hardest material, 
the cold work steel grade 1.2379 showed in comparison lower hardness and thermal stability: the 
initial value at RT was measured as ~740 HV10 and subsequently hardness decreased linearly with 
temperature to ~675 HV10 at 350°C . The softening behaviour showed increased dependence with 
temperature in the range 350°C to 600°C, dropping to ~400 HV10. This behaviour was even more 
marked at higher temperatures, with hardness dropping to ~100 HV10 at 800°C. 
Both hot work tool steel grades 1.2343 and 1.2367 were not among the hardest martensitic alloys but 
proved to be remarkably stable up to 600°C. Their initial har dness values were 415 and 640 HV10 at 
RT, respectively, slowly decreasing to ~300 and 465 HV10 at 600°C. However, higher temperatures 
brought significant softening behaviour, with hardness values at 800°C being measured below 
150 HV10 for both steel grades.

The softest alloys under consideration, the bainitic/martensitic grades 1.7709 and 1.7711, showed RT 
hardness values close to 250 HV10, although their thermal stability was superior to the rest of th e 
martensitic materials tested, as can be deducted by the lower slopes in Fig. 7b. Hardness at 600°C was 
measured to be close to 200 HV10 for both materials, although at 800°C hardness was greatly reduced 
to 50 and 80 HV10, respectively. 
Further analysis of the Arrhenius plot casts additional light into the hardness behaviour of the chosen 
alloys. For several of them, three different slopes have been found, with softening rates that increase 
with testing tempe rature. However, hardness values and even transition temperatures differed 
significantly for the selected materials, likely due to the influence of microstructure and chemical 
composition. 
 
3.3. Austenitic alloys 
The HT hardness behaviour of this set of materials was significantly different than that of both the 
ferritic/pearlitic and martensitic alloys previously described. In particular, comparatively large 
hardness drops were experienced by this set of austenitic steel grades u nder moderate temperatures. 
From RT up to 300°C, hardness losses of ~14 % and up to 27 % were found respectively for austenitic 
grades 1.3401 and 1.4301, compared to values close to 10 % for the martensitic 1.3343 or the 
ferritic/pearlitic 1.1191. This softening trend for austenitic alloys at moderate temperatures can also be 
observed in the Arrhenius plot for higher values of 1/T (compare Fig. 8b for instance with Fig. 6b, 
corresponding to the ferritic/pearlitic set of materials). 
In absolute terms, t he hardest alloy in this class was the cold-worked steel grade P750. Its measured 
hardness decreased steadily from 375 HV10 at RT to ~290 HV10 at 400°C. Between 400 and 600°C 
softening was observed to be very slow, reaching ~280 HV10 at 600°C, but it again increased at 
higher temperatures. In any case, the lowest hardness m easured, ~210 HV10 at 800°C, was still high 
compared to the other austenitic alloys , suggesting that the cold -worked condition stay ed nearly 
unaffected at HT. 
The second hardest grade 1.3401, Hadfield steel, showed a RT hardness of 285 HV10. However, its 
thermal stability was comparatively low: after a n initial softening up to 300°C its hardness dropped

## Page 22

considerably, from ~245 HV10 at 300°C to ~60 HV10 at 800°C. The chromium - and nickel -rich 
grades 1.4301, 1.4571 and 1.4581 showed a similar behaviour: they were softer than Hadfield steel at 
RT, with hardness values ranging between ~160 and 190 HV10, but their softening rates at HT were 
lower and for this reason they were regarded as more stable than grade 1.3401. For 1.4301, however, 
there were hints of a faster hardness drop at temperatures higher than 700°C, with a measured 
hardness value at the maximum temperature of 800°C as low as 70 HV10. 
 
3.4. Composite alloys 
As plotted in Fig. 9 a, the hardfacing FeCrNbBWC showed the highest hardness values up to a 
temperature of 500°C, and reaching 1070 HV10 at RT likely due to the two-layer deposition process. 
Its hardness remained almost unaffected up to 300°C , but at temperatures higher than 500°C a 
significant softening was observed, down to ~350 HV10 at 800°C. 
The second hardest alloy, FeCrNbBC, showed lower hardness values, starting from ~ 1000 HV10 at 
RT and s oftening moderately up to 500°C . A more steep decrease in hardness was observed above 
500°C (see Fig. 9b), reaching ~276 HV10 at 800°C. 
The hardfacing FeCrNbC showed also high hardness during testing, with a measured initial value 
close to 875 HV10 at RT. This lower value was expected to be caused by the one -layer deposition 
process, allowing for increased mixing with the substrate [31]. This material experienced a gradual 
softening al l over the chosen temperature range compared to the previously mentioned composite 
alloys, reaching ~300 HV10 at 800°C. 
The vanadium-rich martensitic hardfacing FeCrVC (see Table 1) showed even lower hardness values. 
Hardness at RT was measured as ~600 HV10, decreasing slowly to ~460 HV10 at 600°C and even 
faster at HT, down to ~150 HV10 at 800°C. 
 
4. Discussion 
The study of the HT hardness behaviour of steels and iron based alloys is complex due to the diversity 
of chemistries involved, and the important role of heat treatment and mechanical history. However, the 
choice of alloys within the present work allowed us to identify several common trends for the different

4.1. Ferritic alloys 
It has been reported that increased carbon contents up to 1 wt % raise the hardness of carbon steels up 
to temperatures of 300°C [7]. In the context of the present study, this explains the higher RT and hot 
hardness shown by grade 1.1191 compared to 1.0050 (with respective carbon contents of 0.45 and

## Page 24

0.30 wt %). However, this does not account for the higher hardness shown by grade 1.00 37, with a 
lower carbon content of 0.17 wt % and a similar grain size. On the other hand, grade 1.5415 was found 
to be softer, but it showed a slower hardness drop at temperatures higher than 600°C ( as shown in 
Figs. 6a and 6b). This increased thermal stab ility was attributed to its molybdenum content. Finally, 
the grey cast iron grade 0.6025 was the softest material within this category up to 700°C, as the 
graphite present in its microstructure may lower the overall hardness shown by cast iron. 
Taking into account additional factors influencing hot hardness, it was found that the temperature for 
the initial formation of austenite (AC1) was between 725 and 745°C for grades 1.0037 , 1.0050 and 
1.5415, according to available datasheets [37-39]. Thus, some degree of austenitisation was expected 
at the maximum testing temperature of 800°C for all three materials, although its effect in the 
measured hot hardness could not be estimated. For the grade 1.1191, on the other hand, both AC1 and 
AC3 (full auste nitisation temperature) were reported to be lower than the maximum testing 
temperature of 800°C [40], thus a complete transition to FCC microstructure was expected during hot 
hardness measurements for this grade, being possibly the source of the observed s lower softening 
between 700 and 800°C (Fig. 6a). 
The evolution of HT hardness was found to be quite similar for all of the ferritic materials (see the 
slopes in Fig. 6b), probably as a consequence of their low alloyed chemical composition. In particular, 
this set of materials showed low temperature dependence from RT up to 500 °C . A similar behaviour 
has also been previously reported for the yield stress of ferritic steels up to temperatures close to 0.4 
Tm [41]. 
The d eformation mechanisms for BCC materials up to 0.6 Tm have been reported to be dislocation 
glide, followed at higher temperatures by thermally activated dislocation glide and dislocation climb . 
In our case significant hardness drops were clearly observed at 500°C (~0.4 Tm), suggesting that the 
steep softening at HT is caused by a transition to power -law creep as the main deformation 
mechanism. Also, a s observed in deformation mecha nism maps for BCC steel grades [41], a third 
deformation regime exists at low temperatures, as yield stress increases rapidly with decreasing 
temperature for temperatures lower than 0.15 Tm, due to lattice resistance to dislocation glide . 
Plasticity in BCC materials is determined mainly by the glide of screw dislocations, whose activation

## Page 25

energy is highly temperature dependant. This is the source of the characteristic bri ttle-to-ductile 
transition (BDT) observed for BCC alloys at low temperatures . However, in our case the BDT of iron 
was predicted to occur below room temperature, i.e. below the minimum temperature chosen for 
hardness measurement and therefore this initial regime was not observed during testing. 
 
4.2. Martensitic alloys 
Within this class, the hardest allo y was found to be grade 1.3343, likely due to the contribution of 
hard-carbide forming element s such as vanadium and tungsten [42] and the secondary hardening 
during annealing. The second hardest alloy, grade 1.2379, featured abundant primary chromium 
carbides and again secondary hardening, expected to increase RT hardness. However, the lack of HT 
stabilising elements such as molybdenum may explain the significant hardness drop starting at the 
comparatively low temperature of 350°C. Durostat B2, the low alloyed martensitic grade 
manufactured by voestalpine GmbH, showed a similar plasticity behaviour . In particular, it was 
expected that the mar tensitic microstructure provided a base hardness value, ~524 HV10 at RT in this 
case. However, the lack of strengthening chemical elements kept RT hardness lower than that of the 
alloys with hard carbides, such as grade 1.3343. Additionally, it also led to lower hardness at HT, even 
at moderate temperatures such as 300°C. 
The softest alloys within this class, the bainitic/martensitic grades 1.7709 and 1.7711 , proved 
nonetheless to be the most stable at HT despite their low alloy content. This has been attributed to the 
high resistance of bainitic phases to HT softening , as reported by [43]. For the fully martensitic alloys 
within this class, grades 1.2343, 1.2367 and 1.3343 showed high thermal stability up to 600°C, 
featuring also molybdenum contents between 1.3 and 5 wt %. As already stated, this chemical element 
was reported to increase HT strength in steel [13], so it was expected to slo w down material softening 
at HT. Additionally, it was found that increasing levels of vanadium led in general to higher RT 
hardness for this set of martensitic alloys. 
Martensitic steels feature a body -centred tetragonal lattice and their HT hardness behaviour at lower 
temperatures is expected to be controlled by dislocation glide, with a rather low temperature 
dependence. This is in agreem ent with the low softening rates as seen in Fig. 7b . For higher

## Page 26

temperatures, in the power -law creep regime, steep hardness drops were observed above 600-700°C 
for all of the chosen martensitic grades . An additional intermediate regime with moderate softening 
could also be observed in the Arrhenius plot for several of the tested alloys (1.2379 , 1.7709 and 
Durostat B2, for instance) between 300 -350°C and 600 -700°C. This will be further discussed in 
Section 4.5, comparing it to the results reported in the available literature. 
 
4.3. Austenitic alloys 
In absolute terms, the austenitic steel grade P750 turned out to be the hardest material within this class 
for the entire temperature range . The cold-working process used on the alloy was expected to increase 
the dislocation density in the material and thus its strength. Additionally, the significant molybdenum 
content was again assumed to increase the mechanica l stability of the alloy at HT. The resistance of 
grade P750 to HT softening was regarded to be higher than that of the other chosen austenitic alloys, 
as it can be deduced from the less steep hardness curve in Fig. 8b. 
A hardness drop at temperatures higher than 300°C was observed for the grade 1.3401 (Hadfield 
steel), attributed to the lack of HT streng thening elements. As for the softer chromium -rich alloys, 
both grades 1.4571 and 1.4581 showed lower absolute hardness but less steep softening at 
temperatures higher than 300°C, a fact which was again attributed to their molybdenum content. No 
clear effect of chromium content in hardness could be observed, as chromium -rich austenitic alloys 
such as 1.4301 and 1.4571 performed poorly from RT to 700°C compared to Hadfield steel. As in the 
previous case, u p to three different softening rates could be observed for several of the chosen 
austenitic alloys (1.3401 and 1.4301) . These softening stages were identified in the respective 
Arrhenius plots in the form of higher slopes with increasing temperatures. An initial mild softening 
stage was observed from RT up to 300 - 400°C for all of the austenitic alloys (see Fig. 8 b), likely 
related to dislocation glide. A similar behaviour has been observed in deformation mechanism maps 
corresponding to austenitic steel grades such as AISI 304 and 316, in addition to p ure FCC metals at 
moderate temperatures [41]. This has been attributed to the low lattice resistance to dislocation motion 
characteristic of FCC metals.

## Page 27

A second regime with a steeper slope and thus increased softening was observed starting at 
temperatures ranging from 300 to 500°C , expected to be dominated by thermally activated dislocation 
glide like in the case of ferritic steel grades. However, in our case the hardness drop at HT for 
austenitic alloys seem to be less steep than the ones observed for the ferritic grades (compare Figs. 6b 
and 8b). A similar behaviour of yield stress has been reported in the literature, attributed to the lower 
diffusion rates in FCC alloys compared to BCC [41]. 
As already stated, for the 1.3401 and 1.4301 grade alloys a third regime of even faster softening could 
be observed between 700°C and the maximum temperature of 800°C ( ~0.59 Tm). The available 
literature on damage mechanis ms for austenitic steel grades [41] suggested that, under the strain rates 
involved in hardness testing (~ 10 -3 s-1), this HT softening process was due to power law creep as a 
consequence of dislocation climb. 
 
4.4. Composite alloys 
This set of materials showed to be comparatively hard due to their high carbide content. However, it 
must be noted that their strength and mechanical properties are strongly dependent on welding 
parameters such as power input , layer structure or even the s ubstrate used for deposition, so the 
hardness values reported for this material class may not be applicable to similar materials produced 
under different conditions. In any case, t he highest hardness levels within this class were found 
respectively for the hardfacings FeCrNbBWC and FeCrNbBC, very stable up to 300°C in comparison 
with the rest of the composite materials under consideratio n. Both materials showed a boron content 
close to 4 wt %. (see Table 1), a chemical element which has been reported to significantly increase 
RT hardness by means of the formation of complex borides [44]. It has also been stated in the 
available literature that borides such as (Cr,Fe) 2B show high stability at HT [45], which could explain 
the behaviour observed for both boron-containing hardfacings up to 500ºC. 
The high hardness shown at RT by the hardfacing FeCrNbC was attributed to its high niobium content 
(~7 wt %). This strong carbide-forming element has been reported to increase hardness [46], however, 
the lack of other chemical elements providing HT strength led to lower hardness at elevated 
temperatures. Interestingly, in the context of Fe -Ni-B-Si coatings a maximum hardness has been

## Page 28

reported for a niobium content close to 4 wt %, with lower RT micro -hardness being observed for 
higher contents (6 – 8 wt %) [47]. This behaviour was similar to what was observed in our case for the 
composite alloys FeCrNbBWC and FeCrNbC (see Table 1 and Fig. 9), although it must be noted that 
[47] referred to partly amorphous coatings. 
In the case of FeCrVC, vanadium carbides w ere expected to contribute to high hardness levels due to 
their elevated mechanical strength [48]. However, the overall hardness of FeCrVC was observed to be 
lower than for the previous composite alloys. This was attributed to the fraction of softer retained 
austenite present within its microstructure. 
 
4.5. Final considerations 
For further clarity, the slopes observed in the Arrhenius plots have been averaged respectively for the 
ferritic, austenitic and martensitic steel grades, and schematically displayed in Fig. 10. In this case, the 
plot uses ln (H) so that the resulting slopes correspond to the different values of the parameter B (see 
Eq. 3 for details). Two different slopes were observed for the ferritic grades. An initially negligible 
slope dramatically rises more than two orders of magnitude above the transi tion temperature of 
~500ºC. 
The tested austenitic steel grades showed significant differences in hardness behaviour. The calculated 
slope at low to moderate temperatures was higher than the average found for ferritic steels, as already 
stated in Section 4. 3. The austenitic grades showing only two softening regimes experienced a 
transition to a higher slope at HT, with an average value roughly one order of ma gnitude higher than 
in the low temperature region. On the other hand , grades showing up to three soft ening regimes like 
1.3401 and 1.4301 were observed to soften faster at temperatures closer to 300ºC, w ith a slope value 
more than five times the initial one . An even steeper slope was found at higher temperatures, more 
than twenty-five times the value observed below 300ºC. For the martensitic steel grades, the general 
behaviour observed in Fig. 10 consisted of a shallow slope at moderate temperatures, with an average 
value between those observed for the ferritic and the austenitic grades, and a much st eeper slope at 
temperatures above 500ºC, up to two orders of magnitude higher than at low temperature s. For the 
martensitic grades showing three softening regimes, like 1.2379 or 1.7709, a third slope was observed

between the two regimes already described, intermediate to the two already described cases (~1070 K 
compared to ~70 and ~7600 K).

It must be noted here that throughout this study hardness has been used in the Arrhenius plots for all of 
the chosen materials instead of the H/E ratio . This has been suggested to lead to inaccurate results 
especially when calculating activation energies, as reported in [33]. As deducted from Eq. 5, it is the 
ratio H/E and not merely hardness what shows an exponential dependence with the inverse of 
temperature. As Young ’s modulus is observed to decrease with temperature, assuming it to be as a 
constant term all over the investigated temperature range will lead to some degree of error. To prevent 
this, reliable HT Young ’s modulus values are required, but i n the authors’ own experience, limited 
information exists in the available literature for iron -based alloys. Howev er, preliminary analysis of 
the results found for the ferritic alloy 1.1191 were performed for hot hardness data corrected using HT 
Young’s modulus values as reported in [7] for BCC pure iron. A linear regression was performed in 
the corrected Arrhenius pl ot between 600°C and 700°C, immediately below the austenitisation 
temperature of this steel grade, and it returned a value of 5200.2 K for the QC/nR term (see Eq. 5). QC, 
the activation energy for creep was not known for this alloy, but activation energies at temperatures 
lower than 0.6 Tm (lower than 800°C in our case) have been reported to be close to two thirds of the 
lattice diffusion energy of the material, Q L [18]. Making use of this assumption, in addition to the Q L 
for α -iron as reported in [41], 250 kJ/mol, the authors were able to calculate a power -law creep

## Page 30

exponent of 3.8 for the grade 1.1191 ferritic steel at HT, within the interval reported by [49] for high 
temperature power law creep but still far from the value of 6.9 given for α-iron by [41]. 
As already stated, up to three different softening rates have been observed in Arrh enius plots from RT 
up to 800°C especially for austenitic steel grades. However, as the hardness values used were not 
corrected using the temperatu re-dependant Young’s modulus, these findings must be taken with 
caution. In any case, Maugis et al. [33] have reported similar results for the non-corrected HT hardness 
of pure FCC metals, some of them showing a third, more sever e softening regime already at 
temperatures close to 0.5 Tm. Wheeler et al. [35] reported similar findings for ultrafine -grained 
aluminium, this time using modulus -corrected hardness. Consequently, the existing literature supports 
our observation of up to three different softening regimes for several austenitic and martensitic steels 
up to 0.6 Tm, in contrast to the two regimes usually assumed in the previous literature [20,21]. 
Finally, Eq. 3 has showed a good fit with the observed hardness evolution at HT for most of the tested 
materials, in addition to a more solid physical background than the empirical Eq. 2. However, the 
accurate calculation of the softening parameters involved in hot ha rdness requires further knowledge 
on the Young’s modulus temperature dependence. 
 
5. Conclusions 
During this study, the high temperature hardness behaviour of several iron -based alloys with different 
microstructures and chemical compositions has been examined. In particular, the following 
conclusions can be drawn: 
 The softening behaviour of the iron-based alloys is mainly determined by their 
crystallographic structure. 
 Austenitic alloys showed significant softening rates from RT up to 400°C , as similarly 
observed in deformation mechanisms maps for FCC alloys. 
 Eq. 3, suggesting a linear dependence of the logarithm of modulus -corrected hardness with the 
inverse of testing temperature, was linked to dislocation plasticity mechanisms like dislocation 
creep.

## Page 31

 From an engineering point of view, the addition of molybdenum is beneficial for a less 
marked and even delayed alloy softening at HT. 
 Boron-containing alloys were observed to show high hardness values at RT in addition to 
significant stability up to 500°C, attributed to the formation of complex boride phases. 
 Increasing v anadium content in martensitic alloys was qualitatively linked with higher RT 
hardness.
