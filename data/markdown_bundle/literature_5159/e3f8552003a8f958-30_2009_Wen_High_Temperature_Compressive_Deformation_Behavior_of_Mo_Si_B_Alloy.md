ABSTRACT 
 
Alloys based on Mo-Si-B ternary system are of great interest for very high temperature 
structural applications. Although ha ving excellent oxidation resis tance and strength at high 
temperature, the system still faces many chal lenges to be successful, among which 
deformation behavior is one of most important. In this research project, Mo-20Si-10B (at.%) 
alloy was processed such as to yield varying ơ-Mo volume fractions (from 5 to 46%), with 
the balance made up of Mo3Si and T2-Mo5SiB2 phases. The constant strain rate compressive 
deformation tests at 1200, 1300 and 1400°C were performed at strain rates of 10 -6, 10-5, and 
10-4 s-1. The results showed that the stresses required to maintain a given strain rate increased 
with a decrease in temperature and de creased with an increase in the ơ-Mo volume fraction 
at any given temperature. The values of the stress exponents deter mined from the data 
ranged from ~3-9, depending on temperature and volume fraction of ơ-Mo; the activation 
energy for creep was found to be in the range of ~200-600 kJ/mol depending on stress level 
and volume fraction of ơ-Mo. These results were correlated with SEM and TEM 
observations of the damage processes, deformation structures and deformation mechanisms. 
Deformation models were discussed with associated mechanism and test data.

Chapter 1. INTRODUCTION 
 
1.1 Introduction 
Materials for high temperature applic ations are always demanded in advanced energy, 
power and transportation systems. The ideal materials for new generation of energy or 
power systems should have hi gh strength, creep, fatigue and oxidation resistance at 
temperatures in the range of 1000 to 1600°C, which is clearly beyond the capabilities of the 
many metallic materials currently available, including supera lloys and aluminides based on 
nickel and titanium. In addition to temperature requirements, other properties which are 
critical to engineering materials cannot be compromis ed, such as environmental resistance, 
low temperature fracture toughness or ductility, ther mal-mechanical fatigue resistance, and 
so on. 
The intermetallic alloys based on the Mo-Si-B system have been chosen for study in this 
research program because of the following reasons. Firstly, these alloys, having 
microstructures composed of a dispersion of a high volume fraction of intermetallic phases 
in a more ductile solid solution matrix [1], are representative of the next generation of 
refractory alloys which are considered for very high temperature structural applications, 
including gas turbine parts such as blades, vane s, seals and combustors. There have been 
exciting reports indicating that these alloys have excellent oxidation resistance and strength at 
high temperatures, together with adequa te toughness, high melting points, moderate 
densities and high elastic moduli [2,3]. Secondly, although the introduction of these materials

in commercial applications is fo reseen, the alloy system is not mature. There are still 
scientific and technological challenges ex isting at this time. Consequently, considerable 
materials research and development will be required to understand the behavior of these 
alloys and optimize their performance. Fo r example, there is not a comprehensive 
composition-processing-microstructure-mechanical property data base for the alloy system, 
which can serve as a detailed guide for the alloy development. Finally, the behavior of these 
materials systems is of great scientific interest. There are many intrinsic and extrinsic factors 
that may play an influential role on the mechanical properties, deformation/fracture 
behavior and damage evolution process. Most prominent amongst these are processing and 
microstructural effects, and an extremely important aspect of the latter on the deformation 
behavior relates to the volume fraction, length sc ales, physical/mechanical characteristics, 
crystallographic and interfacial aspects of the second-phase intermetallic particles. A detailed 
insight into the effects of these factors, coupl ed with precise models to describe materials 
behavior and to define properties and projections, are just some of the elements that need to 
be addressed.

One of the most important issues that must be investigated is the ability to process 
these materials to yield repr oducible and controllable microstr uctures and in quantities 
adequate for accurate evaluation of properties and phenomena. Although a variety of

Creep behavior is another basic issue rela ting to the design of creep-resistant 
ductile-brittle composite type s ystems and their life prediction. This requires knowledge of 
the creep behavior of the individual constituent pha ses in the system and of the composite 
made up of these phases over a range of temperatures and stresses, together with their 
physical, mechanical, thermodynamic and themo mechanical characteristics/properties. In 
composite systems with microstr ucture composed of a dispersion of hard, intermetallic 
phases in a relatively more ductile matrix, including the one chosen for study in this project, 
i.e. Mo-rich Mo-Si-B, one can envision sev eral possible combinations of materials behavior 
under creep conditions. For instance, for a given stress and temperature, the matrix can be 
deforming in the primary or secondary creep regime, while the second-phase intermetallic 
maybe deforming elastically. By the same token, if the second-phase is also plastic at high 
temperatures, it could be deforming in the prima ry regime while the matrix is in the primary, 
secondary or tertiary regime. It is evident that several such combinations of behavior are 
possible, though the governing constitutive equations will differ for each combination. Apart 
from these, the creep strength, creep process , elastic modulus, ductility and fracture strength 
of the individual phases are also important. Thes e various features will determine whether

Microstructure of the materials and its effects on mechanical properties are also an 
important issue to these types of composites. The investigation subjects include morphology,

interactions between the particles and constrained flow and f racture of the ductile phase

both the interparticle spacing as well the mean free path of dislocations, the materials · 
response to accommodate the compatibility requirements will dominate its strength and 
creep behavior. For the present system, the v olume fraction of the second-phase 
intermetallics may approach 50% and their siz es could be a few microns depending on 
processing and heat treatment, so that p rocesses of cutting and looping of the particles by 
the dislocations may have to be considered.

Chapter 2. BACKGROUND OF PREVIOUS WORK 
 
2.1 Background of Mo-Si-B Alloys 
The successful materials for very high tempe rature structural applications should attain 
a balanced blend of environmental resis tance, low temperature toughness and high 
temperature tensile and creep strength. A few cla sses of materials such as ceramic materials, 
ceramic matrix composites and refractory intermetallic compounds (e.g. Nb3Al, Cr2Nb, Ti5Si3, 
etc.) have been studied in recent years, but limited success has been achieved. From these 
studies, it is increasingly clear that monolithic or composite materials that are based on a 
brittle refractory intermetallic or ceramic matrix as the majority phase are unlikely to satisfy 
the balance in both ambient and elevated temperature properties and environment resistance, 
so that multiphase design approach is essential. Multiphase design exploits the natural phase 
relationship existing in the equilibrium diagram to produce systems w ith microstructures 
composed of a dispersion of hard, intermetallic pa rticles in a refractory, but relatively more 
ductile, solid solution matrix. The re is a great successful history indicating that multiphase 
design in a monolithic material is, by far, the mos t effective and reliable for structural 
components. Actually, the experience with nicke l based superalloys and titanium aluminides 
based on TiAl has again proved that multiphase de sign is the most promising strategy. The 
advantages of multiphase intermetallic alloys can be generally summarized as the following. 
Firstly, thermodynamic and thermochemical comp atibility between the constituent phases is

## Page 20

7 
assured. Secondly, the solid solution on which th e hard and strong intermetallic is based can 
provide ductile-phase toughening and ease of processing. Thirdly, and most importantly, 
microstructure can be manipulated by controlling ch emistry, processing and phase 
transformations either during solidification or in the solid state.

in significant improvements in the high temperatur e oxidation resistance over that of pure 
molybdenum and molybdenum alloys, while maintaining good high temperature tensile 
strength and adequate ambient temperature ductility.

2.3 Oxidation Behavior 
Environmental resistance capability is one of the most important properties for elevated 
temperature materials. The fundamental need fo r environmental resistance is the formation 
of adherent oxide scale under oxidizing condition, so that the integrity of the component 
does not entirely depend on oxidation resistant coating. Studies of the oxidation mechanisms 
of the ơ-Mo+Mo3Si+T2 system have established that the enhanced oxidation resistance is 
provided by the formations of a continuous, st able, protective borosilicate glass layer at 
temperatures between 1000-1300°C, which retards the volatilization of the ơ-Mo phase [10]. 
Studies have shown that the weight change rate of the alloy attained above 1000°C is 
sufficiently slow and thus attractive for high temperature structural applications [2,3]. 
The change in oxidation behavior of ơ-Mo+Mo
3Si+T2 system with increase in 
temperature, which has been extensively investig ated, is shown in Figure 3 and summarized 
as follows. At 350°C Mo begins to oxidize to MoO 3; the MoO3 starts to sublime at ~500°C,

## Page 23

10
and melts at 800°C [12]. At low temperatures from 500 to 600°C, the alloy system oxidizes 
slowly with a small weight gain resulting from the formation of Mo-containing borosilicate 
scale. Further from 600 to 750°C, increasing weight loss occurs with increasing temperature, 
because of the volatilization of MoO 3. Between 800 to 1300°C, the formation of 
borosilicate glass scale begins to provide protection with increa sing temperature, as shown 
by the oxidation mechanism map in Figure 4. In this regime, the process of volatilization of 
MoO3 and formation of borosilicate scale occur simultaneously and continuously compete 
with each other at all temperatures. In the initial stage (~800 to 900°C), MoO 3 volatilization 
is dominant, because the borosilicate glass scal e formed is less viscous, allowing MoO 3 to 
permeate by bubbling through the scale, thus res ulting in a porous structure, which cannot 
prevent the escape of MoO3. In contrast, in the later stage (~1000 to 1300°C), the escape of 
B as B2O3 vapor increases the viscosity of the protective scale so as to retard pore formation 
and reduce the permeation of MoO 3. Therefore, the oxidation resistance capability is 
significantly enhanced by the adherent, pore free and passivating SiO2 scale. 
Unlike most high-temperature materials, there are challenges with oxidation resistance at 
lower temperatures for this alloy system. The temperatures between 800 and 900°C are 
identified as the critical regime, s ince the external borosilicate layer is porous and hence not 
an effective barrier to the escape of MoO 3 vapor. Efforts toward improving the 
low-temperature oxidation resistance are being pursued with alloying additions and coatings 
[9,10].

2.4 Mechanical Properties 
The mechanical properties, including creep, fatigue, fracture toughness, etc., of 
molybdenum and its alloys have been widely studied in order to meet the requirements for a 
variety of applications, such as dies, punche s, furnace components, and so on. With the 
development of ternary Mo-Si-B alloys, the mechanical properties at high temperatures drew 
increasing attention of researchers over the world, and thus there are many reports on both

floating-zone furnace. Deformation of single crystals was performed at 1500°C in five

3.2 Compressive Deformation Test 
Creep rate controlled compressive tests on the samples of the Mo-20Si-10B master alloy 
were conducted at Brown University on a Instr on servo-hydraulic testing machine equipped 
with a high temperature furnace with the strain rate 10 -6, 10-5, and 10-4 s-1 at 1200, 1300, and

to each other, and then placed vertically between silicon carbide platens in the furnace with a

10-6 to 10 -5 s -1 and then to 10 -4 s -1 after each steady load state was reached over a 
strain/deformation of ~4-6%, at each strain rate and the whole test was performed 
continuously at the same temperature; and 2) sing le test, in which a constant strain rate of 
10-5 s -1 was maintained at each temperature. Load-displacement data were monitored and 
recorded and tests were performed till a steady-s tate load with increas ing displacement was 
attained. From the data, the plateau stress, corrected for change in sample dimensions, at 
each strain rate and at a given temperature was determined and then plotted.

4.4 General Discussion 
In this research project, constant strain rate compression tests at 1200, 1300 and 1400°C 
of a nominally Mo-20Si-10B (at.%) alloy, processed to yield five ơ-Mo volume fractions 
from 5 to 46%, were conducted. The results showed that the stresses required to maintain a 
given strain rate increased with a decrease in temperature and generally decreased with an 
increase in the ơ-Mo volume fraction, particularly at 120 0°C. This stress did not vary much 
with ơ-Mo volume fraction at 1400°C a nd the two higher strain rates (10 -4 and 10-5 s-1). The 
stress exponent values were hig h, in the range of 7-9 at 1200°C, but lower at 1300 and 
1400°C, being 5-7 and 3-6, respe ctively. Values of ~7 at 120 0°C and ~4 at 1400°C were 
reported by Alur at et al. [27] in their study of a Mo-6.1Si-7.9B alloy utilizing the similar type 
of constant strain rate compression tests. The values of activation energy were found to vary 
from 200-600 kJ/mol, depe nding on the volume fraction of ơ-Mo and stress level, with the 
higher values being obtained at the lower volume fractions and lower stress levels. 
Further analysis on creep parameters st ress exponent and a ctivation energy was 
performed by taking threshold stress into considera tion at 1200 and 1300°C. The values of 
true stress exponent range from 4.6-5, independent on ơ-Mo volume fraction, which is 
supportive to the power law dislocation mechanism. 
In general, the stress ex ponent and activation energy va lues do indicate that dislocation 
climb-glide mechanisms in the ơ-Mo phase dominate the high temperature deformation 
process. Observations of the micros tructures of the defor med samples provide supporting

## Page 68

55
evidence in the form of refined sub-gra ins and high dislocation density in the ơ-Mo regions, 
whereas the intermetallic particles show little dislocation activity and the presence of cracks. 
As mentioned above, the plateau stresses a re high at 1200°C and show a marked 
increased with an increase in the ơ-Mo volume fraction at the thr ee strain rates studied. The 
stress exponents are also high (7-9). In comparison, the plateau stresses are much lower at 
1400°C and show little or no change with an increase in the ơ-Mo volume fraction, i.e. 
microstructure does not exert much of an effect, and, furtherm ore, the stress exponents are 
also lower (3-6). This difference in behavior is likely to be asso ciated with the relative 
plasticity of the ơ-Mo and intermetallic phases and the effects of temperature on the 
matrix/particle flow stress and plasticity. In other words, the ơ-Mo and intermetallic phases 
exhibit a temperature-dependent flow stress beha vior that is sensitive to strain rate. At 
1200°C, the ơ-Mo is likely to exhibit plasticity, wherea s the intermetallic particles are elastic. 
Under these conditions, the intermetallic partic les would constrain the deformation in the 
ơ-Mo, leading to strain acc umulation/localization, work hardening and higher stresses to 
continue deformation, with recovery proc esses acting to relieve the st rains and balance the 
stress. At 1400°C, on the other hand, the flow stress and work hardening rate of the ơ-Mo 
will be lower and the intermetallic part icles could also be partially plastic. The effect of this 
would reduce the strain localiz ation and stress build-up in the ơ-Mo and intermetallic 
particles and make the deformation more homogene ous and to proceed at lower stresses. 
Further support for these arguments can be f ound in the results of work by Alur et al. [27], 
who based on finite element analysis assuming an elastic-plastic ơ-Mo and elastic

## Page 69

56
intermetallic particles, illustrated that strain localization occurs in the ơ-Mo to a more severe 
extent when the work-hardening rate decreases (i.e. increasing temperature and decreasing 
strain rate) while the intermetallic pa rticles are highly stressed. On the other hand, if plastic 
deformation is permissible in the intermetallic part icles, as would be the case at higher 
temperatures (1400°C) and/or lower strain rates , the strain distribution is homogenized 
substantially and the level of stress build-up in the particles is considerably diminished.

Chapter 5. CONCLUSIONS 
 
The main conclusions arising from this project on the high temperature compression creep 
behavior of Mo-Si-B alloys are outlined below. 
1. The microstructure of these samples is three-phase, consisting of ơ-Mo, Mo 3Si and 
T2-Mo5SiB2 phases, with the volume fraction of the ơ-Mo varying from 5 to ~46%. 
2. The results of constant strain rate compression tes ts show that the stresses required to 
maintain a given strain rate increased with a decrease in temperature and generally 
decrease with an increase in the ơ-Mo volume fraction, partic ularly at 1200°C. This 
stress do not vary much with ơ-Mo volume fraction at 1400°C and the two higher strain 
rates (10-5 and 10-4 s-1). 
3. The values of the stress exponents de termined from the data range from ~3-9, 
depending on temperature and ơ-Mo volume fraction, with those at 1200°C lying 
between 7-9 regardless of the ơ-Mo volume fraction, whereas those at 1400°C increased 
from 3 to ~6 with an increase in ơ-Mo volume fraction. The n values at 1300°C range 
from 5-7, i.e. are between those at 1200 and 1400°C and showed a similar trend with 
ơ-Mo volume fraction as in the 1200°C data. Th ese stress exponent values suggest that 
dislocation climb-glide mechanisms dominate the creep deformation process. 
4. The activation energy, Q, for creep is determined to range from ~200-600 kJ/mol, 
depending on the stress level and volume fraction of ơ-Mo.

## Page 71

58
5. Linear extrapolation technique is used to determine the threshold stress, which is taken 
into consideration at 1200 and 1300° C. The values of threshold stress decrease with an 
increase in temperature, and are higher for the sample with lower ơ-Mo volume fraction. 
6. By incorporating threshold stress, the va lues of true stress exponent, n 0, range from 
4.6-5, and the values of activation energy, Q, become much lower. 
7. OIM observations of the post-test specimens reveal very fine sub-grains (size ~1 Ƭm) in 
the ơ-Mo, which is confirmed by TEM. In addition a high density of 1/2<111> 
dislocations and sub-grain boundaries composed of dislocations are observed in the 
ơ-Mo, which all provide supporting evide nce for the operation of dislocation 
climb-glide mechanisms, consis tent with the measured stress exponents and activation 
energies. 
8. The above results suggest that the bulk of the deformation and strain during high 
temperature compression is carried by the ơ-Mo, with dislocation creep/climb-glide 
mechanisms being operative, with the intermetallic particles providing a varying level of 
constraint to the deformation and strain loca lization depending on temperature and 
strain rate, which is reflected in the flow/plateau stresses and stress exponents.
