Effect of Temperature on Tensile Properties of Laser
Powder Bed Fusion Additively Manufactured
Niobium Alloy C103
ERIC BRIZES, JUSTIN MILNER, ELIZABETH YOUNG-DOHE, ANITA GARG,
and RONALD NOEBE
Niobium (Nb) alloy C103 is commonly used in high-temperature aerospace propulsion
applications due to its strength at temperatures above 1200 /C176 C and low ductile-to-brittle
transition temperature. With the rise of interest in additive manufacturing (AM) for refractory
material processing, it is crucial to ensure that the properties of AM-produced C103 match those
of conventionally processed materials, particularly concerning ductility and high-temperature
performance. This study evaluates C103 produced via laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) by
conducting uniaxial tensile tests from room temperature to 1400 /C176 C in a high-vacuum
environment and comparing the results to those of conventionally processed wrought C103
tested under identical conditions. Results from tensile testing indicated that as-built L-PBF
C103 has comparable tensile properties to wrought (worked) C103, while hot isostatic pressing
(HIP) post-processed L-PBF C103 exhibited similar properties to wrought (recrystallized) C103.
In all conditions, whether L-PBF or wrought, C103 exhibited minima in tensile elongation in the
600–800 /C176 C temperature range; however, this was not accompanied by a change in fracture
mechanism. HIP post-processing marginally improved material density but signiﬁcantly reduced
low-to-intermediate temperature strength due to recovery of the microstructure and grain
growth. These insights into the mechanical behavior of L-PBF C103 enhance conﬁdence in its
use for reliable high-temperature aerospace components.

I. INTRODUCTION
NIOBIUM (Nb) alloy C103 is a refractory material
that has been historically used in high-temperature
aerospace propulsion components. Its ability to main-
tain structural integrity at temperatures exceeding
1200 /C176 C, where nickel-based superalloys fail, makes it
a valuable choice for applications like reaction control
thrusters for orbiting spacecraft, thrust augmenter ﬂaps
in jet engines, and nozzle extensions on upper stage
rocket engines.
[1,2] This alloy comprises niobium with
approximately 10 weight-percent (wt pct) hafnium and
1 wt pct titanium. C103 has a single-phase body-cen-
tered cubic (BCC) structure and exhibits excellent
ductility at room and even cryogenic temperatures, far
exceeding the formability of most other refractory alloy
systems. The superiority of C103 for thin-walled,
high-temperature aerospace structures stems from its
amenability to traditional sheet forming methods like
rolling, deep-drawing, and spinning.
[3]
In recent years, manufacturing technologies have
evolved and changed the focus from wrought processing
of C103 to powder-based methods, including additive
manufacturing (AM) processes such as laser powder bed
fusion (L-PBF), which oﬀer cost-eﬀective production of
near-net shape parts.
[4–6] However, challenges inherent
to AM such as the formation of residual stresses,
microstructural eﬀects including anisotropic grain struc-
tures, and varying levels of interstitial element contam-
ination necessitate careful validation of printed C103
mechanical properties.
[7]
Despite its current use and widespread potential, the
availability of published mechanical property data for
C103 at elevated temperatures is sparse, and for
AM-processed material it is nearly non-existent with
much of the literature coming from manufacturers or
dating back several decades to the 1960s.
[3,8,9] While the

historical studies highlight C103’s versatility, contem-

understanding of C103’s performance, but also provide

This study aims to provide a direct comparison of the
mechanical response of C103 fabricated using L-PBF to
conventionally manufactured wrought C103 material,
focusing on tensile properties as a function of temper-
ature from room temperature to 1400 /C176 C. Key areas of
interest are: (1) the limited data on crucial high-temper-
ature properties ( > 1200 /C176 C), which is essential for
predicting C103’s performance under extreme operating
conditions; (2) the inﬂuence of wrought (worked and
recrystallized) vs powder AM processing techniques and
secondary processing such as hot isostatic pressing
(HIP) on the microstructure and mechanical properties
of C103;
[10] and (3) the variation of ductility surround-
ing a tensile elongation minimum observed around
600–800 /C176 C, raising concerns about potential brittle
failure modes in this intermediate temperature range. [11]
In this work, C103 spherical powder was consolidated
via L-PBF and examined in both the as-built (AB) and
post-HIP conditions. Uniaxial tensile testing from room
temperature to 1400 /C176 C in high-vacuum was employed
to evaluate the mechanical response of L-PBF material
compared to conventionally produced C103 in both the
worked and recrystallized (ReX’d) conditions. Addi-
tionally, characterization of the microstructure and
chemical composition, including interstitial levels,
inform discussion on the inﬂuence of L-PBF and
post-processing on the structure-property relationship
of the printed material. A comprehensive understanding
of the mechanical behavior of additively manufactured
C103 will help provide conﬁdence in the use of printed
components for critical applications and lead to the
development of more eﬃcient and reliable high-temper-
ature systems.
II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
A. Materials and Processing
The L-PBF feedstock used in this study was spherical
C103 powder produced via electrode induction-melting
gas atomization (EIGA) by H.C. Starck and speciﬁed to
a +15/ /C0 53 lm particle size range. Once received, the
particle size distribution (PSD) was measured using a
Horiba Partica LA-950V2 laser scattering analyzer in a
water suspension. The compositions of the starting
powders and consolidated materials were determined by
analytical techniques. Oxygen and nitrogen contents
were measured using a LECO Series 73 6 combustion
analyzer, carbon was measured using a LECO Series
844 combustion analyzer, and the bulk metallic
constituents were measured using a SPECTRO ARCOS
II inductively coupled plasma optical emission spec-
trometer (ICP-OES). The density of the powder particles
was measured using an Anton Paar Ultrapyc 5000
helium gas pycnometer.
L-PBF was performed using an EOS M100 system by
Electro Optical Systems Gmbh (EOS). The system is
equipped with a 200 W ytterbium ﬁber laser (software
limited to 170 W maximum power output) with a spot
size of approximately 40 lm. The C103 builds were
performed in an inert argon atmosphere, where the
oxygen content in the chamber was controlled to less
than 200 ppm. The build plate was composed of pure
niobium and was not actively heated. The processing
parameters for C103 consolidation were optimized in
previous studies by varying the laser power, scan speed
and hatch spacing of cuboid samples
(7 9 7 9 10 mm).
[12] The optimal L-PBF parameters
selected for fabrication of the uniaxial tensile samples
resulted in a volumetric energy density of 169.1 J/mm 3
at a 0.02 mm layer height and produced a material with
a relative density of 99.9 pct or greater. The scan
strategy had a rotation angle of 67 deg between adjacent
layers.
To fabricate the uniaxial tensile samples, cylindrical
blanks were built in a vertical orientation having an
outer diameter of 7 mm and a height of 70 mm.
Fourteen cylindrical blanks were arranged 10 deg askew
of the recoating direction in a grid on the build plate.
Two identical builds were conducted to produce a total
of 28 tensile blanks. Three rectangular blanks and one
feature cube were also built along with the cylindrical
blanks for tensile/creep testing of ﬂat samples and to
serve as a witness specimen, respectively. The results
from the ﬂat samples are not presented in this work. The
total build height of 70 mm required two pauses to reﬁll
the EOS M100 hopper with powder. Reﬁlls of the
hopper did not occur while building the gauge section of
the samples. The blanks were removed from the build
plate via wire electrical discharge machining (EDM). A
subset of the blanks was post-processed by hot isostatic
pressing (HIP) for a duration of four hours at 1500 /C176 C
in argon; samples were wrapped in tantalum foil prior to
the HIP treatment. The as-built (AB) and HIP cylindri-
cal blanks were then machined into round tensile
samples with an overall length of 68.58 mm. The ﬁnal
geometry had a reduced gauge diameter of 3.18 mm, a
gauge length of 19.05 mm, and threaded ends. To serve
as a comparison to the L-PBF C103, conventionally
produced (wrought) C103 rods were also machined into
the same uniaxial tensile geometry. The wrought C103
was tested in both the as-worked (extruded) and ReX’d
conditions and came from two diﬀerent lots of material.
The degree of work resulting from the extrusion process
and the exact recrystallization heat treatment conditions
of the wrought materials are unknown as the materials
were obtained from old stock at NASA Glenn Research
Center.

B. Testing and Characterization
Room-temperature tensile testing was performed in
air on a servo-hydraulic uniaxial load frame with a
constant crosshead velocity of 6.35E /C0 3 mm/s. Strain
was measured using an Epsilon ONE non-contact
optical extensometer. All elevated temperature tensile
testing was performed on an electro-mechanical load
frame equipped with an inert/vacuum chamber contain-
ing a resistance-heated furnace composed of Ta-mesh.
Three R-type thermocouples were tied to the sample
using tantalum wire and the gauge section was covered
in tantalum foil to protect the sample and thermocouple
beads from contamination. The thermocouple on the
center of the sample was used to control the furnace,
and the other two thermocouples were used to monitor
the temperature gradient. Once the samples were loaded
and centered within the furnace, the system was rough
vacuum pumped and purged with argon three times
prior to high-vacuum operation. After the pressure
reached 7E /C0 4 Pa or lower, the sample was heated at a
rate of 17 /C176 C/min. The samples soaked for 20 minutes at
the target test temperature to ensure temperature
uniformity. The temperature gradient across the sample
was generally less than 5 /C176 C. Elevated temperature
tensile testing was also performed at a constant cross-
head velocity of 6.35E /C0 3 mm/s. Total elongation was
measured by total crosshead displacement and com-
pared to a ﬁnal length measurement of the as-tested
sample; the error was determined to be less than 5 pct
between the two measurements. For all tensile tests, the
load was applied parallel to the L-PBF build direction
(Z-direction) or in the extruded direction for the
wrought materials.
After mechanical testing, fracture surfaces of select
samples were imaged using both a Keyence VHX-7000
digital microscope and a Tescan MAIA3 scanning
electron microscope (SEM). Chemical analysis and
metallurgical specimens were harvested from the grip
section of the room-temperature tensile samples. The
specimens were cut, mounted, and polished to view the
microstructure perpendicular to the build direction
(XZ-plane). Optical micrographs were captured and
post-processed in open-source image analysis software
(ImageJ) to determine void fraction, which was used to
estimate material density. The powder morphology, and
the microstructure of the L-PBF and wrought samples
were investigated using a Tescan MAIA3 scanning
electron microscope (SEM). Electron backscatter
diﬀraction (EBSD) measurements were conducted at
an acceleration voltage of 20 keV using an Oxford
NordlysNano detector and reprocessed in AZtec soft-
ware. From a select L-PBF sample, thin electron
transparent foils for transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) examination were extracted using a ZEISS
Auriga focused ion beam scanning electron microscope
(FIB-SEM). TEM was performed using a Thermo Fisher
Talos F200 scanning/transmission electron microscope
(S/TEM).

A. L-PBF Processing
The morphology of the gas atomized C103 powder
feedstock is shown in Figure 1. The powder is mostly
spherical with some irregularly shaped particles and
satellites. The physical properties of the powder are
listed in Table I. The powder particles had a measured
density of 8.833 ± 0.007 g/cm
3, corresponding to a
theoretical density of 99.80 ± 0.08 pct. Particle size
distribution (PSD) analysis resulted in D 10,D 50, and
D90 values of 16.3 lm, 29.2 lm, and 51.5 lm, respec-
tively. The powder ﬂowed well and spread adequately in
the EOS M100 machine.
Figure 2(a) depicts the schematic build layout con-
sisting of 14 tensile blanks with three rectangular blanks
and one feature cube. The completed build of the C103
tensile blanks in the as-fabricated condition are shown
in Figure 2(b). The tensile blanks were built with L-PBF
parameters that resulted in a volumetric energy density
of 169.1 J/mm
3. Table II displays the physical properties
of the L-PBF C103. Occasional porosity-type void
defects were observed in the microstructure. The den-
sity, as measured by the Archimedes method, of the AB
and HIP material both measured 8.822 g/cm
3. This
corresponds to a 99.88 pct relative density as compared
to the as-received powder density measured using a gas
pycnometer. Density was also estimated from polished
cross-sections of the L-PBF samples using image anal-
ysis to determine the area fraction of voids within
optical micrographs. For each condition, 25 micro-
graphs spanning the height of the build taken in the
XZ-plane were analyzed. The density (two-dimensional
defect-free area percent) determined by optical analysis
was 99.92 pct for the AB material and 99.99 pct for
material following the HIP heat treatment.
The determination of density using the Archimedes
method was unable to detect a measurable diﬀerence
between the AB and HIP material. While the overall
level of porosity was very small, measurement of density
using optical techniques based on micrographs of
cross-sectioned samples indicated that the HIP process

element contents ( ~0.23–0.29 wt pct).
The measured chemical compositions for all examined
materials are within the speciﬁcation for C103 except for
the oxygen content of the C103 powder and L-PBF
materials. For wrought products, oxygen content is
speciﬁed to less than 250 ppm because oxygen and other
interstitial elements severely embrittle and signiﬁcantly
impact the workability of C103.
[8] Interstitial oxygen
increases material strength at the expense of ductility.
When exposed to elevated temperatures, hafnium oxide
preferentially forms at grain boundaries.
[12] These
hafnium oxides grow rapidly at temperatures greater
than 900–1000 /C176 C and weaken grain-to-grain adhe-
sion.[14–16] Despite controlling oxygen content to less
than 200 ppm in the build chamber during L-PBF,
oxygen pick-up during processing was not eliminated as
the consolidated material gained 82 ppm of oxygen
relative to the powder feedstock and increased further
during the HIP process.
B. Microstructural Characterization
Microstructural analysis was conducted by EBSD
(Figure 3) on the wrought C103 in the (a) worked and
(b) ReX’d conditions and the L-PBF C103 in the (c) AB
and (d) HIP conditions. For each condition, a band
contrast (left), a band contrast with grain boundary
angle overlay (middle), and an inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF)
map (right) are portrayed. The grain boundary angle
overlay images show the wrought worked and L-PBF
AB materials have a signiﬁcantly greater area of
low-angle ( < 15/C176 ) grain boundaries. Awasthi et al.
[7]
stated that an abundance of low-angle grain boundaries
form in printed C103 due to the high cooling rates in the
L-PBF process. Additionally, the limited driving force
and time for rearrangement of dislocations helps main-
tain a high dislocation density in both the AB and
stress-relieved conditions of L-PBF C103.
[7] These
low-angle boundaries disappear from the microstructure
at exposures above the recrystallization temperature
(e.g., annealing and HIP heat treatments) during a
multi-stage recrystallization and recovery process where
redistribution and annihilation of dislocations causes
the migration of subgrains and low-angle grain bound-
aries into high-angle boundaries as evidenced by the
microstructure of the ReX’d and HIP materials, which
almost exclusively contain higher angle ( > 15/C176 ) grain
boundaries. The IPF maps show that there is a strong
texture in the L-PBF AB material that aligns with the
build direction. This texture is common among L-PBF
consolidated materials as the h001i easy growth direc-
tion in BCC metals orients parallel to the Z-axis of the
build. A similarly strong texture is observed in the
wrought worked material and is aligned with the
direction of extrusion.
Quantiﬁcation of each material’s microstructure is
provided in Table IV. The EBSD average grain statistics
show that the HIP material has a signiﬁcantly larger
ECD than the other materials by an order of magnitude
with a value of 250 lm compared to AB and wrought
C103 materials that have ECD values ranging from
10.35 to 27.84 lm. However, it is anticipated that this
disparity in grain size does not contribute to stark
diﬀerences in elevated temperature mechanical behavior
because niobium and its alloys have a weak relation of
grain size to strength.
[17] The L-PBF AB and wrought
worked materials exhibited greater average grain aspect
ratios (3.70 and 4.43, respectively) compared to the
L-PBF HIP and wrought ReX’d materials (2.23 and
1.54, respectively). The grain orientation spread (GOS,
total misorientation within a grain) and maximum
orientation spread (MOS, maximum angle of deviation
relative to the average crystallographic orientation of all
pixels within a grain) provide insight into the relative
misorientation of the crystal lattices within grains of the
C103 materials. The GOS and MOS of the L-PBF HIP
and wrought ReX’d materials are lower with values of
0.29/1.31/C176 and 0.32/1.38 /C176 , respectively. In contrast the
GOS/MOS values of the L-PBF AB and wrought
worked materials are greater at 2.77/7.40 /C176 and 8.66/
24.21/C176 , respectively. An increased degree of intragran-
ular misorientation typically corresponds with increased
strength in metallic materials as additional energy is
required for dislocations to change directions. This is
particularly important for the strengthening of BCC
refractory metal alloys as work hardening is one of the
primary strengthening mechanisms to promote

process. The high dislocation density, in particular, is
expected to signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the mechanical behav-

increasing the stress needed to overcome the barrier for
dislocation motion and ultimately generate material
plasticity. And despite being observable via secondary
electron imaging in the SEM, grain boundary oxide
phases were not detected in the examined TEM foils.
C. Mechanical Testing

IV. DISCUSSION
A. Relating Microstructure to Mechanical Performance
Regarding the performance of BCC refractory metals,
it’s well known that their strength is signiﬁcantly
inﬂuenced by factors like the extent of work hardening
and the level of impurities, particularly at temperatures
below their recrystallization point. Although average
grain size and shape have implications in other perfor-
mance criteria (such as creep life), for uniaxial tension,
historical reports emphasize that the average grain size
has minimal eﬀect on the strength of Group VB
transition metals comprising niobium- and tanta-
lum-based alloys.
[19,20] On this basis, much of the
analysis of C103 elevated temperature performance has
aimed to relate microstructural features like oxygen/
oxide content and dislocation density to tensile strength.
More recent studies of C103 produced via AM
processes generally agree that L-PBF C103 exhibits
higher YS and UTS at room and elevated temperature
compared to its wrought counterparts.
[4,7] The mecha-
nisms behind this strengthening are not fully under-
stood. Philips et al. discussed the role of hafnium oxide
particles observed in EB-PBF C103. [21] In their study, it
was suggested that hafnium-rich oxides primarily
located along grain boundaries did not signiﬁcantly
impact strength. The submicron-sized oxides, unlike in
powder metal HIP and wrought preparations, remained
on the grain boundaries, and minimally aﬀected dislo-
cation pinning and material strengthening. It was a
conclusion of Philips et al. that the oxygen speciﬁcation
limit ( < 250 ppm) for wrought materials may not be
applicable to AM-processed materials.
[21] However, the
eﬀect of exceeding this limit on AM C103 mechanical
properties is still unclear. Awasthi et al. attributed the
increased strength of the L-PBF C103 material to the
high dislocation density and therefore low work hard-
ening capacity.
[7] Their TEM analysis linked high
dislocation density from rapid cooling during the AM
process and a low mean free path for dislocation glide to
greater barriers for dislocation motion, explaining the
higher strength but lower ductility of stress-relieved
L-PBF C103 compared to wrought C103.
[7] This obser-
vation is consistent with microstructural features and
mechanical properties of other alloys systems, such as
the high static strength reported by Aydino ¨z et al. in
L-PBF AB nickel alloy 718. [22]
In this work, the higher YS observed in the wrought
worked and L-PBF AB materials can again be
attributed to the same factors described in the previous
studies: work hardening for the wrought worked mate-
rial and a high dislocation density for the L-PBF AB
material. The wrought worked material maintains its
strength until it reaches its recrystallization temperature,
approximately 1000 /C176 C, after which it softens due to
increased dislocation movement and the initiation of
climb as the dominant deformation mechanism.

Similarly, the L-PBF AB material exhibits increased
strength due to its high dislocation density. The contri-

content, likely occupying interstitial lattice sites within
the Nb matrix, on L-PBF C103 strengthening has yet to
be quantiﬁed.
On the other hand, both the wrought ReX’d and
L-PBF HIP materials show lower YS across the entire
temperature range examined, as neither work hardening
nor high dislocation density strengthening mechanisms
are in play. The primary distinction between the ReX’d
and HIP materials lies in their grain size/shape and
chemical composition. The L-PBF HIP sample has
almost ten times greater ECD (250.3 lm) compared to
the wrought ReX’d material (27.84 lm). With respect to
composition, the wrought ReX’d material has a higher
Zr content (0.29 wt pct) and lower oxygen content
(74 ppm) compared to the L-PBF HIP material
(0.02 wt pct Zr, 595 ppm O). This diﬀerence resulted
in a trend of higher YS/UTS and lower TE across the
evaluated temperature range for the L-PBF HIP mate-
rial. As larger grain sizes typically reduce material
strength and have a minimal eﬀect on Nb-based alloys,
it is anticipated that the higher O content of the L-PBF
HIP material and the presence of hafnium oxide
precipitates throughout the microstructure likely inﬂu-
enced the observed increase in strength and decrease in
elongation compared to the ReX’d material.
B. Understanding the Elongation Minimum
To assess the risk of brittle failure modes in C103,
Wood investigated the unexplained minimum ductility,
which is reported to occur around 760 /C176 C, reducing
elongation at failure to as low as 15 pct.
[11] Wood
concluded that dynamic strain aging is the likely cause
of reduced ductility at elevated temperatures, as
indicated by the presence of Lu ¨ders Bands and
Portevin-Le Cha ˆ telier eﬀects in the stress-stroke
curves.
[11] Dynamic strain aging occurs when small
solute atoms are attracted to dislocations creating
Cottrell atmospheres along those dislocations. Larger
forces can pull the dislocations free of these atmo-
spheres, allowing yielding. However, the presence of
solute atoms slows dislocation movement, increasing
drag, and causing irregular movement and unstable ser-
rated ﬂow. Higher temperatures accelerate solute atom
diﬀusion, promoting this phenomenon and enhancing
the dynamic strain-aging eﬀect up until the recrystal-
lization temperature is reached.
In this work, all four material conditions showed their
lowest TE in the 600–800 /C176 C temperature range. Among
the wrought materials, the worked C103 displayed a
more pronounced decrease in TE compared to the
recrystallized material (11 pct TE compared to 27 pct
TE), which also occurred at a lower temperature
(~ 650 /C176 C vs ~ 750 /C176 C, determined from a 3rd order
polynomial line of ﬁt of tests in the 400–1000 /C176 C
temperature range). This trend is also observable in
the L-PBF consolidated materials, where AB had a TE
minimum of 15 pct, while HIP L-PBF material had
22 pct. Pinpointing the exact temperature of the TE
minimum is not possible with L-PBF material due to
limited data for AB material and consistently low
ductility of the HIP material between 500 and
1000 /C176 C. With regard to the ‘‘ductility’’ minimum
discussed by prior work, the fracture surface images
(Figure 7) and RA data (Table V) do not show a change
to a more brittle failure mode or decreased RA and
therefore ‘‘ductility’’ may be a misnomer. The material
remains very ductile, with ductility determined by tensile
elongation being a somewhat misleading indication due
to the degree of localized deformation that occurs in
material. These observations imply that deformation/

C. On the Impact of HIP Post-processing
NASA’s current manufacturing standards require hot
isostatic pressing or high-pressure heat treatment for all
additively manufactured spaceﬂight hardware.
[23] This is
to ensure uniformity in microstructure and increased
material density, aiming to enhance component consis-
tency, reduce system failures, and most importantly
ensure mission safety. While HIP is commonly used for
other high-temperature alloy systems like Ni- and
Ti-based alloys, our study on elevated temperature
mechanical testing reveals signiﬁcant changes in the
microstructure of L-PBF C103 due to HIP. This results
in a reduction in material strength by a factor of 2–3
over the temperature range from room temperature to
nearly 1100 /C176 C. Also, despite eﬀorts such as careful
cleaning and wrapping in Ta foil, the material still
accumulates interstitial elements during the process. It
can be argued that the HIP parameters used in this study
were not optimized, and there might exist an ideal
processing window to minimize strength loss, limit grain
growth, and close voids. However, the high pressure
required for void closure seems impractical under
typical operating conditions for C103, weakening the
AB L-PBF material unnecessarily.

V. CONCLUSIONS
This study was undertaken to determine the basic
tensile mechanical properties L-PBF Nb C103 as a
function of temperature and to determine how they
compare to typical commercial wrought material.
Through the consolidation of C103 spherical powder
via L-PBF and subsequent assessment in both AB and
post-HIP conditions, this research employed elevated
temperature uniaxial tensile testing in a high-vacuum
environment to compare the mechanical response of
L-PBF material with conventionally produced C103 in
both worked and ReX’d conditions. From this study,
the following conclusions can be drawn:
1. From room temperature up to 1400 /C176 C, L-PBF
consolidated C103 in the AB condition (512 ppm O,
22.23 lm ECD) has YS and UTS values similar to
wrought C103 in the as-worked condition (105 ppm
O, 10.35 lm ECD). The high dislocation density of
the L-PBF AB material (verified via TEM) con-
tributed to the increased strength of the rapidly
solidified AM microstructure.
2. L-PBF C103 following HIP post-processing
(595 ppm O, 250.3 lm ECD) has similar room
and elevated temperature mechanical properties to
wrought C103 in the ReX’d condition (74 ppm O,
27.84 lm ECD). The greater interstitial element
content and the presence of agglomerated hafnium
oxide particles throughout the L-PBF HIP
microstructure resulted in lower tensile elongations
and increased strength compared to its wrought
ReX’d counterpart.
3. The elevated temperature tensile elongation mini-
mum that appears in the 600–800 /C176 C temperature
range is due to deformation localization and is not
observed when using RA as the ductility measure-
ment. For C103 in the wrought worked condition,
fractography showed consistent highly ductile fail-
ure with dimpled rupture from room temperature
through the minimum and into the elevated tem-
perature regime (20 /C176 C, 600 /C176 C, and 1000 /C176 C).
4. HIP post-processing of L-PBF consolidated C103
only marginally improved material density
(99.99 pct vs 99.92 pct), but significantly reduced
low and intermediate temperature strength
(40–60 pct reduction in YS and 30–40 pct reduction
in UTS at temperatures below 1000 /C176 C). Hafnium
oxides coarsened during HIP, but did not impede
grain boundary motion as grains grew around oxide
particles.
