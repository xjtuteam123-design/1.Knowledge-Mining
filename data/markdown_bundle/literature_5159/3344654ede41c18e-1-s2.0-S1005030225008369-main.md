a b s t r a c t 
The ﬁne and uniform lamellar microstructure plays a crucial role in mitigating the room-temperature 
brittleness of TiAl alloys. However, such ﬁne microstructures often suffer from insuﬃcient temperature- 
bearing capacity, and addressing the trade-off between room-temperature (RT) plasticity and high- 
temperature performance is essential for the broader industrial application of TiAl alloys. This study 
combines powder metallurgy and composite processes to develop a ﬁne lamellar microstructure in high 
Nb-TiAl alloys, featuring a synergistic distribution of Si atoms and precipitated phases (TiB/Ti5 Si3 ). The 
TiB phases serve as nucleation sites, promoting equiaxed γ grains at lamellar colony interfaces and re- 
ﬁning the colony structure. This composite lamellar microstructure greatly improves strength and plas- 
ticity at both room and elevated temperatures. Notably, the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl-based composite exhibited a 
sixfold enhancement in room-temperature plasticity compared to the sintered high Nb-TiAl alloy, with 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) rising from 608 MPa to 745 MPa. At 900 °C, the composites retained 
an outstanding UTS of 585 MPa, while the fracture strain was increased to 18.8%. These improvements 
are attributed to the reﬁned lamellar colony, twin/twin intersections during plastic deformation, and 
the formation of stacking faults, all of which enhance work-hardening ability. Additionally, Si solid so- 
lution strengthening, precipitate phase strengthening, and their role in hindering dynamic recrystal- 
lization signiﬁcantly improve high-temperature strength. This microstructural design offers a promising 
approach for developing TiAl-based composites possessing remarkable room and elevated-temperature 
properties.

1. Introduction 
TiAl intermetallic compounds, as an attractive alternative 
to nickel-based high-temperature alloys, offer comparable high- 
temperature performance while possessing signiﬁcantly lower den- 
sity [ 1 ]. These materials have already been successfully imple- 
mented in components such as low-pressure turbine blades in 
aero-engines [ 2 ]. In addition to their lightweight nature, which 
contributes to reduced energy consumption, TiAl alloys also of- 
fer superior high-temperature performance, including excellent ox- 
idation and creep resistance [ 3 ]. In the aerospace industry, the 
TNM (Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B, at.%) and 4822 (Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb, 
at.%) alloys are the most widely applied [ 4 , 5 ]. However, due to its

The low room-temperature plasticity of TiAl alloys is primarily

In industrial applications, thermal deformation processes, such as 
forging, are commonly used to break large grains and reﬁne the 
microstructure, followed by heat treatment to achieve the desired 
lamellar structure [ 9 ]. Emerging fabrication methods, such as addi- 
tive manufacturing, offer the advantage of fast cooling, which pre- 
vents grain growth and typically results in a ﬁne lamellar struc- 
ture [ 10 ]. However, due to temperature ﬁeld inhomogeneity, these

metallurgy offer a uniform, ﬁne microstructure with no segrega-

ment to regulate the microstructure, making them a promising op- 
tion for TiAl alloy part fabrication [ 13 ]. Unfortunately, although 
the ﬁne lamellar microstructure improves the room-temperature 
plasticity, it usually faces the problem of insuﬃcient temperature- 
carrying capacity, which is common to ﬁne-grained microstructure 
in metallic materials [ 14 , 15 ]. Thus, resolving the trade-off between 
RT plasticity and elevated-temperature strength is critical to accel- 
erating the industrial application of TiAl alloys. 
To achieve higher temperature resistance of TiAl alloys, re- 
searchers have employed techniques like alloying and compositing 
to enhance their high-temperature mechanical properties by op- 
timizing microstructure [ 16 , 17 ]. Alloying typically involves adding 
refractory elements like Mo, Nb, and Re [ 18–20 ]. However, these el- 
ements are often β-stabilizers, which can introduce hard and brit- 
tle B2 phases into the microstructure, negatively affecting room- 
temperature plasticity [ 16 ]. Additionally, these elements are costly 
and increase the density of TiAl, which counteracts the goal of 
developing low-cost, lightweight alloys. Notably, the introduction 
of the second-phase particles in TiAl alloys for compositing may 
not only enhance the temperature-bearing capacity, but also re- 
ﬁne the microstructure [ 21 ]. Ceramic particles such as Ti2 AlC, TiB, 
and Y2 O3 , which are lightweight and low-cost, are ideal for com- 
positing [ 22–24 ]. Compositing can also facilitate the design of 
novel microstructures by controlling the distribution of the sec- 
ond phase, such as diffuse, reticulate, or core-shell structures [ 25 ]. 
However, it also brings a new question, i.e., the TiAl alloy it- 
self does not have high plasticity, will the ceramic phase intro- 
duced by compositing exacerbate the brittleness? Studies by Guo 
et al. on Y2 O3 -composited TiAl alloys demonstrated that nano-Y2 O3 
not only improves room-temperature plasticity but also enhances 
strength at 800 and 900 °C [ 24 ]. In contrast, Sun and Yang et al. re- 
ported increased high-temperature strength but observed negligi- 
ble improvement in room-temperature plasticity after compositing 
[ 26 , 27 ]. The impact of compositing on room-temperature plastic- 
ity remains a subject of debate. Therefore, further investigation is 
needed to fully elucidate the impact of ceramic phase composit- 
ing on the microstructure and mechanical performances of TiAl 
alloys. 
In this study, we attempted to develop new microstructures of 
TiAl-based composites combining excellent RT plasticity and high- 
temperature strength by introducing ceramic phases on the basis 
of ﬁne uniform lamellar microstructures prepared by powder met- 
allurgy. Through low-energy ball-milling mixing combined with 
hot-press sintering, SiB6 was introduced into TiAl alloys. A new 
lamellar microstructure combining diffuse solid solution of Si and 
reticular distribution of precipitated phases (TiB and Ti5 Si3 ) was 
designed by taking advantage of the decomposition and diffusion 
of SiB6 at high temperatures and its reaction with TiAl matrix. The 
TiAl-based composites with this microstructure have a plasticity of 
more than 1.2% at RT and maintain high strengths at high tem- 
peratures (706 MPa at 800 °C and 585 MPa at 900 °C), which 
address the insuﬃcient temperature-bearing capacity of ﬁne-grain 
material (strength < 500 MPa). The microstructure evolution after 
compositing was investigated using SEM and TEM, while HRTEM 
provided detailed insights into the interfacial characteristics and 
crystallographic structure of the precipitated phases. Additionally, 
the deformation mechanisms at both room and high temperatures, 
as well as the toughening mechanisms, were explored. This study 
presents a promising approach to overcoming the trade-off be- 
tween RT plasticity and high-temperature strength, offering a path- 
way to optimise the comprehensive mechanical performance of 
TiAl alloys. 
2. Materials and methods 
The study’s raw materials included SiB6 powder (particle size: 
D 50 = 1 μm) sourced from Beijing Zhongke Yannuo New Material 
Technology Co., Ltd., and Ti-45Al-8Nb (at.%) pre-alloyed powder 
(nominal composition, particle size: 45–150 μm) obtained from 
Xi’an Sailon Additive Manufacturing Co., Ltd. Three types of Ti- 
45Al-8Nb- x SiB6 ( x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, at.%) mixed powders with varying 
SiB6 contents were prepared via low-energy ball milling. 
Low-energy ball milling was conducted employing an omni- 
directional ball mill, equipped with vacuum-sealed stainless steel 
milling jars (100 mL) and ZrO2 grinding balls ( d = 5 mm). All 
milling operations were carried out for 8 h at 120 r/min while 
maintaining a 4:1 ball-to-material ratio. To prevent oxidation, pow- 
der weighing and jar encapsulation were carried out in an argon- 
protected glove box. 
Ti-45Al-8Nb pre-alloyed powder and ball-milled Ti-45Al-8Nb- 
x SiB6 ( x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, at.%) mixed powders were sintered us- 
ing a high-vacuum inductive hot-press furnace (RYL-25-19, Boyun- 
tong, China). A cylindrical graphite mold (45 mm inner diameter) 
was loaded with the powder and subjected to simultaneous heat- 
ing and uniaxial pressurization. Sintering was conducted at 1425 
°C for 30 min at 50 MPa pressure. The resulting cylindrical com- 
pacts were 45 mm in diameter and 8 mm thick. The sintered billet 
preparation process is shown in Fig. S1 in Supplementary Informa- 
tion. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) was utilized to 
analyze the phase compositions and crystal structures of both the 
raw powders and sintered samples (scanning angle range: 20 °–
90 °, scanning speed: 5 °/min). Microstructural characterization of 
the raw powders, TiAl alloys, and their composites was carried 
out via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Gemini 450, Zeiss, Ger- 
many) in electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, Symmetry S2, Ox- 
ford) and backscattered electron (BSE). Electron channeling con- 
trast imaging (ECCI) obtained in BSE mode at 20 kV, 2 nA. EBSD 
data were processed with Aztec Crystal software. Electron probe 
microanalyzer (EPMA, EPMA-1720H, Shimadzu, Japan) was em- 
ployed for micro-zone chemical composition analysis. Detailed mi- 
crostructural and phase analyses were performed through trans- 
mission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA) in con- 
junction with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). 
Powder specimens were vibrationally polished to eliminate 
residual surface stresses for microstructure observation. A mixture 
of perchloric acid, n-butanol, and methanol (1:7:12) was used to 
electrolytically polish sintered samples. The electrolytic polishing 
parameters were set at 30 V, −30 °C, and 60 s. TEM samples were 
prepared via ion thinning. Lamellar colony sizes were quantita- 
tively measured from SEM images using the line-intercept method 
with Image-Pro Plus software. 
Tensile test was carried out three times per sample at a room 
temperature strain rate of 1 × 10−4 s−1 , while tests conducted at 
80 0 and 90 0 °C used a strain rate of 5 × 10−4 s−1 . Strain mea- 
surements were obtained using an extensometer. Specimens had 
a gauge size of 10 mm × 2.5 mm × 1.2 mm. The tensile sample 
preparation and test schematic are shown in Fig. S2.

3.3. Phase analysis and elemental distribution 
Fig. 4 shows XRD analysis of sintered TiAl alloy and its com- 
posites. The TiAl alloy mainly consisted of the tetragonal γ phase, 
as well as a small amount of the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) α2 
phase. It indicates the α → α + γ → α2 + γ phase transition 
took place in the furnace cooling process after 30 min of sinter- 
ing in the single-phase α region, where slower cooling would lead 
to a complete α → γ phase transformation. The introduction of 
SiB6 did not signiﬁcantly alter the phase composition of the TiAl- 
based composites, with the γ phase remaining dominant and the 
diffraction peaks of the α2 phase only observed near 41 °. Although 
boride particles and rods were visible in the BSE maps of the com- 
posites ( Fig. 2 ), no corresponding diffraction peaks were detected 
by XRD, likely due to the low volume of boride phases. Further- 
more, the addition of SiB6 introduces the Si element, which ei- 
ther solid-solves in the TiAl alloy matrix or precipitates as silicides 
[ 34 , 35 ]. Generally, the α2 phase shows higher Si solubility than the 
γ phase [ 36 ]. The absence of silicide peaks, combined with the α2 
peak shifted to lower angles ( Fig. 4 ), indicates Si atoms are mainly 
present as interstitial solid solutions in the TiAl matrix. 
Fig. 5 illustrates the surface elemental distribution maps, ob- 
tained via EPMA, for both the TiAl alloy and 0.2SiB6 /TiAl compos- 
ite. In the as-sintered TiAl alloy, Ti, Al, and Nb were uniformly 
dispersed ( Fig. 5 (a–d)). By contrast, for the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite 
( Fig. 5 (e–j)), Ti and Si were uniformly distributed within the ma- 
trix. Notably, Nb and B were enriched along the lamellar colony in- 
terface, whereas Al was relatively deﬁcient in these regions, form- 
ing a discontinuous network distribution. This distribution aligned 
with the granular and rod-like precipitates observed in the BSE 
image, further conﬁrming the presence of borides. In addition, to 
further conﬁrm the existence of Si, the lamellar colony boundaries 
were enlarged ( Fig. 5 (k)). A small amount of Si can be found to be 
point-like scattered enrichment. Incorporating the XRD results re- 
vealed that in the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite, part of the Si was solidly 
dissolved in the matrix, while some precipitated as silicides. To 
summarize, γ , α2 , borides, and silicides made up the majority of 
the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite’s phase composition. However, further 
identiﬁcation of the speciﬁc borides and silicides requires detailed 
TEM analysis.

3.4. The interface and orientation relationship 
Fig. 7 (a) shows the BF image containing ( α2 + γ) lamellae, TiB, 
and Ti5 Si3 . The rod-like TiB phase traversed the α2 and γ lamellae, 
with numerous stacking faults visible within the TiB phase. Nano- 
sized Ti5 Si3 precipitate was located at the intersection of TiB, α2 , 
and γ phases. Detailed EDS data in Fig. 7 (b) further conﬁrmed the 
presence of nanosilicides. 
To further clarify the interface and orientation relationship 
among the matrix and precipitated phases, the blue-boxed region 
containing Ti5 Si3 in Fig. 7 (a) was analyzed using HRTEM. Fig. 7 (c) 
shows the corresponding high-resolution plot, with its fast Fourier 
transform (FFT) displayed in Fig. 7 (d). The spot calibration results 
revealed that the (0 0 02) plane of Ti5 Si3 was parallel to the (0 0 02) 
plane of α2 and the (111) plane of γ phase, i.e., (0 0 02)Ti5 Si3 || 
(0 0 02) α2 || (111) γ. The enlarged image of the Ti5 Si3 and γ phase 
interface shown in Fig. 7 (e) further conﬁrmed that (0 0 02)Ti5 Si3 || 
(111) γ, where the lattice spacing of (111) γ was d = 0.238 nm, and 
the lattice spacing of (0 0 02)Ti5 Si3 was d = 0.267 nm. The atomic 
arrangement at the interface indicated that γ had a coherent in- 
terface with Ti5 Si3 [ 41 ], with a lattice mismatch of 0.12. This sug- 
gested that the lattice distortion at the interface between γ and 
Ti5 Si3 was small. Fig. 7 (f) illustrates a magniﬁed image of the inter- 
face between Ti5 Si3 and α2 , where the (0 0 02) α2 plane was paral- 
lel to the (0 0 02)Ti5 Si3 plane, and the spacing of the grain planes of 
Ti5 Si3 ( d = 0.267 nm) was slightly larger than that of α2 ( d = 0.235 
nm). 
Fig. 7 (g) shows HRTEM images of the interface between TiB 
and the γ/ α2 matrix phases. The HRTEM of TiB conﬁrmed the 
high density of stacking faults within it. The SAED in the corre- 
sponding region of Fig. 7 (g) indicated that the orientation rela- 
tionship between TiB and α2 was: (100)TiB || ( ¯1 010) α2 , [0 ¯1 1]TiB 
|| [1 ¯2 10] α2 ( Fig. 7 (h)). The enlarged atomic arrangement ( Fig. 7 (i)) 
further illuminated the (0 0 02) α2 || (111) γ crystallographic rela- 
tionship among the α2 and γ lamellae. 
3.5. Tensile properties at RT, 800 °C, and 900 °C 
Fig. 8 (a) demonstrates the RT tensile properties of sintered TiAl 
alloy and its composites. With the increase in SiB6 addition from 
0 at.% to 0.2 at.%, the UTS of the TiAl-based composites improved 
from 608 MPa to 745 MPa while plasticity improved from 0.2% to 
1.2%. The room-temperature strength and plasticity were signiﬁ- 
cantly enhanced, with plasticity increasing by 6 times, overcoming 
the limitation of TiAl-based composites, which previously lacked 
room-temperature plasticity [ 25 ]. However, further increasing the 
SiB6 content to 0.3 at.% led to a decrease in UTS and plasticity,

4. Discussion 
In order to reveal the reason why the microstructure-optimized 
0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite has both room-temperature plasticity and 
high-temperature strength, the formation and deformation mecha- 
nisms of the composites were analyzed in detail. The strengthening 
mechanism of compositing was discussed. 
4.1. Microstructure evolution mechanisms 
Fig. 10 presents a schematic diagram illustrating the microstruc- 
ture evolution mechanism of sintered TiAl alloy and its composites. 
Pre-alloyed powders contain a large proportion of the α2 phase 
and a minor proportion of the γ phase, which disappears upon 
reaching the sintering temperature ( > α phase transition temper- 
ature) during the heating process. This phase transition is domi- 
nated by the α2 + γ → α + γ and α + γ → α processes, re- 
sulting in the complete conversion of the powder into the high- 
temperature α phase. Upon applying pressure, powders progres- 
sively densify and form equiaxial α grains. As the furnace cools to 
RT after a pressure holding stage, the lamellar structure forms due 
to the α → α + γ → α2 + γ transitions. The hot pressing and 
sintering process facilitate element diffusion, effectively homoge- 
nizing the powder. Consequently, the segregation in the powder 
( Fig. 1 (i)) is eliminated, and the ﬁnal sintered billet exhibits an FL 
microstructure with a uniform composition. 
When SiB6 powder is added to prepare composites, in addition 
to the phase transformation processes occurring during sintering, 
the decomposition of SiB6 to generate Si and B atoms also takes 
place. Part of the Si and B atoms dissolves solidly into the matrix 
phase, while the excess atoms that surpass the solid solubility limit 
react with the matrix, producing TiB and Ti5 Si3 precipitated phases 
[ 34 , 35 , 60 ]. Due to the grain-reﬁning effect of elemental B, the α
phase’s growing is constrained, while the TiB phase pins the grain 
boundaries, effectively reducing the size of the equiaxial α grains 
[ 61 , 62 ]. Since grain boundaries contain much more defects than 
the grain interiors, the precipitated phases primarily form along 
the grain boundaries. Furthermore, the TiB phase formation pro- 
cess (Ti3 Al + 2B → TiAl + 2TiB) depletes Ti atoms in the localized 
Ti3 Al matrix around it, resulting in the creation of γ-TiAl phase. 
Meanwhile, the TiB phase also acts as a γ nucleation point to pro- 
mote the formation of equiaxed γ grains around it. A similar ob- 
servation has been noted in forged TiAl alloys [ 63 , 64 ]. This explains 
the increase of equiaxial γ along grain boundaries with a higher 
content of TiB precipitated phases. It is also one of the inspirations 
for the design of near lamellar structural composite designs that 
combine excellent room temperature plasticity and strength in this 
paper. Notably, the results from EPMA ( Fig. 5 ) and TEM ( Figs. 6 and 
7 ) clearly show that the TiB phase is enriched with Nb elements, 
which not only facilitates the elimination of β-partialization in the 
powder during sintering, but also Nb’s partial substitution of Ti 
atoms contributes to improve the stability of the B27 structure’s 
TiB phase [ 65 ]. As widely recognized, the introduction of Si into 
TiAl alloys improves their high-temperature mechanical properties 
through solid solution strengthening, and the maximum solution 
limit of Si in TiAl alloys generally being less than 0.2 at.% [ 36 ]. 
Therefore, the precipitation of Ti5 Si3 observed in this study is rel- 
atively small. Finally, a nearly lamellar TiAl-based composite, con- 
taining a signiﬁcant volume of TiB phase, a little of Ti5 Si3 phase,

4.2. Deformation mechanisms of sintered TiAl alloy and its composite 
at RT 
Fig. 11 demonstrates the TEM characterization results of the sin- 
tered TiAl alloy and its composite adjacent to the RT tensile frac- 
ture. A few dislocations were observed in the γ laths and numer- 
ous dislocations were entangled at the α2 / γ interface in the de- 
formed TiAl alloy ( Fig. 11 (a)). No twinning or stacking faults were 
detected. This conﬁrms dislocation slip as the dominant deforma- 
tion mechanism at RT. Fig. 11 (b, c) displays the RT deformation 
microstructures near the TiB precipitate phase in the TiAl com- 
posites. The equiaxed γ grains showed high dislocation densities, 
and in general the slip of 1/2[ ¯1 10] ordinary dislocations dominated 
the plastic deformation of the γ phase [ 66 ]. Dislocation entangle- 
ments and stacking faults appeared around the TiB precipitates, in- 
dicating that the precipitated phase hindered dislocation slip, and 
the resulting stress concentration further promoted the formation 
of stacking faults [ 67 ]. These stacking faults were mainly formed 
by the decomposition of 1/2[ ¯1 10] dislocations, and the speciﬁc re- 
action was: 1/2[ ¯1 10] → 1/6[ ¯2 11] + SF + 1/6[ ¯1 2 ¯1 ] [ 68 ]. Fig. 11 (d) 
illustrates the deformation within the composite lamellae, where 
dislocations, SFs, and twins collectively dominated the deforma- 
tion of the γ lamellae. In particular, the SFs and deformation twins 
were transmitted across the γ/ γ interface to the adjacent laths. 
The SAED pattern of the corresponding junction, shown in the red 
circle, conﬁrmed the presence of γ twins, and the three diffrac- 
tion spot sets indicated that the neighboring γ laths also exhib- 
ited twinning ( Fig. 11 (e)). It is commonly recognized that multi- 
ple γ variants exist in TiAl alloys, which typically exhibit pseudo- 
twinning relationships with the γ matrix [ 69 ]. Additionally, high- 
density twin intersections were discovered in the deformation mi- 
crostructure adjacent to the lamellar colony boundary ( Fig. 11 (f, 
g), taken from two different locations), with high-density disloca- 
tions trapped at the intersecting twins. The SAED pattern at the 
intersecting twins, shown in Fig. 11 (h), again revealed three sets 
of diffraction spots for the γ phase: one for the γ matrix and the 
others for the two twins. HRTEM analysis at the intersections fur- 
ther conﬁrmed the twin intersections ( Fig. 11 (i–l)). 
In summary, the deformation behavior of sintered TiAl alloy 
and its composite is strongly inﬂuenced by their microstructures 
at room temperature. In TiAl alloy, deformation primarily occurs 
through the slip of dislocations within the γ lamellae. Compara- 
tively, the deformation mechanisms in the composites involve dis- 
location slip, stacking faults, deformation twinning, and twin inter- 
sections.

4.4. Compositing improves RT strength and plasticity 
In the sintered full lamellar TiAl alloy ( Fig. 11 (a)), room- 
temperature plastic deformation primarily depends on dislocation 
slip within the γ lamellae. Dislocation plugging at the γ/ γ and 
α2 / γ interfaces can cause stress concentration due to the hin- 
dering effect of dislocation slip. In the absence of coordinated 
deformation mechanisms such as twinning and stacking faults, 
these interfaces are susceptible to premature cracking. Addition- 
ally, the lamellar colony interface is a critical site for dislocation 
plugging, where high stress concentrations can lead to premature 
cracking along these boundaries, ultimately reducing both room- 
temperature strength and plasticity. 
However, in TiAl-based composites, plastic deformation occurs 
not only through slip within the γ lamellae but also within 
equiaxed γ grains near reinforcing phases at the lamellar colony 
interface. The γ grains with tetragonal structure drive more slip 
systems at room temperature compared to the HCP α phase, which 
can effectively coordinate deformation between lamellar colonies

by facilitating dislocation slip transfer across the interfaces, thus 
alleviating stress concentrations and improving plasticity [ 71 ]. Fur- 
thermore, high-strength precipitated phases within γ grains or at 
interfaces hinder dislocation motion in equiaxed γ grains, signiﬁ- 
cantly enhancing strength. 
More importantly, room-temperature deformation of TiAl-based 
composites generates numerous deformation twins and stacking 
faults. The twinning induced plasticity (TWIP) effect, known for 
strengthening and toughening metallic materials like steel [ 72 ] and 
high-entropy alloys [ 73 ], also signiﬁcantly improves the mechanical 
performance of TiAl alloys [ 74 ]. As illustrated in Fig. 11 (d), disloca- 
tion plugging at the lamella interface induces stress concentrations, 
in which 1/2[ ¯1 10] ordinary dislocations decompose to form stack- 
ing faults, and 1/6 < 112 > dislocations shear to form deformation 
twins on the {111} planes [ 68 ]. These twins coordinate deforma- 
tion within the γ lamellae, and the transfer of stacking faults and 
deformation twins between neighboring γ lamellae further coordi- 
nates the plastic deformation, thereby releasing stress and delaying 
lamellar cracking. 
In the equiaxed γ grains, which contain many precipitated 
phases at the lamellar colony interface ( Fig. 11 (c)), dislocation 
entanglement near these precipitates promotes the formation of 
stacking faults, which further mitigate cracking and enhance plas- 
tic deformation [ 67 ]. High-density twin intersections within the 
equiaxed γ grains are an important source of plastic deformation. 
These intersecting twins subdivide the grains into smaller ones, 
effectively limiting dislocation mobility. Additionally, the twin in- 
terfaces act as barriers, leading to a massive dislocation plug, 
which signiﬁcantly enhances the material’s work-hardening capac- 
ity [ 68 ]. This results in outstanding room-temperature tensile plas- 
ticity for TiAl-based composites. In addition to improving plasticity, 
the stacking faults and twins, due to their hindrance of dislocation 
slip, interact with the dynamic Hall-Petch effect to strengthen TiAl- 
based composites [ 75 ]. 
Ultimately, the cooperative interaction between dislocations, 
stacking faults, deformation twins, and precipitated phases over- 
comes the strong-plastic mismatch problem in TiAl-based com- 
posites. The reﬁnement of lamellar colonies and the formation of 
equiaxed γ grains due to the introduction of precipitated phases 
provide an excellent microstructural foundation for both plastic de- 
formation and material strengthening, leading to TiAl-based com- 
posites that exhibit both outstanding room-temperature plasticity 
and high strength. 
4.5. Compositing inhibits high-temperature softening and enhances 
high-temperature strength 
Dynamic recrystallization serves as a key mechanism respon- 
sible for the softening of TiAl alloys at high temperatures, signif- 
icantly inﬂuencing their high-temperature mechanical properties 
[ 76 ]. The mechanism of dynamic recrystallization is grain bound- 
ary migration controlled by atomic diffusion [ 68 ]. Therefore, in- 
hibiting interfacial migration can effectively suppress the alloy’s 
high-temperature softening [ 77 ]. In this study, the introduction of 
borides at the lamellar colony interface, while contributing to pre- 
cipitation strengthening and grain reﬁnement, effectively pins the 
migration of the lamellar colony interface during high-temperature 
deformation, thereby inhibiting dynamic recrystallization. Conse- 
quently, the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite retains 585 MPa strength at 
900 °C. Fig. 13 illustrates the true stress-strain graph and the work- 
hardening rate graph of the 0.2SiB6 /TiAl composite during tensile 
testing at 900 °C. The true stress-strain graph reveals two distinct 
stages: microstrain work-hardening and the onset of dynamic re- 
crystallization. In the ﬁrst stage ( E < εc ), stress rises sharply with- 
out recrystallization. In the second stage ( E > εc ), dynamic recrys- 
tallization begins, yet work-hardening remains dominant despite

As the stress reaches its peak, recrystallization dominates, causing

To further explore the material’s ability to maintain high 
strength at elevated temperatures, the work-hardening rate graph

In summary, the combined effects of inhibiting dynamic re- 
crystallization and enhancing work-hardening capacity signiﬁcantly 
improve the softening resistance and high-temperature strength of 
the composites. 
5. Conclusions 
A novel approach to fabricating high-performance SiB6 /TiAl 
composites using powder metallurgy was proposed, achieving 
an innovative microstructure that signiﬁcantly enhanced both RT 
strength-ductility and high-temperature mechanical performances. 
The inﬂuence of SiB6 on the microstructure evolution of sintered 
TiAl alloy and its composites was thoroughly investigated. Further- 
more, the deformation mechanisms at room and high temperatures 
along with the strengthening and toughening mechanisms, were 
systematically explored. The following summarizes the main con- 
clusions: 
(1) This study signiﬁcantly optimized the microstructure of TiAl 
alloys through compositing, building upon the coarse full- 
lamellar microstructure produced by powder metallurgy. In TiAl 
alloy, a ﬁne lamellar microstructure with diffuse distribution 
of Si atoms and reticular synergistic distribution of precipita- 
tion phases (TiB/Ti5 Si3 ) was formed. The TiB phase acted as 
a nucleation site, reﬁning the lamellar colonies and introduc- 
ing equiaxed γ-grains at the interfaces between these colonies. 
This approach effectively adjusted the TiAl matrix into a ﬁne, 
near-lamellar microstructure, providing a new strategy for de- 
veloping high-performance TiAl-based composites. 
(2) TiAl-based composites synergistically reinforced by solid solu- 
tion of Si atoms and reticulation-distributed TiB/Ti5 Si3 precipi- 
tated phase exhibit excellent RT plasticity and high temperature 
strength. The RT plasticity of 1.2% is 6 times that of sintered

TiAl alloy, with UTS increasing from 608 MPa to 745 MPa. No- 
tably, at 900 °C, the UTS maintains 585 MPa, while the fracture 
strain is increased to 18.8%. This performance overcomes the

typically observed in ﬁne-grained materials. 
(3) The innovative microstructure combining networked precip-

stress concentration at the interfaces between lamellar colonies. 
γ-grains, through dislocation slip transfer between adja-

deformation. Additionally, the introduction of precipitated
