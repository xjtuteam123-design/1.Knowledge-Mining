Elastic parameters of various rafted structures of model Ni-base alloys

1Institute of Physics of Materials, Academy of Sciences of the Czech Republic,

Keywords: Rafting; Elasticity; Ni-base superalloys; Monte Carlo 
Abstract. Two-phase microstructure of ordered cube-shaped precipitates in the disordered matrix is 
characteristic of Ni-base superalloys. This microstructure degrades under the applied stress: 
depending on the stress direction, lattice misfit and elastic parameters of both constituent phases, the 
precipitates coalesce and change their overall shape. Various atomic configurations were modeled in 
this work representing various morphologies of precipitates developed under applied stress. A 
model Ni-base alloy containing six alloying elements typical of advanced Ni-based superalloys was 
used. Generated configurations were further subject to study of elastic parameters by means of 
computer straining experiments. Relaxation of atomic positions in the strained crystal blocks was 
implemented using molecular dynamics calculations with phenomenological Lennard-Jones pair 
potentials and interactions involving three coordination spheres. Changes of elastic parameters due 
to varying precipitates morphology are discussed. 
Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys are important engineering materials used for the most demanding applications, 
e.g. as first stage gas turbine blades [1]. Their microstructure consists of coherent cube-shaped 
precipitates of phase γ′ (L1 
2 ordered lattice) separated by thin channels of phase γ (disordered fcc 
lattice). The size, morphology and volume fraction of the precipitates control the mechanical 
properties of superalloys. Hence the study and understanding the microstructure evolution under 
working conditions is of practical importance. The directional coarsening of γ′ precipitates 
(‘rafting’) under stress at high temperatures (around 1000 °C) is a well known phenomenon since 
60ies [2] and it has been extensively studied both experimentally and theoretically. The crucial 
parameters influencing the rafting morphology and kinetics are the sign and intensity of the applied 
stress, the temperature, the lattice misfit between precipitates and matrix and the difference between 
elastic constants of the two phases [3,4]. The γ′ cubes evolve gradually to transverse plates (rafted 
structure) in a superalloy crystal with negative misfit deformed in tension or in a crystal with 
positive misfit deformed in compression. The opposite two combinations of misfit and stress sign 
lead to forming the longitudinal columns along the stress direction [5]. The rafting under applied 
stress occurs together with regular coarsening at elevated temperatures. The whole process is 
accompanied by an increase of γ volume fraction (the thermodynamic equilibrium being both 
temperature- and stress-dependent). 
The rafted single crystal looses its cubic symmetry and can be regarded as an elastic body of 
tetragonal symmetry [6]. In this work the elastic parameters are calculated for a series of rafted 
geometries using atomistic simulations.

Crystal straining. At first, a calculation of an equilibrium lattice parameter was performed for each 
of the seven tested atomic configurations. Pair interaction energies were calculated up to the third 
co-ordination sphere using phenomenological Lennard-Jones potentials of 8-4 type with parameters 
calculated by Enomoto and Harada [8]. The uniaxial deformation with zero lateral pressure (tensile 
test) was applied along low-index directions [100] and [010].The total energy E of the crystal was 
calculated for eleven discrete values of engineering deformation e between –5% and +5%. The 
dilatation (resp. expansion) of the material in planes perpendicular to tensile (resp. compressive) 
axis was calculated by minimizing the total energy and the Poisson ratios were evaluated. The 
locally relaxed atomic positions were found in every calculation step by means of molecular 
dynamics (200 runs with time interval 2.5 fs). The energy-strain curves were fitted by a fourth order 
polynomial function. For elastic reversible deformation, the work done by the applied stress is equal 
to the increase in the internal energy. Hence, in the limits of linear elasticity, elastic constants are 
proportional to second partial derivatives of the energy at equilibrium unstressed state (first partials 
being zero). 
Results and Discussion 
It was shown earlier [9] that (besides the global relaxation of transversal dimensions) the relaxation 
of local atomic positions in course of straining experiments is vital for an accurate evaluation of 
system energy and derivative parameters. Fig. 1 shows an example of calculated energy changes for 
straining of the symmetric structure (4,4,4) deformed uniaxially along [100]. The curves compare 
the results of calculations on a rigid lattice (top curve), after relaxing transversal crystal dimensions 
(middle curve) and after complete relaxation including local atomic positions (bottom curve). 
Elastic parameters evaluated from the curves are summarized (relative to (4,4,4) structure) in Table 
1 for all modeled cases and for both straining directions. The values obtained with pure constituent 
phases are added for comparison and further discussion. It is seen from Table 1, that the changes in 
elastic parameters caused by γ′ shape changes are very small, as well as the differences between 
elastic parameters along [100] and [010] directions. From this point of view the tetragonal 
symmetry of modeled rafted structures can be regarded to a good approximation as a cubic one.

Summary 
Elastic parameters were calculated using atomistic simulations for a series of geometries 
representing directionally coarsened Ni-base superalloys. Due to shape changes of γ′ precipitates, 
superalloy single crystals loose their cubic symmetry. However, a relative small anisotropy of elastic 
parameters was found in the modeled structures, meaning the symmetry (from viewpoint of elastic 
parameters ) can be still taken as nearly cubic. It was found that the Poisson ratio is 0.363±0.001 in 
all studied two-phase structures.
