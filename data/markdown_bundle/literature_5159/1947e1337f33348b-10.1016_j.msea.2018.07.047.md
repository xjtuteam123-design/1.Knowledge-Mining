ABSTRACT
The eﬀ ect of chemical composition on microstructure and mechanical properties of heavily alloyed beta-tita-
nium Ti-Nb(V)-Cu-Co-Al alloys was studied. The alloys were fabricated by casting into a water-cooled copper
crucible employing relatively high cooling rates. The microstructure of these alloys consists of primary micro-
meter-sized bcc-structured (bcc – body centered cubic) dendrites surrounded by a minor amount of intermetallic
phases. The morphology and volume fraction of the intermetallic phases are strongly a ﬀected by the alloys ’
chemical composition. Particularly, the solubility of Cu and Co in the bcc dendrites of Ti-V-Cu-Co-Al is lower
compared to that of Ti-Nb-Cu-Co-Al leading to a higher volume fraction of the intermetallic phase in the latter
alloy. The high mechanical strength of the Ti-Nb(V)-Cu-Co-Al alloys (yield strength up to 1430 MPa) is mainly
attributed to their multiphase nature and solid solution hardening of the supersaturated bcc-structured den-
drites. Moreover, the large compressive plastic deformability supported by pronounced strain-hardening reaches
several tens of percent. The alloys exhibit a signi ﬁcant strength asymmetry between compressive and tensile
loadings, namely, they are weak and brittle under tensile loading. The tensile brittleness is associated with the
lattice distortion in the bcc-structured dendrites as well as crack initiation at the interdendritic precipitates.
1. Introduction
Common structural materials are at their limits in many applica-
tions and there is a demand on new high-performance materials. The
performance of materials can be improved by designing composite
materials exhibiting better properties than a single constituent does
[1–5] or using novel fabrication methods for common materials [6–10]
or tailoring non-equilibrium microstructures [11–15] or designing
complex microstructural architectures [16–18]. Among others, the
heavily alloyed bcc-structured titanium alloys demonstrate an out-
standing strength characteristics similar to those found for metallic
glass matrix composites [19,20]. These bcc-structured titanium alloys
typically exhibit fracture stresses higher than 2 GPa and reach plastic
strains larger than 15% under room temperature compression [21–23].
These multicomponent alloys consist of ﬁve and more constituent ele-
ments. The concentration of each component is typically not less than
5 at%. The as-cast microstructure of such alloys exhibits a dendritic
morphology. The dendrites are surrounded by eutectic structures or
intermetallics [19,24,25]. The high strength is mainly due to solid so-
lution strengthening of the bcc-structured dendrites [26] and ﬁne in-
terdendritic precipitates or eutectic structures [27]. Above a critical
volume fraction of the interdendritic precipitates, these alloys become
brittle under tensile loading while being highly deformable under
compression [27,28]. Therefore, volume fraction optimization for the
interdendritic precipitates is required to reach a combination of high
strength and tensile ductility in these alloys [19,29,30].

In this work, we have developed two heavily alloyed bcc-structured
titanium alloys: Ti 66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 and Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 (at%).
The alloys were designed using the principles of microstructure ad-
justment to reach high yield strength [29] and based on recently re-
ported high-strength Ti-Nb-Cu-Co-Al titanium alloys exhibiting large
tensile ductility [19,30]. The alloying elements in these alloys can be
divided into two types: elements that preferentially dissolve in the
dendrites – dendritic elements, and elements that are mostly present in
the interdendritic regions – interdendritic elements. Upon solidiﬁ ca-
tion, the primary dendrites mainly contain elements such as Nb, V, and
Al, while such elements as Ni, Cu, and Co partially segregate to the
interdendritic region to form intermetallic phases [19,25,27]. We have
substituted Nb by V to tune the chemical composition of the primary
dendrites and to study the e ﬀect of the resulting microstructure on the
mechanical behavior of these alloys.
2. Materials and methods
The alloys ingots were fabricated from pure elements (99.9 wt%) by
arc-melting under argon atmosphere. At ﬁrst, Ti and Nb as well as Ti
and V were alloyed to produce intermediate alloys and then the re-
maining pure metals were added and melted together. The ingots were
remelted at least three times to obtain chemical homogeneity. As next,
the master alloys were cast into a water-cooled Cu crucible (12 mm in
diameter) to obtain 100 mm rod samples that were used for the sub-
sequent investigation.
X-ray di ﬀractometry (XRD) (STOE STADIP) with Mo-K
α1 radiation
together with the X′ Pert High Score Plus software was used for struc-
tural investigation of samples. XRD patterns were analyzed by a com-
bination of Rietveld (for bcc phases) and LeBail (for TiCo-type inter-
metallics) reﬁnements using FullProf. Peak pro ﬁles were modeled using
the asymmetric pseudo-Voigt function implementation proposed by
Finger, Cox and Jephcoat [31] superimposed on a stepwise linear
background. Bcc phases were set up as random solid solutions with site

(coherent scattering domain) size and isotropic (long range) lattice

end, the observed pro ﬁle breadths were corrected for instrumental
broadening using a Si-powder standard. To accurately model the Bragg

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 presents the experimental and re ﬁned XRD patterns of the
Ti66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 and Ti66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloys. In both alloys 3
phases are present, namely two bcc-structured (later bcc, space group:
Im-3 m) Ti phases of di ﬀerent unit cell size and a simple cubic TiCo-type
intermetallic phase (later TiCo, space group: Pm-3m)( Fig. 1). Forma-
tion of the TiCo intermetallic phase in Ti-Nb-Co-Cu-Al alloys was pre-
viously reported in [19,30].

being stronger in the Nb containing variety than in the V containing

larger strains in the Nb alloy. Table 1 compares the microstructural

the re ﬂection broadening represents deviations from the mean lattice
parameter that occur over several unit cells. This implies a long-range
character giving rise to domains (or grains) of di ﬀering lattice para-

(segregations, concentration gradients) due to the dendritic
solidiﬁcation. The static displacements related to U
iso on the other hand
are local on the atomic scale. Atomic size mismatch in a solid solution
may be visible through U

correlation between compositional complexity and the overall atomic
displacement parameters U
iso exists [34,35]. It is key to note that the
overall Uiso contain both thermal motion and local static displacement
about the mean position (given by the lattice parameter). The values
reported for the two alloys studied here ( Table 1) are similar to those
found in other HEAs [36].
Similar to the analysis by Maiti and Steurer [36] we estimate the
static component of U

the measured overall Uiso given in Table 1. First, we approximate the
thermal contribution for each alloy by forming the average of the
thermal U

sponding elemental mole fraction. This yields an estimated thermal
displacement of 0.0067 Å 2 for the Nb-variety and 0.0069 Å 2 for the V-
variety. The di ﬀerential to the total overall Uiso may be attributed to
local displacements from static disorder caused by e.g. atomic size
mismatch. The corresponding values are given in Table 1 and range
from 1 to 2.3 ∙10−2 Å2, which are comparable to those for bcc TaNbHfZr
[36].

Fig. 3 shows the elemental distribution in the studied alloys. Some
alloying elements, so-called interdendritic elements, such as Cu and Co
are mainly located in the interdendritic areas while other elements, so-
called dendritic elements, such as Nb, V and Al are dissolved in the
dendrites. However, the dendritic phases of both alloys also contain Cu
and Co, as can be seen from Table 2.
It is well known, e.g. from equilibrium phase diagrams, that Cu is
immiscible with both Nb and V at equilibrium conditions and room
temperature [38]. Obviously, presence of Cu in the dendrites ( Table 2 )
can be explained by the high cooling rate during sample fabrication.
The concentration of these interdendritic elements (Cu and Co) in the
dendrites of the Ti
66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy is about twice larger com-
pared to that of Ti 66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2. This fact explains the relatively
low volume fraction of the intermetallic TiCo phase in
Ti66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2. The primary dendrites precipitate and grow while
their solidiﬁcation front pushes out Cu and Co into the remaining melt.
The TiCo precipitates once the concentrations of Cu and Co reach a
certain composition. Since the solubility of Cu and Co in the dendrites
of Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 is higher, the dendrites reach larger sizes until
the required chemical composition in the remaining melt is achieved.

This is in accordance with the observed microstructure ( Fig. 2). The
intermetallic TiCo phase is a solid solution mainly containing Cu and a
minor concentration of “dendritic elements”. The latter is probably due
to the fabrication of samples under non-equilibrium conditions, i.e.
with high cooling rates.
EDX analysis indicates that concentration of each of the ﬁve ele-
ments present in the bcc dendrites of Ti
66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy is not
less than 5 at% ( Table 2). This manifests a similarity between the cur-
rent β-titanium alloys and high-entropy alloys [39]. The multi-
component composition of the bcc dendrites in the current titanium
alloys leads to severe lattice-distortion evidenced by broadening of the
corresponding X-ray di ﬀraction peaks ( Fig. 1). The severe lattice dis-
tortion is also observed in high-entropy alloys and used to explain their
high strength, particularly for bcc-structured high-entropy alloys [40].
The mechanical behavior of the current high-strength titanium alloys is
discussed below.
Fig. 4 shows the stress-strain curves of the current alloys probed
under both compressive and tensile loading. Under compression, the
alloys demonstrate high yield strength values of about 1350 and
1430 MPa for Ti
66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 and Ti 66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2, respec-
tively, combined with large deformability ( Table 3). In contrast, under
tension, the current alloys are weak and brittle and exhibit premature
fracture before yielding ( Fig. 4 ). The high compressive strength of the
current titanium alloys is supposedly to be related to the solid solution
strengthening, the multiphase structure of alloys and the lattice dis-
tortion e ﬀects. To discover the origin of the brittleness of the alloys
under tension, the microstructure of the tested tensile samples was
analyzed by scanning electron microscopy. As a typical example, the
ﬁndings for the Ti
66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy will be discussed in the
following in more detail.
Fig. 5 displays the microstructure of a polished
Ti66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 tensile sample after fracture. There are a few
large cracks visible on the surface; one of those caused rupture of the
sample ( Fig. 5a). The crack path consists of numerous kink events
(Fig. 5b - d). At the same time, the bcc dendritic phase shows some
plastic deformation by slip in the areas close to the path of the crack
(Fig. 5c). Detailed microstructural analysis of the blunted crack tip
(Fig. 5d) reveals numerous small cracks at the interface between the bcc
dendritic and intermetallic TiCo phases as well as in the interior of the
intermetallic TiCo phase. These observations indicate that the crack
leading to the ﬁnal fracture followed the well-connected network of
intermetallic particles observed previously for di ﬀerent heavily alloyed
titanium alloys [27,41]. Unlike in Ti
66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2, the TiCo
particles in the Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy are hardly interconnected
(Fig. 2 d). However, this alloy fractures even at lower stresses under
tensile loading. This suggests that tensile brittleness of the current al-
loys is partially related to the lattice distortion e ﬀect similar to that in
high-entropy alloys.
Analysis of fracture surface reveals more details about the origin of
the failure. In particular, the inset of Fig. 6a shows that the fracture in
the Ti
66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy originates from microstructural defects
such as pores. Such defects were not found on the fracture surface of the
Ti
66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy. This suggests that fracture in the latter case
originated from sites with high stress concentration, e.g. sharp edges.
The morphology of the fracture surfaces is notably di ﬀerent for both
alloys. In the case of Ti
66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2, the fracture features are
much ﬁner as compared to that of Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2. This is due to
the ﬁner microstructure as well as the larger volume fraction of the
interdendritic eutectics in the Ti 66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy. The inter-
dendritic eutectic structures contribute to crack de ﬂections. On the
contrary, the cleaved grains of the Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 alloy are
smooth enough to resolve ﬁne microstructural features ( Fig. 6d inset).
The morphology of the fracture surface of Ti 66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 shows
clearly distinguishable cleavage steps and feather patterns ( Fig. 6c and
d), as it is typical for brittle cleavage fracture [42].
4. Conclusion
In summary, we have designed two heavily alloyed bcc-structured
titanium alloys, namely Ti 66Nb13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2 and
Ti66V13Cu8Co6.8Al6.2. These alloys mainly consist of bcc dendritic
crystals and a minor amount of the intermetallic TiCo phase. The TiCo
phase is homogeneously distributed along the grain boundaries of the
dendrites. The multicomponent composition of the bcc dendrites leads
to severe lattice-distortion in the bcc phase evidenced by broadening of
the corresponding X-ray di ﬀraction peaks. The compression fracture
strength values of the alloys exceed 2 GPa while their compression
deformation values reach up to 47%. Alloying by Nb results in slightly
higher yield strength values (1430 MPa) of the Ti-Nb-Cu-Co-Al alloy as
compared to that of the V-alloyed one (1350 MPa). Although both al-
loys are highly deformable under compression, these are extremely
brittle under tensile loading. Our results prove that the excellent me-
chanical properties under compressive loading of these metastable
materials cannot be used to predict the deformation behavior under
tensile loading. The tensile brittleness is associated with the lattice
distortion e ﬀect of the bcc-structured dendrites as well as crack initia-
tion at the interdendritic precipitates. However, further understanding
of the tensile deformation mechanisms at the nanoscale is required to
design tensile ductility while keeping high strength in these heavily
alloyed titanium alloys.
