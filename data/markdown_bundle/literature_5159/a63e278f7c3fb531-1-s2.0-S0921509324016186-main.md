ABSTRACT
High-speed extrudability is critical for the efficient production of metallic extruded products. This study employs 
a differential-thermal (DT) extrusion method to achieve an ultra-high extrusion speed of 83 m/min for the newly 
developed Mg-5Bi-3Al-1Zn (BAZ531, wt%) alloy while maintaining good surface quality. Finite element simu -
lations confirmed that the DT extrusion heat remained below the alloy ’ s initial melting point, preventing surface 
cracks. The DT process refined the average grain size to 15.2 μ m, 0.6 times that of the equal-thermal (ET) 
extruded alloy, by delaying dynamic recrystallization due to a lower temperature increase. The BAZ531-DT alloy 
exhibited excellent mechanical properties, with a tensile yield strength of 283 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 
331 MPa, and elongation-to-failure of 19 %. Grain refinement and strong texture enhanced strength, while 
refined grains improved ductility by effectively suppressing twinning. This work demonstrates significant po -
tential for advancing high-speed extrusion of Mg-Bi-based alloys with superior mechanical performance.
1. Introduction
Wrought magnesium (Mg) alloys, as compared to cast Mg alloys, 
have significant potential in various industries, e.g., aerospace, auto -
motive, and electronics. Extrusion processing is a common method to 
fabricate various Mg products (e.g., plate, bar, and profile) with high 
dimensional accuracy [ 1 ]. Extrusion parameters determine the micro -
structures and the corresponding mechanical properties of Mg extruded 
products to a great extent. For the past few years, numerous researchers 
devoted to developing high-performance commercial Mg alloys through 
controlling the extrusion parameters. For example, Lv et al. [ 2 ] reported 
a high tensile yield strength (TYS of ~400 MPa) ZK60 alloy prepared by 
conventional extrusion at 180 
◦

alloy that was extruded at 10 m/min. Similar cases also occurred in

grain size, along with the unexpected TYS of ~283 MPa and the high EL

new level in high-speed-extruded Mg alloys helping to expand their

3. Results and discussion
3.1. High-speed extrudability
Fig. 1 c shows the macro-profiles of the BAZ531-ET/DT alloys. It can 
be seen that the BAZ531 billet exhibits considerable deformation ca -
pacity at a larger extrusion ratio of 278:1 far higher than that in the 
literature [ 7 – 18 ]. By the views of large magnification ( Fig. 1 d and e), 
there are obvious hot cracks at the surface of the BAZ531-ET alloy; while 
for the BAZ531-DT alloy, it shows a good surface quality without hot 
cracking defects, indicating that the DT extrusion technique is an 
effective approach to break through a new record for 
high-speed-extruded Mg-Bi-based alloys at a die-exit speed of 83 m/min.
The OM observations of the BAZ531-ET alloy surface are illustrated 
in Fig. 1 f – h. Coarse primary cracks nucleate at the edge of the bar due to 
larger friction heat between the extrusion die and billet, and these cracks 
propagate towards the center with a vertical depth of ~400 μ m ( Fig. 1 f). 
Also, they are at a ~60
◦
angle to the ED. Wang et al. [ 19 ] also observed 
the oxidation and periodic cracks in an as-extruded Mg-Gd-Y-Zn-Mn 
alloy profile, which was mainly attributed to local melting and 
re-solidification in the profile surface, because of the higher surface 
temperature than the melting point of the alloy. In Fig. 1 g, we can find a 
large number of fine micro-cracks around coarse primary cracks. The 
average length of them is about 25 μ m. From the large-magnified view, 
these fine micro-cracks mainly occur at GBs and extend along GBs 
( Fig. 1 h). Such inter-granular cracking typically occurs during 
high-temperature deformation, as the GB strength is lower than that of 
the intra-granular regions in this context [ 20 ].
To better compare the temperature variations of alloy surface during 
the ET and DT extrusion processes, the corresponding FE simulations 
were conducted in Fig. 2 . In our previous work [ 17 ], it has been proved 
that the FE simulation of the extrusion process is highly reliable for the 
BAZ531 alloy, compared to the experiment process at different 
conditions. In this work, Fig. 2 a and b shows the visualizations of the ET 
and DT extrusion simulation processes. With the billets are extruded to 
the die-exit, the surface temperature gradually increases according to 
the visual result from gray (low temperature) to yellow (high tempera -
ture). When the billet is away from the die-exit, the surface temperature 
has a descending trend with a transition from yellow to gray. By a 
careful view, the surface temperature increment (yellow near the 
die-exit) during the ET extrusion process seems to be higher than that 
(orange near the die-exit) during the DT extrusion process.
Fig. 2 c illustrates the results of point traces from P1 and P2 (marked 
by Fig. 1 a and b) that are located at the positions deviated 8 mm from 
the center to the edge of the billet. The temperature raises very slowly at 
the early stage of the ET and DT extrusion processes (before 0.8s). When 
the billet is extruded near/to the die-exit (0.8 – 1.1s), there is a sharp 
increase in the temperature. For the ET extrusion process, the maximum 
value of the surface temperature of the alloy reaches 573 
◦
C, while it is 
534 
◦
C for the DT extrusion process. At the last stage of the ET and DT 
extrusion processes (after 1.1s), the surface temperatures gradually drop 
due to heat exchange with the air. Based to the DSC curve of the as- 
homogenized BAZ531 alloy, as shown in Fig. 2 d, its initial melting 
point is 556 – 561 
◦
C. Based on the simulation result of the ET extrusion 
process, the maximum surface temperature (573

hot cracks on the alloy surface at such high die-exit speed.

3.2.2. Microstructure observation at area B
Fig. 5 shows the microstructures of the ET and DT extrusion billets at 
area B. When the extrusion process proceeds to area B from area A, those 
lenticular twins “ disappear ” and are replaced by the generation of DRX, 
as illustrated in Fig. 5 a and d. It has been reported that 86
◦
{10 – 12} 
extension twin boundaries are mobile and can relieve stress concentra -
tion by their motion during the deformation [ 24 , 25 ]. Therefore, with the 
extrusion proceeds, 86

{10 – 12} extension twin boundaries move, 
which causes the growth of twins. As the twins grow to a certain extent, 
their boundaries overlap with GBs, resulting in the illusion that these 
twins disappear. Such a twin growth phenomenon has also been 
observed at the intermediate stage of the extrusion process in a 
Mg-0.3Ce (wt%) alloy, showing a complete twinning process [ 26 ].
The DRXed grains (with a grain orientation spread (GOS) less than 2
◦
[ 27 ]) of the ET and DT extrusion billets, extracted from Fig. 5 a and c, 
display in Fig. 5 b and d, respectively. Their area fractions account for 
~58.9 and ~32.2 % of the whole ( Fig. 5 e), respectively. With the DRX 
generates the average grain sizes of the whole extrusion billets decrease 
significantly to ~53.4 and ~84.6 μ m ( Fig. 5 e), respectively. Undoubt -
edly, more DRX fraction gives rise to more obvious grain refinement.
Fig. 5 f and h illustrate the pole figures of the ET and DT extrusion 
billets, respectively. As we see, there is a significant transition from a 
random crystal orientation at area A to an approximate basal fiber 
texture with [ 11 – 20 ] double components at area B. Meanwhile, the 
corresponding maximum pole intensities of the extrusion billets sharply 
reduce at area B compared to area A. Owing to more DRX fraction, the 
maximum pole intensity of the ET extrusion billet (~10.2) is lower than 
that of the DT extrusion billet (~14.9). Fig. 5 g and i correspond to the 
pole figures of the extracted DRX regions in the ET and DT extrusion 
billets, respectively. A weak and dispersive approximate basal fiber 
texture is observed at each DRX region in the extrusion billets. There -
fore, it is reasonable to believe that the DRX causes the texture change.

a high tendency of DRX during hot deformation [ 28 ]. Continuous DRX 
(CDRX) and discontinuous DRX (DDRX) are typical DRX mechanisms.

to a strain-induced boundary migration (SIBM) mechanism [ 31 ]. The 
original flat GBs migrate to the side with high dislocation density, 
inducing a crystalline core. With the extrusion process, numerous new 
crystalline cores are generated at the GBs and grow into chains. Fig. 7 c

ET and DT extrusion billets at the (0001) pole figures, respectively. 
Similarly, the c-axis of the DRXed grains are also approximately 
perpendicular to the ED, meaning that the GB bulging nucleation 
mechanism is beneficial for forming the approximate basal fiber texture 
in the ET and DT extrusion billets.
Fig. 8 demonstrates the PSN nucleation of the DDRX mechanism. The 
area around the large-sized second phases (beyond 1 μ m) is extracted in 
Fig. 8 a and b (the ET extrusion billet) or Fig. 5 e and f (the DT extrusion 
billet). These second phases result from the fragmentation of residual 
eutectic compounds that existed in the as-homogenized BAZ531 alloy 
during the extrusion [ 17 ]. Around the second phase, we find a certain 
number of fine grains distinguished as the DRXed grains ( Fig. 8 c and g), 
as a typical PSN nucleation. Robson et al. [ 32 ] pointed out that dislo -
cations are easy to accumulate surrounding the large particles ( > 1 μ m) 
to form sub-boundaries during the hot deformation, and subsequently 
these sub-boundaries rapidly migrate leading to the creation of new 
HAGBs. The orientation distributions of the DRXed grains resulted from 
the PSN nucleation at the (0001) pole figures in the ET and DT extrusion 
billets are shown in Fig. 8 d and h, respectively. Unlike the previous 
reports [ 21 , 29 , 30 ], in this work, the DRXed grains nucleated by the PSN 
have the characteristics of basal fiber texture. So, the PSN nucleation 
provides an alternative channel to generate the approximate basal fiber 
texture in the ET and DT extrusion billets.

3.2.3. Microstructure observation at areas C and D
Fig. 9 shows the microstructures of the ET and DT extrusion billets at 
area C. As the extrusion process proceeds to area C from area B, as seen 
in Fig. 9 a and c, the area fraction of the deformed grains gradually de -
creases in each extrusion billet. More regions are occupied by the DRXed 
grains, which are extracted in Fig. 9 b and d. Through the statistical 
analysis ( Fig. 9 e), the area fractions of the DRXed grains are ~80.6 and 
~73.8 % in the ET and DT extrusion billets, respectively. The corre -
sponding average grain sizes become ~17.0 and ~19.8 μ m. With the 
DRX, the texture type remains while the maximum pole intensity de -
creases in the ET and DT extrusion billets at area C ( Fig. 9 f and h) 
compared to area B ( Fig. 5 f and h). Due to more DRX fraction, the ET 
extrusion billet has a lower texture intensity (9.8) than the DT extrusion 
billet (11.9). Meanwhile, there is a similar case for the texture in the 
DRXed grains of the ET and DT extrusion billets ( Fig. 9 g and i).
Fig. 10 shows the microstructures of the ET and DT extrusion billets 
at area D. As displayed in Fig. 10 a and c, the deformed grains are 
eliminated from area C to area D, accompanied by a complete DRX 
structure in each extrusion billet. However, the grain size heterogeneity 
is more significant in the ET extrusion billet, where the area fraction of 
coarse grains is higher than that in the DT extrusion billet ( Fig. 10 b and 
d). The average grain size of the former (14.8 μ m) is larger than that of 
the latter (11.3 μ m), as shown in Fig. 10 e and f. These results mean that 
the ET extrusion billet takes place the DRX growth in advance, because 
the extrusion heat is higher than that in the DT extrusion billet at area D 
(die-exit), as shown in Fig. 2 c. In addition, we also find that the 
maximum pole intensity never decreases with increasing DRX fraction, 
but increases to 10.3 from 9.8 in the ET extrusion billet ( Fig. 10 g), as a 
result of the DRX growth that induces a texture strengthening reported 
by the extensive literature [ 35 ]. Unlike the former, the DT extrusion 
billet continuously presents a reduction in the maximum pole intensity 
to 7.5 from 11.9 ( Fig. 10 h).
3.2.4. Microstructure observation at area E
The microstructures of the ET and DT extrusion billets at area E away 
2000 mm from the die-exit are shown in Fig. 11 . Compared with area D, 
the DRX growth for the extrusion billets further occurs at area E, because 
of the residual extrusion heat. Herein the average grain size of the ET 
extrusion billet reaches about 23.8 μ m, which is still larger than that of 
the DT extrusion billet (approximately 15.2 μ m) in Fig. 11 a – d. A similar 
grain refinement effect was also observed in a BA53 alloy subjected to

Even though the increased strength of the alloy generally gives rise to 
the decreased elongation, the DT alloy bar has a higher EL than the ET 
alloy bar despite its higher TYS and UTS. The SF acts as a bridge between 
the texture and the ductility of Mg alloys. High SF for basal slip is an 
indicator of high room-temperature ductility [ 41 ]. In this work, the ET 
and DT alloy bars show a similar SF for basal slip (0.13), when they are 
applied to the tensile loads along the ED. As a result, texture is not the 
main reason causing the EL difference between the ET and DT alloy bars. 
In addition, Huang et al. [ 42 ] revealed that the occurrence of voids 
worsening the ductility needed a small stress in the materials with coarse 
particles. Both the ET and DT alloy bars contain a certain number of 
micro-phases whose the average phase size is only about 2 μ m which is 
insufficient to induce large voids between the secondary phases and the 
matrix. Therefore, the secondary phase is also not the main aspect that 
gives rise to the EL difference between the ET and DT alloy bars.

1) Through the DT extrusion approach, the BAZ531-DT alloy for the 
first time was successfully extruded at 83 m/min showing a good 
surface quality. However, the BAZ531-ET alloy bar surface generated 
numerous hot cracks (including macro-cracks with a vertical depth

ET extrusion approach. Such a difference was mainly attributed to 
forming higher extrusion heat on the surface exceeding the initial 
melting point of this alloy during the ET extrusion process compared 
with the DT extrusion process.
2) At the early stage of the ET and DT extrusion processes, a certain 
amount of {10 – 12} extension twins were activated. Compared with 
the ET extrusion billet, the DT extrusion billet had a higher twin 
fraction. Dislocation slip was observed in the form of orientation 
gradient in the former rather than the latter. As the extrusion pro -
ceeded, the DRX occurred by the CDRX, GB bulging, and PSN 
nucleation mechanisms. Due to more orientation gradients in the 
deformed grains, the ET extrusion billet exhibited a higher DRX 
fraction than the DT extrusion billet. At the extrusion die-exit, the ET 
and DT extrusion billets exhibited a complete DRX structure, but the 
former had a larger average grain size because of higher extrusion 
heat than the latter. At the area (alloy bar) away from the extrusion 
die-exit, the DRX growth further occurred. The final average grain 
size of the ET alloy bar was larger than that of the DT alloy bar. The 
DRX behavior resulted in forming a typical basal fiber texture in the 
alloy bars.
3) The BAZ531-DT alloy bar maintained a higher strength (showing the 
TYS and UTS of 283 and 331 MPa, respectively) and ductility 
(showing the EL of 19 %) under the ultra-high-speed extrusion than 
the BAZ531-ET alloy bar. The GB strengthening and texture 
strengthening provided the dominant contributions (~39 and ~51 
%) on the TYS, along with the secondary phase strengthening (~10 
%). The resulting ductility was primarily attributed to its refined 
grains, to some extent suppressing the twinning effectively.
