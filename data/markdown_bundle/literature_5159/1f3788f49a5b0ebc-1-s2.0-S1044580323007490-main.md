ABSTRACT 
In most metallic materials, the strength-ductility trade-off serves as an inevitable scenario. Therefore, improving 
the room/high-temperature strength/ductility of high Nb-TiAl alloys based on a lamellar microstructure is 
important for practical applications. In this work, a novel microstructure with fine lamellar colonies and a certain 
number of residual γ grains was developed through hot extrusion in the near α phase region. The results indicated 
that the lamellar colony size and spacing of the as-cast alloy were significantly refined by hot extrusion. The as- 
extruded Ti-45Al-8Nb-(W, B, Y) alloy demonstrated a good combination of high strength and ductility. The 
tensile strength and ductility were 1129 MPa and 2.04% at room temperature (RT), respectively, and 744 MPa 
and 51.20%, respectively, at 850 
◦
C. A simultaneous improvement in strength and ductility below ductile-brittle 
transition temperature (DBTT) could be attributed to the abundant deformation twins and their intersections, as 
well as prominent strain working caused by high-density dislocations in the γ phase. Due to dynamic recrys -
tallization, the ductility was significantly enhanced when stretched over DBTT. This extrusion-assisted micro -
structure tailoring strategy could be extended to other TiAl systems and may serve as a useful solution for 
addressing the significant engineering constraints posed by the low RT ductility and poor hot-working capa -
bilities of high Nb-TiAl alloys. 
1. Introduction 
High Nb-containing TiAl alloys (high Nb-TiAl) serve as promising 
lightweight structural materials due to their low density (~4.0 g/cm
3
), 
high specific strength, and specific modulus, as well as excellent 
oxidation and creep resistance at high temperatures, offering great 
application potential in the manufacturing of aircraft and automotive 
engines [ 1 – 4 ]. However, the intrinsic brittle nature of high Nb-TiAl al -
loys and the lack of effective processing methods to achieve satisfactory 
mechanical performance at room temperature (RT) and high tempera -
tures exceeding 750 
◦
C have hindered their widespread use [ 1 , 5 ]. 
Studies have attempted to effectively address the strength-ductility 
trade-off issue of high Nb-TiAl alloys. The strength and ductility serve 
as important mechanical properties in metal materials, although they 
are usually mutually exclusive, with gains in strength inevitably 
occurring at the expense of ductility, and vice versa [ 6 , 7 ]. Recently, 
certain effective strategies have been reported, such as preparing poly -
synthetic twinned (PST) TiAl single crystals and additive manufacturing; 
however, these strategies have high costs due to their complicated 
processing technologies [ 8 , 9 ]. In terms of industrial-grade poly -
crystalline TiAl alloys, grain refining serves as an accessible technology 
for improving strength and ductility. Hot extrusion deformation is also 
an effective pathway for grain refinement because it can break down the 
coarse as-cast microstructure [ 10 ]. Compared with forging and rolling 
processes, during hot extrusion, the material is subjected to triaxial 
compressive stress, resulting in a more uniform microstructure. 
The hot extrusion process has been successfully used in other ma -
terials to construct fine-grained structures, such as aluminum alloys [ 11 ] 
and magnesium alloys [ 12 ]. The formation of fine-grained structures can 
improve interfaces to enhance material strength and ductility, poten -
tially solving the aforementioned issues associated with certain TiAl 
alloys. In recent years, studies have attempted to address hot extrusion 
deformation in other TiAl alloys, such as TNM, TiAl-(Mo, Si), and TiAl- 
(V, Cr) alloys [ 13 – 15 ]. Liu et al. [ 15 ] successfully achieved a Ti-42Al-6 
V-1Cr alloy with a deformed microstructure using hot extrusion, with a 
yield strength (YS) of 881 MPa and an elongation of 2.4% at RT. How -
ever, compared with the TiAl-based alloys mentioned earlier, high Nb- 
TiAl alloys have been shown to be more brittle at RT [ 1 ]. Previous

3.2. Tensile behavior at various temperatures 
Fig. 5 shows the tensile properties of the as-cast/extruded Ti-45Al- 
8Nb-(W, B, Y) alloys at various temperatures. The representative stress- 
strain curves of the samples stretched at RT and 850 
◦
C are shown in 
Fig. 5 (a) and (b), revealing that after hot extrusion, both the strength 
and ductility significantly improved compared with those of the as-cast 
billet. As shown in Fig. 5 (a), a noticeable work-hardening trend was 
observed in the strain-stress curve of the as-extruded sample, which 
explained why it exhibited high strength and ductility. Work-hardening 
behavior comparable to the Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B alloy with tailored 
triple-phase triple-state and triple-phase dual-state structure was 
described in Ref. [ 28 ]. The precise values of UTS, YS, and δ are presented 
in Fig. 5 (c) and Table 1 . Specifically, the UTS, YS, and δ values of the as- 
extruded samples were 1129 MPa, 955 MPa, and 2.04%, respectively, 
increasing by 80.93%, 58.64%, and 213.85%, respectively, compared 
with the as-cast samples, as shown in Fig. 5 (d). The UTS, YS, and δ 
values increased from 460 MPa, 392 MPa, and 17.16% (as-cast) to 744 
MPa, 595 MPa, and 51.2% (as-extruded) at 850 
◦
C, respectively. 
Compared with previous investigations of as-rolled and forged high Nb- 
TiAl alloys [ 29 – 42 ], as shown in Fig. 6 , in this study, we demonstrated 
that the as-extruded Ti-45Al-8Nb-(W, B, Y) alloys had the highest tensile 
strength and excellent ductility at RT and 850 
◦
C, especially at RT. In 
general, this trade-off relationship between strength and plasticity has 
been widely accepted, with significant gains in strength typically ach -
ieved at the cost of plasticity [ 7 , 28 ]. In this work, the strength-ductility 
trade-off issue of high Nb-TiAl alloys was overcome by hot extrusion to 
obtain a tailored microstructure with fine lamellar colonies and a certain 
number of remanent γ grains ( Fig. 3 ). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5 
(c), the tensile ductility of the as-extruded sample reached 20.48% at 
800 
◦
C, indicating that the ductile-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) 
after extrusion was significantly lower than that of the as-cast sample. 
Therefore, high plasticity implied a favorable hot-working capacity.
