ABSTRACT
Addition of reinforcing particle is a potential strategy to counteract the significant strength degradation of ti -
tanium alloys at their maximum service temperature of 600 
◦
C. However, a long-standing challenge for particle- 
reinforced titanium matrix composites is their poor ductility and limited formability. In this work, directed 
energy deposition (DED) was employed to fabricate Ti60 alloy with dispersed TiC particles, using carbon- 
decorated C/Ti60 composite powders as feedstock. The reinforced TiC particles, with a volume fraction of 
approximately 9.8 %, consist of submicron TiC span across multiple α laths and nanoscale TiC particles within α 
laths. Due to the introduction of TiC particles, the average width of α phase has decreased from 1.18 μ m to 0.92 
μ m. The TiC particles significantly enhance the high-temperature strength of the Ti60 alloy by 11.7 %, 11.0 %, 
and 10.6 % at 600 
◦
C, 650 
◦
C, and 700 
◦
C, respectively, through a combination of Orowan strengthening, grain 
refinement and solid solution strengthening mechanisms, while retaining excellent elongation of 16.3 %, 42.3 % 
and 47.1 %. Additionally, the nanoscale TiC particles form coherent interfaces within the matrix, resulting in 
lattice distortions that increase the proportion of low-angle grain boundaries. This phenomenon can provide 
sufficient driving force for the early triggering of the Dynamic recrystallization (DRX) process, resulting in a 
lower recrystallization temperature in the TiC/Ti60 composites compared to the Ti60 alloy. This work represents 
a significant advancement in overcoming the trade-off between strength and ductility in particle-reinforced ti -
tanium matrix composites and offers a promising avenue for the next generation of high-temperature light alloys.
1. Introduction
Aerospace engine components are invariably subjected to harsh en -
vironments characterized by extreme temperatures, pressures, and 
complex stress profiles. This necessitates that titanium alloys exhibit 
excellent mechanical properties under high-temperature conditions 
while achieving a balance between strength and elongation [ 1 , 2 ]. 
State-of-the-art high-temperature titanium alloys, such as those in the 
Ti – Al – Sn – Zr – Mo – Si system [ 3 ], including IMI834 [ 4 ], Ti1100 [ 5 ], and 
Ti60 [ 6 ], are designed to operate effectively at maximum service tem -
peratures of 600 
◦
C, demonstrating commendable creep resistance and 
high-temperature tensile strength. However, beyond this threshold, the 
alloying elements become increasingly reactive, complicating stability 
maintenance and resulting in a significant decline in performance as 
temperatures rise [ 7 ].
To address this challenge, particle-reinforced titanium matrix com -
posites (PRTMCs) have been proposed as a promising solution for 
enhancing mechanical properties, which can generate multiple 
strengthening mechanisms, such as load transfer strengthening, Orowan 
strengthening, grain refinement, and solid solution strengthening [ 8 , 9 ]. 
For example, Imayev et al. [ 10 ] reported that the high-temperature 
tensile strength of (TiB + TiC)/VT18U composites fabricated through 
casting and hot forging improved by 31.7 % compared to the matrix, but 
at the cost of a 40 % reduction in ductility. A similar trend was observed 
by Zheng et al. [ 11 ], underscoring the inherent trade-off between 
strength and ductility when reinforcing titanium alloy with particles.

The reason for strength-ductility dilemma is that the introduced parti -
cles can effectively impede dislocation motion during deformation, thus 
enhancing strength [ 12 , 13 ]. However, the accumulation of dislocations 
around particles can lead to stress concentration, which in turn creates 
weak spots that severely undermine ductility [ 14 ]. Consequently, 
extensive efforts have been dedicated to resolving this strength-ductility 
trade-off in PRTMCs, effectively suppressing the initiation and propa -
gation of cracks in high-temperature titanium alloys during service, 
thereby improving fatigue and creep resistance. Furthermore, the 
simultaneous enhancement of strength and ductility in 
high-temperature titanium alloys also implies an increase in material 
toughness, which is particularly crucial for extending the service life of 
critical aerospace components [ 15 ].
Mechanical performance of PRTMCs is strongly dependent on the 
size [ 16 , 17 ] and distribution [ 18 , 19 ] of reinforcing particles. With re -
gard to size refinement, additive manufacturing (AM) has gained 
attention due to its rapid solidification characteristics [ 20 – 22 ]. In the 
study by Radhakrishnan et al. [ 23 ], it was reported that the ultra-high 
cooling rate (10

K/s) of directed energy deposition (DED) refined 
TiC particles to the micron scale, significantly enhancing the hardness of 
the Ti – 6Al – 4V matrix to 660 HV. Additionally, Yan et al. [ 24 ] investi -
gated TiC/Ti – 6Al – 4V composites fabricated via selective laser melting 
(SLM) and demonstrated that the in-situ synthesized TiC particles were 
refined to the nanoscale due to its higher cooling rate (10

resulting in an impressive tensile strength of 1517 MPa. However, due to 
the higher cooling rate of SLM, the internal stress levels in the 
TiC/Ti – 6Al – 4V composites are elevated, which leads to a reduction in its 
ductility to approximately 1 %, making it difficult to ensure formability. 
Therefore, in the process of fabricating PRTMCs, DED offers a clear 
advantage over SLM [ 25 ]. Moreover, AM technologies, with their strong 
convective flows in the molten pool, theoretically enable a more uniform 
distribution of reinforcing particles [ 26 ]. Numerous studies have high -
lighted that the uniform distribution of reinforcing particles is critical to 
improving the ductility of PRTMCs. Unfortunately, current research on 
PRTMCs fabricated via AM still struggles with achieving uniform par -
ticle distribution. This is primarily due to the stringent requirements on 
the selection of precursor materials for AM processes. Specifically, using 
large precursors can lead to incomplete reactions during fabrication, as 
shown by Zhang et al. [ 27 ], where residual B
4
C particles in 
DED-fabricated (TiB + TiC)/TC4 composites resulted in only modest 
strength improvement and a drastic reduction in ductility to 1.8 %. 
Conversely, when small precursors are used, such as carbon nanotubes 
or graphene, their tendency to agglomerate leads to unreacted precursor 
residues in the final product, preventing uniform particle distribution 
[ 24 ].
In this work, carbon-decorated C/Ti60 composite powder was used 
as the feedstock. The selection of Ti60 alloy as the matrix material is 
based on its widespread use in aerospace applications, particularly in 
components subjected to high temperatures, such as turbine blades and 
compressor discs. This alloy offers a combination of high strength, 
excellent creep resistance, and thermal stability, making it an ideal 
candidate for high-performance applications in extreme environments 
[ 28 ]. Using DED technology, we then successfully fabricated TiC/Ti60 
composites with ultrafine, uniformly dispersed TiC particles. The me -
chanical properties of Ti60 alloy and TiC/Ti60 composites were inves -
tigated through high-temperature tensile tests, and the strengthening 
mechanisms and ductility improvements of TiC/Ti60 composites were 
explored through high-temperature in-situ scanning electron micro -
scopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analyses. This 
work provides new insights into the evolution of high-temperature 
mechanical behavior in PRTMCs with uniformly dispersed reinforce -
ment particles and offers a promising approach to resolving the 
long-standing strength-ductility trade-off, broadening the potential ap -
plications of PRTMCs in extreme environments.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Material preparation
Ti60 alloy (nominal composition in wt.%: Al 5.7, Sn 4.0, Zr 3.5, Mo 
0.4, Si 0.4, Nb 0.4, Ta 1.0, Ti bal.) powder with a size range of 45 – 105 
μ m prepared via the plasma rotating electrode process in Fig. 1 (b) and 
nano carbon powder with an average diameter of 40 nm in Fig. 1 (c)
were employed as raw materials. Fig. 1 (a) shows the schematic of the 
satelliting technique [ 29 , 30 ] for C/Ti60 composite powder: Ti60 pow -
der and nano carbon powder were subjected to ball milling in a plane -
tary ball mill for a duration of 4 h to obtain mixed powder with 
uniformly dispersed carbon. The rotation speed was 200 rpm and the 
ball-to-material ratio was 2:1. Subsequently, 2 wt% aerosol (2.7 vol% 
polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution) was sprayed into the mixed powder 
and stirred for 10 min, allowing the nano carbon powder to firmly 
adhere to the surface of Ti60 powder particles. As is well known, poly -
vinyl alcohol (PVA), an organic compound with the chemical formula

2.2. Directed energy deposition
This work was conducted on a self-made direct energy deposition 
(DED) system, which is comprised of a YAG pulse laser with a wave -
length of 1064 nm, a three-axis CNC worktable for precise movement 
control, an inert atmosphere protective cover to prevent oxidation and 
contamination, a high-precision adjustable powder feeder, and a powder 
feeding nozzle [ 32 ]. The substrates for the additive manufacturing 
process were pure Ti plates, measuring 110 mm × 50 mm × 6 mm. Prior 
to the experiments, the surface of substrates were first sanded with 
sandpaper to remove oil stains and oxide scales, followed by cleaning 
with acetone and drying to ensure a clean and reactive surface for better 
material adhesion. The two consecutive layers of samples were scanned 
back and forth, with the Z-axis of the CNC worktable automatically 
adjusted to a predetermined height of 0.15 mm after each layer is 
scanned. The process parameters optimized through preliminary 
research [ 29 ] are as follows: a laser current of 70 A, a scanning speed of 
3 mm/s, a laser spot of 1 mm, a laser power of 160 W and a pulse width 
of 7 ms. These parameters were found to effectively promote the in-situ 
formation and relatively uniform distribution of TiC particles.

The specific formation process of the recrystallized grains can be 
clearly understood from the results shown in Fig. 8 , which indicates 
significant differences in the dynamic recrystallization (DRX) behavior 
of Ti60 alloy and TiC/Ti60 composite at various deformation tempera -
tures. Generally, the distribution positions of DRX grains provides 
important criteria for determining the type of Dynamic Recrystallization 
(DRX), as detailed in the study by Dong et al. [ 58 ] and Gupta et al. [ 59 ]. 
The discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) tends to occur at 
β / β grain boundaries and the continuous dynamic recrystallization 
(CDRX) tends to occur with grains. The DDRX involves three stages: the 
bulging of GBs, closing of GB bulges and formation of DDRX grains. 
Studies have shown that when the radius of GB bulges (R) is greater than 
the critical radius (Rc), the DRX nucleus is considered to be formed [ 60 ]. 
For grain boundary with a length of 2L, it reaches a critical value when 
the boundary bulge becomes hemispherical, i.e. Rc = L. The GBs 
continue to bulge and DRX grains grow further after nucleation. As 
illustrated in Fig. 8 (b) – (e), after high-temperature tensile testing at 
650 
◦
C, Ti60 alloy exhibits a large number of DDRX grains forming at the 
β / β grain boundaries (GB), while no significant recrystallization occurs 
within the grains. This phenomenon indicates that DDRX can occur even 
at lower ductility in the Ti60 alloy, suggesting that this process will 
consistently take place during high-temperature deformation.
Apart from the typical DDRX observed at β / β grain boundaries, the 
TiC/Ti60 composites also exhibits recrystallized grains within the inte -
rior of the grains after high-temperature tensile testing at 650 
◦
C. This 
intragranular recrystallization is generally associated with CDRX, which 
proceeds through progressive lattice rotation [ 61 ]. As shown in Fig. 8
(h) – (k), the DRX process of TiC/Ti60 composites is accompanied by a 
notable evolution in grain orientation, resulting in a significant increase 
in the number of < 11 2 0 > oriented grains. In addition, as the content of 
recrystallized grains increases, the texture strength significantly en -
hances, further reflecting the lattice rotation occurring during DRX 
process in TiC/Ti60 composites after high-temperature tensile test, as 
shown in Fig. 9 . Therefore, the occurrence of recrystallization in this 
study results from the combined effects of DDRX at the grain boundaries 
and CDRX within the grains.

The occurrence of dynamic recrystallization (DRX) typically requires 
a synergistic interaction between elevated temperature and high dislo -
cation density, as widely reported in prior studies [ 62 ]. Higher tem -
peratures promote atomic diffusion, facilitating the rearrangement and 
annihilation of dislocations, while high dislocation densities provide 
stored strain energy, which acts as the driving force for nucleation of 
recrystallized grains. Therefore, DRX is only triggered when both tem -
perature and dislocation density exceed critical thresholds. This concept 
is schematically illustrated in Fig. 13 (a): in the orange region, both 
conditions are met, and DRX occurs; in the green regions, either the 
temperature or dislocation density is insufficient, and DRX is sup -
pressed. To further support the rationale that DRX is more readily trig -
gered in TiC/Ti60 composites than in Ti60 alloy, as illustrated in Fig. 13
(a), we conducted KAM analysis on the as-builted Ti60 alloy and 
TiC/Ti60 composites. The results demonstrate that the TiC/Ti60 com -
posites exhibit significantly higher initial local misorientation due to the 
introduction of TiC particles, which correlates with a higher dislocation 
density prior to deformation. This directly supports the vertical axis of 
Fig. 13 (a), confirming that the TiC/Ti60 composite is more likely to 
surpass the dislocation density threshold required to trigger DRX 
process-requires a comparatively lower temperature to reach the orange 
recrystallization region than the Ti60 alloy.

ments are not directly imaged in this study, their presence and behavior 
are inferred from recrystallized grain distribution, misorientation pat -

In TiC/Ti60 composites ( Fig. 13 (b)), the introduction of hard TiC 
particles increases the initial dislocation density in the α laths and 
promotes further dislocation accumulation during deformation. These 
particles act as obstacles to dislocation motion, leading to local strain 
concentration and dislocation pile-up. Under tensile stress at 650 
◦

high dislocation density provides sufficient energy to trigger DRX even 
at moderate temperatures. The dislocation rearrangement into subgrain 
boundaries, followed by boundary migration, leads to the formation of 
equiaxed recrystallized grains, as observed experimentally ( Fig. 8 (h)).
In contrast, the Ti60 alloy ( Fig. 13 (c)) exhibits a much lower initial 
dislocation density. Although tensile stress at 650 
◦
C introduces more

spread DRX. Dislocations tend to accumulate at α / α or α / β boundaries, 
leading to localized strain without the formation of uniform recrystal -

consistent with the EBSD maps ( Fig. 8 (b)).
Therefore, Fig. 13 is intended to provide a conceptual framework to 
interpret the microstructural differences observed between the Ti60 
alloys and TiC/Ti60 composites under identical thermal-mechanical 
conditions, highlighting the critical role of particle-induced dislocation 
density in accelerating dynamic recrystallization.
5. Conclusion
In this work, TiC-reinforced Ti60 composites were successfully 
fabricated using Directed Energy Deposition (DED), achieving a uniform 
and fine dispersion of reinforcing particles. The microstructural features 
and high-temperature deformation mechanisms were systematically 
analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows: 
1. The DED process, characterized by ultra-high cooling rates and 
complex thermal cycling, has resulted in the uniform dispersion of 
TiC particles (with a volume fraction of approximately 9.8 %) 
throughout the Ti60 matrix without any sighificant agglomeration, 
which consists of submicron TiC particles span across multiple α 
laths and nanoscale TiC particles within α laths. Additionally, the 
hindrance to the migration of phase boundaries due to the presence 
of TiC particles reduced the average width of α laths from 1.18 μ m to 
0.92 μ m.

2. The dispersed TiC particles significantly enhanced the tensile 
strength of the composite across a range of temperatures. Compared 
to the Ti60 matrix, the tensile strength of the TiC/Ti60 composite 
increased by 95 MPa, 56 MPa and 37 MPa at 600

3. At 650

and ductility, with a tensile strength of 565 MPa and an elongation of 
42.3 %. This improvement is attributed to the role of nanoscale TiC 
particles in accelerating dynamic recrystallization (DRX). These 
particles promoted dislocation accumulation and increased the 
density of low-angle grain boundaries, thereby facilitating the early 
onset of both continuous and discontinuous DRX processes.
Overall, this work demonstrates that DED is a powerful technique for 
achieving uniform particle dispersion and microstructural refinement in 
titanium matrix composites. The resulting improvements in high- 
temperature mechanical properties hold significant potential for appli -
cations in aerospace and other demanding environments where struc -
tural reliability at elevated temperatures is critical.
