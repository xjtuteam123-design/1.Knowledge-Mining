ABSTRACT 
Low alloy wear-resistant martensitic steels are increasingly prevalent in moderate-to-high temperature appli -
cations in machinery. In this study, the reciprocating sliding wear behavior of a designed low alloy martensitic 
steel (herein called HTP) against silicon nitride has been investigated within the temperature range of 
300 – 500 
�
C and compared to similar results for another wear-resistant steel (NM400). A commercial high 
temperature friction and wear tester was used. The evolution of the microstructure and properties of HTP and 
their influence on the wear process was investigated as a function of temperature. Results have shown that as the 
tempering temperature was increased to 500 
�
C, the wear depth was significantly reduced. This is attributed to 
increasing the strength and hardness by 25%. As a result, high temperature wear resistance of the HTP was 
increased by a factor of 1.7 times relative to alloy NM400. By delaying the decrease of dislocation density and 
carbide precipitation during the tempering of the martensite, the transformation of microstructure from 
tempered martensite to tempered sorbite could be hindered, thereby improving the thermal strength of the HTP 
alloy ’ s matrix. The material removal mechanisms was observed to change from fatigue spalling and abrasive 
wear to oxidative wear and plastic deformation when the temperature was increased from 300 
�
C to 500 
�
C. 
1. Introduction 
In recent years, high temperature friction and wear of steel have 
become increasingly prominent in engineering. The loss of steel mate -
rials under high temperature conditions can be better controlled by 
understanding these processes. Therefore, research in this area has 
attracted the attention of many. The wear process is generally evaluated 
by changes in the structures and properties of materials in high tem -
perature environments, without considering other factors [ 1 – 4 ]. 
Berns et al. [ 5 ] found that the structures containing precipitates or 
carbides exhibited excellent wear resistance at high temperatures due to 
the avoidance of barriers and delayed dislocation movement, thus pre -
venting recrystallization and inhibiting the hardness reduction of the 
materials. Wang et al. [ 6 ] studied the effect of microstructure on the 
high temperature wear of 3Cr2W8V steel. The major parameters for the 
determination of the wear resistance of 3Cr2W8V steel were the 
precipitated carbide and austenite grain sizes in the microstructure and 
the high temperature hardness of the material. Another study demon -
strated that DM die steel containing a large amount of manganese had 
high tempering resistance and activation energy at high temperature. 
This was explained by the fact that manganese can dissolve into a 
precipitated phase, thus it delayed the coarsening of carbides at high 
temperatures and improved the softening resistance of materials [ 7 ]. Yu 
et al. [ 8 ] showed that the thermal hardness and high temperature wear 
resistance of a CoCrFeNi alloy could be improved by adding niobium. 
Smith [ 9 ] studied the sliding wear of 316 stainless steel samples in air 
under conditions of 20 – 550 
�
C, determining that the mechanical prop -
erties of the sub-surface layer played a major role in the wear. 
Metallic materials inevitably react with oxygen atoms in the air to 
form oxides at high temperatures. In 1930, Fink first discovered the 
effect of oxidation on the wear of steel [ 10 ]. Quinn and colleagues [ 11 , 
12 ] have studied the oxidative wear of ferroalloys in depth, and they 
proposed an oxidative wear model. Most of the subsequent research on 
oxidative wear originated from Quinn ’ s research. Wilson et al. [ 13 ] 
offered another important oxidative wear model, suggesting that a glaze 
layer formed on the worn surface during high temperature sliding wear 
could prevent metal-to-metal adhesion. However, according to Wang 
and others [ 14 – 16 ], the strength of the matrix must be considered when

In general, the high temperature wear of iron and steel materials is a 
concern when using hot working die steel and tool steel. Regarding low 
alloy wear-resistant martensitic steel for construction machinery, there 
are few reports on the wear behavior at different temperatures. Under 
hot forming and other harsh working conditions, the service environ -

martensite steel occurs within this service temperature range, resulting 
in a series of dislocation density change, carbide precipitation, 
martensite structure recovery, and recrystallization. As a result, the 
structure and mechanical properties change, including thermal hard -
ness, thermal stability, and high temperature fracture strength, affecting 
its high temperature wear resistance. It is proposed that the softening 
resistance of the martensite steel matrix, which contains strong carbide- 
forming elements including molybdenum, is determined by the recovery 
of alloy carbides and martensite structures with high dislocation den -
sities [ 19 – 22 ]. 
The aim of this work was to characterize and understand the high 
temperature wear behavior of a designed low alloy wear-resistant 
martensitic steel (HTP) in 300 – 500 
�
C environments. In addition, the 
effect of the evolution of mechanical properties, such as structure and 
hardness, on the wear behavior of HTP at these temperatures was also 
studied. The tempering process of the steel at medium and high tem -
peratures was modified by altering the composition of conventional 
NM400 wear-resistant martensitic steel, specifically by changing the 
carbide-forming element of the matrix.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Initial microstructure and mechanical properties 
The microstructure of HTP steel, obtained by quenching and 
tempering, was determined to be primarily lath martensite. Fig. 3 a 
shows a martensite packet in the original austenite grains, and the 
austenite grain boundaries are clearly visible in the microstructure. The 
martensite packet was composed of multiple laths, which were generally 
arranged in parallel to each other, and ε carbides were distributed in 
the martensite matrix [ 23 ]. NM400 wear-resistant steel, a type of 
tempered martensitic steel, was selected as the comparison material as 
shown in Fig. 3 b. 
The average Vickers macrohardness value and the 40 
�
C impact 
energy of HTP steel are 417 HV and 180 J, respectively. The average 
hardness and impact energy of NM400 wear-resistant steel are 419 HV 
and 40 J, respectively. The hardness value is nearly equal to that of HTP 
steel, while the impact energy is about 3 times smaller than that of HTP 
steel. It has been shown that the carbon content of the low alloy wear- 
resistant martensitic steel largely determines its hardness [ 24 ]. 
Therefore, the Vickers macrohardness of the two steels is nearly the 
same because the carbon content of the two steels is about equal, thus 
meeting expectations in the composition design. The impact energy is 
related to the original austenite grain size of steels [ 25 ]. The grain size of 
HTP steel is smaller than that of NM400 wear-resistant steel as shown in 
Fig. 3 , so the impact energy is relatively higher. 
3.2. Microstructure and mechanical properties at different temperatures 
Fig. 4 shows the mechanical properties of HTP and NM400 wear- 
resistant steel at different temperatures. It is shown that the tensile 
strength of the two steels decreases with increasing temperature. How -
ever, the tensile strength of HTP steel decreases at a slower rate.

The decrease in ultimate tensile strength of the two steels is due to 
the decrease in deformation resistance and the increase in deformation 
temperature. There are two main reasons for the reduction of high 
temperature deformation resistance of the experimental steel. First, the 
binding force between atoms is weakened. At high temperatures, atoms 
in metals gain energy, enhancing mobility and intensifying vibrations. 
As a result, attraction between atoms decreases, and the slip resistance

4. Conclusions 
The aim of this study was to investigate the relation between the 
microstructural transformation as well as mechanical properties and the 
tribological response of two low alloy wear-resistant martensitic steel 
during sliding wear at 300 – 500 
�

work were as follows: 
(1) The high temperature strength of HTP and NM400 decreased 
with increasing temperature. The Mo element improved the 
tempering stability of HTP. Compared with NM400, the high
