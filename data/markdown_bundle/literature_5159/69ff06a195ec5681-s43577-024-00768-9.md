Unprecedented fracture 
toughness reported in a  
tailored NbTaTiHf refractory 
medium‑entropy alloy
I
n many engineering applications, high 
resistance to fracture is an important 
material property. “Fracture toughness” 
is a critical property for materials sub-
jected to extreme stress and strain. There 
is usually a tradeoff between a mate-
rial’s strength and fracture toughness, 
undermining the usefulness, for exam-
ple, of some high-strength alloys. This 
shortcoming particularly affects many 
body-centered-cubic (bcc) complex 
concentrated alloys (medium- and high-
entropy alloys, i.e., with a combination 
of three or four metals or a combination 
of five or more metals, respectively), 
especially those composed of refractory 
elements with 
high-tempera-
ture strength. 
The low frac-
ture resistance 
challenge was 
tackled in a 
study published 
in a recent 
issue of Science 
(https:// doi. org/ 
10. 1126/ scien 
ce. adn24 28) of 
 Nb45 Ta 25 Ti 15 
Hf15, a complex 
concentrated 
refractory alloy, 
by groups of 
researchers 
led by Rob -
ert Ritchie 
at the Law-
rence Berke-
ley National 
Laboratory and 
the University 
of California, 
Berkeley, with 
Diran Apelian 
at the Univer -
sity of Califor -
nia, Irvine, and 
Enrique Lav-
ernia at Texas 
A&M Univer -
sity, College 
Station.
The research 
team pro-
duced an alloy 
whose frac -
ture toughness 
significantly 
outperforms 
other refractory high- and medium-
entropy alloys (RHEAs/RMEAs). Their 
approach involves the selection of con-
stituent elements for favorable phase 
stability at high temperatures and the 
promotion of the activation of multiple 
slip systems, extreme temperature test-
ing, and intensive kink band formation 
analysis. The investigation was car -
ried out from a cryogenic temperature 
(77 K) to an elevated temperature of 
1473 K.
The researchers achieved remark-
able high fracture toughness, between 
253 MPa.m1/2 and 323 MPa.m1/2. Like-
wise, significant crack-initiation and 
steady-state fracture toughness were 
recorded at elevated temperatures, as 
high as 1473 K.
During the fracture toughness test -
ing, the alloy exhibited stable crack 
growth. The study showed that the 
kink band, a secondary microstructural 
deformation feature, and its entangling 
pattern of dislocations suppressed 
the crack growth in Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 
RMEAs. David Cook, the first author 
of the publication and doctoral stu-
dent to Ritchie, says, “We show that 
in the presence of a sharp crack, kink 
bands actually resist the propagation 
of a crack by distributing damage 
away from it, preventing fracture and 
leading to extraordinarily high fracture 
toughness.”
“What is fascinating about the 
 Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 RMEA is not simply 
its exceptional fracture toughness over 
a wide range of temperatures… but … 
such high toughness involves virtually 
no strain hardening during tensile load-
ing,” says Ritchie.
In a separate correspondence with 
MRS Bulletin, Bernd Gludovatz of the 
University of New South Wales Syd-
ney, Australia, who was not involved in 
this study, says, “This is both surpris-
ing and encouraging, highlighting the 
potential of this alloy system as a basis 
for the design of new structural materi-
als. However, one must remain cautious 
and consider the limitations of the study 
that are noted by the authors, namely, 
that the samples that were used were 
too small for some of the conditions

According to Bernd Gludovatz, 
“[S]uch exceptional mechanical per -
formance over that extremely wide 
temperature range … should point the 
community to conduct similar tests in 
oxidizing environments since achieving 
good oxidation resistance remains a sig-
nificant challenge for the field of refrac-
tory alloys.” Punit Kumar, a co-author 
and postdoctoral researcher at Ritchie’s 
laboratory, adds, “The Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 
RMEA alloy was able to beat even (the 
best) cryogenic steels, clocking in at 
over 25 times higher toughness than 
typical RMEAs at room temperature.” 
This finding could be a paradigm shift 
in alloy development and deformation.
