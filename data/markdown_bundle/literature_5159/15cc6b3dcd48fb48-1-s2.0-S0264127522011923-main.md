abstract
Topologically close-packed (TCP) phases seriously endanger the properties of Superalloy. Investigating
the microstructure of the TCP phases will provide a crystallographic reference for the design of superal-
loys with better properties. The l phase is a common TCP phase in superalloys. It belongs to the space
group R-3m, and has a hexagonal structure with ﬁve Wyckoff sites of 3a, 18h, 6c1, 6c2 and 6c3. In this
paper, occupation of the three kinds of atoms at ﬁve Wyckoff sites of the
l phase in Ni-Cr-Mo model
superalloy was determined using aberration-corrected High-Angle Annular Dark-Field Scanning
Transmission Electron Microscopy (HAADF-STEM), QSTEM software simulation and ﬁrst-principles calcu-
lations. It was found that Mo atoms with larger size and smaller electronegativity tend to occupy 6c3, 6c1
and 6c2 sites in order of priority. Ni atoms mainly occupy the sites with coordination number (CN) of 12
at 3a and 18 h, and 3a sites contain more Cr atoms than 18 h sites.

1. Introduction
Superalloys are important structural materials in the aviation
industry. To achieve excellent high temperature performance, sev-
eral of refractory elements are usually added into superalloys,
which results in formation of topologically close-packed (TCP)
phases during the service process [1–3]. The TCP phases are a class
of intermetallic compounds with high coordination numbers of 12,
14, 15, and 16 atoms [4–6]. The special close-packed structure
makes them very hard and brittle. TCP phases often act as a source
of cracks and signiﬁcantly shorten the lifetime of superalloys [7–
9]. On the other hand, the enrichment of refractory elements in
TCP phases reduces the solid solution-strengthening effect
[10,11]. Therefore, the precipitation of TCP phases should be
avoided as much as possible. Investigating the crystal structure
of the TCP phases is helpful for understanding its formation mech-

The l phase is a typical TCP phase in superalloys, especially in
alloys with high Mo and W contents [7,10,11]. The structure of
the l phase has ﬁrstly been determined by Arnfelt and Westgren
in 1935 by X-ray diffraction basing on the stoichiometric ratio
A7B6 [4,12]. The l phase belongs to the space group R 3
/C0
m, and
one unit cell contains 39 atoms, occupying ﬁve Wyckoff sites
[13,14]. Fig. 1 is a schematic of a hexagonal unit cell of l phase.
In a typical binary l phase structure such as Co 7Mo6, Co atoms
occupy 3a and 18 h sites with coordination number (CN) of 12,
Mo atoms occupy 6c1 (CN15), 6c2 (CN16), 6c3 (CN14) sites
[4,15]. Fig. 1 (b) and (c) show the atomic arrangement of the
l
phase under the [1 1 0] and [1 1
/C0
0] zone axis, respectively.
Later studies showed that most of the binary l phases don’t
conform to a stoichiometric ratio [13]. Wagner et al. found that
two kinds of atoms were mixed at 3a sites for the l phase in the
Co-Nb alloy [16]. Sluiter also found the mixture of Ni and Nb atoms
at 3a sites in Ni-Nb alloy, while Ni atoms have a strong preference
at 18 h sites. The physical reason is the difference of nearest-
neighbor environments. The 12 coordination atoms of the 3a sites
consist of 6 Ni atoms and 6 Nb atoms, while that of 18 h sites con-
sist of 5 Ni atoms and 7 Nb atoms, and the Ni-Nb bond is stronger
than Ni-Ni and Nb-Nb pairs [17]. Using the X-ray diffraction
method, Joubert et al. found similar occupancy characteristics at
two 12 coordination sites in Ta-Ni and Nb-Ni binary systems,
and developed the 4 sublattice (SL) model within the CALPHD
approach [13,18]. Recently, Wu et al. investigated the l phase
structure in more than ten binary systems including Fe-Mo, Nb-
Fe, and Ta-Ni by ﬁrst-principles calculations. In most binary sys-
tems, the size factor plays a decisive role, and the 18 h sites favor
small atoms more than the 3a sites and the 3SL model is proposed
[19]. At present, there is no uniﬁed understanding of the rules of
inﬂuencing element occupation in the l phase of various systems.
However, the TCP phase structure in multicomponent alloys is
much more complex than that in binary systems. As we know,
the experimental data on the crystal structure of the TCP phase
in the multi-alloy system is still scarce [20]. More effort is neces-
sary for investigating the structure of the multicomponent TCP
phases [10,21–23].
In this paper, the crystal structure of l phase at atomic scale
was investigated in ternary Ni-Cr-Mo model superalloy using
spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope.
The occupancy tendencies of different metal elements in
ﬁve Wyckoff sites were analyzed. The crystal model was estab-
lished for ﬁrst-principles calculations and QSTEM simulations,
and the occupancy tendencies of different types of metal elements
in each site of the l phase were analyzed, which would bring new
insight into the mechanism of l phase formation.
2. Experiment and simulation
Table 1 lists the nominal composition of the Ni-Mo-Cr model
superalloy. The button ingots were prepared using a high-
vacuum arc melting furnace. To get uniform composition and
microstructure, the sample was solution treated at 1270 ℃ for
8 h using a GSL-1500X tube furnace, sealed in quartz tubes to pre-
vent oxidation. To get TCP phases, the thermal experiment was
performed at 900 ℃ for 15 h, also using the GSL-1500X tube
furnace.
Transmission electron microscope samples were prepared by
the electrolytic double spray method using 10 % perchloric acid
ethanol solution at /C025 /C176C. The microstructure was investigated
using transmission electron microscopes FEI Tecnai T20 and FEI
Talos F200X. HAADF-STEM images were obtained using an FEI
Titan G2 60-300 probe-corrected scanning transmission electron
microscope at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.
HAADF-STEM image was simulated using QSTEM code devel-
oped by Koch [24]. First-principles calculations were performed
using CASTEP [25]. The exchange correlation function was LDA
approximation, the pseudopotential was chosen as the mode-
conserving pseudopotential method. The plane wave kinetic
energy cut-off was converged to 860 eV, and the k point was set
as 6 /C26 /C22.

4. Discussion
Three points can be known from experimental data. (1) Ni
atoms mainly occupy CN12 sites at 3a and 18 h, and 3a sites con-
tain more Cr atoms than 18 h sites. (2) Mo atoms occupy at 6c1,
6c2 and 6c3 sites, the Mo atoms at 6c2 are signiﬁcantly less than
at the other two sites. (3) Mo atoms of an insufﬁcient number
may be complemented by Cr atoms.
The experimental results show that the two CN12 sites of 3a
and 18 h are mainly occupied by two elements with smaller atomic
size, Ni and Cr. However, the 3a sites contain more Cr atoms. From
the point of electronic factor, the outermost electron conﬁguration
of the Ni element is 3d
84s2, the outermost electron conﬁguration of
the Cr element is 3d 54s1, and the outermost electron conﬁguration
of the Mo element is 4d 55s1. Although Cr has a smaller atomic
radius, it has an outer electronic conﬁguration similar to that of
Mo, which is less electronegative than Ni. As shown in Fig. 6 (a)

The three sites of 6c1, 6c2 and 6c3 are usually considered to be
occupied by refractory elements with large atomic size in superal-
loys, such as Mo element [9–11]. In our experiment, when Mo
atoms are not enough to occupy these three sites, the 6c2 sites will
be ﬁrstly replaced by Cr atoms. The reason for this phenomenon is
that we have mentioned above that these two elements have sim-
ilar outer electron arrangements, that is, similar electronegativity.
In addition, from the experimental results of Fig. 3 , the atomic
columns at the 6c1 sites are always arranged in one bright and
one dark. The average intensity of the bright column is about
51500, and the average intensity of the dark column is about
50500. The brightness intensity values of the atomic columns at
the 6c3 sites are about 51500, and the brightness intensity values
of the atomic columns at the 6c2 sites are between 50000 and
51000. This indicates that the content of Mo element at 6c2 is
the lowest, and the content of Mo at 6c3 is the highest. The bright
atomic column at 6c1 is equivalent to 6c3, while the dark atomic
column is equivalent to 6c2. This may be due to the fact that the
Cr atom is most likely to occupy the 6c2 sites, followed by the
6c1 sites, but the Cr atom content is very few. After occupying
one of the 6c1 sites, the adjacent 6c1 sites are still occupied by
the larger Mo atoms. Therefore, the priority of refractory element
Mo may be 6c3 > 6c1 > 6c2. Since they have 14,15 and 16 coordi-

5. Conclusion
In this paper, the crystal structure of l phase in Ni-Cr-Mo alloy
was studied by spherical aberration correction electron micro-
scope, and the occupation of different types of atoms at ﬁve Wyck-
off sites was determined assisted by QSTEM simulation and ﬁrst-
principles calculation. The main conclusions are summarized as
follows.
(1) In the 3a sites, Ni and Cr mixed occupancy, in the 18 h sites
are mainly Ni, Mo is mainly located at B sites.
(2) When the content of Mo is not enough to ﬁll all B sites, Cr
atoms will occupy other B sites due to similar
electronegativity.
(3) The priority order of Cr atoms replacing Mo atoms is 6c2,
6c1, 6c3.
