ABSTRACT 
α -titanium alloys are extensively applied under cryogenic environment and thereby their cryogenic mechanical 
properties and service failures deserve to be deeply studied. Here, tensile behaviors and damage-fracture char -
acteristics of Ti-2.5Cu α -titanium alloy were systematically investigated at liquid-nitrogen temperature (LT) plus 
room temperature (RT) for comparison. It is found that the LT samples possess superior strength and ductility 
concurrently. Both yield strength ( σ
y
) and ultimate tensile strength ( σ
b
) improve more than 70%, whilst total 
elongation at fracture ( A ) adds more than 33% in comparison with the RT counterparts. Meanwhile, the LT 
samples are stretched mainly by uniform strain without noticeable necking. Fracture analysis indicates that being 
different from typical cup-and-cone fracture surface in the RT samples, the ‘radial ’ fracture surface appears in the 
LT samples. Such specific fracture surface is constructed by two characteristic morphologies of crack-initiation 
zone at sample surface and the ensuing crack-propagation zone. Furthermore, it is seen that a large number 
of micro-voids are dispersed underneath fracture surface in the RT samples, whereas the number of micro-voids 
is sharply reduced at LT. Nevertheless, Vickers hardness at the position is always much higher in the LT samples. 
These novel mechanical properties and damage-fracture characteristics in the LT samples are closely associated 
with the prevalent deformation twinning in microstructure. Deformation twinning itself and plentiful twins- 
induced dynamic Hall-Petch effect steadily accommodate plastic deformation, contributing to the simulta -
neous enhancement of strength and ductility. This process also results in high stress state in samples, increasing 
the sensitivity of surface cracking. Hence, the ‘radial ’ fracture morphology is finally formed. These findings 
enrich our fundamental understanding on deformation-damage-fracture process of α -titanium alloys, and also 
provide implicit guidance for the design of titanium alloys with high performance. 
1. Introduction 
The lightweight, high strength, good processability and excellent 
corrosion resistance have promoted the increasing use of titanium alloys 
in a number of industrial fields [1 – 4] . One of the applications refers to be 
as cryogenic-temperature components such as storage tanks, pipelines of 
liquid fuel rocket engines and liquid fuel rocket motor pipes [5] . The 
relevant research on titanium alloys under cryogenic environment thus 
becomes essential for better utilizations. Ti-2.5Cu as binary α -titanium 
alloy combines the formability and weldability of pure titanium with 
improved mechanical properties. The alloy is generally used as sheet, 
forgings and extrusions under annealing conditions for the manufacture 
of components, such as bypass ducts of gas turbine engines and jet en -
gines [6 – 10] . More importantly, the alloy exhibits excellent combina -
tions of strength and ductility at cryogenic temperature compared with 
room temperature [11] . The cryogenic mechanical properties and the 
underlying deformation mechanism thus deserve to be deeply studied in 
order to fundamentally understand the issue and further advance the 
performance. 
The mechanical properties and deformation behaviors of titanium 
alloys at cryogenic temperatures has been extensively studied over the 
past few decades. It has been reported that the strength and ductility of 
Ti-2.5Cu alloy were significantly improved at cryogenic temperature 
(77 K) in comparison with room temperature (293 K) [11,12] . This was

respectively. They found that the ultimate tensile strength and 
elongation-to-fracture dramatically increased at 77 K. The increase in 
ultimate tensile strength is attributed to the dramatic improvement of 
critical resolved shear strain (CRSS) for dislocation slip and the 
Hall-Petch effect of {10 12} < 10 1 1 > twinning [14] . As for enhanced 
ductility, multiple slips and extensive twinning increased the capability 
of work hardening and uniform plastic flow. However, another work by 
this group on Ti-3Al-3Mo-3Zr alloy with equiaxed microstructure 
manifested that ductility did not increase as much as ultimate tensile 
strength at 77 K and 20 K, and instead decreased with temperature 
dropping [15] . The ductility degradation is attributed to the fact that the 
{10 12}-type extension twin, which usually coordinates slip deformation 
of α -parent, was not extensively activated at cryogenic temperature. For 
Ti-5Al-2.5Sn alloy, another α -titanium alloy largely used at cryogenic 
temperature, it is reported that the alloy has a significant increase in 
strength as the testing temperature decreases [16 – 18] . Recent studies 
proposed that deformation twinning of the Ti-5Al-2.5Sn ELI alloy 
coordinating crystallographic orientation and dislocation gliding has a 
considerable contribution to elongation. Furthermore, the contribution 
of twinning to the ductility of Ti-5Al-2.5Sn ELI alloy improves with 
temperature dropping [19] . At the same time, the large improvement in 
strength under cryogenic temperature is ascribed to the dislocation 
strengthening effect, and some dislocation configurations such as 
dislocation tangles, dislocation cells and dislocation walls are accord -
ingly developed [19] . Aside from α titanium alloys, the mechanical 
properties and deformation behaviors of α + β titanium alloys and β 
titanium alloys at cryogenic temperature were studied as well. 
Ti-15Mo-2Al metastable β titanium alloy has been reported to reach 
ultra-high tensile strength of 1535 MPa at 77 K and 1725 MPa 20 K, 
while a sharp decrease in elongation [20] . Furthermore, it is found that 
all occurrences in deformation process involve premature intergranular 
fracture, which is independent of the testing temperature [20] . Never -
theless, once deformation twinning is activated, excellent comprehen -
sive mechanical properties can be still obtained even through the 
temperature is lower down to 20 K [21] . For α + β titanium alloys, there 
exist a great number of investigations on tensile properties of Ti-6Al-4 V 
alloys at cryogenic temperature, and almost all of them focus on the 
microstructural features of mechanical properties [22 – 26] . 
Up to now, few studies have been conducted on fracture character -
istics of titanium alloy at cryogenic temperature, especially for α -tita -
nium alloys, and even fewer studies on the combination of fracture 
characteristics and corresponding mechanical behaviors even though 
this topic is rather important for comprehensive understanding of 
deformation-damage-fracture process. In this study, Ti-2.5Cu α -titanium 
alloy as prototypical material was mechanically tested at liquid-nitrogen 
temperature plus room temperature for comparison, and the resulting 
fracture features together with deformation behaviors were systemati -
cally investigated by coupling various characterization techniques. The 
temperature-dependent deformation and fracture characteristics were 
disclosed and discussed. This study will enrich our fundamental un -
derstanding on deformation-damage-fracture features of titanium alloys 
at cryogenic temperature, and bear practical implication for developing 
titanium alloys with high performance.

Tensile tests were categorized into two groups according to testing

the samples are immersed into liquid nitrogen for 15 min to cool down 
completely. Due to rapid volatilization, it is necessary to replenish liquid 
nitrogen to maintain the immersion during the testing. Five samples 
were tested to ensure the repeatability of tensile properties.

3.3. Fractographies 
Fig. 4 shows the fracture surface of tensile samples and the corre -
sponding magnification morphologies. For the RT samples, one can see 
cup-and-cone fracture surface with a central flat ‘fibrous ’ zone and a 
peripheral shear lip, indicating typical ductile fracture, see Fig. 4 (a). 
Fig. 4 (b)-(c) are the magnification images of the fibrous zone in Fig. 4 (a). 
There are a lot of micro-voids and dimples with the size about 15 µ m in 
fibrous zone. Each micro-void should correspond to a microcrack- 
nucleation site. This indicates that the formation of fibrous zone in -
volves void nucleation, growth and coalescence, and the RT samples 
suffers from micro-void-coalescence fracture [27] . By contrast, the 
fracture surface of the LT samples does not show the typical 
cup-and-cone morphology. Instead, the entire fracture surface is relative 
flat. More importantly, it is found that crack initiates at the sample 
surface and propagates from one end to the other in a radial shape. This 
means that the LT samples encounters the ‘radial ’ fracture, and the 
specific fracture feature is schematically illustrated in Fig. 4 (d) for clear. 
The detailed features of crack-initiation zone and crack-propagation 
zone are further characterized. Two regions of ‘e ′ and ‘g ′ were chosen 
to represent the two zones, as marked by yellow rectangle and blue 
rectangle in Fig. 4 (d), respectively. Fig. 4 (e-f) are the magnification 
images of region ‘e ′ . The fracture surface is fairly flat, on the top of which 
numerous dimples can be seen. The dimple size was measured to be 
10 µ m on average. In addition, there is no clear evidence of the presence 
of any micro-voids and secondary cracks in this region. Fig. 4 (g-h) are 
the magnification images of region ‘g ′ . Notably, the fracture surface 
become rather rough in comparison with Fig. 4 (e). On close observation, 
the region is distributed by micro-voids and dimples, most of which are 
big and deep. Furthermore, several secondary cracks are found to exist 
as well, suggesting that the region is subjected to strong stress 
concentration.

3.4. Hardness measurement 
Vickers hardness was measured on the longitudinal sections of the 
tensile samples along the above two directions, see Fig. 6 . The two di -
rections are schematically illustrated in the figure together. When 
hardness measurement was conducted along the longitudinal direction 
from near fracture surface to away from it, the hardness value tends to 
decrease in the RT samples, see Fig. 6 (a). This indicates that stress 
concentration is gradually relieved when fracture surface is far away. 
Hardness measurement in the LT samples also shows a similar 
decreasing trend, see Fig. 6 (b). Such hardness variations in both samples 
are consistent with the void variations in Fig. 5 (a-b), where the number 
of micro-voids is accordingly getting less and less. 
When hardness measurement was carried out along the transverse 
direction in the RT samples, the hardness values in the vicinity of frac -
ture surface are plotted in Fig. 6 (c). It is seen that the values almost 
remain constant at different positions and the average value was 
calculated to be 262 HV. This finding is in accordance with the fracture 
characteristics of the RT samples in Fig. 4 (a-c), which is described as a 
rather homogeneous surface without obvious regional difference in the 
fibrous zone. However, for the LT samples, Vickers hardness has a 
remarkable increase from crack-initiation zone to crack-propagation 
zone, see Fig. 6 (d). This means that progressive stress concentration 
occurs when the position approach to the final fracture. Hence, it is 
reasonably thought that there is more intense deformation near crack- 
propagation zone than crack-initiation zone, resulting in the appear -
ance of secondary cracks on fracture surface and more micro-voids on 
the longitudinal section, all of which are well consistent with Fig. 4 (g-h) 
and Fig. 5 (c) . Additionally, it is worth noting that the hardness of the LT 
samples is always higher than that of the RT samples regardless of the 
measurement direction. This can be closely associated with the high 
proportion of uniform plastic strain in the LT samples, which endows the 
alloy more intense work hardening.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Enhanced uniform ductility at LT 
Generally speaking, for materials with hexagonal close-packed 
(HCP) crystal structure, the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) of 
dislocation gliding increases significantly with decreasing temperature 
[28,29] . In contrast, the CRSS for deformation twinning hardly change 
from room temperature to cryogenic temperature, therefore it is easier 
to activate deformation twinning at cryogenic temperature, which plays 
a huge contribution to the low-temperature tensile deformation of α ti -
tanium alloys [28 – 32] . The stretching of Ti-2.5Cu alloy at LT activates 
deformation twinning, which generates high density of twins within 
α -grains and divides α -grain into numerous small ‘rooms ’ , where sub -
sequent dislocation motion is confined to the “ rooms ” , thus significantly 
reducing dislocation mean free path and triggering work hardening, i.e . , 
so-called dynamic Hall-Petch effect [33 – 37] . Moreover, dislocation 
gliding becomes quite difficult at LT, so the barriers to dislocation mo -
tion by twin boundaries appears to be much stronger [2,25,38] . In other 
words, this dynamic structural refinement has a similar effect to that of 
fine-grain strengthening, resulting in a significant improvement in ma -
terial strength [39,40] . Twins are essentially the product of uniform 
shear along a specific direction in a specific plane under the action of 
shear stress [41] . The crystallographic orientation in the homogeneous 
shear region is accordingly changed, causing some of which were in 
unfavorable slip systems switch to the orientations that facilitate the 
occurrence of dislocation gliding, stimulating further deformation. The 
alternating twinning and slipping compensate each other to promote 
larger material deformation, which contributes to higher plasticity [42] . 
In addition, deformation twinning reconfiguring crystal orientation and 
relieving dislocation entanglement [43] , improves deformation coordi -
nation amongst grains and thereby effectively releases stress concen -
tration ( Fig. 7 (g-i)). This is the reason why micro-voids rarely appears at 
the α / β

4.2. Temperature-dependent fracture processes 
The β
t 
consists of eutectoid α and Ti
2
Cu ( Fig. 2 (d)). Since Ti
2
Cu as an 
intermetallic compound is a hard phase, deformation is initiated pref -
erentially in the α -grains [50] . In the process of deformation at RT, a 
large number of dislocations glide within α grains, and with the increase 
of strain, the dislocations move to the α / β
t 
interfaces. Hence, there exists 
a strong strain incompatibility between the two constituents. This will 
lead to a gradual increase of stress concentration at the α / β t interface. 
When the stress concentration cannot be effectively relieved, 
micro-voids initiate to nucleate. Once the number of voids reaches a 
certain level and they linkup together, secondary cracks will be formed 
and final fracture is caused. For ductile fracture, the tensile strength is 
generally much lower than the crack-propagation stress, with the result 
that necking occurs prior to material failure [27] . The true tensile stress 
in the necking region is much higher than that in the non-necking zone, 
so the void linkup and subsequent processes will be restricted to this 
region. For the above reason, the cup-and-cone fracture surface ( Fig. 4 
(a)) and the noticeable necking ( Fig. 3 ) can be seen in the RT samples. 
Under the influence of high stress concentration at the α / β
t 
interfaces, β
t 
is also deformed to a certain extent. Since β
t 
presents a pearlite-like 
lamellar morphology, it is easy to observe the bending of the lamellae 
( Figs. 5 (a) and 8 (e)). It is generally believed that dimples are the product 
of dislocation movement, so the presence of high-density dimples can be 
observed in fracture surface of the RT samples, while the high per -
centage of necking indicates that the material has sufficient strain to 
withstand the nucleation and coalescence of micro-voids ( Fig. 5 (a)). On 
the other hand, the work hardening stage of the samples deformed at RT 
is much lower than that at LT, and the true stress reached by the samples 
is accordingly lower, so that secondary cracks are not easily observed in 
the fracture surfaces ( Fig. 4 (c)). 
As noted, it can be seen that deformation at LT can activate high 
density of twins, rendering twinning as the dominant deformation 
mechanism [51 – 53] . This allows a large number of twin-matrix in -
terfaces to become an obstacle to dislocation slip [54] . Meanwhile, when 
the onset of slipping becomes difficult, plastic deformation will proceed 
through the coordination of twinning, which causes the continuous in -
crease of uniform plastic strain, allowing the material to reach extremely 
high stress level. Generally speaking, crack initiation is highly sensitive 
to sample surface condition when material is in high stress level 
[55 – 57] . Crack tends to nucleate at some surface defects such as 
machining defects, etc. The fracture surface at LT exhibits a ‘radial ’ 
characteristic ( Fig. 4 (d)), which should be caused by high local stress in 
the bar samples. The sample surface inevitably contains some tiny de -
fects, which will be dramatically magnified when the external stress 
level is high enough, making them the most sensitive area for cracking. 
Once the stress level reaches to fracture strength, crack initiates at the 
surface defects. Crack subsequently propagates from one end to the 
other end, forming a ‘radical ’ fracture morphology in the LT samples 
( Fig. 4 (d)). Nevertheless, crack propagation is highly affected by the 
following two factors. On the one hand, the elastic strain as a result of 
the deviation of atomic equilibrium during deformation improves the 
system energy in the form of the elastic strain energy, which will drive 
crack extension in order to relax the deviation and render atoms back to 
the balance position [27] . One the other hand, plastic zone is produced 
in front of crack tip, which will blunt crack tip and in turn retard crack 
extension [27] . For the LT samples, more especially, deformation 
twinning as an activated deformation mode continuously triggers

5. Conclusions 
Tensile behaviors and the resulting damage-fracture characteristics 
of Ti-2.5Cu α -titanium alloy were systematically investigated at liquid- 
nitrogen temperature (LT) plus room temperature (RT) for compari -
son. The main conclusions are drawn as follows: 
(1) The LT samples possess superior strength and ductility concur -
rently. Both yield strength ( σ
y
) and ultimate tensile strength ( σ
b
) 
improve more than 70%, whilst total elongation at fracture ( A ) adds 
more than 33% in comparison with that in the RT counterparts. Mean -
while, the LT samples are stretched mainly by uniform strain without 
noticeable necking like that in the RT samples. 
(2) Typical cup-and-cone fracture surface appears in the RT samples, 
while it is the ‘radial ’ fracture surface that arises in the LT samples. Such 
specific fracture surface is constructed by two characteristic morphol -
ogies of crack-initiation zone at sample surface and the ensuing crack- 
propagation zone. 
(3) A large number of micro-voids are dispersed underneath fracture 
surface in the RT samples, whereas the number of micro-voids is 
dramatically reduced in the LT samples. Nevertheless, Vickers hardness 
at the positions is always higher in the latter. 
(4) Ordinary dislocation gliding is prevalent in the RT samples, but 
instead deformation twinning becomes the dominant deformation mode 
in the LT samples. Deformation twinning itself and pronounced 
twinning-induced dynamic Hall-Petch effect steadily accommodate 
plastic deformation, contributing to simultaneous enhancement of 
strength and ductility. This process also results in high stress state in 
samples, increasing the sensitivity of surface cracking. Hence, the 
‘radial ’ fracture morphology is formed in the LT samples.
