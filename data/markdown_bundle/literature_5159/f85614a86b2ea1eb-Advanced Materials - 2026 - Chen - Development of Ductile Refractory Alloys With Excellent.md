2.2 PowderMetallurgy
Powdermetallurgyisespeciallyusefulwhenrefractoryalloysare
difficult to produce using ingot metallurgy. It generally consists
of several key steps, including powder preparation and mixing,
powder compaction, and sintering (Figure2b)[29]. A variety of
sintering techniques, such as hot press, hot isostatic pressing,
and spark plasma sintering, have been extensively developed to
producebulksampleswithahighrelativedensity.Inparticular,
applying external fields like pressure, electric, microwave, and
coupled multi-physic fields could enhance the driving force of
sintering, thereby promoting densification at relatively lower
temperatures [8, 33]. Different from casting, powder metallurgy
can effectively suppress the compositional segregation and the
formationofdendrites.
Inpowdermetallurgy,thegrainsizecanbereducedtohundreds
of nanometers with fine powders and grain growth inhibitors
[8]. For instance, the average grain size can decrease to 200
nm in W with the addition of the grain growth inhibitor HfO2
[34]. However, the impurities, such as C, O, and N, are more
easily introduced during powder preparation [35, 36]. Thus, the
mitigation of impurities becomes a critical challenge in powder
metallurgyofrefractoryalloys.

Themechanismsgoverningtheductile-to-brittletransitionhave
long been a controversial topic in refractory metals and alloys.
Previous studies indicated that the DBTT is controlled by the
competition between crack tip blunting and cleavage [52–54].
Early models, such as the Rice criterion, proposed that the
ductile behavior of BCC metals is governed by their ability to
emitdislocationsfromcracktips[ 55].Thecontinuousandrapid
nucleation of dislocations could generate a large plastic zone
to suppress crack propagation. Besides dislocation nucleation,
dislocation mobility is also considered to play a dominant role
in determining the ductile-to-brittle transition. Hirsch et al.
emphasized that the materials undergo brittle fracture when
dislocations cannot migrate rapidly at the crack tip to facilitate
crack-tipblunting[ 56].Gumbschetal.alsopointedoutthatthe
high dislocation mobility facilitates the activation of dislocation
sources, enabling sufficient dislocations to move toward the
crack tip [57]. Both experimental characterization and atom-

ductile-to-brittle transition correlates with kink-pair formation
on screw dislocations, highlighting the critical role of screw
dislocations in controlling this behavior [58–60]. Recent studies

4.1 EnhancingIntrinsicDuctility
Three ductility criteria have been proposed to quantitatively
evaluate the intrinsic ductility of refractory metals and their
alloys, including the Pugh ratio (G/B)[ 125], Cauchy pressure
[126],andvalenceelectronconcentration(VEC)[ 127].
Pugh proposed an empirical criterion to predict whether the
material is brittle or ductile in terms of the ratio between the
shear modulusG and bulk modulusB [125]. Many studies have
s h o w nt h a tm a t e r i a l sw i t haG/B ratio below 0.4–0.6 tend to
exhibitductiledeformation,whilethosehavingvaluesabovethis
thresholdarepronetobebrittle[ 128,129].Borgesetal.evaluated
theG/BratiosofvariousrefractorymetalsandalloysusingDFT
calculations (Figure7a)[ 98]. It was found that incorporating
group IV elements (Ti, Zr, Hf) into RHEAs can effectively
enhance their intrinsic ductility by reducing the shear modulus
G[98].
Another widely employed criterion is the Cauchy
pressure proposed by Pettifor [ 126]. In cubic crystals,
Cauchy pressure is defined as the difference between
two elastic constants, C12 − C44. It describes the atomic
bonding direction and serves to identify the bonding
types. A positive Cauchy pressure ( C12 − C44 > 0)
suggests predominantly non-directional metallic bonding,
which is favorable for plastic deformation. Conversely, a
negativeCauchypressureindicatesastrongdirectionalcovalent
bonding, typically associated with brittle failure upon the
external load. Senkov et al. found that the normalized Cauchy
pressure (C12 − C44)/B for cubic materials exhibits a linear
correlation with the Pugh ratio, suggesting that the two criteria
are equivalent in evaluating the intrinsic ductile properties of
materials[128].
The VEC criterion has been used by Sheikh et al. to reveal the
ductilityofRHEAs[ 130].Theoreticalcalculationsdemonstrated
that the intrinsic brittleness of pure W and Mo primarily arises
from their high VEC, making the Fermi level located in a
stable region of the electronic band structure. As a result, a
large tensile strength is required to trigger shear instability,
causing a cleavage fracture prior to shear deformation [131,
132]. Adding transition metals with less VEC in W and Mo,
such as group V or IV elements, can effectively lower the
Fermi level position. This behavior could facilitate shear insta-
bility to promote dislocation nucleation at the tensile loading,
thereby suppressing cleavage fracture [127, 133]. As shown in
Figure 7b, the VEC is smaller than 4.5 in ductile HfNbTiZr
and HfNbTaTiZr, and has larger values in more brittle RHEAs
[130].

6.3 Oxidation-ResistantCoatings
Thedepositionofanti-oxidationcoatingsonthesurfaceiswidely
recognized as the effective method to improve the oxidation
resistance of refractory alloys at high temperatures. They could
form a stable and adherent layer that impedes oxygen ingress
while maintaining good compatibility with the substrate. Sili-
cide coatings have received extensive attention among various
coatings[212,213].TheglassySiO 2 couldenhancetheoxidation
resistance of refractory alloys at temperatures up to 1100◦C
[213]. However, as the temperature further increases, the faster
diffusion and evaporation of Si lead to incomplete coverage
by SiO2. This promotes oxygen ingress and porosity formation,
therebyreducingtheoxidationresistanceandtheservicelifetime
[214].
To address this issue, some modifications have been made
to silicide coatings. First, the addition of B promotes the
formation of glassy borosilicate scale, which enhances the
self-sealing behavior of pores or cracks in the coatings,
thus preventing oxygen from penetrating to the substrate
[215, 216]. Second, silicides doped with multiple transition
metals can also effectively suppress the evaporation of silicon
oxides and thus improve the oxidation resistance of the
coatings. A slurry sintered (Nb,Ti,Mo)Si 2-(Ti,Nb,Mo)5Si3
biphasic composite coating has been reported to be able
to protect C-103 from oxidation for 100 h at 1500◦C[ 217].
Third, introducing oxides with a higher coefficient of
thermal expansion in silicide coatings [218] or a diffusion
barrier/buffer layer between the silicide coating and the
substrate could reduce the risk of cracking during thermal
cycling. For example, a new silicide coating modified with
Y2O3-stabilized ZrO2 was developed on a Mo-based alloy
[219]. This coating consists of an outer MoSi2-ZrSi2-SiO2 layer
and an inner MoSi2 layer. This modified coating possessed
superior oxidation protection at high temperatures with a
mass loss of only 0.96 mg/cm2 after 22 h of exposure in air at
1725◦C.
At extremely high temperatures above 1800◦C, strong oxi-
dation and ablation conditions render silicide coatings less
effective due to a high mass loss. Then, ultra-high temper-
ature ceramic composite coatings, typically composed of car-
bides/borides and silicides, are taken to protect the refractory
alloys.Underultra-high-temperatureoxidation/ablation,thesur-
facecarbides/boridesareconvertedintoporousrefractorymetal
oxides, while the silicides are transformed into a fluid, glassy
SiO2 phase.Thismoltensilicaeffectivelyinfiltratesandsealsthe
poresoftherefractoryoxideskeleton,formingaprotectivebarrier.
Furthermore, the volatilization of SiO2 is partially inhibited
by this porous refractory metal oxide. As a result, the ultra-
high temperature ceramic composite coating exhibits superior
oxidation/ablationresistancecomparedtopuresilicidecoatings,
particularly at temperatures above 1800◦C. For instance, the
linearablationrateofalow-pressureplasma-sprayedZrB 2-MoSi2
composite coating was reported to be as low as 0.032µm/s
under plasma ablation testing at a surface temperature of
1950◦C[220].

7.2.2 StrengtheningatExtremelyHighTemperatures
It is well-known that the strength drops obviously in metals
when the temperature increases above∼0.6Tm due to the ther-
mally driven evolution of microstructures, such as dislocation
annihilation, grain coarsening, and second-phase growth. Most
strengthening mechanisms that work well at room temperature
become less effective at high temperatures. Thus, it is very
important to design a novel microstructure with good heat-
resistance. As we mentioned above, the prevalent approaches
forelevatinghigh-temperaturestrengthinrefractoryalloysfocus
on incorporating hard second phases into the ductile matrix.
Someembeddedphaseswithcore-shellstructurecanexhibitslow
growth rate due to the good thermal stability of particle/matrix
interface. A few cases have shown that incorporating core-shell
particles could help to promote the high-temperature strength
in Al alloys [224] and Cu alloys [225]. Besides core-shell second
phases, embedding B2 intermetallics in BCC matrix provides
another promising way to maintain excellent thermal stability
at elevated temperatures owing to their good lattice coherency.
The coherent B2/BCC interface not only acts as a strong barrier
to impede dislocation motion, but also effectively mitigates the
stressconcentrationtosuppresscracking.

7.2.3 High-TemperatureOxidationProtection
The inherently poor oxidation resistance of refractory alloys
significantlylimitstheirlong-termserviceathightemperatures.
Volatile oxides such as Ta2O5,M o O3,a n dN b2O5 can be formed
when the temperature increases above a few hundred degrees
Celsius, resulting in catastrophic oxidation. Depositing robust
oxidation-resistant coatings on the refractory alloys offers a
promising pathway to protect the matrix at elevated tempera-
tures. The oxidation duration of coatings is strongly affected by
the mismatch of thermal expansion coefficient and chemical
interdiffusion during prolonged exposure at high temperatures.
Thus, it is essential to design a multilayer architecture in
order to mitigate the risk of catastrophic spallation. A ceramic
interlayer with a thermal expansion coefficient close to that
of the substrate can effectively reduce thermal stresses. More
importantly, a diffusion barrier layer between the coating and
the substrate is usually required in order to suppress the
interdiffusion of detrimental elements. At temperatures above
1800◦C, the ultra-high temperature ceramic components could
enhance the thermal stability by forming a porous refractory
oxide skeleton that supports the glassy phase and reduces
SiO2 volatilization. The glassy phase in the outer layer could
promote sealing of surface cracks, thereby preventing oxygen
ingress.Overall,aneffectiveoxidation-resistantcoatingneedsto
havemultipleproperties,includingresistancetodiffusion,stress
accommodation, and self-healing ability to withstand extreme
environments.
7.2.4 Data-DrivenMachineLearningApproaches
Recently, data-driven machine learning (ML) has emerged as
a powerful tool to accelerate thediscovery of novel refrac-
tory alloys. A multi-objective optimization strategy integrat-
ing machine learning, genetic search, and cluster analysis
has been successfully employed by Wen et al. to search
for RHEAs with superior strength and ductility [226]. The
predicted Zr13Nb27Mo26Hf13Ta21 RHEA exhibits a compressive
yield strength of ∼940 MPa at 1200◦C and a compressive
fracture strain of 17.2% at room temperature. Inspired by the
physics-informed ML method, a comprehensive ML strategy
with integrated material knowledge is proposed by employ-
ing physical parameters (such as VEC, atomic size differ-
ence, mixing enthalpy, mixing entropy, and average melting
point) to capture the thermodynamic and atomistic proper-
ties of alloys, thereby providing a physical mechanism for
predicting mechanical properties. Qi et al. integrated high-
throughput ML with Monte Carlo simulations supplemented
by DFT calculations to effectively navigate phase selection
and mechanical property predictions [227]. The predicted Al-
enriched RHEA with single ordered B2 phase exhibits a
remarkable mechanical property, including a compressive yield
strength up to 1.7 GPa and a fracture strain exceeding 50%
[227]. However, most of previous studies focus on the predic-
tions of compressive properties in less ductile RHEAs. More
effort should be made to promote the discovery of ductile
refractory alloys with excellent tensile properties based on
the development of multi-objective optimization frameworks,
physics-informed ML and their integration with atomistic
calculations.
