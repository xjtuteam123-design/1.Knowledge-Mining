Glass formation, corrosion behavior and mechanical properties
of bulk glassy Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb alloys
Chunling Qin a,*, Wei Zhang b, Katsuhiko Asami b, Naofumi Ohtsu b, Akihisa Inoue b
a Japan Science and Technology Agency, Sendai 980-8577, Japan

Abstract
Bulk glassy (Cu 0.6Hf0.25Ti0.15)100/C0xNbx (x = 0–8 at.%) alloys with maximum diameters of 2.5–4 mm were produced by copper-
mold casting. The corrosion rates in 1 N HCl and 3 mass% NaCl solutions signiﬁcantly decrease with an increase in Nb content.
The 6 at.% Nb alloy shows distinct spontaneous passive region in the HCl solution. The alloys with a larger amount of Nb are spon-
taneously passivated with lower passive current densities and higher pitting corrosion potentials in the NaCl solution. X-ray pho-
toelectron spectroscopy analysis revealed that the formation of Hf-, Nb- and Ti-enriched surface ﬁlm on the alloys could be
responsible for the high corrosion resistance of the Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb alloys in HCl and NaCl solutions. Young /C213s modulus, compressive
fracture strength and compressive plastic strain are 130 GPa, 2405 MPa and 2.8%, respectively, for the 4 at.% Nb alloy, 126 GPa,
2305 MPa and 2.1%, respectively, for the 8 at.% Nb alloy.

1. Introduction
Amorphous alloys usually have high strength, soft
magnetic properties and good corrosion resistance,
which are diﬀerent from the corresponding crystalline
alloys; hence they are considered to have many potential
applications as advanced engineering materials [1,2].
However, amorphous type alloys have mainly been fab-
ricated in the form of ribbons, thin ﬁlms and ﬁne wires
because of their very high cooling rate requirements.
The limitations of size and shape as well as low thermal
stability have restricted their ﬁelds of application. Since
the ﬁndings of glassy alloys with a large supercooled li-
quid region in the Mg–Ce–Ni [3] and La–Al–Ni [4] sys-
tems, followed by the successes in producing bulk glassy
alloys in the Ln- [5] (Ln = lanthanide metals) and Mg-
[6] based systems by a copper-mold casting method,
great eﬀorts have been devoted to developing new glassy
type alloy systems with high glass-forming ability
(GFA) and large three-dimensional material shapes.
Recently, a number of bulk glassy alloys have been
synthesized in various multicomponent systems such as
Zr- [7,8], Ti- [9], Fe- [10], Pd–Cu- [11], Co- [12], Ni-
[13] and Cu- [14–16] based alloys. Among these bulk
glassy alloy systems, one of the most important alloys
can be regarded as Cu-based alloys exhibiting good
mechanical properties. In 2000, Inoue et al. succeeded
for the ﬁrst time in synthesizing Cu-based bulk glassy al-
loys in Cu–Zr–Ti [14], Cu–Hf–Ti [15] and Cu–Zr–Hf–Ti
[16] systems. Multicomponent Cu-based bulk glassy
alloys were prepared in the diameter range up to
4 mm, and exhibited high fracture strength of 2000–
2160 MPa as well as distinct plastic strain of 0.8–1.7%
[14–16], much larger than observed in other bulk glassy

2. Experimental procedure
Master alloys with nominal compositions of
(Cu
0.6Hf0.25Ti0.15)100/C0xNbx (x = 0–8 at.%) were pre-
pared by arc melting mixtures of the pure metals of cop-
per, hafnium, titanium and niobium in an argon
atmosphere. Bulk alloys in a cylindrical form with a
length of about 50 mm and diameters up to 4 mm were
fabricated by copper-mold casting. The structure of the
specimens was examined by X-ray diﬀraction (XRD)
using Cu Ka radiation and transmission electron micros-
copy (TEM). The thermal stability associated with the
glass transition temperature ( T
g) and crystallization
temperature ( Tx) for the glassy alloys was investigated
by diﬀerential scanning calorimetry (DSC) at a heating
rate of 0.67 K/s. The liquidus temperature was deter-
mined by diﬀerential thermal analysis (DTA) at a heat-
ing and cooling rate of 0.033 K/s.
The corrosion behavior of the glassy alloys was eval-
uated by weight loss and electrochemical measurements.
Prior to corrosion tests, the specimens were mechani-
cally polished in cyclohexane with silicon carbide paper
up to grit 2000, degreased in acetone, washed in distilled
water, dried in air, and further exposed to air for 24 h
for good reproducibility. Electrolytes of 1 N HCl,
3 mass% NaCl, 1 N H
2SO4 and 1 N H 2SO4 + 0.001 N
NaCl solutions open to air were used at room tempera-
ture (about 298 K). The corrosion rates were estimated
from the weight loss after immersion in the solutions
for one or two weeks. The weight loss for each alloy
was measured three times and the average value was
used for corrosion rate estimation. Electrochemical
measurements were conducted in a three-electrode cell
using a platinum counter electrode and a Ag/AgCl refer-
ence electrode. Potentiodynamic polarization curves
were measured at a potential sweep rate of 50 mV min
/C01
after open-circuit immersion for about 20 min when the
open-circuit potential became almost steady. The polar-
ization curves were repeatedly measured at least three
times to conﬁrm reproducibility. After the corrosion
tests, surfaces of the alloys were observed by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM).
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measure-
ments, for surface analysis of the specimens before and
after immersion in solutions, were performed by using
a photoelectron spectrometer with monochromatized
Al Ka excitation (h m = 1486.6 eV). The composition of
the surface ﬁlm and the composition of the underlying
alloy surface were quantitatively determined by a previ-
ously proposed method using the integrated intensities
of photoelectrons under the assumption of a three-layer
model of the outmost contaminant hydrocarbon layer of
uniform thickness, the surface ﬁlm of uniform thickness,
and the underlying alloy surface of X-ray-photoelec-
tron-spectroscopically inﬁnite thickness [19,20].
Mechanical properties under compressive deforma-
tion mode were measured at room temperature with an
Instron-type testing machine. Young/C213s modulus was mea-
sured using a strain-gauge meter. The gauge dimensions
of specimens were 2 mm in diameter and 4 mm in height,
and the strain rate was 5 · 10
/C04 s/C01. The fracture surface
was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

three empirical rules [1,25,26]: (1) multicomponent sys-
tem consisting of more than three elements, (2) signiﬁ-
cant atomic size mismatches above 12%, and (3)
suitable negative heats of mixing. In the present Cu–
Hf–Ti–Nb alloys system, as discussed in previous re-
search [18], additional Nb element has nearly zero or po-
sitive heats of mixing against the base constituent
elements of Cu, Hf and Ti [27], leading to a decrease
in the degree of the satisfaction of the three empirical
rules as compared with the Cu–Hf–Ti ternary alloy.
The decrease is concluded to result in a decrease in
GFA through the decrease in DT
x and Tg/Tl. The rela-
tion among DTx, Tg/Tl and maximum sample thickness
for the present alloys indicates that the GFA is closely
related to both of DT
x and Tg/Tl for the formation of
bulk glassy alloys. Moreover, it has been pointed out
that the T
g and Tl of glassy alloys reﬂect the degree of
bonding force among the constituent elements
[15,16,28]. As shown in Table 2 , T
l increases with an in-
crease in Nb content, though Tg almost remains at a
constant value. The higher values of Tl allow us to pre-
sume that the bonding forces among Cu, Hf, Ti and Nb
elements are stronger than those among Cu, Hf and Ti
elements. As a result, the higher compressive fracture
strength of the present bulk glassy Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb alloys
is suggested to originate from the stronger bonding
forces among the constituent elements due to the pres-
ence of Nb element. On the other hand, the positive or
nearly zero heats of mixing for Cu–Nb, Hf–Nb and
Ti–Nb atomic pairs seem to reﬂect the larger plastic
strain. Due to the positive or nearly zero heats of mix-
ing, the atoms in such atom pairs would easily move rel-
ative to each other and assist plastic deformation. That
is why the addition of these elements results in an in-
crease in the fracture strength as well as plastic elonga-
tion of the alloys.

alloying elements in the equilibrium state and hence
allowing selective alloying of strongly passivating ele-
ments. It is well known that niobium is one of the most

5. Conclusions
With the aim of developing a new bulk glassy alloy
with high corrosion resistance as well as good mechanical
properties, the glass-forming ability, mechanical proper-
ties and corrosion resistance of the Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb glassy
alloys were investigated. The results obtained are sum-
marized as follows:
(1) Bulk glassy alloys with a maximum thickness
(t
max) of 2.5–4 mm were synthesized in the
(Cu0.6Hf0.25Ti0.15)100/C0xNbx (x = 0–8 at.%) system
by copper-mold casting. These alloys exhibit a
large supercooled liquid region ( DT
x) of 38–60 K
and a high reduced glass transition temperature
(T
g/Tl) of 0.62–0.63, indicating high GFA and high
thermal stability of the supercooled liquid, though
the glass-forming ability decreases with addition of
Nb.
(2) The addition of Nb is eﬀective for improving the
corrosion resistance of the alloys in all examined
media. All the alloys possess high corrosion resis-
tance in H
2SO4 and H 2SO4 containing a small
amount of chloride ions solutions. In HCl solu-
tion, the 6 at.% Nb alloy shows a distinct sponta-
neous passive region. In NaCl solution, all the
Nb-containing alloys exhibit spontaneous passive
region; there is a lower passive current density
and higher pitting corrosion potential with a larger
amount of Nb.
(3) The formation of the Hf-, Nb- and Ti-enriched
surface ﬁlm on the alloys could be responsible
for the high corrosion resistance of the bulk glassy
Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb alloys in HCl and NaCl solutions.
(4) Young /C213s modulus, compressive fracture strength
and compressive plastic strain are 130 GPa,
2405 MPa and 2.8%, respectively, for the 4 at.%
Nb alloy, and 126 GPa, 2305 MPa and 2.1%,
respectively, for the 8 at.% Nb alloy. The addition
of Nb, with zero or positive heats of mixing
against the other constituent elements in the
Cu–Hf–Ti–Nb system, may be the reason for the
simultaneous achievement of high strength and
large plastic elongation.
