Abstract: Fretting fatigue behavior of Ni-base single crystal (NBSX) superalloys was 
investigated based on in-situ scanning electron microscope (SEM) observations. Evolutions of 
slip lines and initiations of cracks were observed. Slip lines were found on sp ecimen free 
surface along three typical directions. Short cracks were observed to initiate along the slip line. 
Crystal plasticity constitutive model considering cyclic hardening effect wa s employed. 
Crystal plasticity finite element method (CPFEM) simulation showed the activations of 
crystallographic slip systems at contact region. The slip plane with the maxim um plastic slip 
was determined as dominate slip plane. Equivalent plastic strain was proved effec tive to 
predict crack initiation. The simulation results including predicted slip lines, crack initiation 
locations and orientations were in good agreements with observations. 
Keywords: fretting fatigue; crystal plasticity; slip lines; in-situ SEM experiment 
 
1. Introduction 
Fretting occurs when two contact surfaces are subjected to small amplitude oscillat ory 
motion which is usually tangential and in micrometer range. The failures caus ed by fretting 
are major concerns in a wide range of industries. These include the dovetail j oints between

## Page 3

compressor blades and disks in gas turbines and aircraft engines. According to the survey of 
US Air Force, fretting fatigue is responsible for about one-sixth of the hi gh-cycle-fatigue 
failures in engines, and this leads to high maintenance costs (about six million dollars per 
annum) [1-3]. 
The fretting experiment was first performed by Ruiz [4], and developed by other 
researchers [2, 5-11]. As NBSX superalloys have been widely used on high perf ormance 
blades, particular attention is paid to the fretting fatigue between N BSX blades and disks in 
recent years. Huang et al. conducted fretting experiments of NBSX at room temper ature and 
the effect of crystallographic orientation on the fretting response was studied. It was found the 
friction in the <110> direction is higher than that in the <100> direction under a high enough 
normal contact load. They attributed this difference to the plastic deformation in the surface 
layer and the correlative microstructure changes, both of which were coupled with the crystal 
orientation [12]. Arakere et al. carried out the real object experiments under room temperature 
using the PWA1422 single crystal turbine blades used in the NASA engines. In their work, a 
significant difference in crack formation locations was showed under different crystal 
orientations. Microscopy observations revealed the cracks initiated at carbi de precipitations 
along {111} octahedral slip planes [13]. Murthy et al. developed fretting fatigue tests at 610 ć 
and fatigue lives were obtained under a range of loading conditions [14]. Matlik et al. 
performed 650 ć fretting fatigue experiments under a various of primary and second 
orientations and crystallographic and noncrystallographic cracks are categorized [15]. Their 
work shows the formation and propagation of fretting crack are affected by orienta tion and 
temperature. At low and medium temperature crack initiates along {111} octahedral s lip 
planes, indicating that crack nucleation is a result of crystallographic sli p. However, in most 
experiments the observations of crack behavior are conducted by fractographic analysis after 
the test or interrupting the test. Few attempts are made to develop techniques on in-situ 
observations of microstructure evolution including formation and propagation of slip li nes 
and short cracks. 
Dissimilar anisotropic fretting contact theory based on Stroh formalism and singul ar 
integral equations (SIE) was developed by Rajeev and Farris [16]. Their study indicates that 
the surface contact tractions do not vary substantially with crystal orientati on under a

## Page 4

quasi-static fretting load, which has also been reported by Fleury et al [ 17]. However, the 
subsurface stress distribution is a strong function of crystal orientation [18]. SIE theory takes 
consideration of anisotropic elasticity, Coulomb friction and partial slip. Matrix equat ions are 
obtained containing the stress as unknown value to be solved. To obtain the stress solution, 
complex numerical integration and iteration should be undertaken. This theory has a few 
limitations because of its rigorous hypothesis including plain strain and semi- infinite 
condition, and it loses sight of plasticity. Many investigations suggest the contac t regions 
come up with obvious plastic deformation and plasticity plays a significant role in crack 
formation [12, 19]. In light of the shortcomings of current research, crystal plasticity theory is 
developed. With the advent of finite element method and computing technology, crystal 
plasticity finite element method (CPFEM) starts to be employed on the simulatio n of fretting. 
Considerable efforts have been made on the modeling of polycrystalline and texture [20-23]. 
Meanwhile, there have been several studies which focus on the CPFEM simulation of 
microstructure evolution in a single crystal. These including the simulation of crystallographic 
slip patterns, plastic zones, activated and dominate slip systems [24-26]. Combi ned with the 
experimental observations of slip lines, the simulation was found to be an effective method to 
predict the crack initiation, and it can well explain the effect of cr ystal orientation on crack 
initiation. Sabnis et al. studied cylindrical indentation on a NBSX superalloy [25] and notched 
NBSX tensile specimens [24], and they used Visibility Index to measure the visibil ity of 
different slip systems. Biswas et al. investigated interaction between a notch an d cylindrical 
voids under a tensile loading. These loading forms are quasi-static (tensile or indentation). 
However, the simulation of crystallographic slip in a single crystal under fret ting fatigue load 
is seldom studied. Besides, in these studies only postmortem SEM observations were 
conducted to obtain the images of slip lines. With the development of real-ti me fatigue 
observation technique, in-situ fatigue microscopic image can show the formation and 
evolution of slip lines, which opens up a whole new range of possibilities t o reveal the 
deformation mechanisms during fretting fatigue process. 
Researchers have previously pointed out that fretting accelerates crack ini tiation rapidly 
[11, 27]. Matlik et al. indicates fretting fatigue is distinguished from pl ain fatigue in the early 
stages of crack formation and growth [27]. When the crack grows beyond the inf luence area

## Page 5

of the fretting contact stresses, the methods to model crack propagation of plain fatigue can be 
applied on fretting fatigue. So it is extremely important to explore the modeli ng of fretting 
fatigue crack formation process. There have been several studies of crack formation 
prediction on isotropic materials. Ruiz et al. proposed a parameter 
k considering interface 
stress and slip displacement. However, Iyer and Mall found in some situations this parameter 
does not work well [28, 29]. Some other models have taken friction and wear into 
consideration. Ciavarella et al. used a frictional energy dissipation parameter [1, 30]. Davies 
and Hojjati-Talemi introduced damage factor into constitutive relation to ca lculate cyclic 
stress-strain response [31, 32]. However, most of these investigations focus on iso tropic 
material. Only a small fraction of research involve the prediction of fre tting fatigue crack 
formation, including crack formation locations and orientations in NBSX superall oys [12, 13, 
27]. 
To summarize, it is the purpose of this paper to provide further insight into the formation 
and evolution of fretting slip lines and short cracks, using experimental and CPFEM method. 
The prediction approach of crack formation and propagation will be discussed. The paper is 
structured as following. Section 2 introduces the technique used to conduct in-situ fr etting 
fatigue experiment. Section 3 introduces the description of crystal plastici ty constitutive 
model considering cyclic hardening effect. The computational aspects are presented next, 
followed by the dominant slip plane approach. Section 4 presents the observations of slip 
lines and the comparisons with simulation results. Crack formation and propagation is a lso 
discussed in this section. Section 5 gives the conclusion. 
 
2. Set-up of in-situ fretting fatigue experiment 
The experiment was conducted with a servo-hydraulic testing system using in-si tu SEM. 
Experimental fixtures, including specimen, contact pads and normal force rig (Fig . 1(a)) are 
held in the vacuum chamber of SEM. The SEM is connected and controlled with a computer 
and the specimen platform can be moved within a 10 ×5 mm field of view (FOV) during the 
test process, making it possible to focus on every potential crack formation site. 
In order to carry out in-situ SEM observations during fatigue tests, the f retting fixture 
must be designed specially. On the basis of ASTM [33, 34] and JSME S015 [35], the fret ting

## Page 6

rig is modified with two circular arc chamfering on both edges to allow th e electron beam to 
reach the specimen surface unhindered (Fig. 1(a)). This seemingly minute modification 
enables real-time microscopy observation of specimen surface, including the evolution of slip 
lines and the formation process of crack. 
The specimen and pads are made up of NBSX superalloys, which are developed for high 
performance turbine blades. In most applications which aim to investigate engine blade to 
disk assemblies use a single-crystal nickel to contact against a polycrystalli ne nickel alloy. 
However, in present paper the material of the pads does not affect the studies on the 
mechanism. So a single-crystal nickel pad is applied in our study. The geometries of 
specimen and pads are shown in Fig. 1(b). The specimen is of dog-bone shape with thick ness 
of 1.2mm. The contact pad has a larger thickness (2mm) to ensure a total contact all t hrough 
the thickness direction during the fatigue test. The pads have two 0.75mm radius cylindrical 
feet. The fretting test is configured to be bridge type and cylinder-on-f lat line contact (Fig. 
1(c)). The length direction is [001] and the normal direction of contact sur faces is [110]. This 
orientation combination is chosen because it is the most severe case in the ana lytical analysis 
of Fleury et al [17], meanwhile [001] and [110] are the best primary orienta tion and 
secondary orientation in low cycle fatigue, respectively [36]. X-ray diffraction method is used 
to measure the crystallographic direction of testing slabs. An angular deviation of primary 
orientation and of secondary orientation are within 5° and 8°, which are in the acceptable 
range. To reveal the surface micromorphology, the specimen surfaces are polished to a mirror 
finish with 0.15 μm diamond paste.

3.2 Computational aspects 
A 3D finite element model is investigated in this paper. The geometries of computational 
model is the same as the experimental assembly illustrated in Fig. 1. In considerat ion of the 
symmetry of the assembly, a half-model is undertaken in the actual modeling to r educe the 
computational cost (Fig. 2(a)). The boundary and load conditions are also kept consistent with 
the experimental conditions. The 3 degrees of freedom of left side of specimen is const rained

## Page 9

as fixed boundary. The plane of symmetry on specimen is imposed with a symmetry boundary 
condition. Two load steps are set. First, a constant normal force is applied on the pad along 
y 
direction in global coordinates, thus the contact interaction between the speci men and pad 
takes effect. Then in the second load step, a bulk load is applied on the right side of specimen 
along x direction in global coordinates. In order to simulate the actual fatigue load, nam ely, 
to study the material behavior under reciprocating fretting motion, the bulk load is set as an 
alternating loading spectrum, and the loading period, maximum and minimum forces are 
identical with the experimental set-up. Due to the computational cost, the number of fatigue 
loading cycle should be a limited value, but it should ensure the back stress parameter ( ) X /g68
evolution to reach an equilibrium state. In this paper the loading cycle number is chosen as 10. 
Convergence is an important issue to be solved in a contact problem because contact is a 
highly-nonlinear problem [44]. To ensure the convergence of the solution, a small 
displacement load is applied on the pads to establish the contact interaction between pads and 
specimen before the normal force and bulk load are applied. Besides, the time increment is set 
to be small enough to ensure the convergence. More than 100 increments are used to appl y 
the bulk fatigue load. The mesh convergence is ensured by the criteria that th e contacting 
compression stress change is less than 5% when the mesh size is reduced by half. 
The interaction between specimen and pad is implemented as finite sliding 
surface-to-surface contact to avoid possible numerical divergence especially when fr iction is 
taken into consideration [23]. Frictional contact is imposed using penalty techni que and the 
friction coefficient is set to be 0.3 referring to Steijn’s friction test data [45] and Matlik et al. 
[27]. As is reported by Frederic et al, the results of penalty method are close to t he results of 
Lagrange multipliers method [46]. The penalty method is used because it is much more 
economical in computational cost of the analysis than the Lagrange multipliers m ethod and 
the latter method often causes severe convergence problems. The contact master surfa ce is 
applied on the pad and the specimen side surface to be the slave surface, because the m esh on 
the pad is coarser than the specimen. Fig. 2(b) illustrates the FE mesh use d for simulation. At 
contact region both specimen and pad surface are arranged with refined high-dens ity mesh to 
catch the stress concentration. In 
x direction, the element size of the innermost layer is in 
micron dimension. 10 seeds are applied uniformly along the thickness using sweep mesh

## Page 10

technique. This element size can not only meet the computational requirements but also be 
concordant with the smallest recognizable crack size in common SEM observation. T he 
specimen contains a total number of 41076 elements. At the contact region 32000 eight -node 
linear brick elements with incompatible modes are used because this element type is 
conformed to have better performance in contact circumstances [47]. The pad c ontains 6015 
elements of the same element type. 
The specimen and pad are assigned with the material property which has been defined in 
Section 3.1, using a user material subroutine (UMA T) of the FE platform. The com putation 
was performed with ABAQUS 6.13. Incremental line search is used in the constitutive update 
and Newton-Raphson (N-R) iteration scheme is employed to determine the shearing rates on 
each slip system [19, 23, 48].

5. Conclusion 
This paper focus on the observation and simulation of slip lines and short crack s in 
fretting fatigue. Slip lines of three typical directions are found on speci men free surface and 
the observed microcracks are in the direction of the slip lines which are induced by the slip 
plane 
(111) and (111) . This indicate crystallographic slip is the main mechanism of 
plastic deformation in fretting fatigue under our loading conditions. The slip l ines evolve and 
result in the formation and propagation of cracks. 
CPFEM simulation with an elastic-plastic crystallographic constitutive formula is 
applied on the fretting fatigue finite element model. Dominate slip systems and planes are 
obtained from simulation. The predicted slip lines are found to be in good agreements wi th 
those observed in the experiment. The visibility of slip lines are analyzed ac cording to the 
visibility of slip systems and plastic deformation which is quantified by equivalent plastic 
strain. 
Simulation and observation show that dramatic plastic slip occurs at the edge of conta ct 
region where the crack initiates. Equivalent plastic strain is confirmed as an ef fective value to 
characterize the local plastic deformation and can be a criterion to predic t the crack initiation 
location. CPFEM considering cyclic hardening effect shows the slip evolution under fa tigue 
loading. The slip region expands dramatically at the contact edge, which is correspondi ng to 
the crack formation and propagation in the experiment.
