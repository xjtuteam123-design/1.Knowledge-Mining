1. Introduction
GH4099 is a nickel-based superalloy widely used in various indus-
tries, particularly in aerospace and high-performance applications,
due to its exceptional high-temperature strength, oxidation resis-
tance, and corrosion resistance. [1] Its thermal strength primarily
arises from the coherent strain strengthening resulting from
the precipitation of the heat-resistant γ 0 phase ( γ 0-Ni3(Al, Ti))
within the matrix. [2,3] Additionally, the presence of carbide
precipitates enhances matrix stability [4] and contributes to high-
temperature strengthening at temperatures between 800 and
900 °C.[5] The inﬂu e n c eo fc a r b i d e s( s p e c iﬁcally their types, volume
fraction, size, and morphology) on the high-temperature proper-
ties of superalloys is complex and not fully understood. [6,7] For
instance, early-formed MC-type carbides can improve high-
temperature strength but are often brittle, which may lead to crack
initiation and propagation.[8,9] In the case of
the IN718 superalloy, MC carbide clusters
(25.7– 39.3 μm) have been identi ﬁed as key
sites for crack initiation, potentially leading
to catastrophic rupture. [10] Therefore, the
distribution and morphology of carbides
are critical factors in enhancing the high-
temperature mechanical properties of
nickel-based superalloys.
MC carbides are primarily located at
grain boundaries and within the grains,
where they impede dislocation movement
and contribute to the stability of the grain
boundaries, thereby improving the mechan-
ical properties of nickel-based superal-
loys.[11] High-temperature creep studies
have demonstrated that MC carbides
undergo plastic deformation under stress,
allowing them to sustain the applied load
and reduce stress concentrations at the
MC carbide/matrix interface. [12] However,
prolonged aging or aging at elevated temper-
atures induces chromium segregation along
dislocation lines, enhancing dislocation

M23C6 carbides.[13] Subsequently, crack propagation through the

of the alloy ’sm e c h a n i c a lp r o p e r t i e s .[14] In contrast, Kolotukhin
et al. investigated the microstructural evolution during thermal
solidiﬁcation of nickel-based superalloys and found that fragile
clusters of molten MC carbides may act as nucleation sites. [15]
This effect increases the undercooling, reduces the critical nucle-
ation radius, and reﬁnes the overall grain structure of the material,
thereby improving its mechanical properties. [16] Therefore, opti-
mizing the distribution and stability of MC carbides is crucial
for improving the high-temperature performance and durability
of nickel-based superalloys.
Particle-reinforced nickel-based composites have emerged as
a compelling solution to simultaneously enhance the strength
and toughness of high-temperature alloys. [17] Numerous stud-
ies have shown that ceramic or oxide particles can act as effec-

tion associated with γ 0 phase coarsening or dissolution, there by
improving high- and room-temperature strength, as well as
resistance to wear and creep. [19]

based superalloys to improve their microstructural characteristics and high-
temperature mechanical properties. The introduction of nano-NbC substantially
reﬁnes grain structure, facilitating a tra nsition from columnar-to-equiaxed
microstructures and promoting the formation of ﬁner MC-type carbides. This
results in signi ﬁcant enhancements in strength, c reep resistance ,a n dt h e r m a l

comes at the cost of reduced ductility. A n excessive concentration of nano-NbC
(2.0 wt%) leads to carbide agglomeration, which adversely affects both strength
and ductility. Transmission electron microscopy analysis reveals excellent

enhances load transfer ef ﬁciency and thereby improves mechanical properties.
These ﬁndings suggest that GH4099 superalloys, when enhanced with optimal
amounts of nano-NbC, are promising for a dvanced applications requiring high-

However, the practical implementation of such reinforcement
strategies often encounters signi ﬁcant challenges. For example,
IN718C reinforced with micrometer-sized TiN particles shows
substantial improvements in high-temperature strength, but
the interface between the particles and the matrix is prone to
cracking, resulting in a dramatic loss of ductility. [20] Similarly,
oxide dispersion-strengthened Ni-based alloys incorporating
Y2O3 show enhanced matrix strength and thermal performance.
Yet, due to dif ﬁculties in achieving uniform dispersion during
ball milling and sintering, Y 2O3 often segregates, undermining
overall alloy integrity.[21] These cases highlight two common lim-
itations in nanoparticle-reinforced metal matrix composites: poor
interfacial bonding and inhomogeneous particle distribution.
In parallel, alloy design strategies focusing on grain re ﬁne-
ment have also proven effective. For instance, K3 and K4169
alloys exhibit a 9.9% – 15% increase in yield strength through
grain reﬁnement, although this often comes with reduced grain
boundary ductility due to oxidation-induced embrittlement at ele-
vated temperatures.[22] A more tailored approach lies in the com-
positional tuning of ﬁne-grained superalloys, such as TM-321, by
introducing elements like Ta and W. These elements stabilize the
γ 0 phase and promote the formation of thermally stable MC car-
bides, thereby enhancing both tensile and creep resistance at
moderate temperatures. [23] In single-crystal superalloys, similar
beneﬁts have been observed, where Ta and W suppress harmful
TCP phases and contribute to improved grain boundary stability
via solid solution and precipitation strengthening mechanisms.
One notable route to achieving ultra ﬁne microstructures
is through thermomechanical processing. In Alloy 718, for
instance, cold deformation combined with δ-phase pinning
yields grain sizes as small as 0.75 μm, leading to a 14% increase
in ultimate tensile strength and improved high-temperature duc-
tility.[24] The precipitated δ-phase acts as an effective grain growth
inhibitor. These insights provide the conceptual foundation for
the current work.
Building on this foundation, our study introduces nano-NbC
particles into GH4099, a nickel-based superalloy, as a novel strat-
egy for concurrent strengthening and toughening. Unlike previ-
ously studied carbides such as TiC or WC, the use of NbC in such
a system remains unexplored, marking this work as a pioneering
contribution. Nano-NbC particles serve dual functions: they act
as potent heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidi ﬁcation,
facilitating the formation of equiaxed grains and re ﬁned micro-
structures; and they promote a uniform dispersion of secondary
MC-type carbides. These nanoscale particles also enhance dislo-
cation interactions and stabilize grain boundaries, contributing
to a signi ﬁcant improvement in both strength and ductility. A
comparative analysis of nanocarbide systems (TiC, WC, NbC)
in terms of particle size, wettability, interfacial bonding, and ther-
mal stability —presented in Table 1 —further underscores the
advantages of NbC as an effective reinforcing phase for GH4099.
In composite materials, the selection of reinforcing phases
must consider factors such as elastic modulus, thermal expan-
sion, particle size, thermal stability, and density. [25] For instance,
oxide nanoparticles exhibit low wettability with the matrix, which
can lead to reduced strength due to agglomeration and grain
boundary segregation. [26] Nickel-based alloys face similar issues
with wettability when combined with nitride ceramics. [27] In con-
trast, carbide nanoparticles enhance high-temperature strength
and offer good wettability and bondability with superalloys. [28]
However, the high cost, processing complexity, and tendency
for TaC and WC ﬁbers to dissolve at high temperatures limit
their widespread application. [29,30] Titanium (Ti) and niobium
(Nb) are common components of primary MC carbides in
nickel-based superalloys. [31] NbC, which has a face-centered-
cubic (FCC) structure similar to that of nickel-based alloys,
shows good wettability. [32,33] As exogenous additives, NbC
nanoparticles facilitate the in situ modi ﬁcation of GH4099 car-
bides, making them a promising approach for enhancing the
high-temperature properties of GH4099 in service. In this
work, NbC nanoparticles are proposed as reinforcers to tailor
the microstructure of GH4099 superalloy and improve its
high-temperature performance.
2. Experimental Section
2.1. Material Preparation
In this study, nano-NbC particles were synthesized using ther-
mal arc plasma nanopowder preparation technology. [34] High-
purity niobium and carbon powders were thoroughly mixed,
compacted, and placed into a graphite crucible as the anode.
A tungsten rod was used as the cathode, and high-purity argon
gas was introduced into the experimental chamber. The current
was maintained at a stable 120 A during the powder preparation
process. The voltage and arc irradiation area were controlled by
adjusting the distance between the tungsten rod and the crucible.
After synthesis, the resultant powder was passivated for a speci-
ﬁed duration before being removed from the chamber. The X-ray
diffraction (XRD) pattern and microstructure of the NbC nano-
particles are shown in Figure 1 a,b.
The melting process was carried out using a 50 kg vacuum
high-frequency induction melting furnace. The matrix composi-
tion of the GH4099 superalloy is listed in Table 2. To enhance the
bonding between the nano-NbC particles and the GH4099 spher-
ical powder, as well as to improve the dispersion of NbC nano-
particles in the melt, the nano-NbC particles were mechanically
mixed with micrometer-sized GH4099 raw powder at a mass
ratio of 1:10. The resulting mixed powder was then wrapped
in high-purity nickel foil and compacted using a ﬂat oil pump

The high concentrations of solid solution elements such as Cr,
Mo, and W in GH4099 resulted in low ﬂuidity and increased seg-
regation risks. To address this issue, the resulting ingot was sub-
jected to a solution treatment. The ingot was ﬁrst cut into regular
blocks measuring 70 /C220 /C220 cm and placed into a tube fur-
nace. The temperature was then ramped at 10 °C min /C01 from
room temperature (20 °C) to 1120 °C over a period of 110 min,
surpassing the critical temperature (1050 °C) to facilitate the pre-
cipitation of the γ 0 phase from the γ matrix. The ingot was held at
1120 °C for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution of the segregated
elements into the matrix. Subsequently, the furnace was opened,
and the ingot was removed for air cooling to maintain a homo-
geneous solid solution structure. Following this, the ingot was
heated at the same rate to 800 °C, where it was held for 8 h before
being cooled to room temperature in the furnace. This ageing
treatment promoted the preferential precipitation of the γ 0 phase
and the formation of ﬁne secondary phase particles.
2.2. Experimental Procedure
Microscopic metallographic characterization was conducted on
samples post-heat treatment. The samples were sectioned using
an electrical discharge machining wire cutter, from which
2 mm-thick discs were extracted at 2 cm above the base of each
sample. Subsequently, the microstructure was examined following
a meticulous metallographic preparation process, which included
grinding and etching. The preparation for microscopic analysis
involved sequential polishing of each sample using sandpapers
of varying grits: 200#, 400#, 800#, 1000#, 1500#, 2000#, and
3000#, culminating with a ﬁnal polish using diamond polishing
paste with a particle size of 1 μm. For metallographic etching, a
solution composed of 15 g CuSO 4, 3.5 mL H 2SO4,a n d5 0 m L
H2O was applied for 120 s to reveal the microstructure. Carbide
distribution was assessed using another etching solution of 1 g
KMnO4,1 0 m LH 2SO4, and 90 mL H 2O for 30 s. Observations
of the microstructure were performed using a Primotech metal-
lographic microscope from Zeiss Optical Instruments.
Further analysis to explore the impact of nano-NbC particles
on the GH4099 superalloy ’s microstructure, speci ﬁcally con-
cerning grain structure and dendritic MC carbides, was con-
ducted. The polished samples underwent electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD) analysis using a high-resolution ﬁeld emis-
sion scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT800, Japan
Electronics). Prior to EBSD, the sample surfaces were re ﬁned
by argon ion polishing for 30 min. The EBSD images were then
processed with ImageJ software to assess grain size and radial
grain distribution. Grain size data were gathered from 200 ran-
dom measurements across 200 grains, and statistical averages
were computed. Comparative analysis was performed to under-
stand the microstructural and grain structural variations in
samples with different NbC additions.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Effect of Nano-NbC Addition on Microstructure
Figure 3 presents macroscopic metallographic images, EBSD
patterns, and particle size distributions for the central region
of the bottom slice of the sample, both before and after heat treat-
ment. As illustrated in Figure 3a, the as-cast GH4099 sample
exhibits a microstructure composed of coarse dendrites with a
columnar crystal orientation. The average radial dendrite size,
calculated using ImageJ software, was measured at 469.65 μm.
This structure is typical of directional solidi ﬁcation under high
cooling rates, where γ 0 and MC phases are formed along the
grain boundaries. [35] Upon the addition of a small quantity of
nano-NbC, the microstructure, as shown in Figure 3b, trans-
forms to one featuring irregular spherical crystals, with the
coarse columnar structure being substituted by shorter den-
drites. The average diameter of these spherical crystals was deter-
mined to be 386.25 μm. As the content of nano-NbC increases,
the microstructure further evolves into smaller granular crystals,
as shown in Figure 3c,d, with average diameters decreasing to
332.08 and 220.72 μm, respectively.
A clear trend emerges: with increasing nano-NbC content in
the GH4099 alloy, the coarse dendritic structure progressively
transforms into shorter dendrites, which ultimately vanish, giv-
ing rise to spherical crystals. The diameter of these spherical crys-
tals diminishes as the nano-NbC content rises, eventually leading
to the formation of granular crystals. This transformation is
attributed to a decrease in the degree of supercooling and an
increase in the critical instability radius (R), making dendrite for-
mation more challenging. The presence of nano-NbC particles
and MC carbides around the spherical crystals aids in facilitating
easier grain boundary movement while simultaneously inhibit-
ing further crystal growth. [36]
Nano-NbC particles act as heterogeneous nucleation sites dur-
ing solidi ﬁcation and subsequent heat treatment. By reducing
the nucleation energy barrier and increasing surface activity at
nucleation sites, these particles signi ﬁcantly enhance the nucle-
ation rate of equiaxed grains, suppress the growth of columnar
grains, and ultimately result in grain reﬁnement and an equiaxed
grain transformation. This revised description better re ﬂects the
microstructural features observed in our SEM and EBSD analy-
ses, without relying on thermodynamic or kinetic constants that
lie outside the measurable scope of our study. Hunt (1984) pro-
posed a widely accepted criterion for columnar-to-equiaxed tran-
sition (CET), and Kurz & Fisher (1998) provided a systematic
overview of solidi ﬁcation and nucleation theory. [37,38]
According to the CET model proposed by Hunt, the formation
of equiaxed grains depends on the combined effects of liquid
undercooling, temperature gradient, and nucleation site density.

and substantially increase the available surface area for

3.2. Effect of Nano-NbC on MC Carbides
This study analyzed representative regions from the GH4099
matrix alloy and samples modi ﬁed with 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt%
NbC. For each group, 50 optical micrographs with a 20 μm scale
bar were randomly collected across different areas of the speci-
men. Using ImageJ software, MC-type carbides were identi ﬁed
and quanti ﬁed. The number and average size of the carbides
were statistically analyzed and presented as bar charts.
The quantitative results revealed signi ﬁcant changes in car-
bide distribution and morphology with varying NbC content.
In the GH4099 base alloy, the average number of carbides
was 7.4 per sample, with an average area of 17.543 /C2103 px2.
With 0.5 wt% NbC, the carbide number increased to 9.3, and
the average area decreased to 12.214 /C2103 px2. At 1.0 wt%
NbC, the number rose to 14.4 per sample, and the average area
further decreased to 6.421 /C2103 px2, indicating re ﬁned and
well-dispersed carbides. However, at 2.0 wt% NbC, the number
of carbides decreased, and the average area increased to
8.655 /C2103 px2, suggesting carbide agglomeration.
Figure 4 illustrates the impact of NbC content on MC-type car-
bide morphology. In the base GH4099 sample (Figure 4a), MC
carbides exhibit long, chain-like, and skeletal structures, with
lengths between 20 and 50 μm and widths of 2 – 5 μm. These
sparsely distributed carbides are located at grain boundaries
and dendritic arms. This morphology results from the high pour-
ing temperature, which limits carbide precipitation and pro-
motes the growth of large, elongated MC carbides.
With 0.5 wt% NbC (Figure 4b), the carbides transform into
block-like islands with a near 1:1 aspect ratio, evenly distributed
within grains and along grain boundaries. The average particle
size is around 10 μm, demonstrating the re ﬁning effect of NbC.
At 1.0 wt% NbC (Figure 4c), the MC carbides are further
reﬁned into equiaxed particles ranging from 2 – 5 μm. These ﬁner
carbides are uniformly dispersed within the grains and grain
boundaries, indicating improved distribution and re ﬁnement
with higher NbC content.
For the 2.0 wt% NbC sample (Figure 4d), the number of MC-
type carbides increases, accompanied by some agglomeration
within grains and at grain boundaries. This suggests that excessive
NbC results in carbide clustering, as the large quantity of NbC

The room-temperature tensile fracture morphology of the
GH4099 matrix alloy composites with varying nano-NbC addi-

GH4099 specimen, shown in Figure 10a, exhibits numerous

matrix, some of which have fractured. The distribution of dim-

dimples. As the load increases, dislocations accumulate around
the MC-type carbides, leading to interactions between them.
This buildup eventually surpasses a critical point, causing
the fracture of the MC carbides, which, in turn, helps the matrix
support higher loads, thus improving both strength and
toughness.

4. Conclusions and Outlook
Nano-NbC/GH4099 composites exhibit dramatic gains in tensile
strength and yield strength, rising from 806.9 – 922.7 MPa (UTS)
and from 490.3 – 585.8 MPa (YS) at room temperature, and from
595.3 to 620.0 MPa (UTS) and from 487.3 to 528.9 MPa (YS) at
800 °C. These enhancements —14.3% and 19.5% at 25 °C, and
4.2% and 8.4% at 800 °C —underscore the potent strengthening
effect of 1.0 wt% nano-NbC.
Microstructural analysis attributes these gains to synergistic
mechanisms: grain re ﬁnement through heterogeneous nucle-
ation, re ﬁned MC-type carbide precipitation, and Orowan dislo-
cation blocking. Nano-NbC particles disrupt dendritic growth
and suppress columnar structures, stabilizing the microstructure
across a wide temperature range. Beyond 1.0 wt% NbC, however,
carbide clustering emerges, indicating an optimal loading
threshold.
The dual bene ﬁt of elevated strength at ambient and elevated
temperatures positions the 1.0 wt% NbC composite as a compel-
ling candidate for high-temperature structural components —
aircraft turbine discs, gas-turbine blades, and similar demanding
applications. To balance further gains in strength with ductility,
four optimization avenues are under exploration. NbC loading:
Fine tune content within 0.5 –1.0 wt% to avoid carbide agglom-
eration. Particle dispersion: Employ ultrasonic processing or
silane-based surface treatments to achieve uniform nanoparticle
distribution. Heat treatment: Combine 1120 °C solution treatment
with short ( ≤4 h) ageing at 850 °C to control MC-carbide
precipitation and grain growth. Multiscale toughening: Introduce
trace nano-oxides or micrometer-scaleﬁbrous phases to establish a
hierarchical toughening network.
Looking ahead, key barriers to industrial adoption must be
addressed. Cost reduction will hinge on streamlining high-purity
NbC synthesis, currently reliant on energy-intensive arc-plasma
methods. Scalability demands manufacturing processes that
ensure consistent particle size and dispersion at scale. Long-term
stability under thermal fatigue and oxidation also requires inves-
tigation; combining NbC with complementary high-temperature
phases or advanced surface coatings may further bolster
performance.
By integrating these optimization and scale-up strategies,
nano-NbC reinforcement promises a versatile platform for
next-generation nickel-based superalloys, marrying strength,
ductility, and high-temperature resilience.
