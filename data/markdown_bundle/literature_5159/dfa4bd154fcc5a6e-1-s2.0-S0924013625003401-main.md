ABSTRACT
With the continuous advancement of the aviation industry, the demand for high-temperature alloys in aircraft 
engines has been increasing significantly. However, in the fusion welding of precipitation-strengthened nickel- 
based alloys with high Al and Ti content, crack formation and the precipitation of brittle phases remain major 
challenges that severely affect welding quality. This study investigates the fundamental principles governing the 
weldability and mechanical behavior of such alloys, focusing on the GH4251 alloy welded by electron beam 
technology. By optimizing the welding parameters, synergistic control of MC carbides and solidification cracks 
was successfully achieved, resulting in a room-temperature tensile strength of 1471 MPa — equivalent to 92 % of 
the base metal. Experimental and simulation results reveal that solidification cracks and MC carbides within the 
weld seam act as stress concentration sites under external loading, thereby accelerating macroscopic fracture of 
the joint. The occurrence of solidification cracks is further promoted by instantaneous tensile stresses and high- 
angle grain boundaries during welding. Moreover, a high density of dislocations was observed at the semi- 
coherent interface between MC carbides and the γ matrix, accompanied by pronounced tensile strain within 
the carbides themselves. Overall, this research provides new insights into the relationships among welding pa -
rameters, microstructural evolution, and room-temperature tensile strength in high Al and Ti precipitation- 
strengthened nickel-based superalloy joints.
1. Introduction
High-temperature superalloys, particularly precipitation- 
strengthened nickel-based superalloys, are widely recognized as the 
preferred materials for aero-engine applications owing to their superior 
static load resistance, fatigue strength, and creep resistance. To enhance 
thrust-to-weight ratio and fuel efficiency, elevating the turbine inlet 
temperature has emerged as a critical strategy for improving aero- 
engine power performance [1,2] . However, this advancement places 
more stringent demands on the temperature-bearing capacity and me -
chanical properties of materials used in hot-section components [3,4] . 
GH4251, a newly developed γ ′ -precipitation-strengthened nickel-based 
superalloy, exhibits excellent thermal stability and mechanical perfor -
mance at service temperatures up to 800
◦
C, making it a promising 
candidate for turbine discs and other key structural components in 
aero-engines [5,6] .
Given the extremely high manufacturing cost of new aero-engine 
components, fusion welding has emerged as a promising industrial 
repair technology. Among various approaches, electron beam welding 
(EBW), a typical high-energy-density melting process, is particularly 
suitable for joining high-temperature alloys due to its advantages of high 
depth-to-width ratio, high efficiency, and minimal contamination [7,8] . 
However, when applied to high Al and Ti precipitation-strengthened 
nickel-based superalloys, the high energy input of EBW induces rapid 
heating and cooling cycles as well as steep temperature gradients. These 
conditions generate significant tensile stresses during solidification [9, 
10] , while the increased temperature gradients facilitate liquid film 
formation along dendrite boundaries [11,12] . Together with the rapid 
thermal cycling, these factors significantly increase the susceptibility to 
solidification cracking [13,14] . To mitigate solidification cracking, re -
searchers have primarily focused on regulating the welding heat input to 
reduce crack susceptibility. Studies have shown that increasing the heat

critical role of welding-induced stress in crack formation; however, the 
relationship between microstructural evolution and crack initiation in

reduction in both room-temperature and high-temperature elongation

in influencing crack formation and in dictating joint mechanical per -

This study systematically investigates the factors influencing solidi -
fication cracking in GH4251 EBW joints, with particular emphasis on 
clarifying the role of high-angle grain boundaries in crack formation. 
Furthermore, a comparative analysis of the MC carbide/ γ matrix inter -
face and the MC carbide/borides interface is carried out, thereby 
elucidating the mechanism by which MC carbides affect the room- 
temperature tensile strength of the joint. Based on these insights, a 
synergistic regulation strategy for both MC carbides and solidification 
cracks is proposed. This approach enables a significant enhancement in 
the room-temperature tensile strength of the joint — achieved without 
any post-weld treatment — while also establishing optimized process 
parameters for coordinated control. The findings of this work provide a 
robust experimental foundation for advancing the processing and 
expanding the application potential of high Al and Ti precipitation- 
strengthened nickel-based superalloys.
2. Experiments
2.1. Welding test
The material used in this experiment is GH4251(BM) alloy, which 
underwent solid solution treatment and aging. Combined with the 
practical application, the electric discharge machining (EDM) wire 
cutting machine and grinding machine were used to process the material 
into 33 mm × 15 mm × 6.5 mm test plate. A backing plate (33 mm ×
15 mm × 3 mm) was affixed to the root side of the test coupon to ensure 
complete joint penetration under all three parameter sets and to stabilize 
bead morphology on the weld crown. The weldment surface is sanded 
with sandpaper to remove oil, oxide films, and cutting marks left from 
the wire-cutting process. Before welding, clean the sheet to be welded 
with an ultrasonic cleaning machine to remove impurities such as oil 
and dirt as much as possible. Finally, the belt weldment is fixed with a 
fixture. The heat input was changed by different welding speeds (Vs), 
which were respectively 360 mm/min, 600 mm/min, 840 mm/min.

3. Results
3.1. Joint surface and cross section morphology
The macroscopic surface morphology of the GH4251 EBW joint is 
shown in Fig. 2 . The results indicate that the joint is free of spatter, 
macrocracks, and other defects. At a welding speed of 360 mm/min, the 
upper surface of the joint becomes noticeably concave, and the back 
height increases ( Fig. 2 a1,a2). This occurs because a reduction in 
welding speed increases welding heat input and prolongs the high- 
temperature residence time in each area of the joint. This results in 
surface evaporation and the flow and contraction of the liquid phase due 
to surface tension [24] . As the welding speed increases, the residual 
height of the welded joint surface becomes continuous and uniform 
( Fig. 2 b1,b2). At a welding speed of 840 mm/min ( Fig. 2 c1,c2), a 
distinct fish-scale pattern appeared on the upper surface of the joint, 
while the weld seam exhibited incomplete penetration into the backing 
plate.
The cross-sectional morphology and microstructure of the joint were 
analyzed and are shown in Fig. 3 . As shown in Fig. 3 a, the joint exhibits a 
typical "nail-shaped" morphology. No macroscopic defects, such as 
cracks or pores, were observed within the joint. The joint comprises 
three distinct regions: the WZ, the HAZ, and the BM. Magnified views of 
regions marked b, c, and d in Fig. 3 a are presented. From Fig. 3 b,c, it can 
be seen that dendrites nucleated at partially melted grains within the 
HAZ and grew along the direction of the steepest thermal gradient, 
perpendicular towards the weld center. As the dendrites extended to -
wards the weld center, the diminishing thermal gradient promoted the 
formation of equiaxed crystal ( Fig. 3 d,e).
3.2. Microstructure characterization
3.2.1. Microstructure characterization of the WZ
3.2.1.1. SEM analysis of the WZ. The microstructure of the WZ is pre -
sented in Fig. 4 . The figure indicates that the microstructure remains 
consistent across the three different welding speeds. As depicted in 
Fig. 4 a1,b1,c1, the WZ primarily consists of columnar crystals. SEM +
EDS ( Table 3 ) magnification revealed the presence of MC carbides, γ / γ’ 
eutectic structures, Boride, and fine γ’ phase in the WZ ( Fig. 4 a2,b3,c2). 
Notably, obvious solidification cracking of varying micron sizes and the 
liquid film backfilling were observed in the γ / γ’ eutectic structure, as 
shown in Fig. 4 a3,b2,b3,c3. Liu et al. [25] study revealed that eutectic 
structures can inhibit the formation of solidification cracks. Ma et al. 
[26] and Sun et al. [27] . also observed that a higher liquid phase content 
during solidification facilitates crack healing or backfilling, thereby 
reducing crack sensitivity. The cracks, eutectic structures, and liquid 
film backfill lengths were statistically analyzed, and their average values 
were calculated. As shown in the Fig. 5 , increasing the welding speed 
from 360 mm/min to 840 mm/min led to an increase in average crack 
size from 1.28 μ m to 5 μ m. The γ / γ’ eutectic content decreased from 
6.19 % to 3.51 %. The average liquid film backfill length decreased from 
3.29 μ m to 0.61 μ m. DSC ( Fig. 6 ) analysis of BM indicates that, during 
the final stage of solidification, the liquid film partially transforms into a 
γ / γ’ eutectic structure at approximately 1265
◦
C. This suggests that the 
formation of solidification cracking is associated with the liquid film 
backfilling.

higher welding heat input ( Table 1 ) [24,28] , which promotes backfilling 
at areas where tearing has occurred. Additionally, compared to the other

4.3. Fracture mechanism of GH4251 EBW joint
Analysis of solidification cracking mechanisms reveals that tensile 
stresses and HAGBs are primary contributors to crack propagation. 
Furthermore, interfacial analysis demonstrates that semi-coherent in -
terfaces between MC carbides and the γ -matrix exhibit limited defor -
mation accommodation capacity, causing significant dislocation pile-up 
at phase boundaries. This generates high interfacial energy and induces 
thermodynamic instability. Under applied loads, these interfaces 
become preferential crack initiation sites, accelerating macroscopic 
fracture and ultimately degrading joint tensile strength.
To better illustrate the fracture behavior of the welded joint under 
external loading, a schematic diagram ( Fig. 21 ) of the joint ’ s tensile 
fracture process is presented. Residual welding stresses generate sub -
stantial dislocation arrays at MC carbide/ γ -matrix interfaces prior to 
external loading ( Fig. 21 a). Concurrently, these stresses exacerbate 
liquid film rupture during terminal solidification in the WZ, initiating 
solidification cracks. Upon external loading, severe stress concentration 
develops at both MC carbide interfaces and pre-existing solidification 
cracks ( Fig. 21 b,c). As loading increases, this concentration intensifies 
progressively, triggering simultaneous crack propagation from both sites 
( Fig. 21 d). This ultimately results in the macroscopic fracture of the 
welded joint.
The factors governing solidification crack formation and the 
fracture-initiating role of MC carbides were systematically examined, 
elucidating the fracture mechanism in GH4251 EBW joints. Based on 
three key process parameters investigated, synergistic suppression of 
solidification cracks and MC carbides enhanced room-temperature ten -
sile strength. At 360 mm/min welding speed, reduced speed increased 
heat input, lowering local temperature gradients ( Fig. 16 ) and transient 
tensile stresses ( Fig. 17 ), thereby suppressing solidification cracking, 
however, it accelerated MC-type carbide nucleation and growth 
( Fig. 11 ). Conversely, higher speeds mitigated element segregation and 
suppressed MC carbide formation, yet significantly increased solidifi -
cation crack density ( Fig. 5 ). At the optimized speed of 600 mm/min,

Based on the above research, the following conclusions can be drawn: 
1) Fractographic analysis of GH4251 EBW joints following room- 
temperature tensile testing reveals that under applied loading, so -
lidification cracks and MC-type carbides in the WZ act as preferential 
stress concentration sites, accelerating macroscopic fracture 
propagation.
2) Reducing the welding speed effectively prevents solidification cracks 
in the WZ of GH4251 EBW joints. Finite element simulations indicate 
that lowering the welding speed to 360 mm/min reduces the average 
instantaneous longitudinal tensile stress during welding by a factor 
of 3.7, due to the decreased temperature gradient (from 503 MPa to 
138 MPa). Simultaneously, the proportion of HAGBs in the weld 
seam decreases (from 92.1 % to 67 %), enhancing grain boundary 
coalescence. These combined effects reduce solidification crack for -
mation and suppress macroscopic fracture of the joint.
3) MC carbides were found to aggravate the macroscopic fracture 
behavior of the joints. Interfacial analysis between the MC carbides 
and the γ matrix revealed the presence of a semi-coherent interface ( δ 
= 12.9 %), where extensive dislocation pile-ups were observed. 
Moreover, pronounced tensile strain was detected within the MC 
carbides themselves, further contributing to fracture susceptibility. 
By increasing the welding speed, both the size and the number 
density of MC carbides in the WZ of GH4251 EBW joints were 
effectively suppressed, thereby mitigating their detrimental 
influence.
4) This study proposes a strategy of tailoring welding process parame -
ters to achieve the synergistic control of MC carbides and solidifi -
cation cracks. At an optimized welding speed of 600 mm/min, the 
GH4251 EBW joint exhibited a remarkable enhancement in room- 
temperature tensile strength, reaching 1471 MPa — equivalent to 
92 % of the base metal strength (1586 MPa).
This study employed a synergistic method to control solidification 
cracks and MC carbides, significantly enhancing the room-temperature 
tensile strength of EBW GH4251 joints. These findings provide guid -
ance for improving the mechanical performance of welds in high Al and 
Ti precipitation-strengthened nickel-based superalloys. Based on this 
analysis, the post-weld heat treatment of the joint can be explored to 
determine whether it further suppresses solidification cracks and MC 
carbides. Additionally, a dedicated vacuum preheating furnace could be 
designed to uniformly preheat the weldment, thereby reducing tensile 
stress during welding and further controlling solidification cracks.
