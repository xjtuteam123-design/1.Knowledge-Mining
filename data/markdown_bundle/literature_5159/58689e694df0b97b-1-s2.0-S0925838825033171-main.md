1. Introduction
Multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) have garnered significant 
attention from the research community due to their intriguing me -
chanical properties [1 – 3] . Despite the inherent strength-ductility 
trade-off in MPEAs, various strategies have been explored to address 
this challenge [4 – 6] . Among these, introducing the hard phase into a soft 
face-centered cubic (FCC) matrix through composition tailoring and 
processing optimization has proven effective in overcoming the 
strength-ductility trade-off [7 – 9] . For example, Zhao et al. [10] demon -
strated remarkable enhancement in strength through the introduction of 
ternary precipitates ( η , γ’ and L2

PHAD (Calculation of Phase Diagrams) technique with the TECH6 
database. The nominal composition of designed alloy is listed in Table 1 .

3.2. Superior tensile properties at ambient and cryogenic environments
Fig. 7 (a) presents the tensile engineering stress-strain curves (in solid 
line) and true tensile stress-strain curves (in dashed line) of PDAA under 
ambient (298 K) and cryogenic (77 K) conditions, with the corre -
sponding mechanical properties summarized in Table 2 . At 298 K, PDAA 
demonstrates superior strength-ductility synergy, with a yield strength 
of 1124 ± 19 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 1494 ± 24 MPa, and a 
fracture elongation of 29.3 ± 1.2 %. At 77 K, PDAA achieves excep -
tional synergy between strength and ductility, showing a significant 
improvement: a yield strength of 1263 ± 11 MPa, an ultimate tensile 
strength of 1893 ± 17 MPa, and a fracture elongation of 43.1 ± 2.1 %. 
The enhancement in ultimate tensile strength reaches around 400 MPa, 
while fracture elongation improves by nearly 50 %. This combined 
enhancement endowed the alloy with the ability to break through the 
classic strength-ductility trade-off dilemma.
Moreover, the strain-hardening behavior of PDAA under both 
ambient and cryogenic conditions displays distinct recovery and up-turn 
characteristics, as shown in Fig. 7 (b). The strain-hardening rate (SHR) 
curves can be categorized into four distinct stages. In Stage I, the SHR 
decreases sharply, marking the elasto-plastic transition stage. In Stage II, 
the SHR exhibits an upward trend, increasing from 2.9 GPa to 4.7 GPa at 
298 K. This phenomenon is commonly observed in heterogeneous- 
structured alloys [28] , some precipitate-strengthened alloys [4] , and 
twinning/transformation-induced plasticity (TWIP or TRIP) alloys [29, 
30] , all of which contribute to enhanced strain hardening and improved 
ductility. After reaching its peak, the SHR declines moderately in Stage 
III due to the saturation of dislocation accumulation. Additionally, 
enhanced dynamic recovery and re-crystallization, stimulated by suffi -
cient strain energy, contribute to this decrease [31] . Finally, in Stage IV, 
the SHR drops sharply, marking the onset of necking as the true 
stress-strain curve intersected with the SHR curve.
Furthermore, the SHR displays a notable temperature dependence. 
At the end of Stage I, the SHR of PDAA reaches a minimal of 3.6 GPa at 
77 K, significantly higher than that of the 2.9 GPa observed at 298 K. 
Although the peak SHR values are similar at both temperatures, the SHR 
at 77 K exhibits a notable increase during Stage III, contributing to the 
observed ductility enhancement. The difference is ascribed to variations 
in deformation mechanisms, which will be examined in detail in Section 
4.1 .

The comparison of ultimate tensile strength and elongation 
underscores the alloy ’ s potential to overcome the longstanding strength-

presents a promising approach for addressing this limitation, offering an 
effective strategy for achieving superior mechanical performance.
4. Discussion
4.1. Precipitate dominated deformation mechanisms
Dislocations, stack faults (SFs), Lomer-Cottrell locks (L-C locks) and 
deformation twins (TWs) are commonly observed in alloys exhibiting 
excellent strength and ductility. The interactions among these lattice 
defects are critical for enhancing mechanical performance. Thus, 
revealing the deformation mechanisms of PDAA under ambient and 
cryogenic conditions is essential for elucidating the improved mechan -
ical properties of PDAA compared to the other alloys at 298 K and 77 K.
The deformation microstructure of PDAA at 298 K, with true strains 
of ~ 8.4 % and ~ 22.3 %, is depicted in Fig. 9 . At a lower strain (~ 
8.4 %), a high density of dislocations are observed ( Fig. 9 (a)), indicative 
of planar slip. As shown in Fig. 9 (b), the larger P1 precipitates act as 
obstacles to dislocation motion, effectively pinning dislocations and 
contributing to strengthening. In contrast, the smaller, more densely 
distributed P2 precipitates are sheared by dislocations, as shown in 
Fig. 9 (d). Dislocations are also observed accumulating at grain bound -
aries and annealing twin boundaries ( Fig. 9 (c)), where sufficient strain 
energy enables Frank-Read sources for dislocation multiplication. At a 
higher strain (~ 22.3 %, near fracture), the microstructure exhibits a 
significantly increased dislocation density and the formation of dislo -
cation cells, suggesting the activation of dynamic recovery during 
deformation. Unlike other L1
2
-strengthened alloys [12,35] , only minor 
SFs are observed in the matrix, indicating a relatively higher stacking 
fault energy (SFE).

(1) Bimodal coherent L1 ₂ precipitates were successfully introduced 
through post-deformation annealing and aging treatments. The 
smaller P2 precipitates, with an average diameter of 37.8 nm and 
a volume fraction of 47.0 vol%, were uniformly dispersed within 
the matrix. In contrast, the larger P1 precipitates, with an average 
diameter of 416.5 nm and a volume fraction of 3.3 vol%, 
exhibited an uneven distribution, resulting in delayed recrystal -

and ductility, surpassing most L1

298 K, the yield strength, ultimate tensile strength and total 
elongation of PDAA were 1124 ± 19 MPa, 1494 ± 24 MPa and 
29.3 ± 1.2 %, respectively. And the mechanical performance of 
PDAA significantly improved at 77 K, with the yield strength of 
1263 ± 11 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 1893 ± 17 MPa and 
total elongation of 43.1 ± 2.1 %. The remarkable increase in ul -
timate tensile strength and total elongation is attributed to the 
improvement in strain hardening rate.
