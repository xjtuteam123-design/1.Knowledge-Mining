ORIGINAL PAPER
Effect of precipitates on high-temperature strength and irradiation
behavior of vanadium-based alloys
Shao-ning Jiang 1 • Li-qun Xu 1 • Fa-rong Wan 2
Received: 25 January 2018 / Revised: 26 September 2018 / Accepted: 30 September 2018 / Published online: 2 November 2018
/C211 China Iron and Steel Research Institute Group 2018
Abstract
The formation of precipitates and their effect on mechanical properties and irradiation damage in V–4Cr–4Ti alloys have
been investigated using transmission electron microscope, high-temperature tensile tests and high-voltage electron
microscope (HVEM). V–4Cr–4Ti alloys were aged for 20–40 h at 873 K, and extremely ﬁne precipitates were produced
during the aging process, which coarsened with aging time. The results of high-temperature (773, 873 and 973 K) tensile
tests showed an increase in strength with increasing aging time (i.e., increasing size of precipitates) while a decrease in the
uniform elongation. There was evidence that presence of precipitates strengthened V–4Cr–4Ti alloys. In situ HVEM
observations showed that the precipitates restricted the growth rate of dislocation loops induced during electron irradiation
at 773 K. The interactions between precipitates and irradiation-induced dislocation loops were discussed. The presence of
particles alleviated the increase in irradiation hardening up to an irradiation dose of 3.42 dpa (displacement per atom). The
precipitates shrank in size during electron irradiation, which was due to the dissolution of constituent atoms of the
precipitates into the alloy matrix.
Keywords Aging /C1 Precipitate strengthening /C1 Electron irradiation /C1 Irradiation defect /C1 Dislocation loop
1 Introduction
Vanadium alloys, especially V–4Cr–4Ti alloys, are con-
sidered as promising structural materials for use in nuclear
fusion reactors, due to their low activation and good cor-
rosion resistance with liquid lithium [ 1, 2]. The operating
temperature range for vanadium alloys is usually
673–973 K. Thus, further methods for achieving high-
temperature strengthening should be considered. One such
method is to add alloying elements such as Cr and W [ 3].
However, the higher Cr content increases the ductile–brittle
transition temperature, while the higher W content shows
no improvement in the high-temperature tensile strength.
An alternative method is dispersion strengthening. It is
known that Y has a larger oxygen afﬁnity than Ti. Some
researchers have examined V–W and V–W–Mo systems
with added Y [ 4, 5] and found that nanoparticle dispersion
was efﬁcient at strengthening vanadium alloys via
mechanical alloying. Nevertheless, Watanabe et al. [ 6]
found that the enrichment of Ti(CON) precipitates with Y
atoms was weak following irradiation. Analysis of these
precipitates by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) showed enrichment by N and Ti atoms.
Aging may cause some elements to segregate or desol-
ventizing impurities to form precipitates. The distribution
of precipitates in the matrix could lead to hardening and
strengthening. It has been reported that the impurity levels
(including carbon, nitrogen and oxygen) in V–4Cr–4Ti
alloy strongly inﬂuence its precipitation behavior and
mechanical properties [ 7, 8]. Despite intensive efforts to
optimize the precipitate characteristics to gain improved
properties at low temperatures, limited information is
available about the behavior of these precipitates at inter-
mediate temperatures, i.e., within the accepted temperature
window (673–973 K) for use of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy in fusion
reactor applications. In addition, there may be plate-like,

Moreover, a fusion reactor would release neutrons with

Finally, the mechanical properties of the alloy would

mechanical properties of ODS RAF (reduced activation
ferritic) steels submitted to low-energy ion irradiation
found an increase in hardness and strength and a decrease
in the Young’s modulus that may be associated with
defects, dislocation loops and types of implanted ions [ 14].
During irradiation, the presence of precipitates may affect
irradiation damage and mechanical properties [ 15]. Wang
et al. [ 16] studied the interaction between preformed TiN
precipitates and the matrix in V–4Cr–4Ti alloy under
hydrogen and helium irradiation and found that the size of
precipitates affected irradiation hardening. The manner in
which preformed precipitates interact with irradiation
damage under electron irradiation and how potential
changes would affect the radiation resistance of this
material need further investigation.
In this paper, experimental results for the microstruc-
tural evolution and subsequent effects on the mechanical
properties of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy were reported. High-tem-
perature strength was analyzed, considering the effects of
precipitates caused by aging. In addition, the interaction
between preformed precipitates and irradiation damage in
V–4Cr–4Ti alloy was analyzed by in situ observations
using high-voltage electron microscope (HVEM) during
electron irradiation, in the hope of further exploring
advanced materials for improved long-term performance in
fusion reactor systems.
2 Experimental procedure
A V–4Cr–4Ti ingot was prepared using arc melting in an
argon atmosphere and was cut into small pieces by means
of wire-electrode cutting. Table 1 shows the design and
composition of the V–4Cr–4Ti ingot.
These pieces were wrapped with Ta plates and external
Zr foil as gaseous impurity getter and encapsulated with a
quartz tube under high vacuum. They were then solution-
annealed at 1373 K for 2 h, followed by aging at 873 K for
20 and 40 h, respectively. The resultant microstructures
were characterized using a JEOL2010 transmission elec-
tron microscope (TEM). The thickness of the observed area
was approximately 240 nm. Specimens for microstructure
analysis were disks of 3 mm in diameter and 100 lmi n
thickness that were prepared by mechanical pre-thinning,
followed by electro-polishing using an electrolyte of
methanol/sulfuric acid (6:1) at 243 K. Vickers hardness
test was carried out with a load of 9.8 N at RT, and tensile
tests were made at RT, 773 K, 873 K and 973 K. The
strain rate was 5 9 10
-3 mm/s. Tensile specimens had
gauge dimensions of 16.00 mm 9 4.00 mm 9 0.25 mm.
Electron irradiation was carried out using a JEOL-1300
HVEM (Hokkaido University) at 773 K with an acceler-
ating voltage of 1250 kV. The sample was V–4Cr–4Ti
alloy aged for 20 h at 873 K. The irradiation dose was up
to 3.42 dpa (displacement per atom). The electron beam
diameter was focused on 1–2 lm, where the local intensity
was measured by a small Faraday cup and then conﬁrmed
as a Gaussian distribution. The beam current was about
0.9 nA giving a damage rate of about 1.9 9 10
-3 dpa/s.
The irradiation time was 30 min, and the irradiation tem-
perature was kept at 773 K in a double-tilt heating speci-
men holder that was calibrated to a precision of ± 3/C176 . The
evolution of microstructure was studied in situ during the
process of electron irradiation.

ment, and their compositions, sizes, structures and distri-

materials [ 17]. To further understand the differences in
strength following the aging process, a detailed
microstructural evolution analysis was carried out. Fig-
ure 1 shows the microstructures of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy fol-
lowing aging at 873 K for 0, 20, and 40 h. Without aging,
few precipitates were observed in the TEM images due to
the fact that annealing at 1373 K for 2 h dissolved most of
the precipitates which might have formed in the previous
process. With aging for 20 h, a relatively high density of

The precipitates formed in V–4Cr–4Ti alloys are trig-

3.2 Hardening and high-temperature
strengthening
The microstructural instability of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy used in
fusion reactor applications that arose during long-term
exposure to intermediate temperatures resulted in insta-
bility of its mechanical properties. Figure 4 shows its
hardness as a function of the mean size of precipitates in
the alloy, which, as can be seen, was signiﬁcant. Precipi-
tation strengthening is mainly controlled by the properties
of the precipitates, speciﬁcally their sizes and number
densities [ 21]. With an increase in the mean size, the
hardness increased, which can therefore be attributed to the
additional formation of Ti-rich precipitates. Other studies
have also indicated that the hardening of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy
at 973 K results from similar kinds of ﬁne precipitates
[22, 23].
These second phases led to precipitation hardening.
Interestingly, the alloying element Cr appears to enhance
hardening in two ways [ 9]: (1) During precipitate forma-
tion, the strong interaction between Cr and Ti may increase
the number of precipitate nucleation sites. (2) The presence
of Cr in V–4Cr–4Ti alloy retards the recovery of pre-ex-
isting dislocations, enhances oxygen diffusivity and con-
sequently increases the oxygen content in the precipitates.
The tensile behavior of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy was also
studied. As can be seen from Fig. 5, aging treatment caused
increases in yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) and a decrease in uniform elongation (UE).
With longer aging time, the strength increased, while the
elongation decreased. V–4Cr–4Ti alloy aged for 40 h had
the highest YS and UTS, while its UE was very low, only
1.5–2%. V–4Cr–4Ti alloy aged for 20 h had medium val-
ues of YS and UTS, while its UE was in the range of
11.4–12.5%, which did not decrease signiﬁcantly. Thus,
the decrease in the UE of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy aged for 20 h
was only about 1.1% compared with that of unaged V–
4Cr–4Ti alloy. In a word, strength of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy
increased with aging.
The change in strength of the V–4Cr–4Ti alloys was
attributed to the precipitating effect. The appearance of
Ti(CON)-type precipitates as a result of aging at 873 K in
the solid-solution-annealed alloy strengthened V–4Cr–4Ti
signiﬁcantly and reduced its plasticity. For samples aged
for 20 h, the reduction in UE is acceptable and the
increases in YS and UTS are obvious. Thus, aging for 20 h
at 873 K is feasible for strengthening V–4Cr–4Ti alloys.

and a number density of 4.6 9 10

Metal could be strengthened by means of aging. The
effect of strengthening depends mainly on interactions
between dislocations and second-phase particles. The pre-
cipitates formed during aging impede the motion of dis-
locations and thus improve the strength of the alloy. In the
early stages of precipitation, dislocation slipping incises the
precipitate and strengthens the matrix. With continued
aging, the size of the precipitates increases. The dislocation
lines can bend around a precipitate particle by means of
what is known as the Orowan mechanism [ 25]. Disloca-
tions round the dispersion particles and the circles formed
around them. According to dislocation theory, the shear

3.3 Irradiation damage
During service, structural materials are subjected to both
thermal and irradiation effects that cause the evolution of
microstructures through defects and precipitates. To
research the interactions between defects and pre-existing
precipitates, electron irradiation and in situ observations
using JEOL-1300 HVEM were carried out at 773 K on V–
4Cr–4Ti alloy that had undergone aging at 873 K for 20 h.
The irradiation dose was between 0.57 and 3.42 dpa, and
the irradiation time was 30 min. The thermal aging effect
on the growth of precipitates at 773 K for such a short
period of time was expected to be negligible. Figure 6
shows the evolution of microstructures under electron
irradiation. The dislocation loops grew with increasing
irradiation dose. In contrast, the precipitates diminished
and ultimately disappeared. In addition, no irradiation-in-
duced precipitates were observed in as-irradiated V–4Cr–
4Ti alloys [ 27].

The growth of dislocation loops did not lead to a change
in number density. Therefore, the hardening induced by
dislocation loops can be discussed in terms of the mean
size. The larger the size of dislocation loops, the more
severe is the hardening [ 13]. By means of the above
analysis, the degree of irradiation hardening decreased with
increasing quantities of Ti(CON) particles. In this study,
the results show that the existence of ﬁne particles such as
Ti(CON) may alleviate the increase in irradiation harden-
ing to a certain extent by delaying the growth rate of dis-
location loops under electron irradiation up to an
irradiation dose of 3.42 dpa.
In addition, the precipitates shrank in size with
increasing irradiation dose, that is, the constituent atoms
dissociated. This may have been due to the diffusion of
vacancies and atomic displacement of the constituent
atoms of precipitate under electron irradiation. Both of
them aided the dissolution of the constituent atoms of the
precipitate into the alloy matrix [ 31]. Tan et al. [ 32] dis-
cussed this phenomenon in terms of ion irradiation and
speculated that if ion irradiation direction deviated from the
particle length direction, some particles were sectioned
lengthwise and then dissolved, resulting in decreased par-
ticle densities.
The evolution of nanoprecipitates primarily depends on
the particles being orientated in the direction of the ion
beam, because the forward momentum of the ions plays an
important role in particle evolution. In present study, the
ion beam was approximately perpendicular to the particle
length, leading to dissolution of the particles, and conse-
quently decreased particle number density and reduced
particle aspect ratio following high level of irradiation
dose. Zhao et al. [ 33] studied the effect of carbide pre-
cipitates on the evolution of dislocation structures in
CLAM steel (China low activation martensitic steel) during
irradiation and found that the growth rate of the dislocation
loops induced by irradiation may be restricted by the
presence of a ﬁne MC phase as the precipitate/matrix
interface might act as an effective sink to trap irradiation-
induced point defects. Wan et al. [ 15] thought that the
ordered crystal structure of the pre-precipitates might be
destroyed by ion implantation. The shrinkage in the size of
precipitates during irradiation would result from the fact
that precipitates were partially dissolved into the matrix
and decreased size. The instability of preformed precipi-
tates and the subsequent changes in their mechanical
properties under irradiation need further study.
4 Conclusions
1. Aging at 873 K led to the formation of Ti-rich pre-
cipitates. The size of these precipitates increased,
while their number density decreased with aging time.
2. The hardness at room temperature and high-tempera-
ture (773, 873 and 973 K) strength of the alloy
increased due to the presence of precipitates.
3. Considering the precipitate effect, this study found that
the mechanical properties of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy were
improved. The optimum heat treatment conditions
were aging at 873 K for 20 h, followed by annealing at
1373 K for 2 h.
4. In situ observations of V–4Cr–4Ti alloy aged at 873 K
for 20 h indicated that the presence of precipitates
restricted the growth rate of dislocation loops and
alleviated the increase in irradiation hardening up to an
irradiation dose of 3.42 dpa. Additionally, the pre-
formed particles shrank in size and ultimately
disappeared.
