Abstract
A new heat-resistant Fe-based cast alloy has been designed making the best use of the CALPHAD method. The objective was to replace the
material for precision cast products made from Ni-based superalloys with improved Fe-based alloys originally developed for lower temperature
applications.
The key to the design is to balance the amount of strengthening γ ′-Ni3(Ti, Al) precipitates and brittle σ phase, and to simultaneously decrease
as-cast microsegregation. Contours of the equal phase fraction were calculated over the isothermal diagrams of the Fe–Ni–Cr–Ti–Al–Nb system,
and provided useful guidelines for compositional modiﬁcations. The microsegregation was also evaluated by Scheil-type simulation in order to
reduce micropores caused by oxidation of the solidifying front.
The developed cast Fe-based alloy showed desirable strength at the envisioned service temperatures and ﬁlled the open range of heat resistance
between Ni-based superalloys and the original alloy.

1. Introduction
Heat-resistant parts in automobile engines are mostly made
of high alloy steel castings containing 15–25 mass% Ni and Cr.
The Fe–Ni–Cr solid solution matrix as well as M 6C carbides
provide both elevated temperature strength and oxidation
resistance in an exhaust gas atmosphere.
In the 1980s, increasing automotive engine power outputs
led to the development of the VE28 alloy for exhaust
valves [ 1]. At the time, this component required the highest
heat resistance. A typical composition of the VE28 alloy
was Fe–40Ni–20Cr–3Mo–2.3Ti–1.1Al (mass%). Because of
the oxidizing conditions in which the valves operated, a
higher Cr content was needed and ﬁne γ ′-Ni3(Ti, Al) coherent
precipitates were used to strengthen the matrix. Depending on

On the other hand, Ni-based superalloys are commonly used
for turbine blades, such as in power plants. Among auto parts,
the only application of superalloys was for the turbine wheel
of the charging system, which was an inevitable choice in case
that the existing Fe-based alloys fall below the requirements.
Applications, however, were limited since it costs too much in
terms of both raw material and vacuum casting.
Currently, however, we are seeing an increase in the number
of turbo-charging applications for diesel engines, where the ex-
haust gas temperature is lower than that of gasoline engines.
Thus, there is a strong demand for less expensive alloys for
use in turbine wheels. VE28 exhibits a much higher elevated-
temperature strength than conventional heat-resistant steels [ 2],
but is clearly inferior to Inco713C, a typical superalloy, at tem-
peratures in excess of 873 K. Nevertheless, the material cost
is much lower and new alloy development has been motivated
based on the idea of enhancing the precipitation strengthening
of VE28.
In this article, the design concept of new alloys has been
quantitatively addressed, and an efﬁcient way of optimizing
equilibrium phases in multicomponent alloys is reported.

2. CALPHAD work and experimental investigation
2.1. Requirements and alloy design
In view of the envisioned temperatures of diesel exhaust
gases, the proof strength needs to be comparable with that
of Inco713C up to 1023 K. Thus, one of the alloy design
concepts is strengthening with more γ ′ and simultaneously
avoiding more brittle phases such as σ . Secondly, due to their
complex shapes, turbine wheel production has no alternative
process to precision casting. The control of the as-solidiﬁed
microstructure is therefore of importance to provide good
castability.
To ﬁnd a good balance of each of the phases, multiphase
equilibrium has been repeatedly calculated under the condition
of constant phase fraction. And the constructed contour map of
phases has helped explore the optimal compositions. The soft-
ware package ThermoCalc together with the thermodynamic
database Ni-11 was used for the calculations. The database
has been developed by Saunders [ 3] and released as the older
version of Ni-DA TA from ThermoTech Ltd . Within the soft-
ware scheme, Scheil-type simulations have been implemented
to evaluate segregation caused by solute partitioning between
solid and the liquid phase.
2.2. Experimental work
Alloy ingots of the selected compositions were produced
by vacuum induction melting from the raw materials including
AISI430 stainless steel bars, electrolytic Ni, sponge Ti, Fe–Mo
masteralloy, etc. Castings were made in a preheated metal
die having 6-forked straight long holes of 11 mm diameter.
The cast bars were then solution-treated at 1323 K and
quenched, followed by machining. The machined cylindrical
samples were placed in an argon-atmosphere furnace held
at 1123 K to investigate the precipitation of γ ′ and other
phases. Microstructures and Vickers hardness were evaluated
in a polished cross-section, after etching with the Marble regent
or after electrolytical etching with H 2SO4 + HNO3 + H3PO4
mixed acid. The minor precipitates were also conﬁrmed by
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Peak intensities in a
diffraction pattern usually correspond to the fraction of each
phase. In the case of cast microstructures, however, the
intensities of precipitates can be altered due to large grains
of the matrix. Thus only relative changes in the amount of
precipitates have been seen from the microstructures, and from
the XRD intensities as additional information.
To reveal in advance the problems of castablity in
manufacturing turbine wheel, trial precision castings were
made a few times in the course of alloy development. The
castings were cut and the defects in cross section were
investigated using EPMA area analysis technique.

In order to check the later precipitation of the brittle
phases, the alloys were subjected to an accelerated tempering
at 1123 K. The morphologies of precipitates were revealed by
the electrolytical etching as shown in Fig. 4(d)–(f). Needle-like

designed to have the same mole fraction of σ at 1023 K and
this phase balance is not very different at 1123 K. However, it is
clear from the microstructures that the precipitation of σ phase
is enhanced in the proposed HP1/HP2 alloys than in VE28.
One of the reasons may be the difference in the extent
of segregation which remains even after solution treatment.
It is presumed that the richer in Al or Ti locally, the faster
the σ precipitation. We found that it was not easy to predict
the precipitation kinetics of these alloys. We had to say,
therefore, that alloy design based on CALPHAD equilibrium
calculations cannot fully predict microstructures; however, it
still provides guidelines that are otherwise difﬁcult to obtain
for multicomponent systems.
3.3. Castability and microsegregation
The second requirement, castability of the alloy, is a
prerequisite for precision casting of complex-shaped turbine
wheels. Fig. 6 (a) shows an example of the cross-section of a
trial casting, in which the defects of micropores were frequently
found for VE28 and similarly in the modiﬁed alloys. The VE28
is basically extruded to make valves, but any minor defects
cannot be overlooked in cast parts applications.
It was inferred from the EPMA measurements in Fig. 6 (b)
that the formation of micropores was related to the
microsegregation. The area analysis reveals the enrichment of
Ti and oxygen at the rims of the dendrites surrounding the
pores. The Ti-rich region would likely form the η phase and is
apt to cause surface oxidation. Thus, it is supposed that such fcc
dendrite surfaces lose wettability in contacting each other and
fail in ﬁlling the ﬁnal pores. We have therefore made further
efforts to avoid micropores and to ensure industrial castability
in the atmosphere.
Nb is considered to occupy the same lattice sites as Ti in
γ ′ [4]. The idea is that Nb addition would substitute a part
of Ti without signiﬁcantly changing the hardening effect. The

3.4. Elevated temperature strength
The modiﬁcation of VE28 has thus produced the high
strength HP series and the HC series with high castability. The
improvement in high temperature strength has been conﬁrmed
as shown in Fig. 9 . The proof strength of HP2 is largely
enhanced from VE28, close to 713C in the target temperature
range of up to 1123 K. Meanwhile HC12 shows a slightly lower
strength, and still retains an advantage over the original alloy.
4. Summary
The Fe–Ni–Cr–Mo–Ti–Al–Nb system has been critically
examined to design the new heat-resistant alloy based on VE28.
The CALPHAD calculations efﬁciently spotted the two types
of new alloy compositions, of which the mole fraction and
constitution of γ ′ precipitates are controlled as summarized in
Fig. 10.
We could pay attention to the possible differences between
the equilibrium calculation and the real precipitation behavior
of the σ phase in the modiﬁed HP alloys. The microsegregation
was also revealed by test castings and resulted in further
