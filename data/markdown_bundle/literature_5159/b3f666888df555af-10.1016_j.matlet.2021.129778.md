high-entropy alloy at room and cryogenic temperatures

abstract
The Al 0.6CoCrFeNi2Mo0.08V0.04 high-entropy alloy was prepared by vacuum induction melting. The alloy
presented dendrite and inter-dendrite microstructure and showed excellent strength-ductility synergy
at room and cryogenic temperatures. At room temperature, the yield strength reached 400 MPa while
the elongation exceeded to 39%. With the decrease of temperatures, the yield strength, fracture strength,
and the elongation were all improved. The maximum yield strength reached 560 MPa at /C0196 /C176C and the
elongation exceeded 42%. The fracture mechanism was mainly a dimple-type ductile fracture.

1. Introduction
Recently, high-entropy alloys (HEAs), proposed by Yeh in 2004
[1], have become a research hotpot. HEAs generally include at least
ﬁve principal elements with the concentrations of each element
being between 5 and 35 at. % and usually form stable simple solid
solution structures [1,2]. Among the HEAs, the single-phase face-
centered cubic (FCC) alloys showed an excellent cryogenic tensile
properties [3]. Unfortunately, it is difﬁcult to achieve a good bal-
ance between high strength and high plasticity. For examples,
the yield strength and fracture strength of both CrMnFeCoNi and
CoCrFeNi HEAs were only ~ 220 MPa and ~ 500 MPa, respectively
[4]. Many researchers have tried to solve the shortcomings of the
single-phase FCC HEAs, such as the alloying approach to solid solu-
tion strengthening [5], age precipitation to second-phase strength-
ening [6], thermomechanical treatment to ﬁne grain strengthening
[7], and adding particles to dispersion-strengthening [8]. Besides,
the strength also can be improved by varying the compositions
of HEAs. The Al 0.6CoCrFeNi HEA with FCC and body-centered cubic
(BCC) phases had a high strength [9]. The eutectic HEAs with FCC
and ordered BCC (B2) structures exhibited the elongation, yield
strength, and fracture strength exceeding 10%, 500 MPa, and
1000 MPa, respectively [10]. But the increase in strength was made
at expense of plasticity. Based on the above research status, the
authors tried to design an alloy by controlling the volume percent-
age of strengthen phases and concurrently adding minor alloying
elements to obtain excellent tensile strength-ductility synergy at
room and cryogenic temperatures. Then, the Al 0.6CoCrFeNi2Mo0.08-
V0.04 HEA was designed and prepared. And the microstructure and
tensile properties at different temperatures are investigated.
2. Experimental
A 4Kg Al 0.6CoCrFeNi2Mo0.08V0.04 (marked as Mo 0.08V0.04, the
subscript is atomic ratio) ingot was prepared by vacuum induction
melting. All raw materials was industrial pure (Al, Co, Ni, Mo, V:
99.9 wt%; Cr, Fe: 99.5–99.6 wt%) and cleaned in an ultrasonic
vibrometer containing absolute ethanol. Before melting, the gra-
phite casting mold and the MgO melting crucible was pre-heated
and kept at 600 ℃ for 1 h to remove the water vapor. After the vac-
uum reached to 1 /C210/C02 Pa, melting and casting were performed
in a vacuum of 0.06 MPa by backﬁlled to argon (Ar). The liquid
metal was held at the temperature of 1500 /C176C for 10 min before
poured into graphite casting mold. Finally a ingot with a diameter
of 60 mm and a height of 220 mm was obtained. The specimens cut
from the ingot were mechanically polished and etched by aqua
regia alcohol solution. A scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss
Supra 55) equipped with an X-ray energy dispersive spectrometer
(EDS) was used to observe the microstructure and composition of
Mo0.08V0.04 alloy. The crystal structures were identiﬁed by the high
resolution x-ray diffractometer (XRD, EMPYREAN) with Cu radia-
tion target. The scanning rate was 4 /C176/min and the 2 h scanning
range was set from 20 /C176to 100/C176. In the tensile test at room temper-
ature, the bar-shaped samples with a gauge-length of 20 mm, a
width of 3 mm and a thickness of 2 mm were adopted and tested

3. Results and discussion
The as-cast Mo 0.08V0.04 HEA presented typically dendrite (DR)
and inter-dendrite (ID) morphology, as seen in Fig. 1 (a). Besides,
Fig. 1 (b) showed that the grains was almost fully or partially sur-
rounded by the light phases, which was called grain boundary wet-
ting phenomenon [11]. The elemental mapping analysis results
(Fig. 1 (d) to Fig. 1 (k)) showed that the interdendrite was rich in
Al and Ni elements but poor in Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni elements while
all seven elements seemed to be more evenly distributed in den-
drite. The XRD result shown in Fig. 1 (c) indicated that the HEA
was composed of two solid solution phases with mixed FCC and
B2 structures. And the lattice constants of the FCC and B2 phases
were calculated to be 0.359 and 0.293 Å which similar to CoCrFeNi
alloy and NiAl intermeallic [12], respectively. It can be inferred that
the dendrite was Co,Cr,Fe,Ni-riched FCC phases while the inter-
dendrite was Al,Ni-riched B2 phases. Table 1 showed the chemical
compositions of dendrite and interdendrite, which again proved
the formation of FCC and B2 phases.
The formation rules of FCC and B2 phases can be explained by
the group theory [13]. In Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni HEA system, the high mix-
ing enthalpy atomic group (solid solution group) consisted of Co,
Cr, Fe, and Ni elements while the low mixing enthalpy atomic
group (intermetallic group) was composed of Al and Ni elements
[13]. Table 2 lists the chemical mixing enthalpies of the atomic
pairs [14]. The Al 0.6CoCrFeNi2 alloy matrix in this research can be
decomposed into Ni 0.6Al0.6 intermetallic phases and CoCrFeNi 1.4
solid solution phases. The Mo element was mainly soluble in den-
drites because of its much greater solid solubility in FCC phases
than in B2 phases. The uniform distribution of element V in den-
drite and inter-dendrite was due to its high solid solubility in both
FCC and B2 phases.
The alloy exhibited an excellent strength-ductility synergy at
room and cryogenic temperatures, as seen in Fig. 2 . At room tem-
perature (RT), the yield strength reached 400 MPa while the elon-
gation exceeded to 39%. With the decrease of temperatures, the
yield strength, fracture strength, and the elongation were all
improved. The maximum yield strength reached 560 MPa at
/C0196 /C176C and the elongation exceeded 42%. Normally, as the tem-
perature decreases, the decrease in the thermal energy of the
Peierls-Nabarro barrier will make the movement of dislocations
difﬁcult, resulting in a decrease in ductility. The effects of Peierls-
Nabarro force were more pronounced in BCC phases than that in
FCC phases [10,15]. However, the ductility was even better at
low temperatures in some FCC-based HEAs [3]. In this paper, the
volume fraction of B2 phase was much smaller than that of FCC

It is of interest to note that from the tensile curves there was no
obvious necking phenomenon happened although a great deal of
plastic deformation has occurred. To further study the tension
necking phenomenon, the Nakazima tests were conducted to
map the full-ﬁeld strain distribution. As seen in Fig. 3, in the stages
of elastic deformation the strain was distributed almost uniformly.
When the plastic deformation started, strain localization occurs
within a narrow band. With the plastic deformation proceeded,
more localized strain areas emerged. The deformation of high-
strain areas and the low-strain areas alternatively processes [10].
The maximum localized strain reached 85% when the fracture
occurred. The surface of the fractured sample appeared to be
lumpy like a strong muscle group, which implied its super plastic
deformation ability.

4. Summary
A 4 Kg dual-phases Al 0.6CoCrFeNi2Mo0.08V0.04 high-entropy
alloy with dentrite and inter-dentrite microstructures was pre-
pared. The alloy had a mixture structure of FCC and B2 phases
and exhibited excellent strength-ductility synergy at room and
cryogenic temperatures. The excellent tensile properties were
attributed to the strengthening of B2 phases and the solid solution
strengthening of minor elements. The inconspicuous necking was
attributed to the presence of more localized strain areas and the
alternatively deformation of high-strain areas and the low-strain
areas.
