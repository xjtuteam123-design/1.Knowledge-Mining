STRENGTH AND HEAT RESISTANCE OF NEW 
ANTIFRICTIONAL POWDER METALLURGY 
MATERIALS BASED ON NICKEL 
Yu. F. Shevchuk and T. A. Roik 
UDC 621.762 
The high-temperature strength and heat resistance of new powder metallurgy antifrictional materials 
based on the powder metallurgy nickel alloy ÉP975 were investigated. The effect of alloying elements on 
the structure and properties of the new materials at high temperatures was studied. It is shown that the 
developed materials can be successfully used in friction units operating at temperatures up to 900 °C in 
air. 
Keywords: high-temperature strength, heat resistance, antifrictional materials, friction units. 
The creation of heat-resistant antifrictional materials capable to operate under very difficult frictional conditions 
is required for progress in the development of gas turbines, energy conversion systems and other branches of high-
temperature engineering on which the present and future possibilities of energetics depend. Components for such 
friction units are produced by traditional casting methods and (only relatively recently) from sintered materials based on 
nickel and cobalt. The use of such components is limited by the high cost and shortages of the initial powders [1]. 
Nevertheless, compared with the cost of materials for machine construction that of the heat-resistant materials in many 
cases is not decisive  the determining factor is their superior use properties and stability. 
In this connection the methods of powder metallurgy, which permit wide variation in the concentrations of 
alloying elements and addition of any materials as well as structural refinement, are particularly valuable and 
undoubtedly superior to traditional casting procedures. Use of these methods for the creation of heat-resistant 
antifrictional materials is also advantageous from the point of view of economics: expenditures for costly materials (Ni, 
Mo, W, Nb, etc.) can be reduced by 50% compared to the traditional methods [2]. 
In the Institute for Problems of Materials Science, National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine, a group of high-
temperature antifrictional materials based on the powder metallurgy nickel alloy ÉP975 with additions of the solid 
lubricant CaF
2 have been developed [3]. These materials are capable of use in air under severe friction conditions at 
high temperatures (up to 900°C) and heavy loads (up to 7 MPa). These conditions are characteristic for the operation of 
friction units in gas turbines and plants. The tribological properties of these materials are substantially better than those 
of the cast and sintered materials presently used for operation under such conditions. In addition to superior 
antifrictional properties, these materials must possess high strength and heat resistance. 
With this objective in view an investigation was made of the effect of alloying elements on formation of the 
phases responsible for the high-temperature strength and heat resistance of antifrictional materials based on the powder 
metallurgy alloy ÉP975 with additions of CaF
2, produced by UkrNIIspetsstahl (Zaporozhje). The chemical composition 
of the materials is (mass%): 0.038-0.076 C; 8.65-9.31 W; 7.6-9.5 Cr; 2.28-3.04 Mo; 1.71-2.09 Ti; 4.75-5.13 Al; 1.71-
2.59 Nb; 9.5-11.4 Co; 4.0-8.0 CaF
2, remainder Ni. 
The optimal carbon concentration in heat-resistant alloys based on nickel is 0.01-0.08% [4]. This carbon 
concentration is sufficient to produce the amount of carbides in the investigated materials necessary to obtain strength at 
high temperatures without the loss of ductility. Alloying elements predetermine the formation of phases responsible for 
increased strength and stability at high temperatures [5].

Materials were produced from spherical powders of alloy ÉP975 with additions of calcium fluoride by hot 
isostatic pressing (HIP) for 4 h at 1210°C in argon at a pressure of 140 MPa [3]. Heat treatment was performed in order 
to optimise the morphology of dispersed phase precipitates and control the amount and distribution required for 
improved high-temperature strength: quenching (hold at 1240°C for 4 h, air cool) followed by aging at 910°C for 16 h in 
air. Quenching from a high temperature produces a more uniform distribution of alloying elements. High-temperature 
annealing followed by aging results in a uniform precipitation of strengthening phases which improve the mechanical 
properties of the alloy (strength and plasticity) and the reliability of components. The structure of the heat-treated 
materials (Fig. 1) was investigated with aid of the reflected light photomicroscope Neophot-32 (Germany). 
Investigation of the crystal structure at the atomic level by electron diffraction (electron microscope JEM-
ARM1000, maximum resolution 0.1 nm) showed (Fig. 2) that quenching from high temperatures leads to the formation 
of a solid solution with a face centered cubic lattice (a = 0.3527 nm). In order to establish the presence of one or another 
phase, and also its crystal structure, the data on interplanar distances and line intensities were compared with tabulated 
values. If the data agreed within the limits of error the phase was identified. 
Heating a quenched (supersaturated) solid solution leads to its decomposition upon aging, which proceeds in 
several stages. First, Al and Ti atoms in the solid solution are redistributed, leading to local enrichment in these 
elements. Next, a second phase appears in the places of increased Al and Ti concentration. Two phases with the same 
crystal lattice (FCC) but with somewhat different parameters (0.358 and 0.360 nm) were revealed by x-ray diffraction: a 
phase impoverished in aluminum and titanium, i.e. γ-phase, and enriched (precipitated), i.e. γ′-phase (Fig. 2). The 
proximity of lattice parameters of the γ- and γ′-phases leads to the fact that at high temperatures, as is known [6], they 
are coherently bonded. 
Electron selected area diffraction revealed the presence of η-phase Ni
3Ti with a hexagonal lattice ( a = 
= 0.51010 nm, c = 0.83067 nm) in platy form. It is known [7] that transformation of the γ′-phase into the η-phase is 
accelerated at 850°C, but this is not complete even after prolonged annealing (more than 1000 h). The aging of ÉP975 + 
CaF2 alloys occurs because not only titanium and aluminum are present in the matrix, but also molybdenum, niobium 
and tungsten. In this case intermetallic phases of the types A 3B (Ni 3Ti, Ni 3Al), AB 3 (NiAl 3 is ε-phase), AB (NiTi, 
NiAl), Me2Mo ((Ni, Co) 2Mo) are precipitated, as identified with the aid of the x-ray microdiffractometer JDX-MAPI 
(Japan) and subsequent calculation of interplanar distances and lattice parameters. 
Thus, following heat treatment the matrix structure of composites based on the powder metallurgy alloy ÉP975 
is a very inhomogeneous alloyed solid solution containing dispersed particles of intermetallics and precipitates of 
alloying element carbides along the grain boundaries.

Titanium and aluminum are the principal strengthening elements forming, under the given heat treatment 
regimes, γ′-phase intermetallics of the type Ni 3(Ti, Al) and Ni 3Al and also the phase Ni 2TiAl with a BCC lattice ( a = 
= 0.5860 nm), as revealed by x-ray microdiffractometry. These phases interact with the solid solution and precipitate 
from it in the form of finely dispersed particles which distort the crystal lattice of solid solution and produce 
strengthening. 
The presence of Mo and W in the solid solution (Fig. 3) was determined with the aid of the Auge microprobe 
JAMP-10SX (Japan). These strengthen the alloy at high temperatures thanks to the formation of MoNi4 (β-phase) with a 
tetragonal lattice ( a = 0.5683 nm, c = 0.3590 nm), δ-phase MoNi with a tetragonal lattice ( a = 0.9107 nm, c = 
= 0.8852 nm) and the phase WNi4 with a BCC lattice (a = 0.5730 nm, c = 0.3553 nm). 
The addition of Mo and W to the solid solution increases its heat resistance by retarding the processes which 
weaken the alloy at high temperatures. Niobium in the nickel matrix increases the hardness and hot-strength of the alloy. 
In distinction from other elements, niobium produces stronger grain refinement due to formation of the carbides NbC, 
α -, β-, and γ-Nb2C, identified electronographically and located at the crystal boundaries [7]. As a result, a fine-grained 
structure is created in which boundaries between the original powder particles of alloy ÉP975 are absent (Fig. 1). Thus, 
niobium provides a combination of stress-rupture strength and ductility and increases the thermal stability of the solid 
solution. Cobalt has a positive effect on the hot-strength of the powder metallurgy alloy ÉP975. In addition to the 
intermetallic Ni3Al, phase containing cobalt (Ni, Co, Cr) 3Al appear in it. As already mentioned, titanium leads to the 
formation of a strengthening γ′-phase of the type Ni3(Ti, Al), in which cobalt partially replaces nickel (Ni, Co, Cr)3Ti. It 
is known that the amount of strengthening γ′-phase is greatest at a cobalt concentration of approximately 10-15% [7]; 
the investigated composite contains 9.5-11.4% Co. 
The carbide phase in the metallic matrix of the composites are compounds of the alloying elements (MeC, 
Me3C, Me 7C3, Me 23C6, Me mMenC) located on the grain boundaries. These are Cr 7C3, Cr 23C6, NbC, TiC, Nb 2C,

The described structural features of composites based on the powder metallurgy nickel alloy ÉP975 containing 
particles of calcium fluoride (solid lubricant) provide a microheterogeneous structure with high antifrictional properties 
[3] combined with high strength at temperatures up to 900 °C in air. The strength properties of cast [8] and powder 
metallurgy alloy ÉP975 and the new material ÉP975 + CaF are given in Table 1. 
The strength and ductility of the powder metallurgy alloys were determined by standard methods [9-11]. The 
mechanical properties, including the 100 h stress rupture strength at 750 and 850 °C, are sufficiently high and above 
those of similar standard alloys (the strength properties of the composite ÉP975 + 6% CaF
2 are somewhat reduced by 
the addition of brittle CaF2). This can be explained by the smaller grain size, better distribution of alloying elements and 
the absence of liquation phenomena characteristic of cast heat-resistant alloys. The relative elongation δ and reduction 
of area ψ are approximately the same, which illustrates the isotropic properties of the materials in the longitudinal 
direction (characterized by δ) and in the transverse (characterized by ψ ). 
The heat resistance of the materials was investigated in air for 1, 5, 10, 25, and 50 h at 800 and 900 °C which 
correspond to the operating temperatures of a number of friction units in energy installations. The mass ( ∆P/P, %) and 
dimensions (∆h, mm; ∆h/h, %) of specimens of ÉP975 + 6% CaF 2 during oxidation in air at 800 and 900 °C were 
measured (Table 2). Analysis of the results indicates that the materials are sufficiently heat-resistant. Metallographic 
analysis of oxidized specimens (Fig. 4) did not reveal internal oxidation. X-ray diffraction analysis also did not detect 
oxide phases. 
Alloying elements, particularly their complex combination, have different effects on the heat resistance of 
alloys. Niobium (up to 4%) has no substantial effect in nickel alloys while chromium strongly increases their heat 
resistance. Tungsten produces the formation of a very porous scale, but since the melting and vaporization temperatures 
of tungsten oxide are above the operating temperatures of the materials lowered heat resistance appears at higher 
temperatures. Aluminum increases heat resistance thanks to the formation of a stable oxide film on the surface of the 
material. For Al > 3% (as in our case) this film consists predominantly of refractory Al
2O3. Molybdenum combines with 
oxygen to form a low-melting and volatile oxide with melting point 795°C. However, as is known, its negative effect (as

Thus, new antifrictional materials based on powder metallurgy nickel alloy ÉP975 with additions of CaF 2 
possess high strength and heat resistance at elevated temperatures and can be recommended for use in air at
