ABSTRACT
This study prepared a WE54 alloy with excellent mechanical properties through hard-tube rotary swaging and 
aging treatment. The microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of the WE54 alloy during high- 
temperature rotary swaging (HT-RS), room-temperature rotary swaging (RT-RS), and subsequent aging treat -
ment were studied in depth. The results indicate that the mechanical properties of WE54 alloy can be signifi -
cantly improved by rotary swaging and subsequent aging treatment. Compared to the extruded alloy, the as- 
swaged alloys are subjected to dynamic recrystallization. The grains are refined to 4.58 μ m and 3.93 μ m in 
HT-RS and RT-RS alloys, respectively. Meanwhile, a high density of dislocations is generated within the RT-RS 
alloy. Following the aging treatment, numerous β’ phases precipitate perpendicular to the basal plane, effectively 
hindering the slip of basal dislocations and enhancing the mechanical properties of the alloys. After aging at 
225
◦
C for 6 h, the RT-RS alloy exhibits optimal mechanical properties, with a tensile strength of 465 MPa, a yield 
strength of 424 MPa, and an elongation of 4.5 %. The superior mechanical properties of the alloy are attributed 
to fine grain strengthening, precipitation strengthening, and dislocation strengthening.
1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys possess attractive properties such as low density, 
high specific strength to stiffness, and high electromagnetic shielding 
properties and are promising candidates for automotive, aerospace, and 
military applications [1 – 4] . As a commercial rare earth (RE) magnesium 
alloy, WE54 (Mg-5.3Y-3.5RE-0.5Zr, wt%) alloy exhibits attractive 
properties at room and elevated temperatures, which is of great interest 
to the aerospace industry [5,6] . However, its widespread application is 
often limited by its low ultimate tensile strength (~ 285 MPa) and poor 
ductility [7,8] .
Severe plastic deformation (SPD) is very useful in improving the 
mechanical properties of magnesium alloys. At present, there are several 
popular SPD processes, such as equal channel angular pressing (ECAP), 
accumulative roll bonding (ARB), high-pressure torsion (HPT), and 
multidirectional impact forging (MDIF) [9 – 13] . These processes induce 
significant grain refinement, dislocation strengthening, and texture 
modification, thereby significantly enhancing the mechanical properties 
of Mg alloys. Alberto et al. [14] employed a combination of 
equal-channel angular pressing (ECAP) and ultrasonic surface rolling 
processing (USRP) on Mg-2Nd-0.6Y-0.5Zr (wt%) alloy, achieving 
significant grain refinement and high-density dislocation formation, 
which increased the tensile strength of the alloy from 262 MPa to 
330 MPa. Zhang et al. [15] fabricated an Mg/Al composite plate by 
hard-plate accumulative roll bonding (HP-ARB). The results indicated 
that the mechanical properties of the composite plates were significantly 
improved after HP-ARBed. Ren et al. [16] developed twins and nano -
grains in Mg-5.91Gd-3.29Y-0.54Zr (wt%) by HPT, and the hardness of 
the alloy was substantially enhanced. Lu et al. [13] prepared an 
Mg-Zn-Y-Zr alloy with random texture and low yield strength anisotropy 
using the MDIF method. However, due to their high cost, low processing 
efficiency, and small sample size, most SPD methods are still limited to 
the laboratory scale [17] .
Recently, many findings suggest that rotary swaging (RS) can 
effectively refine grains and enhance the mechanical properties of 
magnesium alloys. RS is an efficient and low-cost method because the 
sample size is not limited. Wan et al. [18] prepared nanocrystalline 
Mg-8Gd-3Y-0.4Zr alloys by the RS method, with further-aged alloys 
demonstrating a yield strength of 650 MPa. Chen et al. [19] reported 
that the mechanical properties of Mg-Mn-Al-Ca-Zn alloy were enhanced 
by the rotary swaging process with tensile strength of 350 MPa and 
elongation of 12.1 %. Although RS is suitable for decreasing production

The Schmid factor (SF) distribution can reflect the activation ten -
dency of various slip systems during the deformation. The following 
equation can calculate the SF: SF = cos φ cos λ , where φ is the angle be -
tween the normal to the slip plane (N) and the direction of the applied 
stress ( σ ), and λ is the angle between the slip direction and the direction 
of the applied stress [36] . The relationship between the Schmidt factor 
(m) and the resolved shear stress ( τ ) satisfies the Schmidt ’ s law: τ = m ⋅ σ
[36] . Fig. 8 shows the SF frequency distribution and corresponding 
average SF values of different deformation modes in HT-RS and RT-RS 
alloys. The SF values of main slip systems in different alloys were 
calculated, including {0001} basal < a > slip, { 10 10 } prismatic 
< a > slip, { 10 11 } first-order pyramidal < a > slip, and { 11 22 }
second-order pyramidal < c + a > slip [37,38] . The average SF basal 
< a > slip values for the HT-RS and RT-RS alloys are 0.26 and 0.20. For 
the prismatic < a > slip, the SF values are 0.41 for the HT-RS alloy and 
0.43 for the RT-RS alloy. As for pyramidal < a > slip and pyramidal 
< c + a > slip, the SF values for the HT-RS and HT-RS alloys were 0.45, 
0.46, and 0.45, 0.46, respectively. The SF values for the basal < a > slip 
are comparatively lower than those for the non-basal < a > and 
< c + a > slips in both HT-RS and RT-RS alloys. In particular, the RT-RS 
alloy exhibits a lower SF value for basal < a > slip, which indicates 
that more grains in the RT-RS alloy are in hard orientation. According to 
Schmidt ’ s law, a lower Schmid factor means that for a given applied 
stress, the resolved shear stress on the basal slip system is lower. As a 
result, it is more difficult to initiate and propagate dislocations along the 
basal plane in grains with a hard orientation, leading to a higher resis -
tance to deformation. Generally speaking, grain boundaries can promote 
the activation of non-basal < a > slip. In the HT-RS and RT-RS alloys, the 
SF values of { 10 10 } prismatic slip, { 10 11 } first-order pyramidal slip, 
and { 11 22 } second-order pyramidal slip are high. Consequently, due to

that many fine particles dynamically precipitated at the grain boundary 
during the rotary swaging process, and these precipitates appear as 
chains, as depicted in Fig. 9 (a). The sub-grains were also observed

and a certain number of dislocations are distributed along the sub-grain 
boundaries (red arrows). Those dislocations will subsequently propagate 
to grain boundaries, leading to the formation of LAGBs. With the RS 
strain increasing, more dislocations form, and the initial grains are 
refined further. Fig. 9 (c) presents the high-magnification image of the 
chain-like precipitates. These precipitates are nano-scale and sur -
rounded by high-density dislocations (red arrows), which suggests that 
the phase can pin dislocations effectively and thus strengthen the alloy. 
The EDS results for the precipitates are given in Fig. 9 (d). These fine 
particles are rich in Gd, Y, and Nd elements, which can be identified as 
the β -Mg
5

dislocations introduced by the RS process can tangle and accumulate at

For particles larger than 1 μ m, they primarily promote dynamic recrys -
tallization through the particle stimulated nucleation (PSN) mechanism. 
For particles smaller than 1 μ m, the particles can inhibit the dynamic 
recrystallization process of the alloy by hindering the dislocation 
movement [41,42] . As the dynamic precipitates in the RT-RS alloys are 
relatively small (~180 nm), their primary effect in the RS is pinning 
dislocations. Some twins are observed in the RT-RS alloy, as depicted in 
Fig. 9 (g). These twin boundaries can also act as obstacles for gliding 
dislocations, thus improving the mechanical properties of the RT-RS 
alloy.

4. Discussion
4.1. Dynamic precipitation behavior during rotary swaging
As shown in Fig. 4 , the RE solutes in the Mg solid solution are 
dynamically precipitated during the RS process. The dynamic precipi -
tation behavior is different in HT-RS and RT-RS alloys, as the pre -
cipitates are much smaller and denser in the RT-RS alloy. It has been 
reported that the dynamic precipitation in Mg alloys is related to the 
high density of lattice defects from the deformation process [31,46 – 48] . 
In this study, the HT-RS alloy is kept at 420 ℃ for 15 min between RS 
passes, i.e., the alloy remained at a high temperature during the RS 
process. However, the RT-RS alloy is no longer heated between RS 
passes after being heat-treated at 420
◦
C for 30 min before RS. Appar -
ently, the defects (such as vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries) 
in the RT-RS alloy are higher than those in the HT-RS alloy. These lattice 
defects can serve as nucleation sites for precipitation, which facilitates 
the nucleation of the precipitate. Besides, the solubility of RE elements 
can also affect the dynamic precipitation behavior. It can be seen from 
the phase diagrams of the Mg-RE alloys that the solubility limit of the Y, 
Gd, and Nd elements are decreased with the lowing of temperature [3] . 
The supersaturated RE elements lead to dynamic precipitation. There -
fore, the RE elements in the RT-RS alloy can provide a much more sig -
nificant driving force for dynamic precipitation. Lowering the RS 
temperature has a dual advantage for dynamic precipitation. Firstly, it 
increases the defect density, offering more nucleation sites for precipi -
tation. Secondly, a lower RS temperature leads to supersaturated RE 
elements, enhancing the potential for dynamic precipitation.

5. Conclusions
In this paper, a hard-tube rotary swaging + aging treatment method 
is used to improve the mechanical properties of WE54 alloy. The 
microstructure evolution of the WE54 alloy in the rotary swaging pro -
cess and aging treatment are investigated, the strengthening mechanism 
was analyzed in-depth, and the following conclusions are obtained:
(1) The WE54 alloy is subjected to dynamic recrystallization, and 
dynamic precipitation occurs during rotary swaging. Grains of HT-RS 
and RT-RS alloys are refined to 4.58 μ m and 3.93 μ m, respectively. A 
high density of dynamic precipitates is formed in HT-RS and RT-RS 
alloys, and the precipitates in the RT-RS alloy are denser and smaller, 
generating a more substantial precipitation-strengthening effect.
(2) The mechanical properties of HT-RS and RT-RS alloys are 
significantly improved compared with those of the as-extruded alloy. 
The RT-RS alloy exhibits a better mechanical performance with tensile 
strength, yield strength, and elongation of 390 MPa, 335 MPa, and 10.5 
%, respectively.
(3) The nano-sized β ′ precipitates on the prismatic planes during 
aging, effectively hindering the basal or non-basal dislocation slip. The 
as-aged RT-RS alloy exhibits optimal mechanical properties, with a 
tensile strength of 465 MPa, a yield strength of 424 MPa, and an elon -
gation of 4.5 %. The high mechanical properties of the alloy are mainly 
attributed to fine grain strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and 
precipitation strengthening.
