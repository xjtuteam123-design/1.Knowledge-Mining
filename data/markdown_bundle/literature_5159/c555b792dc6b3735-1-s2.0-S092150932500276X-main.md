1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), also known as multi-principal element 
alloys (MPEAs) [ 1 ], present a novel design concept with vast composi -
tion space compared to traditional single-element alloys [ 2 ]. These al -
loys tend to form single-phase solid solutions, consisting of at least five 
major elements in nearly equal proportions, with FCC, BCC, or mixed 
phases [ 3 – 7 ]. RHEAs typically composed of high melting point ele -
ments, such as Ti, V, Nb, Cr, W, and Zr demonstrate excellent 
high-temperature properties [ 8 – 11 ]. However, the high density severely 
limits their processing and applications. The emergence of lightweight 
RHEAs transcends the performance constraints of conventional alloys, 
offering a synergistic enhancement that meets the dual demands for 
high-temperature resistance and reduced weight in materials. A 
lightweight design is achieved by reducing the density of the alloy 
through the selection of lighter elements, including aluminum, magne -
sium, and silicon [ 12 – 14 ]. The element selection [ 15 – 17 ], phase 
composition [ 18 ] and properties [ 19 – 21 ] of lightweight RHEAs have 
been extensively studied. Among these alloys, Ti
3

high tensile strength and ductility.

technology that combines 3D modeling and high-energy laser technol -

create complex shapes, high throughput preparation [ 22 ], and 
compatibility with other processes [ 23 ]. Meanwhile, extraordinary 
metallurgical processes of L-DED offer unique advantages in multiple 
alloying. Additive manufacturing (AM) techniques have been success -
fully employed for the preparation of HEAs [ 24 ]. However, the

Post-processing methods such as hot isostatic pressing (HIP) [ 27 ], 
laser remelting [ 28 ], and thermo-mechanical processing (TMP) [ 23 ] 
have been widely employed to address the inherent defects in AM ma -
terials. HIP effectively eliminates porosity and enhances ductility, but 
may cause grain coarsening at high temperatures and pressures, which 
can compromise mechanical performance [ 29 ]. Laser remelting, while 
effective in improving surface quality and hardness, risks excessive 
melting and grain growth, limiting its effectiveness in certain applica -
tions [ 30 ]. Cold rolling, a key TMP method [ 31 ], improves strength 
through dislocation accumulation, phase refinement and texture evo -
lution, but typically reduces ductility due to excessive strain hardening 
[ 32 ]. However, appropriate heat treatment following cold rolling can 
restore ductility while retaining strength, as shown in studies on RHEAs 
such as HfNbTaTiZr cold-rolled sheets, where full recrystallization of the 
cold-rolled structure occurred after annealing at the appropriate tem -
perature, significantly improving tensile ductility without sacrificing 
strength [ 33 ]. The initial material employed in conventional cold rolling 
is cast ingots, contain almost no dislocations. As a result, the deforma -
tion energy (driving force for recrystallization) accumulated after each 
rolling is relatively low. Instead, AM leads to the formation of rich 
dislocation networks and anisotropy, which accelerates the accumula -
tion of stored deformation energy and enhances the storage of additional 
deformation energy, thereby transforming these shortcomings of AM 
into advantages [ 34 ].

4. Discussion
4.1. Deformation mechanisms
The microstructural anisotropy of AM samples is caused by its rapid 
solidification and vertical transfer of heat flow to the substrate [ 41 ]. 
Fig. 4 a illustrates the AP sample with uneven grain size and orientation. 
After rolling, as shown in Fig. 4 b, the grains are significantly 
fragmented, resulting in a higher distribution of LAGBs, which are 
indicative of stored deformation energy. During annealing, the grains 
recrystallize, forming smaller and randomly oriented grains. The CR-900 
sample, in particular, exhibits a fine equiaxed grain structure with an 
average grain size of 35 μ m, approximately three times smaller than that 
of the AP sample. In addition, the second phase was found to be 
distributed at GBs in both CR-600 and CR-700 samples.
By analyzing the Kernel average misorientation (KAM) distribution 
of different samples as shown in Fig. 8 , insights into the dislocation 
density and local strain within the alloy can be obtained. In the AP

dislocations are eliminated at high temperatures to form new grain

From the SEM images of the fractured side of the CR-900 sample, it 
can be observed that there is clear evidence of necking after stretching, 
and distinct shear bands are visible in the enlarged view ( Fig. 9 ). The 
distribution of these shear bands is both dense and uniform within the 
grain, which indicates that the material has undergone effective plastic 
deformation. In addition, the distribution of shear bands along the 
tensile fracture indicates that the extensive sprouting and cross-diffusion 
of shear bands can effectively resist tensile stresses and dissipate strains 
to share macroscopic plastic deformation [ 31 , 42 ].
To investigate the influence of microstructure on the deformation 
mechanism of RHEAs after annealing, the neck shrinkage of the CR-900 
sample was examined using TEM after room temperature tensile testing. 
Fig. 10 a illustrates the formation of dislocation microbands with varying 
spacing during the stretching process. The activation of multiple slip 
systems promotes dislocation entanglement near the microbands, 
enhancing the strain-hardening capacity of the RHEAs ( Fig. 10 b). 
Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 10 c, dislocation pinning and the forma -
tion of dislocation loops, which are typical mechanisms during slip, are 
clearly observed. Fig. 10 d highlights the development of a cellular 
microstructure resulting from extensive cross-slip, characterized by 
dislocation cells and high-density dislocation walls (HDDWs) sur -
rounding the cell blocks.

strain hardening, effectively delaying premature necking in the CR-900 
sample. The activation of multiple slip systems and the associated 
dislocation entanglement are known to significantly enhance strain 
hardening [ 43 , 44 ]. Similarly, dislocation pinning and loop formation 
act as additional mechanisms contributing to the strain-hardening pro -
cess [ 9 ]. The formation of a cellular microstructure through cross-slip,

annealing is provided in Fig. 11 . In the AP state, the internal micro -

local mismatches and the potential formation of micropores. These de -

grain refinement caused by work hardening. The rolling process induces 
significant residual stress within the material, resulting in uncoordi -
nated motion that impedes dislocation movement and creates substan -
tial stress concentrations, thereby reducing the ability of material for 
plastic deformation.
As the annealing temperature increases, the microstructure of the 
alloy undergoes significant changes. In the CR-600 sample, a large 
number of secondary-phase precipitates form and are distributed along 
the grain boundaries. These precipitates substantially increase the 
strength of the alloy but also lead to localized stress concentrations 
during loading, which can cause early fracture of the material [ 48 ]. In 
contrast, the CR-700 sample has some recrystallized grains and a smaller 
size and amount of C14 precipitates, which provides some improvement 
in ductility. It is worth noting that CR-800 sample shows a significant 
increase in ductility. Recrystallization effectively reduces the accumu -
lation of local dislocations and inhibits the formation of voids, thereby 
lowering the risk of premature fracture. At 900 
◦
C, grains with high 
strain energy storage are more likely to recrystallize [ 4 ], which further 
refines the grain structure and enhances the mechanical properties of the 
alloy.
The excellent plasticity observed in CR-900 sample is mainly 
attributed to the fact that recrystallization not only refines the grain size 
but also improves the grain orientation in the crystal microstructure. 
The internal grains exhibit a random distribution with low dislocation 
density which allows for facilitating dislocation movement during 
deformation ( Fig. 10 ). A schematic diagram of microstructure evolution 
after rolling and annealing is provided in Fig. 11 . To gain a more 
comprehensive understanding of the excellent matching strength and 
ductility exhibited by CR-900 sample, it is essential to comprehend its 
strengthening mechanism.
4.2. Strengthen mechanisms
The CR-900 sample, demonstrating an excellent balance of strength 
and ductility, is selected to analyze the contributions of various

CR-900 sample is calculated to be approximately 932 MPa. A histogram 
( Fig. 12 ) illustrates the contributions of to the yield strength, alongside 
the experimentally measured yield strength for comparison. It is evident 
that solid solution strengthening plays a dominant role in reinforcing 
strength, while grain refinement serves as a significant growth point, 
achieving significant strengthening effects without a significant reduc -

4.7 % lower than the experimental result. This discrepancy can be 
attributed to additional strengthening effects, including the oxygen and 
the nitrogen strengthening effect. These factors, while not explicitly 
accounted for in the theoretical calculation, contribute to the enhanced 
mechanical performance observed experimentally.
In summary, the CR-900 sample demonstrates a comprehensive 
strengthening effect achieved through solid solution strengthening, GBs 
strengthening and dislocation strengthening, resulting in a calculated 
yield strength of 932 MPa. However, the experimental yield strength of 
the alloy measures 976 MPa, which is slightly higher than the calculated 
value. This discrepancy may be attributed to two factors. Firstly, the 
elevated oxygen and nitrogen content in the alloy could facilitate the 
formation of ordered interstitial complexes. This outcome corroborates 
the findings of He and Cui et al. [ 58 , 59 ], which have demonstrated that 
such complexes can impart unparalleled enhancements in strength and 
ductility in solid solutions comprising intricate components. Secondly, it 
should be noted that the assumed inherent parameters of the material 
used for calculations may not precisely match those of the actual alloy 
composition. These findings underscore the need for further analysis and 
optimization. Future research could explore integrating AM with syn -
chronized cold rolling, where modified rolling tools compress the 
molten pool during printing. This real-time grain refinement approach 
enhances mechanical properties and offers a promising pathway for 
developing alloys with superior performance.
