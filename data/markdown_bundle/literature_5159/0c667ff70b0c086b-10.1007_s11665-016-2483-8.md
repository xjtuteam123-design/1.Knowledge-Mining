Mechanical Properties and Deformation Mechanisms
of Mg-Gd-Y-Zr Alloy at Cryogenic and Elevated
Temperatures
Bin Chen, Jing-Xu Zheng, Chao-Ming Yang, Yi-Xin Chen, San-Chen Cao, Zhi-Xian Zhao, Xiao-Ling Li, and Chen Lu
(Submitted March 9, 2016; in revised form October 11, 2016)
In this study, mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms of Mg-Gd-Y-Zr alloy at temperatures
ranging from 77 K to 523 K have been investigated. The effects of temperature on the mechanical prop-
erties, deformation mechanism, and fracture mechanism are discussed. The results show that the strengths
of alloy decrease gradually while the elongations increase progressively with increasing temperature. The
maximum ultimate tensile strength of the alloy as high as 442 MPa is obtained at 77 K. As the temperature
increases from 77 K to 523 K, the ultimate tensile strength of the alloy decreases from 442 MPa to 254 MPa
and the elongations increase from 6.3% to 28.9% gradually. The study veriﬁes that the deformation at 77 K
is predominated by basal slip and 10 /C2212fg = 10/C2211hi deformation twinning system. At 223 K, lots of twins
emerge primarily at grain boundaries. At 373 K, all dislocations are proved to be Æaæ dislocations. At 523 K,
although basal slip is still the dominant deformation mechanism, non-basal slip systems also become
activate.
Keywords cryogenic temperatures, elevated temperatures, me-
chanical properties, Mg-Gd-Y -Zr magnesium alloy
1. Introduction
Nowadays, designing lightweight spacecraft structures
attracts more and more attention owing to their extensive
applications in the space exploration. Applications of this sort
have spurred interest in the cryogenic properties of high-
strength lightweight materials because these products and
components must work in extreme environments (Ref 1-3). For
instance, the surface temperature at the poles on Mercury and
the Moon is 174 K and 159 K, respectively (Ref 4). During the
night on the Moon, the temperature falls down to 93 K. It is
also assumed that the minimum surface temperatures in the
permanently shadowed regions of the crater in the polar regions
may be even lower than 50 K (Mercury) and 40 K (Moon) (Ref
5). Although there are dramatic variations for quoted temper-
ature data, temperature difference between day and night on the
surface of the Moon is undoubtedly more than 300 K. In order
to withstand such extreme environments, lunar rover vehicles
have to be made of lightweight materials that can be tolerated
from 93 K to 423 K (Ref 6).
The low density and high speciﬁc strength of magnesium
alloys are particularly suitable for lightweight applications such
as automotive, aerospace, aeronautical, and electronic products.
However, their poor mechanical properties and corrosion
resistance hamper the widespread applications of conventional
Mg-based alloys (Ref 7-10). Its poor corrosion resistance is the
result of formation of non-protective surface corrosion products
and galvanic interactions with alloying compositions, impuri-
ties, or other metals because magnesium lies at the active end of
the galvanic series of metals. Because of the close-packed
hexagonal (HCP) crystal structure of magnesium, the mechan-
ical properties of magnesium alloys are relatively poor.
Recently, the addition of rare earth (RE) elements is found to
be an effective way to improve the mechanical properties of
magnesium alloys (Ref 11-14). These alloys are attractive for
the automotive and aerospace industry in a wide range of
applications. However, only a few of studies have been focused
on the properties and mechanisms of magnesium alloy at
cryogenic temperatures (Ref 15). This is due to that the
previous studies on magnesium alloys pay speciﬁc attention to
the rising demand for major powertrain applications that
operate at elevated temperature, such as automatic transmission
cases up to 448 K and engine blocks up to 473 K. Therefore,
there is an urgent demand of researches into the properties and
mechanisms of magnesium alloy at cryogenic temperatures due
to their favorable cryogenic behaviors which could meet the
demand of aerospace industry.
As one of the newly developed RE-containing magnesium
alloys, Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloys exhibit excellent mechanical prop-
erties and outstanding creep resistance (Ref 16, 17). The
addition of Gd and Y improves the mechanical properties of the
alloys by the formation of uniformly dispersed (Gd, Y)-rich ﬁne
precipitate within the matrix, while the addition of Zr reﬁnes
the grain size effectively (Ref 18). Many investigations related
to the microstructure and mechanical properties of the Mg-Gd-
Y -Zr alloys at elevated temperatures have been conducted (Ref
19-21). Although mechanical property data exist for a number
of Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloys, to date, few researches about its
mechanical properties at cryogenic temperatures have been
conducted and reported.
In this work, we have chosen Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloys as the raw
material to investigate their performance at cryogenic temper-

2. Experimental
The Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloy in the present study was prepared
from pure Mg and Mg-30Gd, Mg-25Y , Mg-25Zr master alloys
(wt.%) by melting in an electrical resistance furnace under the
protection of mixture gas of SF 6 and CO2. The ingot was solid
solution-treated at 773 K in a sulfur atmosphere for 10 h
followed by quenching in water. The alloy was hot extruded
into rods with the extrusion ratio 30:1 at 673 K.
Tensile properties of the Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloy were evaluated at
the temperature range from 77 K to 523 K. Flat tensile
specimens were machined from the extruded bar. Tensile tests
were carried out in a universal materials testing machine using
specimens with a gauge length of 10 mm, a thickness of 2 mm,
and a width of 3.5 mm. All tensile tests were carried out until
fracture. At least three samples were repeated for each condition.
The chemical compositions of the alloy were determined by
inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-
OES) analysis and are given in Table 1. The microstructure was
characterized by an optical microscope (OM, ZEISS Axio Imager
A1 m), scanning electron microscope (SEM, JEOL JSM-7600F),
and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100).
The specimens for OM were etched with alcohol solution
containing 4% of nitric acid (vol ume fraction). Specimens for
TEM observation were prepared by cutting thin slices from the
tensile tested specimens near the fracture, followed by grinding-
polishing, twin-jet electropolishing, and ﬁnally argon ion milling.

Comparisons are made among the tensile stress-strain curves
of Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloys at cryogenic temperatures and elevated

depends on deformation temperature. Stress increases as
temperature decreases, but this increment is higher at lower
temperatures. It is revealed from the ﬁgure that the stress
increases rapidly at the initial stage of strain (i.e., elastic
region). The strain increase leads to a linear increase in the
stress. After the elastic region, the curves eventually deviate
from this linear proportionality. It is also worth noting that the
shape of the ﬂow curves changes when deformation temper-
ature is altered. The slope of the stress-strain curve decreases
with increasing temperature. At lower temperatures (77,
123 K), the alloy exhibits severe hardening. The rupture occurs
at the peak stress with limited elongation. At temperatures from
173 to 273 K, however, the strain hardening rate is much
lower. The ﬂow curves show the stress increased steadily with
strain, followed by a short region that stress decreases slowly
with strain until fracture. At temperatures higher than 373 K,
the ﬂow curve exhibits relatively poor strain hardening, and the
stress decreasing stage is extended. It illustrates that necking
phenomenon appears at these temperatures (Fig. 2).
3.2 Microstructural Observation
Figure 3 shows the optical microstructure of the studied
alloy tested at various temperatures. Figure 3(a) shows the
microstructure of the specimen deformed at 77 K and profuse
twins that appear in grains due to the limited available slip
systems at relatively low temperature, which are responsible to
premature mechanical fracture. Figure 3(a) shows that a
number of secondary twins emerged. The volume fraction of
twins gradually decreases with increasing temperature (Fig. 3a-
d). The twinning remains one of the dominating deformation
modes up to 373 K. However, there is a drop in twin volume
fraction over temperatures between 373 and 423 K. It indicates
that the dominant deformation mechanism transits from com-
bination of dislocation slip and twinning to dislocation slip.
When the deforming temperature increases up to high temper-
atures (from 423 to 523 K), twinning is rarely observe. The
grain sizes of deformed structure at the temperatures are
approximately equal to that of the initial structure. It illustrates
these temperatures are not high enough to initiate dynamic
recrystallization (DRX). Kaibyshev et al. concluded that the
kinetics of DRX are accelerated when all three main slip modes
(basal, prismatic, and pyramidal) are active (Ref 22). Thus, it is
suggested that non-basal dislocation slips and dislocation climb
are not operated on a large scale to accelerate DRX at the
temperatures in the present study.
3.3 TEM Investigations
TEM examination is carried out in order to investigate in
more detail the inﬂuence of temperatures on deformation
mechanisms of the studied alloy. It is well known that TEM is a
powerful technique for characterizing crystalline defects such
as twins, dislocations, grain boundaries. For example, the
contrast of a dislocation depends on the magnitude of the scalar
product between the reciprocal lattice vector g and the Burgers
vector b, g Æ b. If it is zero or very small, the contrast is very
weak, i.e., the dislocation is invisible. Figure 4 presents typical
TEM micrographs showing the deformation twins and selected
area diffraction pattern (SADP) for specimen deformed at 77 K.
Besides twins, a high density of dislocations is also observed,
which indicates that basal slip is activated. It is no doubt that
basal slip is easiest to be activated because its critical resolved
shear stress (CRSS) is very low, approximately 0.5 MPa in pure
magnesium (Ref 23). The TEM analysis veriﬁes that the
deformation at low temperature is predominated by basal slip
and deformation twinning in the present alloy. The SADP
inserted in Fig. 4 shows that the twin system (marked by arrow)
is 10 /C2212fg = 10/C2211hi . Therefore, twins can be classiﬁed as tensile
twins. It is due to that the contraction twinning generally
requires a much larger CRSS than the tensile twinning (Ref 24,
25). Due to the limited slip system in HCP Mg alloys at
cryogenic temperature, twinning has been proved to play a
signiﬁcant role in plastic deformation.
Figure 5 shows that twin junctions are formed as two twins
intercepted during growth. It reveals that high stress concen-
tration at the junctions leading to defects that distort the twins,
which is also an evidence of those twin-twin intersections, can
make a considerable contribution to the strain hardening. It is
also found that there is a ‘‘bridge’’ across twin at twin
junctions, as marked by an arrow in the dark-ﬁeld image
Fig. 5(c). The contrast of the ‘‘bridge’’ is equals to that of
primary twin, which is an evidence of that the ‘‘bridge’’ has
similar crystallographic orientations with primary twin.

twin boundaries. Figure 6(b) and (c) shows dark-ﬁeld images of

frequently observed, it serves as an indication that non-basal
slip systems may be activated partially at 523 K. Several
investigations show that the activation of Æc + aæ dislocations
motion is energetically more favorable when the temperature is
above 473 K (Ref 29, 30).
3.4 Fracture Morphologies
Several studies have addressed the fracture mechanisms at
ambient temperature in commercial magnesium alloys (Ref 31,
32). However, little research has been devoted to the fracture
mechanisms of RE-containing magnesium alloys, especially in
at cryogenic and elevated temperatures. To gain a better
understanding of the fracture mechanisms in Mg-Gd-Y -Zr
alloy, the fracture surfaces for the specimens are observed using
SEM. Figure 10 shows tensile fracture morphologies corre-
sponding to specimens pulled to failure at tensile temperature
from 77 K to 523 K. Figure 10(a) shows the fracture mor-
phologies under the deformation temperature of 77 K. It shows
a brittle fracture surface, which displays a lot of cleavage
features, generally observed at low deformation temperature.
The fracture surface consists of cleavage facets 5-10 microns in
dimension. It is obvious that intergranular fracture is the
dominant fracture mode at the temperature. The fracture
morphology in low magniﬁcation is also inserted in Fig. 10(a).
The smooth fracture implies poor ductility. Abrupt failure
appears to have taken place across the specimen thickness after
small deformation. Under this deformation condition, the
necking phenomenon is hard to occur due to the fact that the
deformation of fractured specimen is small. Similarly, the

3.5 Deformation Mechanisms
In general, the variety of deformation behavior in most
magnesium alloys is attributed to the occurrence of various
hardening and softening mechanisms that depend on temper-
ature. At low deformation temperature (77 K, 123 K), the stress
increases dramatically to the maximum at the initial stage of
plastic region due to severe strain hardening, which is resulted
from the signiﬁcant increases in the dislocation density and
twin volume fraction. The stress concentration is difﬁcult to be
relaxed at the temperatures due to the high density of
dislocations pileups. Twinning introduces additional obstacles
to dislocation movement (Ref 33). At relatively low temper-
ature, these pileups lead to more serious strain hardening and a
higher ﬂow stress. In this case, the stress only can be relaxed by
fracture. At temperature from 173 to 373 K, the stress increases
rapidly at the beginning of the deformation. The hardening
becomes less pronounced with increasing temperature. The
results indicate that there is a considerable increase in
elongation at the temperatures. At relatively high temperature
(423, 473, 523 K), softening plays a more and more important
role in deformation, resulting in a gradual decrease in peak
stress. Generally, the higher the deformation temperature is, the
more activated the movable dislocations are, which results in a
decrease in ﬂow stress. However, regions where strain hard-
ening and softening become balance or work softening is more
pronounced are not observed in the present study. After the
peak stress point, the decreasing stress indicates the occurrence
of necking (Ref 34).
4. Conclusions
In this study, mechanical properties and deformation
mechanisms of Mg-Gd-Y -Zr alloy at cryogenic and elevated
temperatures have been investigated. The following conclu-
sions are drawn based on the present investigation.
1. With the increase in temperature, the strengths of alloy
decrease gradually while the elongations increase pro-
gressively. As the temperature increases from 77 to
523 K, the UTS of the alloy decreases from 442 to
254 MPa and the elongations increase from 6.3 to 28.9%
gradually.
2. The TEM analysis veriﬁes that the deformation at low
temperature is predominated by basal slip and
10/C2212fg = 10/C2211hi deformation twinning system. At 223 K,
lots of twins emerge primarily at grain boundaries. When
the deforming temperatures are increased up to high
423 K and above, twinning is rarely observe. At 373 K,
most of the Æaæ dislocations are aligned to the trace of
the basal plane, and basal slip controlled the deformation.
At 523 K, although basal slip is still the dominant defor-
mation mechanism, non-basal slip systems also become
activate partially.
3. The fracture behavior ranges from brittle fracture to duc-
tile fracture as the temperature changes from low to high.
The necking phenomenon is observed in specimen tensile
tested at high temperatures.
