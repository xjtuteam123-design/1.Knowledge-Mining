Strength and fracture of two hot-rolled Ti–Al–Si–Nb
dual phase alloys based on Ti 3Al
L.T. Zhang* ,1, J.S. Wu, W. Hua, G.H. Qiu
Laboratory for High Temperature Materials and Testing, School of Materials Science and Engineering,
Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai 200030, PR China
Received 14 May 1999; accepted 16 November 1999
Abstract
Ti–Al–Si–Nb dual phase alloys are mainly composed of a2-Ti3Al matrix and Ti 5Si3 silicide phases. In this paper, two alloys (402
and 405) whose Si contents are 2 and 5 at% respectively were arc melted and hot-rolled into sheets with di/C128erent amounts of
deformation. The silicide phase (Ti,Nb) 5(Si,Al)3 was broken up into small pieces and redistributed in the a2 matrix during the hot-
rolling. Improved strength and ductility of the two alloys were observed after hot-rolling, which can be attributed to both the ﬁnely
distributed reinforcement silicide phase and reﬁnement of the matrix grain size. The mechanical properties of the two alloys are
dependent on their volume fractions of the silicide phase: the strength of alloy 405 is higher than that of alloy 402, while alloy 402 is
more ductile than alloy 405. The brittle–ductile transition temperature of the two dual phase alloys is between 600 and 800
C. The
surface slip on the dual phase alloys was also observed. Obvious separation between the (Ti,Nb) 5(Si,Al)3 particles and the a2 matrix
is found on the fracture surfaces obtained at high temperature, showing dimple-like morphology. # 2000 Elsevier Science Ltd. All
rights reserved.

1. Introduction
a2-Ti3Al based alloys are attractive for application as
structural material at elevated temperatures due to their
inherent light weight, relatively high melting point and
high sti/C128ness [1]. During the past two decades or so,
many studies have been focused on ﬁnding ways to
improve the ductility, high temperature strength and
oxidation resistance of Ti
3Al alloys. It has been found
that the introduction of Ti 5Si3 as a second phase via Si
alloying in the a2 matrix can potentially improve some
properties such as strength and creep resistance of the
Ti
3Al based alloy. Studies from 1988 to 1996 on the Ti–
Al–Si ternary system found a monovariant line repre-
senting a eutectic reaction of the matrix and the Ti
5Si3
phase extending from the Ti-rich region to the TiAl
region [2,3] and proved that eutectic reactions between
the matrix and the silicide phase could be manipulated
through material processing to create microstructures
that were similar to those of more costly intermetallic
matrix composites. Based on this idea, the research
group at GKSS developed a novel hypoeutectic Ti–Al–
Si–Nb dual phase intermetallic alloy in which (Ti,N-
b)
3(Al,Si) is the matrix phase and (Ti,Nb) 5(Si,Al)3 is the
second phase [4]. Alloys with nominal composition Ti–
23.5Al–6.5Si–10Nb (at%) processed via ingot metal-
lurgy exhibit excellent creep and oxidation behaviour
[5]. However, the coarse, inhomogeneously distributed,
brittle Ti
5Si3 phase formed via eutectic reaction leads to
poor tensile elongation and low toughness at room
temperature. Breaking down the brittle silicide phase
contained in the matrix is, thus, a key issue in achieving
a good balance between the strength and ductility in this
alloy. Preliminary research by Qiu, who applied thermo-
mechanical processing to the dual phase Ti–Al–Si–Nb
system, showed the improvement of strength and
toughness through obtaining ﬁne and homogeneously
distributed (Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 phase in the a2 matrix [6].

2. Experimental procedure
The 400-g cake-like ingots of two alloys Ti-24Al–
11Nb–2Si (labelled as 402) and Ti–24Al–11Nb–5Si
(labelled as 405) were arc melted in a laboratory furnace
under an argon atmosphere. The ingots were melted 4
times and annealed at 1150
C for 30 h to ensure chemi-
cal homogeneity. After annealing, these ingots were
wrapped in a stainless steel sheet and hot-rolled at ade-
quately high temperature (1200 C) by several rolling
passes (each pass accounted for a small amount of defor-
mation) to the ﬁnal deformation amounts of 65, 75 and
90% respectively. The deformation amounts were calcu-
lated regarding the reduction in thickness of the ingots
before and after hot-rolling to the initial thickness of the
ingots. The microstructure of the as-cast and as-rolled
alloys was characterised by optical metallography. Tensile
specimens with a gauge dimension of 8 32m m
3 and
compression specimens with the dimension 448m m3
were cut from the as-rolled slab along the rolling direc-
tion by spark erosion. Some of the compression speci-
mens were metallographically polished and slightly etched
to reveal surface slip after introducing a small amount of
plastic deformation. Both the tensile and compression
tests were performed in air on a Shimadzu AG-100kN
screw driven test frame at room temperature to elevated
temperatures. The cross head speed was 0.2 mm/min for
both compression (strain rate 4.210
ÿ4 sÿ1) and tensile
(strain rate 4.210ÿ4 sÿ1) tests. The surface slip and
fracture surfaces of the specimens were investigated on a
Hitachi S-520 scanning electron microscope (SEM).

3.1. Microstructure before and after hot-rolling
The optical micrographs of the two alloys in both the
as-cast and as-rolled states are displayed in Figs. 1 and
2. In the as-cast state, the microstructures of the two
alloys are essentially hypoeutectic [Figs. 1(a) and 2(a)],
in which Ti
5Si3 is the second phase and 2 is the matrix.
The 2 matrix region consists of Widmannsta ¨tten laths
which are typical in the a2 based alloys with Nb addi-
tion. The matrix grain size di/C128ers greatly in the as-cast
state for the two alloys: 380 mm for alloy 402 and 60
mm for alloy 405 [compare Fig. 1(a) with Fig. 2(a)]. The
silicide phase whose amount is proportional to the Si
content in the alloys formed network along the grain
boundary via eutectic reaction upon cooling. Previous
investigations [2,8] on the microstructure of the dual
phase alloy revealed that the maximum Si solubility in the
a
2 matrix was about 2 at% and the Ti 5Si3 silicide phase
also dissolved certain amounts of Nb (roughly even dis-
tribution in Ti 5Si3 and a2) and Al. Thus the 5–3 silicide
phase is conventionally written as (Ti,Nb)5(Si,Al)3.
After rolling, the a2 matrix grains were obviously
elongated along the rolling direction and reﬁned, and
the coarse (Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 network on the grain
boundary was also broken up (Figs. 1 and 2). The grain
size of the matrix determined by the width of the silicide
band is reduced to 45 mm for alloy 402 and 20 mm
for alloy 405 [Figs. 1(b) and (c), 2(b) and (c)]. At 65%
deformation, the (Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 network is likely to
redistribute along the elongated grain boundary without
much change in particle size [Figs. 1(b) and 2(b)], indi-
cating that the coarse (Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 phase was not
suciently reﬁned into individual particles at this level
of deformation. At higher deformation amounts (75 and
90%), the (Ti,Nb) 5(Si,Al)3 phase was further broken up
into small particles and redistributed more uniformly in
the a
2 matrix [see Figs. 1(c), 2(c) and (d)].

3.3. Tensile properties
Figs. 5 and 6 compare the tensile properties of the as-
cast and as-rolled alloys at temperatures from RT to
800
C. The strength of the two alloys is found to
decrease slightly from RT to 600 C and steeply from
600 to 800C (Fig. 5). Alloy 405 exhibits higher strength
but is more brittle than alloy 402 in tension, which is the
same as for compression. As mentioned above, since the
solubility of Si in the a

and Nb contents in the matrix for alloys 402 and 405 are
almost the same. Therefore, the higher strength of the
hot-rolled alloy 405 compared with the hot-rolled alloy
402 is due to the higher volume fraction of the silicide
phase. On the contrary, the plastic elongation of the two
alloys is higher for the one with lower Si content (Fig. 6)
in most occasions, which is in accordance with the
compression tests (Fig. 3). However, there is no evi-
dence that the plasticity at room temperature was
improved by hot-rolling as shown in Fig. 6. This might
be due to the fact that although the size of the (Ti,N-
b)

is not ﬁne enough especially when compared to P/M-
processing [7], since coarse silicide particles are harmful
to the ductility of the alloy. The temperature dependent
plastic elongation of the alloys is similar for both ten-
sion (Fig. 6) and compression (Fig. 3): there is a tiny
increase in plastic elongation from RT to 600

signiﬁcant increase from 600 to 800 C. Thus it can be
concluded that the brittle-ductile transition temperature
(BDTT) of these dual phase alloys is higher than 600

previous observations [9].

4. Discussion
Hot-rolling has a great e/C128ect on the microstructure,
mechanical properties and fracture surfaces of the Ti 3Al
based Ti–Al–Si–Nb dual phase alloys. The e/C128ects of
microstructure on the overall mechanical properties of the
dual phase alloys are two fold: morphology of the (Ti,N-
b)
5(Al,Si)3 silicide phase and the grain size of thea2 matrix.
The improvement of strength after hot-rolling can be
well explained by the fact that hot-rolling reﬁned the
silicide phase in the matrix. As shown in the surface slip
of the specimen (Fig. 4), the ﬁne silicide particles do not
have much e/C128ect on the plastic deformation of the a
2
matrix [Fig. 4(a)] whereas the coarse silicide particles
greatly interact with the slipband in the a2 matrix [Fig.
4(b)]. The coarse silicide phase in the as-cast state
embrittles the alloy in two ways: (1) the coarse silicide
phase itself contains microcracks and thus is a crack
source; (2) large cracks are easy to initiate at the inter-
face between the coarse silicide phase and the matrix.

only breaks up the coarse silicide network but also
reﬁnes the matrix grain. After rolling, reﬁnement of
both the silicide phase and matrix results in improved
plasticity of the alloys. The abnormal drop in plasticity
of alloy 405 with 90% deformation amount may be
attributed to the growth of the matrix grain after many
times of heating.

5. Conclusions
Two Ti
3Al based Ti–Al–Si–Nb dual phase alloys (402
and 405) were hot rolled and tested in this investigation.
It can be concluded that:
1. After hot-rolling, the strength and ductility of the
two Ti–Al–Si–Nb alloys (402 and 405) are
improved compared with the as-cast state, which is
attributed mainly to the break-up and redistribu-
tion of the coarse brittle (Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 phase
particles in the a2 matrix and partly to the reﬁne-
ment of the a2 matrix grain.
2. Observation of the surface slip on the dual phase
alloys indicates that plastic deformation occurs in
the a
2 matrix without harmful interaction with the
ﬁne silicides but is impeded by the coarser silicide
particles.
3. Alloy 405 is stronger than alloy 402 because of the
higher volume fraction of the silicide phase
(Ti,Nb)
5(Si,Al)3 in the a2 matrix; but alloy 402 is
more ductile than alloy 405.
4. The brittle-ductile transition temperature (BDTT)
of the two dual phase alloys is found to be higher
than 600
C and lower than 800C.
5. At room temperature both alloys fracture in quasi-
cleavage mode. The bonding between the silicide
phase and the a2 matrix is weak. At high tem-
peratures, obvious separation between the silicide
phase and the matrix is found, exhibiting dimple-
like morphology.
