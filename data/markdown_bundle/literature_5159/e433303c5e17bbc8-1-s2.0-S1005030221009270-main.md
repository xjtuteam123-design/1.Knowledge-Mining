Transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) endows material with continuous work hardening ability, which 
is considered as a powerful weapon to break the strength-ductility tradeoff. However, FCC based alloys 
with TRIP effect can not get rid of the “soft” feature of the structure entirely, resulting in insuﬃcient 
yield strength. Here, a Co x Cr 25 (AlFeNi) 75- x high-entropy alloy is designed. NiAl phase is used as strength- 
ening component to improve yield strength, while TRIP effect ensures plasticity. Compared with the pre- 
vious TRIP high-entropy alloy, its yield strength is nearly doubled, and the uniform elongation is more 
than 55% at room temperature. Furthermore, the corresponding multiphase microstructure evolution and 
deformation mechanisms are investigated. Signiﬁcantly, stacking faults and /Sigma13 twin boundaries are con- 
ﬁrmed to be the nucleation sites of HCP phase by HAADF-STEM. Ingenious composition design and proper 
heat treatment process make it a perfect combination of precipitation strengthening and transformation- 
induced plasticity, and thus guide design in the high-performance alloy.

1. Introduction 
Nowadays, the attainment of high strength and high ductility 
in materials is a vital requirement for most engineering appli- 
cations. Unfortunately, increasing strength leads to ductility loss, 
an effect referred to as the strength-ductility trade-off [ 1 , 2 ]. Over 
the years, many scholars have been devoted to the study of ma- 
terial strengthening and toughening, trying to break the balance 
or weaken the effect as much as possible [3–5] . Transformation- 
induced plasticity, i.e. , the TRIP effect, is considered a powerful 
weapon to achieve this goal [3] . Martensitic transformation is acti- 
vated at varying local stress or strain levels during the tensile test, 
and continuous strain hardening is obtained. Exactly, some dual-/ 
multi-phase steels [ 6 , 7 ], TRIP steels [6–8] , bainite steels [9] with 
TRIP effect have excellent mechanical properties. 
High-entropy alloy (HEA), a new concept of alloy design, has 
attracted extensive academic interests since it was ﬁrst proposed 
in 2004 [10] . Compared with the traditional alloy, HEA reduces 
the limit of composition, so researchers can design the alloy more 
skillfully. In addition, some strengthening methods of traditional 
alloy are also applicable to HEA. Interestingly, the result may be

occurs in the process of cooling with the change of Mn con-

content of Ni and increasing Co are considered to be an effective 
method to promote the transformation. Without exception, they 
will undergo phase transformation during the tensile process, thus 
obtaining better mechanical properties. 
However, there is a question that must be raised: all the above 
alloys are based on the high-entropy alloy with FCC matrix, which 
is doomed to its lower strength, especially the yield strength. Actu- 
ally, their yield strength is all about 350 MPa [ 3 , 12–14 ], this is far 
from enough. Therefore, Huang et al. [15] introduced TRIP effect 
into BCC based high-entropy alloys, using the high strength nature 
of BCC alloys and transformation induced plasticity, a win-win sit- 
uation is achieved. 
Different from Huang’s ideas, this paper still focuses on the FCC 
based high-entropy alloy with TRIP effect. So how can we over- 
come the “soft” of FCC structure? Precipitation strengthening, a 
basic but effective method to improve the strength, is widely ap-

phase microstructure evolution, mechanical properties and defor- 
mation mechanisms have also been investigated. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Alloy preparation 
Ingots with normal compositions of Co x Cr 25 (AlFeNi) 75- x (at%) 
(x = 45, 51 and 57) alloy system studied in this work were pro- 
duced by arc-melting under a high purity argon atmosphere, and 
the purities of the constituent element were better than 99.9 wt%. 
Each ingot was remelted 5 times to assure their chemical homo- 
geneity and then drop-casted into a copper mold. The as-cast in- 
got with dimensions of 40 × 40 × 4.5 mm 3 was hot rolled at 
800 °C with a rolling reduction ratio of 67% (thickness changed 
from 4.5 mm to 1.5 mm) and subsequent recrystallization anneal- 
ing at 1050 °C in an Ar atmosphere for 6 min followed by water- 
quenching. 
2.2. Microstructure characterization 
EBSD measurements were carried out in a ﬁeld-emission FIB- 
SEM (crossbeam 550) equipped with a symmetry detector. The 
EBSD specimens were mechanically ground and polished, then Ar- 
gon ion polished. TEM characterizations were conducted on JEM- 
ARM200F. All TEM samples with 3 mm in diameter were cut from 
the gauge length of tensile samples interrupted at different strains 
and then ground to ∼50 μm followed by twin-jet electro-polishing 
using a mixed solution of HClO 4 : C 2 H 5 OH = 1: 9 with a direct volt- 
age of 30 V at a temperature around -20 °C. 
2.3. Tensile test 
Dog-bone shape tensile specimens with a gauge length of 
10 mm were cut from the annealed sheet along the rolling direc- 
tion. Each side of tensile specimens with a thickness of ∼1.1 mm 
and a gauge width of ∼3.5 mm was polished down to a 1500-grit 
SiC paper. Room temperature tensile tests were conducted on a 
AG-xplus 100KN machine at a rate of 1 × 10 −3 s −1 . 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Mechanical properties 
The mechanical response of three alloys was examined un- 
der uniaxial tensile test at the room temperature and represen- 
tative engineering stress-strain curve was plotted in Fig. 1 (a). 
All three alloys show excellent mechanical properties, especially 
Co 51 Cr 25 Al 8 Fe 8 Ni 8 , which has a yield strength close to 700 MPa 
and a plasticity of more than 55%. A good combination of high 
strength and good ductility can be readily achieved in this precip- 
itation strengthening HEA with TRIP effect. Actually, the mechani- 
cal performance can be further improved by carefully tailoring the 
alloy composition and optimizing the heat-treatment condition, a 
systematical study will be carried out in the future. In the remain- 
der of this article, Co 51 Cr 25 Al 8 Fe 8 Ni 8 alloy was selected as the re- 
search object, and deformed specimens with different strains (in- 
dicated by grey stars in Fig. 1 (a)) were analyzed by EBSD and TEM. 
Fig. 1 (b) compares the uniform elongation and yield strength of 
Co 51 Cr 25 Al 8 Fe 8 Ni 8 alloy with various FCC based TRIP high-entropy 
alloys and FCC-type alloys with precipitation strengthening. These 
alloys strengthened by a single strategy are inevitably constrained 
by the strength plasticity trade-off, that is, the upper left and lower 
right regions of Fig. 1 (b). What’s exciting is that our alloy has the 
advantages of both strengthening methods, ﬁrmly occupying the 
central area of the picture, breaking the strength plasticity balance, 
and the speciﬁc details will be described later.

3.4. Evolution of microstructure and deformation mechanisms 
It has been conﬁrmed that yield strength of the annealed al- 
loy with dual phase structure is greatly improved compared with 
the previous TRIP HEA. Among them, NiAl phase, as a reinforc- 
ing component, plays an important role. According to relevant re- 
search, the hardening from NiAl phase is no more than the follow- 
ing points [ 17 , 37 ]: (I) coherency strengthening, (II) modulus mis- 
match strengthening and (III) order strengthening. For a certain 
system, only the proportion of each reinforcement method is dif- 
ferent. These have been extensively studied, not the focus of this 
paper. It is relatively easy to apply precipitation strengthening, but 
this reinforcement is not expected to break the strength-ductility 
trade-off. So, we are more concerned about how to maintain ex- 
ceed 55% uniform elongation at such a high strength. 
To reveal deformation mechanisms of the studied alloys, mi- 
crostructure evolution during tensile was investigated. Fig. 5 shows 
the EBSD phase maps under different strain, and Fig. 6 summarizes 
the change of twin boundary length and HCP phase proportion. It 
should be noted that the distribution of NiAl phase in the matrix 
is heterogeneous ( Fig. 2 (a)). In order to make the statistical results 
comparable, we scanned each sample several times to ensure the 
proportion of NiAl is as close as possible. From the EBSD images 
and Fig. 6 , we can mainly see two obvious changes with the in- 
creasing local strain: (I) HCP phase is experiencing to grow out of 
nothing and gradually increased, especially after the strain reaches 
∼20%. (II) meanwhile, the length of /Sigma13 twin boundary decreases 
sharply. In this way, problem (I) will collapse by themselves, the 
heat treatment process of this alloy takes into account both pre- 
cipitation strengthening and TRIP effect. So far, above two ques- 
tions have been perfectly answered. Next, we will use TEM to re- 
veal more details.

4. Conclusion 
In this paper, EBSD, TEM, STEM and other means were used 
to systematically study Co x Cr 25 (AlFeNi) 75- x alloy. The main con- 
clusions are as follows: (I) Through ﬁne composition design and 
corresponding heat treatment process, precipitation strengthen- 
ing and TRIP effect can coexist; (II) With the synergistic effect 
of precipitation strengthening and TRIP, Co 51 Cr 25 Al 8 Fe 8 Ni 8 alloy 
has a yield strength of ∼700 MPa and a uniform elongation of 
more than 55% at the room temperature; (III) in the tensile pro- 
cess of Co 51 Cr 25 Al 8 Fe 8 Ni 8 alloy, deformation mechanism changes 
from stacking fault deformation to transformation-induced plastic- 
ity; (IV) during the martensitic transformation, HCP phase nucle- 
ates at stacking faults and /Sigma13 twin boundaries.
