1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have been focused on since the
emergence of the notion ﬁrstly put forward by Yeh et al. in 2004
[1–6]. They were suggested to contain at least ﬁve constituents
with a concentration of 5–35 at.% for each, which have been
also termed as multicomponent alloys, multi-principle-elemental
alloys or complex component alloys. Although HEAs have many
complex compositions, lots of them can form single-phase solid
solutions, such as face centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic

or hexagonal close packed structures, which conduce to some dis-
tinctive mechanical properties [7–10].
Among the vast HEA systems, the single-phase FCC CrFeCoNi
alloy is an attractive system, which possesses high strength and
ductility at low temperatures due to the onset of nanotwins
[3,11–15]. Yet, at medium-high temperatures, the strength of CrFe-
CoNi HEA drops dramatically to a low level [11,16]. As a promising
structure alloy, the strength of the CrFeCoNi HEA at medium- and
high-temperatures needs to be improved signiﬁcantly to satisfy
the requirement of practical applications, since some parts may
serve in rigorous environments which cover a large temperature
range, such as austenite steels [17–19]. To obtain desirable high-
temperature mechanical
 properties, extensive systematic work
incorporating multiply strengthening methods is expected.

Among the widely used strengthening methods, precipitation
strengthening has been considered as an effective way for strength-
ening both at low and high temperatures. A desirable strengthening
effect could be achieved by elaborately tailoring the features of
precipitates (morphology, size, fraction and distribution) through
annealing [4,20–26]. During the past years, some efforts by pre-
cipitation strengthening have been devoted to enhancing the
high-temperature mechanical properties of the CrFeCoNi-related
HEA systems [27–31]. A typical example is the widely investigated
L12 phase, analogs to the phase structures in superalloys. By intro-
ducing Ti and Al elements, He et al. [32] signiﬁcantly improved
the room-temperature strengths of the (CrFeCoNi)94Ti2Al4 HEA,
which was attributed mainly to the nanosized L12 phase precip-
itation. Daoud et al. [33] found that L12 (∼46 vol.%) and B2 phases
(< 5 vol.%) improved the high-temperature tensile properties of
the Al10Co25Cr8Fe15Ni36Ti6 HEA. Jen et al. [34] studied the L12
phase formation in the AlxCo1.5CrFeNi1.5Tiy HEAs and indicated
that the high hardness at high temperatures is attributed to the L12
phase precipitates. The Ni45-x(FeCoCr)40 (AlTi)15Hfx (x = 0 at.%, 0.2
at.%) HEAs were designed to obtain /CR/ /CR′ dual-phase microstruc-
tures and high strengths at high temperatures [35]. Further, He
et al. [28] found an enhancement in ﬂow stress in the tempera-
ture range of 1023–1173 K in the same composition. However, the
volume fraction of L12 phase decreases with the increasing temper-
ature and especially, undesirable coarse incoherent discontinuous
precipitation (DP), i.e. brittle L21 phase, occurs at grain boundary
[32]. The volume fraction of L21 phase can be very high depend-
ing on the microstructures and heating conditions, e.g. as high as
∼60% in the CrCoNi-base alloys. As reported by Chang et al. [29],
coarse DP colonies can crack grain boundaries of the CrFeCoNi-
base HEAs at high temperatures, thus signiﬁcantly reducing hot
mechanical properties [29]. Moreover, the coarsening rate constant
of L12 phase is about 1.82 × 10−28 m3/s at 1073 K [36], showing a
poor thermostability, which may not be an optimal precipitates
for high-temperature strengthening. Another representative high-
temperature strengthening phase, B2 phase, was usually used at
the temperature range of 823−923 K [18,33,37,38]. Wang et al.
[38] have studied the B2 phase transformation of AlxCrFeCoNi
HEAs and indicated the hot hardness transition temperature of
820−930 K, about 0.5 Tm. Wang et al. [39] have investigated the
tensile properties of B2-enhanced Al0.7CoCrFe2Ni HEA at 923 and
973 K, and achieved the ultimate strengths of 468 and 340 MPa,
respectively. Yet, the ductile-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)
of B2-NiAl appears below 1023 K and the coarsening rate of B2-
NiAl in Fe-15Al-10Ni ferrite alloy at 973 K is 1.10 × 10−28 m3/s [40],
relatively larger than NbC. In their work, the investigated temper-
atures are above the DBTT point, so the B2-NiAl precipitates are
readily deformable, leading to weaker or even no hardening effect.
Consequently, controllable precipitates with a high thermostability
and great strengthening effect are needed. Among the commonly
used strengthening phases, NbC carbide with a high thermosta-
bility, has been extensively used in heat-resistance steels, which
is expected to improve notably the high-temperature strength of
CrFeCoNi HEA. In addition to the high thermostability due to the
strong afﬁnity between Nb and C, the coarsening rate constant of
NbC carbide 2.67 × 10−33 m3/s [41] is much lower in contrast to the
L12 phase (1.82 × 10−28 m3/s) and other phases (e.g. L21 and B2),
indicating much stronger resistance to coarsening [41,42]. Besides,
NbC carbide shows attractive physical properties such as high
hardness, ultrahigh melting point (3873 K), high elastic modules
and high thermal /electrical conductivities, making it an excel-
lent candidate of strengthening phase for high-temperature service
[43–46]. In consideration of the aforementioned factors, NbC pre-
cipitation strengthening is considered as a promisingly effective
way to enhance the hot strength of CrFeCoNi HEA. However, the
related research is still lacking, which needs to be elaborately stud-
ied.
In this work, we added various contents of Nb and C into the cast
CrFeCoNi HEA. The microstructure was adjusted by solid-solution
treating the ingots and aging to achieve optimal NbC precipitates.
Generally, the cast CrFeCoNi alloy has a high elongation but with an
inferior yield strength due to the coarse grains [47]. However, cast-
ing is an important technical approach in industries that enables
the production of complex-shape components. The schematic rep-
resentation of microstructure design is shown in Fig. 1. Columnar
dendrites are expected to form during solidiﬁcation with some
coarse NbC precipitates in interdendrites in consideration of its
strong afﬁnity. After solid solution, most NbC should be dissolved
and ﬁne particles are expected to precipitate during aging. Coarse
grains were maintained ﬁnally, which is beneﬁcial since they are
stable at high temperatures compared with ﬁne grains [48,49]. Fur-
ther, tensile properties at selected high temperatures of 673, 873
and 1073 K were tested based on the selected optimal composi-
tion and aging parameters. Responsible strengthening mechanisms
were also discussed.

3.2. Tensile properties
Representative tensile engineering stress–strain curves of three
HEAs tested at 673–1073 K are present in Fig. 6, and the correspond-
ing column charts of yield strength (/ESCy), ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) and elongation to fracture (ε) are shown in Fig. 7. The base
HEA CrFeCoNi has very low /ESCy and UTS values at three tempera-
tures. As the temperature increases, /ESCy, UTS and ε of the CrFeCoNi
HEA decrease, especially the elongation showing a rapid reduc-
tion trend to only 14% at the highest tested temperature 1073 K,
indicating an intergranular fracture mechanism deformed at high
temperatures [55]. However, the introduction of Nb and C sig-
niﬁcantly increases both the /ESCy and UTS at all temperatures. The
addition of 0.5 at.% can greatly improve the /ESCy values by 67%, 145%
and 113% at 673, 873 and 1073 K, respectively. Further increas-
ing the alloying element content to 1 at.% is unable to strengthen
further, instead, leads to a slight reduction of the /ESCy value. Simi-
larly, 0.5 at.% Nb and C remarkably increase the UTS values while
doubling the Nb and C contents slightly decreases the UTS value.
All alloys exhibit relatively high elongations at three temperatures.
The elongation is decreased with increasing addition of Nb and C
content at 673 K, however, the 0.5 at.% Nb/C-doping increases the
values at higher temperatures, especially at 1083 K up to a dramat-
ically high value of 58%, compared with 14% for the base alloy. In
summary, the 0.5 Nb/C HEA shows an optimal strengthening effect
at tested temperatures.

Fig. 8 highlights the representative fracture surfaces of the base

3.4. Mechanism analysis
3.4.1. Microstructure evolution and stability
Two types of carbides were formed after aging, although we
considered the stable feature of NbC than other possible carbides
within the constituent elements. The mixing enthalpy between Nb
and C is −102 kJ/mol, much lower than that between Cr and C (−31
kJ/mol), let alone those between other elements [57]. This implies
a much stronger tendency of NbC formation, some M23C6 carbides,
however, also precipitate along the grain boundaries. NbC carbides
are derived from different routes: microscale ones that are residual
NbC after uncomplete solid solution and submicro-sized spherical
particles that precipitate during aging. respectively. For the lamel-
lar NbC in the as-cast microstructures, similar feature has been
observed, which was deemed as from the eutectic reactions at the
last solidiﬁcation regions [51,52,58]. At the beginning of solidiﬁca-
tion, the proeutectic FCC-structured matrix forms from the liquid
phase with the solutes (mainly Nb and C from the (CrFeCoNi)-Nb
and (CrFeCoNi)-C pseudo-binary phase diagrams [12,59]) rejected
into the solid-liquid interface front gradually; until a moment with
a very high fraction solid, the eutectic reaction occurs, leading to
the formation of lamellar structures. Considering the tolerance of
quartz tubes, efﬁciency of heating treatment and energy saving,
solid solution treatment was conducted at 1473 K for 4 h and 1523
K for 2 h. Thus NbC carbides with strong stability could not be
dissolved completely, which was indicated by Zhou et al. [45]. As
shown in Fig. 9, the size of NbC precipitates after 1073 K/100 h
aging seems no obvious change comparing to that after 1073 K/1
h aging. After solid solution treatment, the solute segregation was
eliminated and some incompletely dissolved NbC precipitates with
micro scale were retained. Further aging induced ﬁne spherical NbC
carbides, which could have a coherent relationship with the FCC
matrix. The coherent relationship between NbC and FCC matrixes
in many HEAs with similar compositions, e.g. CrMnFeCoNi [42],
austenitic alloys [41,60] are similar.
NbC particles have been proved to have a strong resistance to
coarsening [41,45]. NbC particles are coherent with the matrix so
that the elastic strain energy from lattice mismatch at the precip-
itate/matrix interface can be weak, therefore, the morphology is
primarily determined by minimizing the surface area of the pre-
cipitates, which leads to spheroidal particles [36]. The coarsening
rate could be expressed by the Ostwald

the formation of M23C6 precipitation [23]. Fig. 5(c)gives evidences
that the M23C6 phase has an orientation relationship with the
matrix: (111)M23C6 // (111)matrix; [011]M23C6 ∥ [011]matrix. Low crys-
tal indices of (111) have low energy and conduce to minimize the
formation energy of M23C6. Fine intergranular M23C6 precipitates
are also stable at high temperatures, which have been used in creep-
resistant alloys through reducing grain boundary sliding [17,67].
However, M23C6 precipitates have a higher coarsening rate con-
stant 4.78 × 10−30 m3/s at 923 K [67]. As reported by Kaneko et al.
[68], discrete ﬁne M23C6 precipitates in the austenite 316 L stain-
less steel will agglomerate to ﬁlm-like coarse precipitates at 923 K.
Thus, the morphology of the M23C6 precipitates should be tailored
elaborately.
It has been recognized that when loaded at high temperatures,
coarse grains are favorable in the microstructure stability, com-
pared with ﬁne grains which are apt to grow rapidly [69]. In the
current case of the castings, all the alloys including base HEA CrFe-
CoNi and carbide-strengthened HEAs comprise very coarse grains.
Due to the absence of the thermomechanical deformation to break
up the microstructures, coarse grains were maintained ﬁnally after
aging. At higher temperatures (873 and 973 K) for the CrMnFeCoNi
HEA, intergranular fracture occurred due to grain boundary sliding,
which was reported by Abbasi et al. [55]. Coarse grains compared
with ﬁne grains can weaken grain boundary sliding, resulting in
stronger resistance to fracture, i.e. enhancement of ductility. There
are mainly two reasons responsible for the high stability of the
coarse grains at high temperatures. Firstly, it is widely known that
the kinetics of grain growth is mainly driven by the grain boundary
energy, /CR, which is dependent on the misorientation, /DC2, i.e. /CR = /CR0/DC2
[70]. Since the misorientation /DC2 of ﬁne grains is larger than coarse
grains, the grain boundary energy of ﬁne grains is larger than that
of coarse grains. Therefore, microstructures composed of coarse
grains, compared with those of ﬁne grains, possess correspond-
ing low interface energy, which conduce to the tardy grain growth.
Another factor to improve the thermostability at high temperatures
is the Zenner pinning effect of the carbides present throughout the
grains. Two types of carbides, distributed in intragranular and inter-
granular regions (Figs. 4 and 5), contribute to the strong restriction
of grain boundary migration. In the investigated three temper-
atures, both carbides are thermodynamically stable below their
dissolution temperatures. Especially, the very high thermostability
of NbC is favorable to its particle sizes and rapid increasing of num-
ber density, even at higher temperatures. Zhao et al. [41] indicated
that NbC carbides in austenitic stainless steels keep a high ther-
mostability at 1023 K during long thermal aging. Thus, the formed
microstructure including both coarse grains and carbide precipi-
tates has a very high thermostability, which provides a basis for
high-temperature applications.

3.4.2. Strengthening mechanism
The high-temperature tensile properties (Fig. 6) indicate the
signiﬁcant strengthening of carbides of the CrFeCoNi HEA. How-
ever, the strength increment is not proportional to the addition
of the contents of Nb and C. Doubling the addition of both
elements (from 0.5 to 1) results in a slight increment of the
strength at 673 K or no further increasing but instead, decreas-
ing at 873 and 1073 K. Thus, the addition of 0.5 at.% Nb and
C was considered as the optimal composition for strengthening.
Our previous work clearly showed the remarkable strengthening
effect of carbides (mainly NbC) in the CrMnFeCoNi HEA deformed
at room temperature which was attributed to the Orowan by-
pass mechanism [42]. The general strengthening mechanisms,
e.g. grain-boundary strengthening, solid-solution strengthening
and precipitation strengthening, could play a different role in
strengthening at high temperatures. It is noted that here the
dislocation strengthening was ignored due to the undergoing of
sufﬁcient anneals. Since the samples for tensile tests were cut
parallel to the pouring direction of melts, the loading direc-
tion is considered to be perpendicular to the columnar grain
growth direction (roughly heat extraction direction). Since the
obstacle effect of grain boundaries is a long-range force [71], its
strengthening is taking effect at high temperatures as well, albeit
not so signiﬁcant. Nerveless, dynamic recrystallization (DRX) and
dynamic recovery (DRV) frequently occur at high temperatures
[28,72,73], resulting in softening in the work hardening process.
When coming to the solid-solution strengthening, due to the
strong combination between Nb and C, little Nb and C are dis-
solved in the matrix thereby neglecting its effect. It is considered
that signiﬁcant strengthening is mainly attributed to the precipi-
tates.
As shown in Fig. 6, the work hardening processes below and
above 873 K are obviously different. At 673 and 873 K, the work
hardening rate for three alloys is relatively high compared to that
at 1073 K. Stepanov et al. [73] have investigated the hot deforma-
tion behavior of CrMnFeCoNi HEA, and revealed that the volume
fraction of recrystallized grains is less than 4.5% below 873 K and
∼40% at 1073 K. Therefore, it can be inferred that the strengthening
mechanism below 873 K is similar with that in ambient tempera-
ture, but at 1073 K,
 DRX occurs resulting in signiﬁcant softening. At

important part in resistance to coarsening of the microstructures
[45,74].
As shown in Fig. 6, the elongation increases at the tempera-
ture range of 673−1073 K, in addition to the yield strength. The
improvement of elongation can be mainly attributed to the ﬁne
intergranular M23C6 carbides. The sharply decreased elongation
of the CrFeCoNi HEA at high temperatures is caused by the grain
boundary sliding [17]. Fine intergranular M23C6 carbides are effec-
tive in suppressing the nucleation of micro-voids and cracks by
reducing the grain boundary sliding and act as the barriers against
crack propagation [17], so the elongation is obviously improved in
current carbide-strengthened HEAs. In addition, ﬁne intergranu-
lar M23C6 carbides and intragranular NbC carbides can remarkably
improve the resistance to creep at high temperatures due to their
high stability [17,45].
Dislocation climb usually exists and acts as the main defor-
mation mechanism at high-temperature plastic deformation. He
et al. [28] have investigated the strengthening mechanism of L12
precipitates in (CrFeCoNi)Ti2Al4 HEA, and the main deformation
mechanism is dislocation climb at 1123−1173 K, and dislocation
movement similar to that at room temperatures. In this work, three
alloys are all tested below 1073 K, so it can be inferred that dislo-
cation sliding and bypassing NbC carbides to generate dislocation
loops dominate the deformation mechanism, and the existence of
DRX and DRV resulting the softening at 1073 K. The strengthening
mechanisms is schematically illustrated in Fig. 10, indicating that
the main strengthening mechanisms is precipitation strengthening
from NbC and M23C6 carbides and slight grain boundary strength-
ening.
In summary, CrFeCoNi HEA is signiﬁcantly strengthened by car-
bides in the temperature range from 673 to 1073 K. Below 873 K,
precipitation strengthening mainly resulting from NbC and M23C6
carbides is the dominate mechanism, while at 1073 K, there prob-
ably occurred DRX and DRV which lead to a softening in the plastic
deformation process. Fine intergranular M23C6 carbides remark-
ably improve the elongation of the CrFeCoNi HEA by suppressing
the nucleation of micro-voids and cracks through reducing grain
boundary sliding at high temperatures. Nerveless, dispersed ﬁne
and homogeneous carbides are still worth pursuit in future works,
and more details about the deformation mechanisms in higher tem-
peratures are needed too.
4. Conclusions
In this study, as-cast (CrFeCoNi)-xNb-xC HEA (x = 0 at.%, 0.5 at.%
and 1 at.%) were prepared by drop cast, solid-solution treatment
and aging to generate an FCC matrix with carbide precipitates. The
microstructure and high-temperature mechanical properties were
systematically investigated and compared with the CrFeCoNi HEA.
The obtained conclusions can be summarized as follows.
(1) The coarse microstructure is relatively stable and NbC carbides
exhibit an excellent resistance to coarse at high temperature
attributing to ideal precipitates to generate the remarkable
strengthening effect.
(2) The yield strength CrFeCoNi-0.5 Nb/C HEA is increased to be
approximately doubled to CrFeCoNi HEA at evaluated temper-
atures, and obtained an increment on elongations.
(3) The main strengthening mechanism in 0.5 Nb/C HEA is the
precipitation hardening by NbC carbides. At 1073 K, DRV and
DRX probably occurred and resulting in softening in the plastic
deformation process.
(4) The CrFeCoNi-0.5 Nb/C HEA exhibits to be an ideal composi-
tion because more Nb/C did not result obvious enhancement
especially above 873 K.
