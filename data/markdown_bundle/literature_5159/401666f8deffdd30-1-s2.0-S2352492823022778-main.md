1. Introduction 
With the continuous development of industry, vibration, and noise 
problems are becoming more serious, affecting the safety and service life 
of machinery and military equipment and posing a great danger to 
people ’ s health [1] . Highly damped alloys with excellent mechanical and 
damping properties are considered the most important and effective 
means to solve this urgent task. Traditional damping alloys are mainly of 
five types: dislocation-type damping alloys, represented by Mg alloys 
[2 – 4] ; ferromagnetic-type damping alloys, represented by 
Fe-Cr/Fe-Al-based alloys [5,6] ; complex-phase damping alloys, repre -
sented by gray cast iron and Fe-Si-C alloys [7 – 9] ; twin-crystal-type 
damping alloys, represented by Mn-Cu alloys [10 – 13] ; in addition to 
Fe-Mn-based damping alloys [14 – 16] . In practical applications, con -
ventional damping alloys are often limited by environmental 
constraints. 
Due to their unique high entropy properties, HEAs usually form a 
simple solid solution structure. They are of interest for their excellent 
mechanical properties [17] , good structural stability [18] , good corro -
sion resistance [19] , and oxidation resistance. Thus, high-entropy alloys 
offer great potential for developing new highly damped alloy materials. 
In recent studies, a few researchers [20] have worked to break the 
barriers of traditional damping alloy composition design and introduce 
the design concept of high entropy into damping alloys [21 – 24] . In this 
study, we focused on the damping properties of the alloy while also 
considering its mechanical properties and prepared a structurally and 
functionally integrated high-entropy damping alloy by designing the 
composition of the high-entropy alloy and optimizing the heat treatment 
process.

2. Experimental procedures 
2.1. Alloy melting 
To minimize the influence of impurity elements on the damping 
ability of the alloy, Fe, Mn, Cr, and Co monomers with a purity of not less 
than 99.99% are used as raw materials and melted in a non-self- 
consuming vacuum electric arc furnace (WK) in this paper. At first, 
the primary materials were added into the copper crucible in order of 
melting point from high to low, then vacuumed to 5.0 × 10

eventually filled with high-purity argon gas as the protective atmo -

ensure clean pumping. The oven temperature is adjusted to melt all the 
monomeric materials and flow freely within the crucible. Before each 
cast ingot is sucked into the water-cooled copper mold, it must be 
repeatedly melted five times. The cast Fe

The high-magnification electron images were acquired using a

independent of amplitude and frequency. However, no plastic defor -
mation occurs in the alloy and no expansion of dislocations under stress 
is involved, so the damping performance depends mainly on the mag -
netic properties of the alloy. In other words, the damping mechanism in 
the low-strain amplitude region is a ferromagnetic damping mechanism. 
In general, the Q
 1 
of ferromagnetic damping alloys increases rapidly 
with strain amplitude and reaches the peak of internal friction, then 
decreases slowly [35] . The S-B theory agrees well with the experimental 
results in the region of low strain amplitude. According to the experi -
mental results, the saturation magnetization strength and residual 
magnetization strength of the alloy increase with the increase of 
annealing temperature. In other words, the magnetic properties of the 
alloy increase with the increase of annealing temperature. On the con -
trary, the damping performance in the low strain amplitude region also 
has the same change rule with the increase of annealing temperature. 
However, when the annealing temperature reaches 1100
◦
C, the 
damping of the alloy not only does not continue to increase but rather 
decreases rapidly, one of the reasons for this is that the coercivity of the 
alloy is inversely increased by the excessively high annealing tempera -
ture. For damping alloys, however, energy loss occurs only when the 
vibrational stress exceeds the energy barrier relative to the domain wall 
motion [37] . The energy barrier is related to the coercivity, the higher 
the coercivity the higher the energy barrier [6] . Thus higher energy 
barriers are one of the reasons for the lower Q
 1 
of the AT-1100 alloy. 
Although the experimental results in the high-strain region deviate from 
the SB theory, the high entropy alloys maintain a wide range of high 
damping strains after annealing at different temperatures. This is 
because there is another damping mechanism at work in the high-strain 
region, so there is no tendency for the usual ferromagnetic damping 
alloys to drop rapidly past the damping peak. This additional damping 
mechanism is attributed to the alloy ’ s motion of the twin interface. 
The twin boundary is a special kind of low-energy coherent bound -
ary, which is relatively coherent and straight during bending vibration. 
Under alternating stress, the twin boundary can effectively absorb the 
vibration energy, thus improving the damping performance of the alloy 
[38 – 40] . According to Fig. 3 and Fig. 5 , it can be seen that the increase in 
annealing temperature directly leads to an increase in the number of 
twin boundaries within the alloy. After annealing at 1000
◦
C, many twin 
structures in parallel arrangement were found ( Figs. 5 ,c2). According to 
He et al. [41] , the magnitude of internal consumption is directly related 
to the movement of twins. As the annealing temperature increases, the 
number of twins inside the alloy increases and then decreases, and the 
damping properties thus also show an increase and then decrease. At 
1100
◦
C annealing, the excessive temperature leads to a sharp reduction 
in the internal annealing twin of the alloy, which manifests itself in a 
sharp decline in damping properties. This is another factor that leads to a 
sharp drop in damping properties during annealing at 1100
◦
C. 
The peak damping values Q
 1
max 
were 0.0595, 0.0643, 0.0701, 
0.0729, and 0.0546 for the as-cast state and after annealing at different 
temperatures, respectively (inset of Fig. 8 ). By comparing the damping 
internal consumption curves of annealed and as-cast specimens, it can be 
found that the damping performance of the annealed alloy is signifi -
cantly improved. The peak damping value of the cast alloy [20] is only 
0.0595, while the highest peak damping value after annealing is 0.0729, 
an increase of 22.5%. Combining the mechanical properties with the 
damping properties, the alloy can obtain the best combined 
strength-plasticity-damping properties when annealed at 1000
◦
C, thus 
achieving the design requirement of structural-functional integration. 
This paper provides a reference of experimental data for realizing 
structural-functional integration of high entropy damping alloys.

In this paper, Fe

high-entropy alloy was annealed at 
different temperatures, and a detailed discussion and analysis of the 
tissue evolution law, as well as the magnetic, mechanical, and damping 
properties of the annealed high-entropy alloy, was carried out. The 
specific conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The Fe

Cr
15
Co
20 
high-entropy alloy treated with different 
annealing temperatures still has a two-phase structure of γ 
austenite (FCC) + ε martensite (HCP). As the annealing temper -
ature increases, the FCC phase fraction gradually decreases, and 
the HCP phase fraction gradually increases, while the 
ε -martensite structure also gradually increases. With the increase 
of annealing temperature, the number of twin boundaries of 
Fe
45
Mn
20
Cr
15
Co
20 
high-entropy alloy showed a trend of first 
increasing and then decreasing. The peak was achieved at 
1000 ℃ annealing. And the alloy forms a regular arrangement of 
square lattice structure after annealing at 1000 ℃ . 
(2) With the increase of annealing temperature, the magnetic prop -
erties of the high-entropy alloys are enhanced. In terms of me -
chanical properties, the tensile strength of the alloy showed a 
trend of increasing and then decreasing, with a peak at 1000
◦
C. 
However, the elongation of the alloy shows a monotonically 
decreasing trend. 
(3) In terms of damping properties, the damping-strain curve of the 
alloy can be roughly divided into a low-strain region and a high- 
strain region. In the low-strain region, the damping increases 
rapidly with increasing strain; in the high-strain region, the 
damping decreases slowly with increasing strain but remains high 
at any level. The highest peak damping value (Q
 1 
= 0.0729) was 
achieved for the high-entropy alloy after annealing at 1000
◦
C. 
Compared with the as-cast alloy, the damping properties of the
