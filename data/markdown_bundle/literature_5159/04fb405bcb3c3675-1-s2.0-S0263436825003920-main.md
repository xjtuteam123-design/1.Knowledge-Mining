ABSTRACT
Ti-Zr-Mo (TZM) alloys inherently face a trade-off between high strength and adequate ductility, but targeted 
second-phase particle engineering can overcome this limitation. In this study, we demonstrate that optimizing 
the size, coherency of second-phase particles and impurity content yields an appreciable combination of strength 
and ductility in a TZM alloy. Micron-scale Zr-enriched particles act as potent nucleation sites for recrystallization 
through particle-stimulated nucleation (PSN), refining the grain structure to approximately 1.5 μ m. This refined 
grain structure substantially enhances alloy strength through the Hall-Petch mechanism. Simultaneously, opti -
mized impurity management at grain boundaries alleviates grain boundary embrittlement, thereby improving 
ductility. Concurrently, a dispersion of nanoscale precipitates with a semi-coherent interface to the matrix 
provides additional strengthening. Consequently, the alloy achieves a high tensile strength (939 MPa) coupled 
with promising tensile elongation (27.4 %). This represents an excellent improvement over conventional TZM 
alloys, underscoring that tailored second-phase particle architectures and impurity control can simultaneously 
increase strength and ductility. The findings provide a clear microstructural strategy for designing advanced TZM 
alloys with superior mechanical performance.
1. Introduction
Ti-Zi-Mo (TZM) alloy, a representative refractory material within the 
molybdenum-based alloy system has attracted considerable attention 
due to its excellent creep resistanece, high-temperature strength, and 
exceptional thermal stability [ 1 – 4 ]. These characteristics, combined 
with its ability to withstand severe thermal and mechanical stress, po -
sition TZM a material of choice across numerous critical realms, 
including aerospace engineering, nuclear fusion reactors, high- 
temperature industrial processes, advanced medical technologies and 
precision electronics manufacturing [ 5 – 13 ]. One of the traditional 
methods for fabricating TZM alloy is powder metallurgy (PM), which is 
cost-effective, minimizes material waste, and allows the production of 
complex shapes with fine-grained and uniform microstructures 
[ 4 , 10 , 14 , 15 ].
In TZM alloys, alloying elements such as Ti and Zr react with inter -
stitial elements to form second-phase particles. These particles purify 
the grain boundaries and effectively pin subgrain boundaries, thereby 
hindering dislocation motion and enhancing both the alloy ’ s mechanical 
strength and its structural stability at elevated temperatures [ 16 – 18 ]. 
However, in TZM alloys fabricated by conventional PM methods, 
second-phase particles are prone to agglomeration, leading to the for -
mation of coarse particles [ 19 – 21 ]. During deformation, local stress 
concentrations arising from coarse particles can facilitate crack initia -
tion at particle-matrix interfaces, ultimately leading to brittle fracture 
and a reduction in ductility [ 22 – 25 ]. Conversely, fine second-phase 
particles effectively mitigate these issues by minimizing stress concen -
tration and enabling grain refinement through grain boundary pinning, 
which enhances the strength of molybdenum alloys through the Hall- 
Petch relationship [ 26 , 27 ]. Moreover, finer grains can reduce the oxy -
gen concentration at grain boundaries in TZM alloys [ 27 – 29 ], thereby 
mitigating its detrimental effect on grain boundary cohesion and 
consequently enhancing the material ’ s elongation [ 27 , 30 ]. As a result, 
recent research on TZM alloys has primarily focused on the fabrication 
of fine second-phase particles. Majumdar et al. explored the densifica -
tion and grain growth behavior of mechanically alloyed Mo-TZM alloy

novel solid-liquid mixing method, which improved mixing uniformity, 
refined particle size, increased the recrystallization temperature, and 
effectively pinned dislocations [ 20 , 37 – 39 ]. The aforementioned studies 
have primarily aimed at refining the size of second-phase particles to 
enhance their grain boundary pinning effectiveness, thereby promoting 
grain refinement and improving the performance of TZM alloys. How -
ever, it is noteworthy that techniques such as ball milling or liquid-phase 
doping, commonly employed to produce fine second-phase particles in 
refractory alloys, often lead to elevated levels of impurity oxygen 
[ 15 , 40 – 43 ], which can adversely affect ductility. Therefore, improving 
elongation requires not only promoting the development of a fine- 
grained structure but also minimizing oxygen uptake during process -
ing, which effectively lowers oxygen concentration at grain boundaries. 
Although nanoscale second-phase particles are commonly employed to 
promote grain refinement, alternative mechanisms can also be effective. 
Coarse particles (micron scale) can induce fine recrystallized grains 
through PSN [ 44 – 48 ]. Therefore, TZM alloys containing coarse second- 
phase particles are also capable of developing fine-grained structures.
In addition to the size of the second-phase particles, their coherency 
relationship with the matrix also has a significant impact on the strength 
and ductility of the alloy [ 21 , 49 – 52 ]. However, the influence of second- 
phase particles with varying coherency relationships on the strength and 
ductility of TZM alloys remains unexplored in the existing literature. In 
this study, starting with an 8-h powder mixing process in a blender, the 
powder was further subjected to ball milling with increasing mechanical 
energy input. TZM alloy samples were then prepared using identical 
subsequent processing steps. Various second-phase particles were 
incorporated into the molybdenum matrix, resulting in a high-strength, 
fine-grained TZM alloy containing a combination of coarse and fine 
second-phase particles (CFP), as well as a high-ductility TZM alloy 
characterized by uniformly distributed fine second-phase particles (FP). 
By primarily comparing the microstructure and mechanical properties 
of the two alloys, this study offers new insights into the PSN mechanism 
driven by coarse second-phase particles, as well as the influence of 
second-phase particle-matrix interface relationships and impurity con -
trol on the strength-ductility trade-off in TZM alloys.

4. Discussion
In the subsequent sections, this study will mainly focus on the 
comparative analysis of microstructural and mechanical property dif -
ferences between CFP and FP alloys within the TZM alloys produced. 
This emphasis is motivated by the distinct contrast in their mechanical 
properties: CFP exhibits the highest tensile strength, whereas FP dem -
onstrates the greatest elongation, making them ideal for examining the 
relationship between microstructure and performance.
4.1. Evolution of second-phase particles

residual oxygen. Conversely, ZrH ₂ decomposes at a higher temperature

diffusivity in Mo (with a diffusion activation temperature exceeding

The difference in the second-phase composition can be attributed to 
the increased oxygen content during the powder preparation process, as 
illustrated in Fig. 11 (a), which reveals that as the mechanical energy of 
the ball milling process increases, the oxygen content in the alloy 
powders also increases. The reduction in particle size associated with 
increased milling energy generates a larger particle surface area, thereby 
facilitating greater oxygen adsorption. Although the ball milling process 
was carried out under an argon atmosphere, oxygen contamination 
inevitably occurred during following processing steps. Additionally, 
Fig. 11 (a) shows that the oxygen content in the forged TZM alloys is 
lower than in the alloy powders, as carbon (C) added in the alloys reacts 
with oxygen during high-temperature sintering to form CO gas, thereby 
reducing the oxygen content [ 60 – 62 ]. The higher oxygen content 
observed in forged CP compared to CFP can be attributed to the less 
uniform distribution of dopant particles within the Mo powder after 
mixing. In CFP, the more homogeneous dispersion of graphite particles 
in the matrix enhances their reactivity with oxygen, facilitating more 
effective oxygen removal and thus reducing the overall oxygen content.
During the sintering process, ZrO ₂ second-phase particles, which 
possess a lower Gibbs free energy, preferentially nucleate. However, 
once Zr is depleted, the remaining oxygen reacts with Ti to form TiO ₂ 
second-phase particles, as TiO ₂ has a lower Gibbs free energy than TiC 
[ 21 ]. Fine TiC particles, which exhibit a semi-coherent relationship with 
the matrix, can only precipitate when the oxygen content in the alloy is 
sufficiently low. In this study, the addition of 1000 ppm Zr into the alloy 
system, assuming complete reaction under ideal conditions, is capable of 
binding approximately 350 ppm O to form ZrO ₂ particles at grain

In the early stages of precipitation, second-phase particles are rela -

matrix. As these precipitates grow, the strain energy accumulated due to 
lattice mismatch increases. To alleviate this strain energy, some initially 
coherent interfaces may transition into semi-coherent interfaces by 
forming dislocation arrays [ 63 ]. In both CFP and FP alloys, the Ti- 
enriched second-phase particles precipitate from the matrix, implying 
that they maintain either a semi-coherent or coherent interface with the 
Mo matrix. In terms of Zr-enriched second-phase particles, the large 
ZrO ₂ particles in FP exhibit a monoclinic crystal structure at RT, which 
differs significantly from the body-centered cubic (BCC) structure of Mo. 
This substantial lattice mismatch results in a relatively large misfit be -
tween ZrO ₂ and the Mo matrix, leading to the formation of a non- 
coherent interface. The coherency relationship between various 
second-phase particles and the matrix plays a crucial role in determining 
the alloy ’ s mechanical properties. This influence will be analyzed in 
detail in the third section.
4.2. Effect of second-phase particles on recrystallization

increasing temperature, making variations in their microstructure pri -
marily driven by changes in grain size and second-phase particles. These 
factors exert a significant influence on the overall performance of the 
alloy. This section specifically focuses on the differences in grain size 
between the CFP and FP alloys, and explores the role of second-phase 
particles in influencing the alloy ’ s recrystallization behavior.

In addition to refining the size of second-phase particles, controlling 
the concentration of oxygen impurities at grain boundaries in TZM al -
loys can also improve elongation. Impurity elements primarily affect the 
interfacial bonding strength at grain boundaries through grain boundary 
segregation, thereby influencing the ductility of Mo alloys [ 73 , 74 ]. 
Specifically, an increase in oxygen concentration at Mo grain boundaries 
reduces bonding energy, promoting brittleness and diminishing elon -
gation [ 30 ]. In the strength and elongation curves presented in Fig. 8 (f), 
the decrease in tensile strength with increasing annealing temperature 
follows the expected trend of reduced grain refinement strengthening 
due to grain coarsening. However, the unexpected decrease in elonga -
tion observed in CP, MP and FP alloys along with the strength can be 
attributed to the increase in oxygen content. Grain coarsening results in 
elevated oxygen concentrations at the grain boundaries, leading to 
embrittlement of the boundaries and promoting brittle fracture, thereby 
reducing elongation. The relatively small decrease in elongation for FP is 
due to the fact that it did not experience significant grain coarsening

lower compared to CP and MP alloys. In contrast, the elongation of CFP 
does not decrease at these two annealing temperatures, as its lowest 
oxygen content prevents significant grain boundary embrittlement. 
Although the coarse second-phase particles in CFP negatively impact 
elongation, they actively react with oxygen at grain boundaries, effec -

to further diluting residual oxygen concentration at the grain bound -
aries. Consequently, CFP maintains relatively high elongation while 
exhibiting appreciable tensile strength, achieving a synergistic 
enhancement in both strength and ductility.
5. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that optimizing second-phase particle size, 
coherency, and impurity control in TZM alloys enables a notable 
improvement in the strength-ductility synergy. The findings reveal that: 
1. Micron-scale Zr-enriched particles promote recrystallization via PSN 
in CFP, refining grains and enhancing strength through the Hall- 
Petch mechanism. Simultaneously, semi-coherent nanoscale TiC 
precipitates provide additional dislocation pinning, contributing to a 
tensile strength of 939 MPa.
2. Grain refinement combined with reduced oxygen uptake during 
processing effectively mitigates the oxygen concentration at grain 
boundaries, thereby enhancing intergranular cohesion and 
improving the alloy ’ s elongation. Consequently, the CFP alloy ach -
ieves a total elongation of 27.4 %.
3. The results underscore that beyond grain refinement, second-phase 
particle engineering, particularly in terms of coherency and impu -
rity control, plays an essential role in optimizing mechanical prop -
erties. This study proposes a novel microstructural strategy for 
developing high-performance TZM alloys with enhanced strength
