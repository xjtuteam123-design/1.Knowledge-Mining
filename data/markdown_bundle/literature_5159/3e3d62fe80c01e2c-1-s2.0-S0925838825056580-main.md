1. Introduction
The low density, high strength, and exceptional coarsening resis -
tance of aluminum alloys make them crucial for automotive and aero -
space industries [1,2] . These alloys are frequently used in load-bearing 
applications operating between 200

Fe-, Ni-, and Ti-based alloys [3,4] . Casting high-strength aluminum al -
loys at elevated temperatures mainly relies on two strengthening 
mechanisms [5] . One type of strengthening mechanism relies on a high 
density of coherent or semi-coherent nanoprecipitates formed via solu -
tion treatment and aging (e.g., L1

dense distributions strongly resist dislocation motion [9] . Therefore, the 
solute atoms forming the nanoprecipitates must have high solubility and 
rapid diffusivity in the aluminum matrix (e.g., Cu, Zn, Mg, Si) [10] . 
However, such nanoprecipitates (e.g., θ ʹ -Al
2
Cu in Al-Cu alloys) coarsen 
rapidly above ~250
◦

thereby limiting the application of traditional aluminum alloys at 
medium-to-high temperatures [12] . Solute atoms with low solubility 
and slow diffusivity (e.g., Sc, Zr, Er, Mn) effectively suppress 
nanoprecipitate coarsening in aluminum alloys. Gao et al. [13] observed 
that Sc addition to Al-Cu alloys promotes Sc segregation at α -Al/ θ ʹ 
interface during aging at 300
◦
C, stabilizing θ ʹ phase dimensions and 
delaying coarsening. Similarly, trace Mn and Zr additions segregate to 
α -Al/ θ ʹ interfaces, significantly inhibiting precipitate coarsening [14] . 
Notably, Mn segregates first to retard coarsening, enabling Zr sufficient 
time to subsequently segregate and further stabilize the precipitates 
[14] .
Another type primarily relies on the strengthening of micron-sized 
intermetallics (IMCs) formed during eutectic solidification reactions, 
with a high-volume-fraction [15] . Unlike L1

Based on the abovementioned background, in this work, ternary Al- 
7Ce-xCu alloys were prepared in as-cast (AC) and T6-treated (T6) con -
ditions. The microstructural evolution behaviors were characterized in 
detail. A proper Cu content was confirmed to be 20 wt% in this system, 
since the eutectics have completely connected with each other, sur -
rounding the soft α -Al matrix. Then, trace additions of Mn and Zr were 
introduced to the Al-7Ce-20Cu alloy to assess their effects on micro -
structure and room- and elevated-temperature properties. The micro -
structure characteristic, strengthening mechanism, and thermal stability 
of the alloys were discussed.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials fabrication
Alloys with compositions of Al-7Ce-xCu (x = 14, 16, 18, 20), named 
as 14Cu, 16Cu, 18Cu, and 20Cu, and Al-7Ce-20Cu-0.5Mn-0.3Zr (named 
as 20CuMnZr) were prepared in this study by using commercial pure Al 
(99.99 %), Al-30Ce, Al-50Cu, Al-10Mn and Al-5Zr as master alloys, 
which were provided by Shandong Al & Mg Melt Technology Co., Ltd . 
Pure Al and the master alloys (Al-Ce, Al-Cu, Al-Zr) were placed into 
clean graphite crucibles firstly and melted at 780
◦
C for 3 h by a resis -
tance furnace. The Al-Mn alloy was finally added and melted at 750
◦
C 
for only 0.5 h to minimize Mn evaporation. Subsequently, C
2
Cl
6 
was 
used for slag removal and melt degassing, and 0.5 % Al-5Ti-1B was 
added for α -Al grain refinement. The melt was stirred regularly to ensure 
homogeneity, maintained at 750
◦
C for 15 min and finally cast into 
copper mold (20 mm in length, 10 mm in width, 70 mm in height). After 
solidification, the ingots were immediately quenched by ice water.
Samples were then taken from the ingots to analyze the micro -
structure and mechanical properties of the alloys in the AC and T6 
conditions. The Al-Ce-Cu alloys were solution-treated at 520
◦
C for 12 h, 
followed by immediate quenching in water and subsequent aged at 
185
◦
C for 24 h. Due to the addition of Zr, the 20CuMnZr alloy required 
a modified heat treatment procedure. It is reported that Zr is prone to 
micro-segregation during solidification, leading to localized composi -
tional variations [36] . To mitigate this, the samples were first subjected 
to an isothermal heat treatment at 400
◦
C for 10 h. The samples were 
then solution-treated at 520
◦
C for 12 h, water quenched, and next 
artificially aged at 185
◦
C for times ranging from 0 to 112 h.
2.2. Microstructure characterization
Microstructural analysis was performed using field emission scan -
ning electron microscopy (FESEM, SU-70; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) 
equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS). Phase 
analysis was conducted by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/max-rB, 
Tokyo, Japan) with Cu K α radiation under the following conditions: 
40 kV operating voltage, 100 mA current, 0.2
◦
/min scanning speed, and 
10
◦
– 90
◦
scanning range. Microstructural details and precipitated parti -
cles after aging were examined using transmission electron microscope 
(TEM, FEI Talos F200X) at 200 kV accelerating voltage, equipped with 
EDS and selected area electron diffraction (SAED). For TEM analysis, 
alloy samples were mechanically milled to approximately 70 μ m 
thickness, punched into 3 mm diameter discs using a punch, and thinned 
with Precision Ion Polishing System (Gatan 695, Pleasanton, CA, USA) 
until perforation occurred. The volume fraction of primary phases, 
eutectic phase width, eutectic spacing and size statistics of nano -
precipitates in Al-Ce-Cu (-Mn-Zr) alloys were measured using SEM and 
TEM images in ImageJ software, respectively.
2.3. Mechanical properties test
Vickers microhardness was measured at RT using an HVS-1000AT/ 
EOS100B Automated Microhardness tester (Hua Yu Zhong Xin Co., 
Ltd, Laizhou, China) with a 500 g load applied for 5 s. Elevated-

4.3. Elevated temperature performance
As discussion above, the RT strength of the alloy arises from a 
combination of GBs strengthening, solid solution strengthening, and 
second-phase strengthening. However, these strengthening mechanisms 
may weaken at elevated temperatures. To more intuitively demonstrate 
thermal stability of the alloy at elevated temperature, isothermal aging 
experiments were conducted at 300
◦
C on T6 20Cu and PA 20CuMnZr 
alloys. The evolution of the Vickers hardness data was analyzed to 
investigate changes in the strengthening mechanisms at elevated tem -
perature, as shown in Fig. 14 . The hardness of all alloys exhibited an 
overall decreasing trend throughout the aging. The initial decrease was 
rapid, with the rate of decrease gradually slowing after 10 h. The known 
precipitation sequence in Al-Cu alloys is: supersaturated solid solution 
→ G.P. I zone → θ ʹ ʹ → θ ʹ → θ [39] . The θ ʹ ʹ nanoprecipitate possesses a 
fully coherent crystal structure with the α -Al matrix. As aging time in -
creases, it gradually transforms into the θ ʹ , which is semi-coherent with 
the α -Al matrix. Both the θ ʹ ʹ and θ ʹ are primary strengthening phases in 
Al-Cu-based alloys [74,75] . When the temperature is elevated and 
maintained above 250
◦
C, the θ ʹ rapidly transforms into the incoherent 
equilibrium θ , leading to a sharp decrease in the Orowan strengthening 
[76,77] . This is confirmed by the rapid hardness decrease observed 
within 10 h for T6 20Cu and PA 20CuMnZr alloys ( Fig. 14 ).
It is well known that the GBs strengthening effect diminishes 
significantly at elevated temperatures (~0.5T

because GB sliding and crystal rotation at elevated temperatures reduce 
the GBs ’ ability to hinder dislocation motion [78] . Introducing IMCs
