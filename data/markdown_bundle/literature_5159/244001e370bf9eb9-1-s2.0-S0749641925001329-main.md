ABSTRACT
The remarkable mechanical properties of high-entropy alloys at room and cryogenic temperatures 
have garnered significant attention in recent years. However, their poor mechanical performance 
at intermediate temperatures has hindered their practical application in many contexts. This 
study examines the effect of Ti addition on the intermediate-temperature tensile properties of 
additively manufactured AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ eutectic high-entropy alloys. The findings demonstrate 
that Ti addition improves the alloy ’ s tensile properties through several vital mechanisms. Ti 
addition significantly increases back-stress, which dominates strain-hardening behavior. At 400 
◦
C, Ti addition promotes the formation of chemically long-range ordered and spinodal decom -
position, facilitating multi-mode dislocation behavior characterized by coexisting planar and 
wavy slip. This enhances work-hardening, thereby achieving improved strength-ductility synergy. 
At 600 
◦
C, the long-range ordered and spinodal decomposition evolved into nanoscale D0
3 
pre -
cipitates that allow dislocation pinning and contribute to high strength while preserving good 
ductility. Moreover, Ti addition induces a rounded dual-phase microstructure, where the face- 
centered cubic phase serves as an adhesive layer, preventing crack propagation along the 
phase boundary. These mechanisms synergistically enhance strength and ductility at intermediate 
temperatures, making Ti-modified AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ high-entropy alloys highly suitable for appli -
cations in the 400 – 600 
◦
C temperature range.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are promising structural materials due to their exceptional mechanical properties, thermal stability, 
and oxidation resistance at room and cryogenic temperatures ( Chen et al., 2024 ). The AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ eutectic HEA (EHEA), first 
designed in 2014 ( Lu et al., 2015 ), is particularly notable for its superior balance of strength and ductility, stemming from the 
coexistence of multiple phases and eutectic microstructures ( Guo et al., 2024 ; Han et al., 2020 ; Jaladurgam et al., 2021 ). This alloy

growth along the [010] direction under K-S orientation relationships ( Tateyama et al., 2010 ), exacerbating BCC/FCC lattice mismatch

Modulating chemical ordering in HEAs is an effective strategy for enhancing their performance at elevated temperatures. By 
controlling atomic arrangements locally, chemical short-range order (SRO) and long-range order (LRO), spinodal decomposition, and 
precipitation could occur, significantly affecting phase stability and mechanical properties by influencing atomic interactions and 
diffusion pathways ( Shuang et al., 2024 ; Xia et al., 2023 ). SRO in CrCoNi HEAs promotes localized planar dislocation configurations 
and restricts cross-slip, improving toughness ( Zhang et al., 2020 ). In FCC (CrCoNi)

Spinodal decomposition in alloy design enables spontaneous phase separation without nucleation, enhancing high-temperature 
HEA performance. The addition of Al into the FeCoNiCr HEA results in a low-mismatch coherent nanoscale spinodal decomposition 
structure, which enhances the stability and elasticity of the lattice, thereby effectively impeding dislocation motion ( Dong et al., 2024 ). 
Chemical composition fluctuations mitigate strain localization, preserving strength and ductility under high-temperature tensile stress 
( Park et al., 2024 ).
Further increasing the structural ordering, i.e., forming nanoprecipitates ( Cao et al., 2020 ; Qin et al., 2024 ), can significantly 
improve phase stability ( Jia et al., 2025 ) and mitigate thermal cracking, thereby reducing the likelihood of defects compromising 
( Zhang et al., 2024 ) mechanical properties and enhancing yield stress (YS) at intermediate temperatures ( Feng et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 
2023 ). The introduction of stable nanoscale precipitates, e.g., nano-L12 precipitates into NiCoCrAl HEA, improves tensile performance 
at 400 – 600 
◦
C through dislocation interactions and Lomer-Cottrell lock formation ( Gao et al., 2024 ). Furthermore, controlled pre -
cipitate phase formation can suppress detrimental phases ( Jiang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ), improving tensile performance in HEAs 
( Liu et al., 2023 ). Optimized precipitate size and distribution minimize harmful grain boundary γ’ precipitates, suppressing crack 
propagation and enhancing tensile properties in the intermediate temperature range ( Cao et al., 2020 ). These advances highlight the 
importance of alloy design and precipitation hardening for elevated-temperature HEA applications.
However, precise control of multiscale chemically ordered microstructures in HEAs remains challenging. In recent years, laser 
additive manufacturing (LAM) has emerged as a promising approach for fabricating high-performance HEAs ( Gu et al., 2021 ), offering 
advantages over conventional metallurgy through process optimization and control of key parameters such as cooling rates and 
thermal inputs ( Q. Liu et al., 2024 ; Sun et al., 2020 ). For example, LAM ’ s rapid cooling capabilities mitigate compositional segregation 
and promote precipitation ( Lin et al., 2024 ), creating hypoeutectic AlCo ₀ . ₄ CrFeNi ₂ . ₇ HEA with enhanced tensile properties ( Song et al., 
2024 ). Further advancements include the optimized thermal gradients facilitating hierarchical precipitation in CoNiCrFeAlTi HEA, 
improving precipitate distribution, and impeding dislocation motion ( Xiao et al., 2023 ). Additionally, LAM ’ s rapid cooling suppresses 
intermetallic compound formation, enabling multiple strengthening mechanisms that enhance both strength and ductility ( Dan et al., 
2024 ). This precision provides a practical pathway for developing multiscale chemically ordered structures and high-performance 
alloys with tailored properties.
This study explores the hypothesis that engineered microstructural heterogeneity with optimized phase distribution can overcome 
the intermediate-temperature weakness in HEAs. Specifically, it is proposed that a rounded dual-phase architecture combined with 
nanoscale precipitation should enable simultaneous strengthening and strain accommodation when the strength difference between 
phases is carefully controlled. To test this hypothesis, Ti was incorporated into the AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ EHEA using laser energy net shaping 
and investigated the chemical ordering heterogeneity, microstructure, in-situ precipitation, and underlying mechanisms at elevated 
temperatures. The results indicate that the addition of 2.5 at. % Ti increases the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) by 25 % at 400 
◦
C and 
significantly improves total elongation (TE) by 560 % at 600 
◦
C. This enhancement stems from a synergistic combination: (1) Ti- 
induced chemical ordering heterogeneity that promotes multi-mode dislocation behavior, (2) nanoscale D0 ₃ precipitates that pro -
vide effective dislocation pinning for hardening, and (3) a rounded dual-phase microstructure that homogenizes the stress field while 
hindering crack formation and propagation. These findings provide valuable insights into microstructure-property relationships in 
complex alloys and establish design principles for developing HEAs with superior performance at intermediate temperatures.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Sample preparations of AlCoCrFeNi
2.1
-Tix HEAs
In this study, Ti powder was added to pre-alloyed AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ EHEA powder to form an AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ -Ti0.15 composition by 
adding 2.5 at % Ti powder (hereafter referred to as EHEA-Ti). Both EHEA and EHEA-Ti specimens were fabricated via Laser Engineered 
Net Shaping (LENS, MR-7, Optomec) with a 2 mm laser beam in an argon environment ( < 100 ppm oxygen). Fig. 1 a shows the LENS 
fabrication process. A 316 L stainless steel substrate without preheating was used. The laser melt pool is shown in yellow/white to 
indicate high temperature, with the solidifying material transitioning from orange to red as it cools. The pre-alloyed EHEA and me -
chanically mixed EHEA-Ti powders shown in Fig. 1 b, were used in the experiment. Gas-atomized AlCoCrFeNi ₂ . ₁ powder (45 – 105 μ m) 
was mixed with high-purity Ti powder ( > 99.9 wt %, 45 – 105 μ m) using a 3D Turbula mixer for 2 h. Samples (35 mm × 15 mm × 5 mm) 
were built layer-by-layer with 90
◦
alternating scan directions and the location of the cutting samples ( Fig. 1 c), using 400 W laser power 
and 6 mm/s scanning speed. The laser nozzle was raised 0.3 mm after each layer. ThermoCalc ™ calculations determined that 2.5 at % 
Ti represents the critical threshold for precipitate formation at 600 
◦
C ( Fig. 1 d). Completed specimens were separated 2.5 mm above 
the substrate via wire-cut electrical discharge machining (EDM). Every sample could only get one slice.
2.2. Microstructural characterizations
The phase composition of the fabricated parts was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku SmartLab 9 kW diffrac -
tometer with Cu-K α radiation (step-scan mode, 0.02
◦
step size). Microstructures and elemental distributions were characterized by 
(scanning) transmission electron microscopy ((S)TEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using a Talos F200X 
G2 microscope operated at 200 kV. Plane-view TEM specimens were prepared by mechanical polishing to predetermined depths. 
Electron-transparent TEM foils (~25 μ m thickness) were obtained through bilateral mechanical polishing and final thinning using a 
Gatan 695 precision ion polishing system. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analyses, conducted with an Oxford C Symmetry S2 
system at 20 kV acceleration voltage in an Apreo 2S field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM), examined the microstructural 
evolution induced by Ti additions. EBSD samples were mechanically ground using 2000-grit SiC paper, polished with a 0.02 μ m SiO ₂ 
suspension, and electrolytically polished in a 10 % perchloric acid and 90 % ethanol solution at room temperature. XRD, EBSD, and 
SEM analyses were performed on undeformed clamping regions away from deformation zones. The unstrained specimens for char -
acterization were extracted from the upper shoulder (grip end) of the same tensile specimens after tension, as shown in Fig. 1 c. 
Specimens tested along the building direction corresponded to the Z-axis of the LENS-fabricated samples, perpendicular to the 
deposition plane.
2.3. Mechanical measurements
All dog ‑ bone tensile samples (10 mm gauge length, 2 mm width, and 2 mm thickness) were then EDM ‑ cut from this same slice to 
ensure a consistent thermal history. The tensile direction was oriented perpendicular to the building direction. Tensile tests were 
conducted at 400 
◦
C and 600 
◦
C using a Zwick/Roell Z020 TEW tensile machine equipped with a non-contact video extensometer at a 
strain rate of 10 ⁻ ³ s ⁻ ¹. The tensile specimens were suspended by the fixture geometry — no direct clamping or pressure was applied to 
the upper shoulder grip end region of the dog-bone tensile samples — so that region experienced no significant plastic deformation and 
served as the unstrained reference, as shown in Fig. 1 c. Specimens were heated at a rate of 10 
◦
C/min to the target temperatures, held 
for 30 min in the air to ensure uniform temperature distribution, and subsequently tested. After testing, samples were water quenched. 
Each test condition was repeated three times for reproducibility.

3.1. Mechanical properties of as-fabricated AlCoCrFeNi
2.1
-Tix HEAs at intermediate temperatures
The typical tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the LENS-processed samples are presented in Fig. 2 a-b. All the tensile en -
gineering stress-strain curves of the LENS-processed samples are shown in Fig. S2a, with statistical analyses of UTS, YS, TE, and the 
product of UTS and TE (UTS × TE) presented in Fig. S2b-c.
Prior to examining the intermediate temperature performance, the room temperature mechanical properties of both EHEA and 
EHEA-Ti alloys were evaluated, as detailed in Fig. S1. At room temperature, EHEA-Ti demonstrates a UTS and YS of 1495 MPa and 
1173 MPa with a tensile elongation of 10 %, representing a 25 % improvement in strength compared to the base EHEA while 
maintaining comparable ductility.
Compared to EHEA, the EHEA-Ti alloy exhibits significantly improved strength and ductility across the intermediate temperature 
range (400 – 600 
◦
C). At 400 
◦
C, the EHEA achieves a UTS of approximately 1085 ± 3.2 MPa, a YS of 794 ± 7.0 MPa, and a TE of 13.0 ±
1.52 %. In contrast, the EHEA-Ti alloy demonstrates a UTS of 1355 ± 41.4 MPa and a YS of 886 ± 98.6 MPa while maintaining a 
comparable TE of 10.2 ± 1.687 %. At 600 
◦
C, EHEA undergoes severe embrittlement, with a drastic drop in UTS (765 ± 8.3 MPa), YS 
(676 ± 10.11 MPa), and TE (2.69 ± 0.19 %). However, the EHEA-Ti retains superior mechanical properties, achieving a UTS of 916 
± 32.1 MPa, YS of 815 ± 27 MPa, and a ductility of 17.71 ± 2.3 %, which is > 5 times greater than the EHEA. Additionally, UTS × TE, a 
critical metric for evaluating the strength-ductility synergy and energy absorption, shows a substantial enhancement with Ti addition. 
As shown in Fig. S2c, from 400 
◦
C to 600 
◦
C, the toughness of the EHEA drops significantly from 14.11 ± 1.2 GPa % to 2.05 ± 0.2 GPa %, 
whereas the EHEA-Ti increases from 13.8 ± 1.6 GPa % to 16.3 ± 2.3 GPa %. These results demonstrate that adding Ti significantly 
enhances the mechanical performance of EHEA at intermediate temperatures. Fig. 2 c compares the intermediate-temperature me -
chanical properties of the EHEA-Ti alloy developed in this study with other high-strength HEAs fabricated through various methods. 
The results indicate that the EHEA-Ti alloy exhibits superior UTS compared to other alloys within the 400 to 600 
◦
C temperature range. 
BCC-based HEAs have traditionally been regarded as promising candidates for replacing nickel-based superalloys for high-temperature 
applications. However, while many BCC-based HEAs demonstrate high ductility at intermediate temperatures, this is often achieved at 
the expense of reduced strength. In contrast, the EHEA-Ti alloy produced using LENS exhibits exceptional strength at intermediate 
temperatures, providing a viable alternative in applications where strength is critical.

4.3. Deformation microstructure evolution at intermediate temperature
Fig. 10 presents a TEM analysis of deformed EHEA and EHEA-Ti samples at 400 
◦
C, revealing distinct deformation mechanisms. 
Planar slip involves the dislocation movement along specific crystallographic planes in an organized manner, forming thin, closely 
spaced slip bands. While these planar slip bands can concentrate stress at interfaces — potentially nucleating cracks — they also 
effectively trap and multiply dislocations, increasing dislocation storage capacity ( Sudmanns et al., 2019 ). While in EHEA-Ti alloy, the 
B2 phase adopts a curved, petal-like morphology, these arched boundaries deflect the slip bands, homogenize the local stress field, and 
markedly reduce such stress concentrations ( Huang et al., 2025 ). Wavy slip, in contrast, involves dislocations following irregular, 
curved paths due to local stress variations, which can lead to dislocation entanglement but may also enable dislocations to navigate 
complex stress fields ( Pham and Holdsworth, 2014 ). In Fig. 10 a, the EHEA sample predominantly exhibits wavy slip characteristics by 
curved dislocation morphology as observed in Bright-field TEM, which only yields moderate hardening ( Δ σ ≈ 210 MPa). The planar 
slip contributes to strengthening through enhanced dislocation storage ( Sudmanns et al., 2019 ) and thus enhances the 
work-hardening. A combination of planar and wavy slip is observed in the EHEA-Ti sample ( Fig. 10 b-c), which synergistically con -
tributes to improved strain-hardening via dislocation entangling and storing, as proved in previous works ( Ma, 2020 ; Otto et al., 2013 ). 
Therefore, the work-hardening degree Δ σ ( = UTS-YS) ≈ 410 MPa in EHEA-Ti, doubling 210 MPa in EHEA, which boosts UTS while 
uniform elongation is preserved. Previous research ( Chen et al., 2021 ) suggests that LRO structures in low-energy facilitate planar slip 
by acting as traps in the potential dislocation distribution ( Wang et al., 2022 ). The planar slip is attributed to LRO structures within the 
B2 phase, as confirmed by HRTEM imaging along the [011] B2 orientation ( Fig. 10 d). The FFT and IFFT patterns in Fig. 10 e-f reveal 
LRO diffraction disks clustering near the projection spot center, consistent with observations in the undeformed EHEA-Ti sample 
( Fig. 5 g ).
Also, dislocation pinning is primarily observed at the B2/FCC phase boundaries, where the phase interface restricts dislocation 
mobility. This effect, indicated in Fig. 10 b (white fillet frame), plays a critical role in HDI strengthening. Together, the synergetic 
mechanisms — LRO-driven dual-mode slip causing enhanced dislocation interaction probability and heterostructure-driven back-stress 
hardening — elevate the work-hardening capacity of EHEA-Ti — enabling superior high-temperature performance compared to the 
purely wavy-slip EHEA alloy.

5. Conclusions
Our findings demonstrate that integrating compositional and morphological design within LAM fabrication facilitates the devel -
opment of high-performance HEAs with improved strength and ductility over a wide temperature range. The evolution of multiscale 
chemical ordering, microstructural heterogeneity, and in-situ precipitation was examined, establishing a link between these changes 
and mechanical responses associated with increasing Ti content at intermediate temperatures (400 and 600 
◦
C). The main conclusions 
are as follows: 
1. The incorporation of Ti significantly enhances the intermediate-temperature strength-ductility synergy of EHEA-Ti. At 400 
◦
C, the 
LENS-processed EHEA-Ti achieves a UTS of 1355 MPa, YS of 886 MPa, and TE of 10 %, reflecting a 25 % increase in UTS compared 
to the EHEA. At 600 
◦
C, Ti addition results in an impressive elongation of 17.7 %, marking a 560 % improvement in TE. The 
toughness metric (UTS × TE) product increases from 2.31 GPa % (EHEA) to 17.7 GPa % (EHEA-Ti).
2. Ti addition induced the formation of long-range ordered structures, effectively stabilizing heterogeneous microstructures. These 
structurally distinct regions facilitated hetero-deformation and elevated back stress. Moreover, the chemically ordered structures at 
intermediate temperatures significantly impeded dislocation motion, increasing effective stress and markedly enhancing strain- 
hardening behavior.
3. Ti addition effectively enhances B2 stability and reduces FCC/B2 lattice mismatch at intermediate temperatures. The reduced 
lattice mismatch enhances interfacial strain compatibility, avoiding stress localization, thus improving ductility.
4. For the EHEA-Ti alloy, Ti addition promotes the formation of spinodal structure, LRO, and nanoscale D0 ₃ precipitates across a wide 
temperature range. The transition from LRO to D0 ₃ precipitates generates chemical ordering heterogeneity that impedes dislo -
cation motion. This promotes mixed dislocation, multiplication, and tangles, thereby enhancing work-hardening capacity and 
strength. Additionally, the stabilized B2 phase contributes to improved fracture resistance by facilitating crack deflection and 
branching through a rounded dual-phase microstructure.
