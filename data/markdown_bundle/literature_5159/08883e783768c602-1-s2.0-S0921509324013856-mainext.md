ABSTRACT
In this study, molybdenum alloy with the nanoscale second phase dispersion distribution (NM-TZM) was pre -
pared by powder metallurgy. Despite maintaining a high yield strength of 893 MPa, the elongation of the as- 
forged MN-TZM alloy has been increased to 25.3 %. In addition, the recrystallization start temperature of 
NM-TZM alloy was increased by 100 
◦
C, reaching 1400 
◦
C. The nanoindentation results indicate that the NM- 
TZM alloy still exhibits a high hardness of 4.40 GPa following annealing at 1400 
◦
C. The addition of nano -
scale second phase can improve the ductility and high temperature stability of the alloy by coupling with dis -
locations and grain boundaries. A new model for the synergistic effect of grain boundaries and intragrains to 
improve ductility is proposed, which provides insight into the reason behind the high ductility of NM-TZM.
1. Introduction
Molybdenum (Mo), as a refractory metal, with the melting point of 
2610 
◦
C, which can stably maintain the body-centered cubic (BCC) 
without phase transition [ 1 – 3 ]. Therefore, Mo and Mo alloys are 
frequently served as candidates for crucial high-temperature applica -
tions, such as fusion reactor components, missiles, turbines and rocket 
nozzles, etc., due to their advantages of excellent high-temperature 
strength, thermal stability, creep resistance and high thermal conduc -
tivity [ 4 ]. However, the poor ductility of Mo due to ductile-brittle 
transition (DBT) at room temperature, which makes the material diffi -
cult to process, greatly limits its application field. Furthermore, the 
recrystallization embrittlement of molybdenum also represents a 
pressing engineering challenge that requires immediate attention. 
Generally, the responsible for low ductility of Mo can be considered for 
two reasons. Firstly, with the structural features of molybdenum, the 
deformation mode of BCC structure principally via the screw disloca -
tions gliding, which, usually, are considerably difficult to activate below 
the DBT temperature [ 5 – 7 ]. Secondly, some impurity solid solution 
elements, particularly O and N, are easy to segregate to the grain 
boundaries (GBs) and thus weakening their strength and resulting in 
brittle fracture [ 8 – 11 ]. Therefore, the improvement of room tempera -
ture ductility and thermal stability of molybdenum alloys has been a 
major research goal in this field for the last half century.
For this purpose, researchers have proposed a variety of strategies, 
including solid solution strengthening, second-phase dispersion 
strengthening, fine grain strengthening, etc [ 12 – 15 ]. The addition of the 
rare earth element rhenium (Re) to molybdenum can reduce the acti -
vation energy of the dislocations while forming solid solution 
strengthening, thereby improving the strength and ductility of molyb -
denum alloys (called solute softening). Nevertheless, the high cost of Re 
precludes the possibility of universal application for Mo-Re alloys. The 
abundant GBs originated from severe plastic deformation can reduce the 
O content per unit area of GBs, and improve the ductility due to grain 
refinement. However, the increase in the number of GBs can cause 
thermal instability of the microstructure [ 16 , 17 ]. The incorporation of 
dispersed second-phase (SP), such as oxides (e.g., La
2
O
3
) or carbides (e. 
g., ZrC), within the alloy can effectively pinning dislocations and GBs,

The Titnaium-Zirconium-Molybdenium (TZM) alloys are solid solu -
tion strengthened alloys in which small amounts of Ti and Zr elements 
are dissolved in the Mo matrix, thereby strengthening pure Mo. In 
addition, Ti and Zr elements will react with C or impurity O to form 
carbides and oxide particles as SP. However, as one of the most widely 
used Mo alloys, the endogenous SP of TZM alloy often shows serious 
agglomeration. For the strengthening and ductility mechanism of mo -
lybdenum alloy, the particle size of the SP is a vitally important technical 
indicator. The control of large-sized particles agglomerated at GBs has 
been an engineering problem to be solved in the field of molybdenum 
alloys. For this reason, Hu Ping et al. added nano-La

ductility [ 24 ]. However, the size and distribution of the SP, intrinsic to

modulated by the extra addition of nano La

necessary to optimize the size and distribution of the endogenous SP for 
conventional TZM alloys. At present, there are few reports on research in 
this field.
In this paper, nano-scale second phase TZM (NM-TZM) alloy rods 
with superior thermal stability and ductility were produced by high- 
energy ball milling combined with ultrasonic dispersion process. The 
potential mechanism of nano-scale second phase to enhance the 
ductility and thermal stability of NM-TZM alloys was investigated.

2.1. Material preparation
In this work, two type of TZM alloy prepared by powder metallurgy

the mixed powder was subjected to a 2 h ball milling process again. In 
order to ensure the accuracy of the experiment, the subsequent pro -

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure of forged alloy
The contrast information of ECCI images is sensitive to localized 
variations in the crystal structure, which can reveal the substructure of 
deformed grains. Grains with strongly localized misorientation show a 
speckled appearance (deformed grains), while grains with low misori -
entation exhibit a uniform contrast (dynamically recovered or recrys -
tallized grains). The ECCI images ( Fig. 2 (a) – (f)) show the typical 
microstructure of forged molybdenum alloy bars, i.e., the transverse and 
longitudinal sections consist of equiaxed grains and elongated grains, 
respectively, which contain a well-developed sub-grain structure. Nor -
mally, the transverse structure would be desired to form uniformly fine 
equiaxed grains during hot forging. The grain structure of NM-TZM 
appeared to satisfy the expected results ( Fig. 2 (a)); while the grain 
shape of commercial TZM was distorted into an irregular shape ( Fig. 2
(d)). Attractively, the curved shaped grains exhibit quite coarse grain 
size characteristics. The formation of this microstructure is presumably 
associated with non-uniform deformation due to the uniaxial deforma -
tion mode of the BCC metal, leading to a synergistic effect between 
adjacent grains to curve the grains. The specific microstructure was 
previously reported for wires of BCC metals (e.g., W, Fe, and Nb) [ 25 ]. In 
addition, the contrast of the curved grains shows strong variability, 
indicating the presence of abundant sub-grain structures confined by 
low angle grain boundary (LAGBs), which causes deterioration in the 
thermal stability of the alloy. This is consistent with the grain details 
shown in Fig. 2 (e).
NM-TZM shows relatively finer grain size and homogeneous grain 
shape, which contributes to the overall performance of the alloy. 
Furthermore, the significant differences in longitudinal structural 
characteristics are discerned between NM-TZM and commercial TZM 
alloys. The longitudinal section microstructure of commercial TZM alloy 
is a sub-grain structure formed after multilateralization. However, the 
traces of NM-TZM multilateralization are not strong. As opposed to 
commercial TZM alloys, some of the grains of forged NM-TZM appear to

In contrast with the detrimental effect of large-sized SP, the nano-

their interactions with dislocations. The interaction between nano -
phases and dislocations in forged NM-TZM alloys was investigated, as 
shown in Fig. 11 . The moving dislocations are unable to bypass multiple 
short-range SP and are pinned, resulting in the formation of dislocation 
pile-up that concentrate stress in the vicinity ( Fig. 11 (f)). It can be 
posited that the segment of dislocation between adjacent SP can be 
classified as a Frank-Read source in response to the action of stress, 
which in turn enhances the ductility of the alloy. The formation of 
parallel corrugated dislocation networks, termed microbands (MBs), is 
observed in NM-TZM alloys. The formation of MBs may be related to the 
inhibitory effect of SP particles on DRX during hot deformation. The 
presence of MBs has been shown to contribute to improved ductility in 
BCC-structured W [ 52 ]. The MBs tend to form at LAGBs or seem to be 
considered as precursors of LAGBs. Due to the poor grain boundary 
coherence, the motion of dislocations is interrupted in HAGBs to form 
dislocation pile-up groups, and become the location where cracks are 
preferentially initiated [ 53 ]. However, LAGBs comprising MBs struc -
tures are equivalent to dislocation arrays exhibiting superior lattice 
coherence [ 54 ]. According to the established rules of dislocation 
movement at GBs [ 55,56 ], the structure of MB creates optimal paths for 
dislocation across GBs, which reduces intergranular cracking due to 
dislocation pile-up. Additionally, the dislocation networks embedded 
within MBs have the potential to function as Bardeen-Herring or 
Frank-Read sources, thereby providing locations for dislocation nucle -
ation [ 57 ]. Fig. 11 (d) seems to provide evidence for this theory. In 
summary, the traditional Mo alloy is mainly based on the double 
cross-slip multiplication mechanism of screw dislocations, and the 
refinement of the particles provides an additional dislocation multipli -
cation mechanism for the NM-TZM alloy, which in turn improves the 
ductility.
