1. Introduction 
Plastic deformation is a consequence of the movement of dislocations through the 
crystal lattice under the action of an applied stress. The fact that significant stresses 
are required to induce plasticity implies that the dislocations encounter obstacles to 
their motion. Plastic deformation of crystalline materials has long been established as 
a thermally activated process dependent on time, temperature, and strain rate. This 
basic concept was first proposed as early as 1925 by Becker [l] and then soon after 
it was realized that thermally activated flow was a typical rate-controlling process. 
The deformation mechanisms are those athermal and thermally activated processes 
which determine the stress at which plastic flow sets in. 
Investigations to determine the rate-controlling mechanism of the thermally 
activated deformations on y'-phase have been carried out by a number of workers. 
Thornton et al. (1970) [2], Randall (1971) [3], and Nicholls (1974) [4] have investigated 
deformation mechanisms of the y'-phase. Nicholls (1974) [a] has found at least five 
modes of deformation, depending on the deformation temperature, that occur in 
y' (Ni&l, 10 atyo Fe). By testing binary single crystal specimens, Copley and Kear 
(1967) [5] found that the specimen tested at 21 and 429 "C showed no strain-rate 
sensitivity and only a slight response was obtained from the specimen tested at 
592 "C. Davies and Stoloff (1965) [6] also found a similar behaviour in polycrystalline 
y '-phase. 
Pascoe and Newey (1971) [7], working on polycrystds of the Ni-rich (48.9 atyo Al) 
NiAl phase, found that the strain-rate sensitivity was strongly dependent on tempera- 
ture, namely below 400 K, and low in the intermediate temperature range.

2. Experimental Procedure 
The alloys studied (Table 1) were prepared in the form of arc-melted ingots (a 80 g). 
Alloys were studied in the three different conditions : (i) as-cast ; (ii) homogenized at 
1260 "C for 48 h and then water quenched; and (iii) honiogenized at 1260 "C for 48 h 
and then water quenched plus aged at 1000 "C for three days and then water quenched. 
Heat treatments were carried out in sealed silica capsules containing argon. 
The compression tests were carried out using an Instron testing machine, converted 
for conipression testing by means of rigs attached to the underside of the moving 
crosshead. All tests were done using a crosshead speed of 5 x 10-3cm/min which 
resulted in a strain rate of 1.3 x s-l. In the past-yield region the crosshead speed 
and chart speed were increased by a factor of ten and then returned to the original 
settings shortly afterwards, to measure the strain rate dependence of the flow stress, 
which is called the "strain-rate cycling" test. Stress relaxation tests were carried out 
by stopping the crosshead. A relaxation period of 20 min was used. These stress 
relaxation data were put into a programme made for Feltham's equation [ll, 121 
prepared by Nicholls and Rawlings [4]. From this computation the effective and 
athermal stress components of flow stress were determined. The decremental unloading 
method was also applied for some cases to determine the effective and athermal 
stresses. It should be admitted that the method fails if either dynamic strain aging 
or recovery occur at the test temperature.

The activation volume for deformation was calculated for each specimen from 
strain-rate sensitivity data. The activation volume was calculated in dimensionless 
units obtained by dividing it by b3, where b is the Burgers vector of the y'-phase, 
measured by X-ray diffractometry for each alloy [S]. The activation volume is about 
lob3 to 50b3 above 700 "C and decreases with increasing temperature for alloys 3 and 4. 
Mechanisms that are likely to be rate-controlling in this temperature range, on the 
basis of activation volume and the properties of the dislocations in y'-phase [lo] are 
(i) cross-slip and (ii) climb. 
Evidence has been reported [lo] about the difference in dislocation structures in 
y'-phase below and above 700 "C in alloys 3 and 4. It was suggested [lo] that this 
difference is due to the change of the slip system from octahedral to cubic system; 
assuming that 700 "C is about the peak temperature of the y'-phase, then it is ex- 
pected that cross-slip of dislocations has been found for deformation at 700 "C [9]. 
But at higher temperatures the possibility of climb processes is also important, and 
evidence was found of climb in y'-phase at 900 "C (Fig. 7 of [9]). The activation volume 
(lob3 to 50b3) in this temperature range is higher than expected on the basis of dis- 
location climb, which requires that Vb M lb3. However, above 750 "C recovery takes 
place which makes the accurate measurement of activation volume impossible. 
In all the alloys investigated only alloy 5, in the homogenized condition, is almost 
single-phase y' with only about 4 vol% of coarse (Ni, Al),Hf, particles [S]. It may 
therefore be expected that in this alloy the deformation mechanisms are the same as 
in the single-phase y'. The activation volume has been determined between 40b3 
and 85b3 below 600 "C and increases with increasing temperature. The likely rate- 
controlling mechanism is the movement of jogged screw dislocations. Evidence of 
jogs on the screw and mixed-type dislocations has been found at low temperature [lo]. 
Nicholls (1974) [a] in his activation analysis has found that overcoming Peierls bar- 
riers, cross-slip between octahedral and cube planes, and intersection of dislocation 
dipoles, in addition to the motion of jogged screw dislocations are the rate-controlling 
mechanisms in y'-phase below the peak temperature. Dislocation dipoles have been 
observed at various deformation temperatures at "low temperatures'' (Fig. 3, Fig. 8 
to 13 in [lo]), which might suggest the operation of the intersection of dislocation 
dipoles as the rate-controlling mechanism. However, the observed activation volume 
(40b3 to 85b3) does not agree with this mechanism, for which an activation volume of 
103b3 to 104b3 is needed. At higher temperatures near 600 "C, cross-slip cannot be 
ruled out since close to the peak temperature (i.e. 600 "C) the change from octahedral 
to cubic slip occurs [16]. The temperature dependence of the homogenized alloy 5 
shows a peak at about x 600 "C (Fig. 3 in [9]). The dislocations in alloy 5 change their 
characteristics below and above 600 "C, i.e. close to the "peak" temperature of the 
alloy, thus suggesting that cross-slip occurs at around 600 "C in the homogenized 
alloy 5. Above 600 "C the observed activation volume (<70b3) decreases which sug- 
gests that cross-slip becomes less favourable above 600 "C. At higher temperatures 
(above 600 "C), from the evidence found in dislocations in the y'-phase [lo], the climb 
of dislocations might become the dominating factor with increasing temperature. 
Alloys 1 and 2 show a comparatively small strain-rate sensitivity and effective 
stress below 400 "C, indicating that thermally activated processes are not prominent 
in this temperature range; however, above 400 "C effective stress and strain-rate 
sensitivity increase rapidly with increasing temperature, indicating that the deforma- 
tion is strongly thermally activated, as in the case of alloys 3 and 4 above 700 "C, and 
alloy 5 above 600 "C. Because of the similarity between flow stress, effective stress, 
and strain-rate sensitivity of these alloys and alloy 5 it is suggested that a similar 
rate-controlling process occurs as mentioned above for alloy 5 below 600 "C.

4. Conclusions 
From the similarity of the temperature dependence of flow stress and athermal stress 
behaviour it is proposed that the temperature dependence of the flow stress results 
from the change of the athermal stress with temperature. 
At high temperatures deformation in the alloys investigated is strongly thermally 
activated, whereas at low temperatures the deformation is not strongly thermally 
activated. The likely rate-controlling mechanisms have been discussed.
