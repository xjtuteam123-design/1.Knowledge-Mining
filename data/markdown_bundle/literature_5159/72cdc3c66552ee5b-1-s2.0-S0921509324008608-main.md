ABSTRACT 
At present, high fraction nano-TiC containing CoCrNi-4wt%TiC composite has been obtained through laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) of the blends of elemental powders and nano-TiC particles. Due to the addition of high 
fraction nano-TiC particles, there is a significant improvement in the strength of the composite to 1886 MPa at 
77K, 1456 MPa at 298K, 528 MPa at 1073K, 300 MPa at 1173K, respectively. Characterized by high scanning 
speed and high laser power, the laser scan strategy combining is effective in preventing the nano-TiC particles 
from agglomeration, and the nano-carbides show a heterogeneous distribution with a higher number density and 
a finer particle size at the bottom of melting pool, the mechanism of which is discussed. The second-phase 
strengthening of the high-density nano-carbides combining dislocation strengthening or grain boundary 
strengthening raises the yielding strength of the composite compared to CoCrNi alloy at cryogenic or elevating 
temperature, respectively. Combining with the deformation-induced nano stack faults/twins plasticity and 
heterogeneous distribution induced (HDI) strengthening, the addition of high-density nano-carbides contributes 
to the high tensile strength and good plasticity of the composite at the temperature in a wide range. 
1. Introduction 
Recently a newly developed materials class, i.e. the 
Fe – Mn – Cr – Ni – Co based high entropy alloy/medium entropy alloys 
(HEA/MEAs) receives intensive attention due to their potentials to ser -
vice as superior structural material at either low or high temperature [ 1 , 
2 ]. In particular, the CoCrNi MEA shows superior comprehensive 
properties, such as outstanding cryogenic [ 3 ] and room [ 4 ] mechanical 
properties, high impact toughness [ 5 ], and high temperature creep 
resistance [ 2 ], due to its low stacking energy at low temperatures [ 3 , 6 ] 
and high phase stability at high temperatures [ 7 ]. Despite these ad -
vantages, the relative low strength of CoCrNi MEA still hinders the alloy 
from practical applications. To compensate the shortage, new fabrica -
tion methods have been developed. For example, Sathiyamoorthi et al. 
raised the cryogenic tensile strength of CoCrNi MEA to ~2 GPa by 
method of high-pressure torsion followed by annealing [ 8 ]. However, 
these methods involve complicated thermal-mechanical treatments 
which are not applicable in the fabrication process of structure with 
complex geometry. Alternatively, additively manufacturing (AM) pro -
duces the geometrical-complex components by directly depositing the 
liquid metal layer by layer according to the pre-designed geometrical 
model [ 9 ]. The process of AM also strengthens the as-built MEAs by the 
formation of cellular structure with high-density dislocations [ 10 ]. The 
AM as-built MEA can be further strengthened by adding the ceramic 
particle reinforcement to the matrix [ 11 , 12 ]. It has been reported that 
the nano-TiC reinforcing particles can enhance the laser absorption of 
the metal powders and eliminate the possibility of spattering during 
laser melting, thus improving the outcome of printing [ 13 ]. Also, the 
high-speed convection within the melting pool during laser melting can 
promote the dispersion of agglomerating nano-TiC particles. However, 
the development of MEA-TiC composite with nano-TiC fraction higher 
than 3 wt% by LPBF still suffers from the agglomeration of nano-TiC and 
high porosity which results in drastic damage in mechanical property, 
especially in the plasticity. Chen et al. reported the addition of TiC in 
CoCrFeMnNi should not exceed 2 wt%, beyond which the tensile elon -
gation decreased to 5 %, in which process the low laser energy input was 
used [ 14 ]. In the study of Li et al., the addition of 5 wt% TiN in CoCr -
FeMnNi results in a low relative density of 95 % [ 15 ]. As indicated by Gu 
et al., the addition of 10 wt% TiC in In 718 alloy leads to a low relative 
density of 96 % [ 16 ]. Fabricating CoCrNi – TiC composite with low laser 
energy input through LPBF, Pan et al. suggested the weight fraction of 
nano-TiC particles should not exceed 2 %, beyond which the printed

In this study, we fabricated the CoCrNi composite with fraction of 
nano-TiC as high as 4 wt% by LBPF. To ensure the efficient synthesis of 
the medium entropy alloy composite, the mixture of element powders of 
Co, Cr, Ni and nano-TiC powders was used to alloy the CoCrNi – TiC 
composite in-situ by LPBF. Then, a thorough examination was con -
ducted to reveal the effect of TiC addition and laser scan parameters on 
the microstructure of the composite. The mechanical properties of the 
printed composite at low and high temperatures were investigated, and 
their strengthening mechanisms were explored accordingly. 
2. Materials and methods 
According to the nominal composition of CoCrNi-4wt%TiC, the 
nano-TiC particles with an average diameter of 40 nm and spherical 
elemental powders of Co, Cr, Ni with an average diameter of around 40 
μ m were mixed. To begin with, the mixed powders were tumble blended 
for 4h, and ball milled for 4h at a powder ratio of 4:1 at 300 rpm. Then, 
the obtained powders were alloyed in-situ by LPBF on the BLT s200 laser 
printing machine with a laser wavelength of 1070 nm and a laser spot 
diameter of 80 μ m, as illustrated in Fig. 1 a. The hatch space was set to 
80 μ m, and the scan direction rotated 67
◦
between each building layer. 
The strip scanning strategy with a strip width of 10 mm was adopted to 
minimize the thermal stress within the sample during preparation. For 
comparison, the medium entropy alloy with a nominal composition of 
CoCrNi was also alloyed in-situ by using the blended powder of Co, Cr 
and Ni. Table 1 lists the processing parameters concerned. The building 
direction (BD) was set as Axis z , and the scan plane was set as Plane x - y . 
The blocks with a size of 10 × 10 × 70 mm ( z × y × x ) were constructed, 
as shown in Fig. 1 b. The dog bone specimens with a gauge diameter of 3 
mm and a gauge length of 20 mm along Axis x were prepared by 
machining the blocks. The quasi-static tensile test of samples 1 and 4 was 
performed at a strain rate of 10

77 K and 298 K, and on the Wance ETM 105D test machine at 1073 K and 
1173 K, respectively. The samples tested at 77K, 1073 K and 1173 K 
were first held for 15 min at the test temperature to ensure the unifor -
mity of temperature distribution before loading. Both scan electrical 
microscopy (SEM) and optical microscopy (OM) were performed on 
Hitachi SUS5000 and Olympus BX51 M machines to observe the

Firstly, the samples were polished to measure the porosity. Then the

machine to observe the microstructure of the matrix. The samples were 
prepared by Electrolytic double jet thinning. The Bruker D8 Advance X- 
Ray diffraction (XRD) machine was employed to capture the XRD 
pattern of Samples 1 and 4.

high laser power enables the stable keyhole melting mode under which 
the deepest melting pools are achieved without causing many keyhole 
pore defects. This observation is consistent with the LPBF printing map 
predicted by simulation for the 316L stainless steel [ 25 ], which is often 
taken as the reference alloy for determining the LPBF processing 
parameter of the Cantor alloys. According to the simulation, the pro -
cessing parameter combination of high laser power and fast scanning 
speed leads to the strong liquid metal backflow from the front wall of the 
moving keyhole, which pushes the vortex in the rear melting pool 
further away from the rear wall of the keyhole. Consequently, the 
collapse of the rear wall to the front wall of the keyhole is suppressed, 
which prevents the keyhole pore defects. As argued by Kusano et al., the 
heat accumulation in previous tracks plays a major role in increasing the 
melting pool depth during LPBF [ 26 ]. The heat accumulation from the 
previous tracks may increase at the high scan speed of 800 mm/s for 
Sample 1 compared to the low scanning speed of 400 mm/s for Sample 
2, leading to a greater depth of the melting pools in the former. A large 
depth of the melting pools is conducive to the in-situ alloying of the 
CoCrNi-4wt%TiC composite, because the melting pool with a consid -
erable depth can be used to remelt the previously solidified melting 
pools repeatedly during the process of layer-by-layer laser scanning, 
thus enhancing the dispersion of the high fraction of nano TiC particles. 
An increase in the liquid flow during the stable keyhole mode melting of 
Sample1 can also promote the dispersion of TiC particles. As shown in 
Fig. 3 c, the depth of the melting pool in Sample 3 is the smallest among 
all the samples, reaching an average of 240 μ m, which is attributed to 
the low laser power used for producing Sample 3. Notably, the big holes

disperse the agglomerating nano-TiC particles, as evidenced by a large

Compared to Sample 4, the addition of 4 wt% of nano-TiC in Sample 1 
significantly improves the tensile yield strength and ultimate strength to 
an average of about 1487 ± 32 MPa, 1886 ± 36 MPa at 77 K, 1120 ± 29 
MPa, 1456 ± 10 MPa at 298 K, 506 ± 34 MPa, 528 ± 34 MPa at 1073 K 
and 283 ± 6 MPa, 300 MPa respectively, such as by about 568 MPa, 520 
MPa at 77K, 422 MPa, 539 MPa at 298K and 195 MPa, 213 MPa at 1073 
K for the pure CoCrNi medium entropy alloy, respectively. Sample 1 
maintains a considerable plasticity with a total elongation of about 10 % 
and 20 % at the deformation temperature of 77 K and 298 K, respec -
tively, despite the inferiority to Sample 4. Notably, the addition of nano- 
TiC in Sample 1 leads to a sharp rise in the tensile elongation at 1073 K 
to about 27 %, which is far higher than the value of 3 % of Sample 4. 
Various high-temperature materials such as the nickel-based superalloy 
often develop brittle intergranular failure, which leads to a failure given 
an engineering strain of 2 – 3% between 873 and 1073 K [ 29 ]. Due to the 
formation of high-temperature stable TiC/Cr23C6 particles at the grain 
or sub-grain boundaries, the cohesion of grain boundaries is enhanced, 
which prevents the occurrence of intergranular sliding and failure, thus 
resulting in the high plasticity of Sample 1 at high temperatures. As

4. Discussions 
4.1. Dispersion mechanism of the nano-TiC particles 
In the process of producing the metallic composite via LPBF, it is 
always challenging to ensure the sufficient dispersion of ceramic nano- 
particle, which is due to the difficulty in dispersing the agglomerated 
nano-particles completely. There are numerous reports that the ball 
milling process is effective in dispersing the nano-particles and enabling 
their uniform distribution in the major alloy micro-powders [ 12 , 14 , 19 ]. 
In spite of this, the agglomerating nano-particles with a size of 10 – 20 μ m 
remain in the micro-powders, as described in this study( Fig. 2 f). Smith 
et al. (2020) reported the success in coating the nano-Y2O3 particles on 
the CoCrNi powders by means of resonant acoustic mixing [ 7 ]. How -
ever, the amount of nano-Y2O3 particles addition is only 1 wt%. 
Another solution is to break the agglomerates through high laser energy 
input, which however causes the keyhole pore defects in many cases. In 
this study, the laser scanning strategy is applied that features a high 
scanning speed and a strong laser power, to break the agglomerates 
without causing too many keyhole pore defects, as shown in Figss. 3 – 4 . 
Despite the occasional agglomeration of nano-particles with a size of less 
than 10 μ m, as shown in Fig. 16 , they appear not to cause deterioration 
in the mechanical performance of the composite, as shown in Fig. 12 . 
One disadvantage of this method lies in the lack of nano-particles at the 
upper side of the melting pools adjacent to the top surface of the com -
posite ( Fig. 7 a – d), as this may reduce the surface strength of those parts 
with a complex geometrical shape. Most recently, Wang et al. proposed 
that the homogeneity of the high volume-fraction TiB2 particles can be 
improved by the re-dispersion of agglomeration regions through the 
overlapping of laser tracks [ 33 ]. However, it is still questioned whether 
the low laser energy input used their research can break the agglomer -
ation of nano-TiC particles used in this study. 
4.2. Dispersion and subsequent uneven distribution of the nano-carbides 
in the composites 
As shown in Fig. 7 , the nano-carbides are unevenly distributed along 
the depth of the melting pool, which may be caused by the significant 
variation in liquid metal flow within the melting pool. Fig. 17 illustrates 
the distribution of nano-carbides within the melting pools and the ma -
trix. As revealed by previous simulation and in-situ observation, the 
temperature and flow speed of the molten metal reach the maximum at 
the bottom of the melting pool. Activated by the depression force 
applied at the tips of the keyhole, this results in the sufficient dispersion 
of the TiC agglomeration into separate particles by the shearing force of 
the liquid flow at the bottom of the melting pool [ 17 ]. The deep keyhole 
can also drive the excess TiC particles to the bottom, as illustrated in 
Fig. 17 a. After the initial melting of the alloy at the bottom of the 
melting pool, the liquid metal is cooled at the fastest rate in the melting 
pools. Reportedly, when the solidification front reaches a critical ve -
locity, the nano-particles are trapped, instead of being pushed by it [ 17 ]. 
As a result, high-density TiC dispersed at the bottom of the melting pool 
remains in the solidified cells and cell walls. In addition, the higher 
cooling rate at the melting pool bottom led to thinner cell structure 
compared to the cell structure at the upside of melting pool ( Fig. 7 a), 
which further dispersed the nano-carbides. At the upper of the melting 
pool, it takes more time for the liquid metal to solidify due to the 
reduced cooling rate. Consequently, the remaining nano-particles are 
driven by the solidification front to accumulate mainly along the 
boundaries of the cells, rather than within the cells, as shown in 
Fig. 7 b – d. Besides, the fraction of un-melted separated nano-TiC is far 
smaller at the upper side of melting pool. Collectively, there is a 
reduction in density and non-uniform distribution of the TiC or 
Cr23C6-type particles at the upper side of the melting pool, as shown in 
Fig. 7 . Fig. 17 a and b illustrates the distribution and precipitation of the 
TiC or Cr23C6-type particles within the melting pools during the process 
of laser melting, while Fig. 17 c and d shows the distribution of the 
nano-particles in the x-z plane that are formed during the track-by-track 
and layer-by-layer building processes. This illustration characterizes the 
uneven distribution of nano-carbides along the bottom boundaries of the 
melting pool, which is consistent with the microstructure shown in 
Figs. 17 e and 7 . As a result, the matrix of the CoCrNi-4wt%TiC com -
posite exhibits heterogeneity in structure due to the uneven distribution 
of nano-carbides. The grey white zones in Fig. 17 e, defined as Part 1 as 
illustrated in Fig. 17 b, correspond to the upper-side of the melting pool 
containing less densely distributed nano-carbides, like zones b,c and d in 
Fig. 7 a. The brown zones, defined as Part 2 as illustrated in Fig. 17 b, 
correspond to the bottom of melting pool containing densely distributed 
nano-carbides, like Zone e in Fig. 7 a. The zone of Part 2 accounts for 
~19 % of the area in the microscopy of Sample 1, according to Fig. 17 e.

5. Conclusion 
In this study, the high fraction nano-TiC containing CoCrNi-4wt%TiC 
composite is prepared through LPBF of the blends of elemental powders 
and nano-TiC particles. This CoCrNi-4wt%TiC composite shows superior 
mechanic properties across a wide range of temperatures from 77k to 
1173 K. Thus, the following conclusions are drawn: 
(1) The laser scanning strategy that combines high scanning speed 
and high laser power can effectively disperse the agglomeration 
of the nano-TiC particles. Also, the high times taken by remelting 
in each building layers facilitates the in-situ alloying of the 
blended elemental powders containing high melting point Cr 
powders. This high depression force applied during the keyhole 
mode melting leads to the heterogeneous distribution of nano- 
carbides with a higher number density and a smaller particle 
size at the bottom of the melting pool. 
(2) The addition of high fraction nano-TiC particles significantly 
improves the strength of the composite to 1886 MPa at 77K, 
1456 MPa at 298K, 528 MPa at 1073K, 300 MPa at 1173K, 
respectively. Meanwhile, plasticity is maintained at a reasonable 
level. The mechanical properties at the temperature in a wide 
range are superior in the AMed alloys. 
(3) The second-phase strengthening of the high-density nano-car -
bides combining dislocation strengthening or grain boundary 
strengthening raises the yielding strength of the composite 
compared with CoCrNi alloy at cryogenic or elevating tempera -
ture, respectively. Combining with the deformation-induced 
nano stack faults/twins plasticity and HDI strengthening, the 
addition of high-density nano-carbides contributes to the high 
tensile strength and good plasticity of the composite at the tem -
perature in a wide range.
