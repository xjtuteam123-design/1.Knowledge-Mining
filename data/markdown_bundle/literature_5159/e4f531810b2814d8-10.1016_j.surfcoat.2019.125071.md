ABSTRACT
The use of γ-TiAl alloys at higher temperatures exceeding 700 –800 °C is restricted due to their inadequate
oxidation resistance. Their durability and high temperature capability can be enhanced by the application of
oxidation resistant coatings. The present study examines the cyclic oxidation behavior of a di ﬀusion Al3Ti coated
lamellar Ti –45Al–5Nb-0.2B-0.2C (in at.%) γ-TiAl alloy during exposure at 800 and 950 °C in air for 500 h. The
eﬀect of oxidation on the coating microstructure and the compression properties at room temperature, 800 and
950 °C are assessed. The coating forms a protective Al 2O3-rich oxide scale, which lowers the overall oxidation
rate and induces steady parabolic weight gain in the coated alloy; unlike the extensive weight loss occurring in
the bare/uncoated alloy during oxidation at higher temperatures. The parabolic oxidation rate constant (K p) for
the coated alloy at 6 –7x1 0 −7 mg2 cm−4 s−2 is two orders of magnitude lower than that of the bare alloy.
Surface embrittlement during oxidation causes deterioration in the compression yield strength of the bare alloy.
On the other hand, the coating prevents oxidation-induced surface degradation of the alloy, which manifests in
improved compression strength of the oxidized coated alloy as compared to that of the oxidized bare alloy.
Though the coating is constituted of the brittle intermetallic Al
3Ti phase, the cracks in the coating do not
penetrate the substrate and are innocuous to the compression properties of the underlying intermetallic γ-TiAl
alloy. The coating is bene ﬁcial in providing oxidation resistance and prevents signi ﬁcant oxidation-induced
deterioration in compression strength of the alloy during high temperature exposure. Besides, the Al 3Ti coating
does not induce signi ﬁcant deterioration in the tensile strength of the γ-TiAl alloy.
1. Introduction
The components made of γ-TiAl alloys are used in the hot sections of
compressor and the down-stream low pressure stages in advanced high-
eﬃciency gas turbine engines [ 1–5]. These alloys are low in density and
exhibit high speci ﬁc strength as well as modulus at high temperatures.
However, the use of the present alloys is limited to temperatures of
750–800 °C due to their inadequate oxidation resistance. Despite con-
taining high Al (45 –48 at.%), the γ-TiAl alloys do not form a protective
layer of alumina scale on the surface, but rather form mixed oxide
layers of TiO 2 and Al2O3 [6–10]. Such oxidation characteristics of the γ-
TiAl alloys are ascribed to the similar thermodynamic activities of Ti
and Al for the formation of their respective oxides and the high growth
rate of TiO
2 [11–13]. The oxide scale formed is porous, does not pro-
vide an e ﬀective barrier against oxidation, and the oxidation of the
alloy continues unabated beneath the oxide scale. The spallation of the
surface oxides leads to surface degradation and loss of material section
thickness. During service, the surface degraded regions can be potential
sites for overstressing and premature initiation of damage and failure.
Therefore, the prevention of oxidation and enhancement of the inherent
oxidation resistance of the γ-TiAl alloys is essential from the point of
view of their applications in gas turbine engines.
The strategies for improving the oxidation resistance of the γ-TiAl
alloys include (i) alloying additions [ 6,14,15], (ii) surface modi ﬁcations
[6,16–18], and (iii) their combinations [ 6,16,18]. It is well established
that the oxidation of Ti to TiO
2 is associated with the formation of
oxygen vacancies, which promote the inward di ﬀusion of oxygen
[8,9,11,19]. The inherent oxidation resistance of the γ-TiAl alloys can
be enhanced by lowering the concentration of oxygen vacancies, which
can be achieved by the addition of refractory elements (Nb, Mo and W)
having higher electronegativity than that of Ti [ 19–29]. Surface mod-
iﬁcations such as halide/ ﬂuorination treatment are reported to enhance
the di ﬀusion of Al on the surface and promote the formation of Al 2O3-
rich scale on the surface [ 17,29]. Oxidation resistant coatings such as
MCrAlY (M = Fe, Co, Ni) are reported to provided good oxidation re-
sistance to the γ-TiAl alloys [ 6,18]. However, since the composition and

scarce in the open literature.
Therefore, the present study examines the microstructural evolution
of a di ﬀusion Al

cyclic oxidation in air at 800 and 950 °C for 500 h, and evaluates the
compression properties at room temperature, 800 and 950 °C. Cyclic
oxidation exposure and the subsequent assessment of microstructure
changes vis-à-vis mechanical properties is pertinent since the engine
components undergo thermal cycling for signi ﬁcant durations during
service. It is demonstrated that the coating is bene ﬁcial in preventing
oxidation induced degradation of the substrate γ-TiAl alloy and imparts
improved compression strength even after oxidation at high tempera-
tures of 950 °C for long durations of 500 h. Microtensile tests carried out
at room temperature indicate that the coating does not cause signi ﬁcant
degradation in the tensile properties of the brittle γ-TiAl alloy.

2. Experimental details
The γ-TiAl alloy, having the composition Ti –45Al–5Nb-0.2B-0.2C
(in at.%), was obtained in the form of disks having dimensions of
100 mm diameter and 12 mm thickness. The disks were prepared by the
non-consumable vacuum arc re-melting (VAR) process. The disks were
ﬂipped and re-melted four times for avoiding segregation and attaining
homogeneity. For the closure of micro-pores and healing of internal
casting defects, the cast disks were subjected to Hot Isostatic Pressing
(HIP) at 1230 °C and 120 MPa pressure for 4 h. Subsequently, the hot
iso-statically pressed disks were solution heat treated at 1230 °C for 1 h
and quenched in air. Aging heat treatment was carried out at 850 °C for
6 h followed by normal cooling in the furnace.
Samples of two di ﬀerent shapes, i.e. (i) buttons having dimensions
of 10 mm diameter and 3 mm thickness, and (ii) cylinders having di-
mensions of 5 mm diameter and 10 mm length, were excised from the
fully heat treated disks by the wire electro discharge machining (EDM)
process. All the samples were grit blasted at 1 bar pressure to remove
the surface oxide layer formed during the EDM process. The samples
were ultra-sonicated (cleaned) in acetone for 5 min to remove the ﬁne
sand particles left over on the surface from the grit blasting. The initial
weight and dimensions of the samples were recorded using a Sartorius
precision digital balance and a Mitutoyo digital micrometer, respec-
tively.
Some of the button and cylindrical samples were applied with a
diﬀusion aluminide coating, which was formed by the pack alumini-
zation process. The powder pack used for the aluminization process was
prepared by mixing of Al, NH
4Cl and Al 2O3 powders in the proportion
15:2:83 (in wt.%), respectively. The samples were buried in the powder
mixture and heat treated at 700 °C for 5 h in a medium of Ar. The Al
powder source provided the Al required for the coating formation on
the γ-TiAl substrate, NH
4Cl served as the activator, and Al 2O3 acted as
an inert ﬁller which prevented sintering of the powder pack during the
aluminizing process. At the high aluminization temperature of 700 °C,
Al-chloride vapors are generated by the reaction of Al with the NH 4Cl
activator contained in the powder pack mixture [ 38–41]. Nascent Al
atoms form by the dissociation of the Al-chloride vapors on the surface
of the substrate, and the Al 3Ti coating forms by the inward di ﬀusion of
Al atoms and reaction with the substrate. The ready formation of the
Al
3Ti phase on the surface of the substrate, i.e. in the coating, is favored
due to the high activity of Al atoms on the surface and the faster dif-
fusion kinetics of Al in the substrate alloy. The detailed mechanisms
associated with the formation of di ﬀusion aluminide coating during the
pack aluminization process have been widely reported in the literature
[38–41].

cylindrical samples was carried out in air at 800 °C and 950 °C in an
automated thermal cycling furnace. Each cycle comprised soaking of

ascertained by wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) using a
CAMECA SX-100 electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA). Standard

Vibromet machines were adopted for the preparation of the cross-sec-

3. Results
3.1. As-deposited coating microstructure
The diﬀusion aluminide coating is dense, has a thickness of ∼50 μm
and exhibits a distinct interface with the substrate alloy ( Fig. 1a). The
coating is constituted of numerous ﬁne grains of the single phase Al
3Ti
(tetragonal, space group 139, a = b = 3.84 Å, c = 8.58 Å) and the
average grain size of the coating is about 1 μm( Fig. 1(b and c)). The
inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) map indicates that, until depths of ∼25–30 μm
from the coating/substrate interface, the grains of Al 3Ti are pre-
ferentially oriented along [001] with respect to the substrate-surface
normal whereas that at farther depths away from the coating/substrate
interface are randomly oriented ( Fig. 1 d). The substrate alloy exhibits
random orientations of lamellar structure and is predominantly con-
stituted of the γ-TiAl phase (tetragonal, space group 123,
a = b = 4.01 Å, c = 4.07 Å), as evident from Fig. 1 (a–d). At the inter
lamellar regions of the substrate alloy, the Ti
3Al phase (tetragonal,
space group 194, a = b = 5.78 Å, c = 4.65 Å) is present. Minor amount
of β/B2–Ti (cubic, space group 229, a = b = c = 3.19 Å) is also de-
tected in the substrate. The above phase constitution of the substrate
alloy, i.e. TiAl, Ti 3Al and β/B2–Ti, is consistent with that reported for γ-
TiAl alloys having similar compositions [ 1–6]. Fine through-thickness
cracks are present in the coating ( Fig. 1a). These cracks develop during
the cooling of the coating from its formation temperature due to the
thermal stresses, i.e. mismatch in the co-e ﬃcient of thermal expansion
(CTE) between the coating and the substrate [ 31,32]. The through-
thickness cracks do not penetrate the substrate and are rather con ﬁned
to the coating.

3.3. Characterization of the phases formed during oxidation
3.3.1. Bare alloy
TiO2 and Al 2O3 phases form on the surface of the bare alloy after
oxidation for various durations, which is evident from the analyses of
the XRD plots ( Fig. 3 a). The TiAl and Ti 3Al phases present in the sub-
strate alloy are also detected ( Fig. 3a). The surface scale consists of two
distinct phases, i.e. bright-faceted blocky phase and gray nodular phase,
which are indicated as regions 1 and 2 in Fig. 4 a. The bright faceted
phase (indicated as 1) corresponds to that of TiO 2 [11,12], whereas the
gray nodular areas (indicated as 2) contains a mixture of ﬁne Al2O3 and
TiO2 phases [ 11,12], which is evident from the corresponding EDS
spectra ( Fig. 4b and c). Extensive cracking and spallation of the oxide
scale is observed for the bare sample oxidized at 950 °C for 500 h
(Fig. 4d). The oxide scale is constituted of multiple layers of oxides,
which is evident from the cross-section micrograph shown in Fig. 5 a.
The formation of several through-thickness cracks in the outer layers of
the oxide scale, cracks along the interlayer boundaries, and delamina-
tion of the oxide layers are observed ( Fig. 5a). The EDS analyses in-
dicates that the oxides correspond to that of Ti, Al and Nb ( Fig. 5b). The
elemental maps indicate that the outer layers are enriched with oxides
of Ti and Al, the intermediate layers are constituted of Ti-oxides,
whereas the innermost layer is rich in oxide of Nb ( Fig. 6). A thin N
enriched layer is present at the interface between the oxide scale and
the substrate ( Fig. 6).

3.4. Localized oxidation near through-thickness coating cracks
For the coated samples oxidized at 950 °C for 300 h and beyond,
localized oxidation degradation is observed at the tip of certain
through-thickness cracks present in the coating, although the coating
remains protective at the other regions ( Fig. 10 ). These through-
thickness cracks, which are associated with localized oxidation attack,
correspond to those present in the as-deposited coating ( Fig. 1). These
cracks provide easy pathways for the ingress of oxygen, which causes
oxidation along the trail of the crack within the coating and at the
coating/substrate interface in the vicinity of the crack tip ( Fig. 10a).
The formation of distinct oxides within the typical through-thickness
coating crack is corroborated by the dark and bright phase contrasts at
the peripheral and the central regions of the crack, respectively
(Fig. 10b). From the correlation with the elemental maps, it is evident
that the peripheral and central regions of the crack are constituted of
the oxides of Al and Ti, respectively ( Fig. 11a,b,c,e). The magni ﬁed
view of the localized oxidation pit formed at the coating-substrate in-
terface and in the vicinity of the crack tip is shown in Fig. 10c. Alter-
nating layers of dark and bright oxides are observed within the oxide
pit. The formation of a reaction zone, emanating from the oxide pit-
substrate interface and radially growing into the substrate, is observed
(Fig. 10 c). The dark and bright oxides within the oxide pit are rich in
oxides of Al and Ti, respectively. Nb-oxide is also present within the
oxide pit ( Fig. 11d), whereas N enrichment is detected at the interface
between the oxide pit and the substrate reaction zone ( Fig. 11f). The
elemental maps indicate that the reaction zone contains oxides of Nb, Ti
(Fig. 11a,c-e and is lean in oxide of Al ( Fig. 11(a,b,e)).
The extent of localized oxidation and degradation within and in the
vicinity the through-thickness coating cracks increases with the in-
crease in oxidation duration, which is clearly evident form the cross-
section micrograph shown for the coated sample after oxidation for 400
and 500 h at 950 °C ( Fig. 12 ). Apart from the increased width of the
oxidized region within the through-thickness coating crack and at the
coating/substrate interface ( Fig. 12a), the lateral spreading of the oxide
aﬀected regions along the coating/substrate interface and their linking
with the oxidized regions formed in the vicinity of the adjacent cracks is

4. Discussion
As alluded to earlier, the as-deposited di ﬀusion aluminide coating
comprises Al 3Ti grains, which grow preferentially along [001] until
depths of about 30 μm from the coating/substrate interface ( Fig. 1d).
The [001] micro-texture of the Al 3Ti phase in the coating can be ex-
pected due to the large (001) interplanar spacing in the tetragonal
lattice and the relative ease of packing of Al atoms at the atomic sites
along the ‘c’ [001] axis, i.e. at the Wyckoﬀ 2b (0,0,0) and 4d (0, ½, ¼)
positions [44]. The through-thickness cracks in the as-deposited coating
form during cooling from the coating formation temperature of 700 °C
to the ambient. The Al
3Ti phase has a higher CTE (13 × 10 −6 K−1) than
that of the γ-TiAl substrate (10.8 × 10 −6 K−1)[ 45–47]. Therefore, the
coating experiences tensile residual stress during cooling, which causes
cracking of the constituent brittle Al
3Ti intermetallic phase ( Fig. 1a).
Among the various aluminide phases reported in the Ti –Al system
(AlTi3, TiAl, AlTi 2 and Al 3Ti), the oxidation resistance of Al 3Ti is su-
perior by virtue of its higher Al content [ 6]. The Al 3Ti phase comprising
the coating forms the protective super ﬁcial oxide scale upon oxidation
(Figs. 3b and 7(c-e) ). The predominant formation of the desirable Al 2O3
phase is facilitated by the fast outward di ﬀusion of Al than that of Ti in
the Al 3Ti phase [ 6,30,48]. Though TiO 2 is also detected in the oxide
scale, the majority of the oxide phase corresponds to that of Al 2O3
(Figs. 3b and 7(c-e) ). The Al 2O3-rich oxide scale forms spontaneously
on the coating surface during the initial stages of oxidation at high
temperatures, which is evident from the high weight gain and high K p
value of 5.31 × 10 −6 mg2 cm−4 s−1 during oxidation at 950 °C for 3
cycles ( Fig. 2c). Once formed, the oxide scale provides an e ﬀective
barrier against the inward di ﬀusion of oxygen, which results in low-
ering of the K p value by an order of magnitude (2 –7x
10−7 mg2 cm−4 s−1) during subsequent oxidation ( Fig. 2c). The oxide
scale ensuing from oxidation of Al 3Ti possesses good adhesion on the
coating surface, resists spallation, and induces steady parabolic weight
gain in the samples over the entire 500 h of cyclic oxidation at 800 and
950 °C ( Fig. 2). The e ﬀective oxidation resistance provided by the

5. Conclusions
The oxidation behavior of bare/uncoated and di ﬀusion Al3Ti coated
lamellar γ-TiAl alloy, having the composition Ti –45Al–5Nb-0.2B-0.2C
(in at.%), is comparatively assessed at 800 and 950 °C during long-term
thermal cycling in air for 500 h ( ∼660 cycles). The concomitant e ﬀect
of prior oxidation exposure on the microstructure and compression
properties at room temperature, 800 and 950 °C are reported. The fol-
lowing conclusions can be drawn from the present study:
(i) The bare alloy exhibits inferior oxidation resistance at tempera-
tures above 800 °C, which is evident from the rapid weight gain
and the corresponding high K
p value of 1.96 × 10−5 mg2 cm−4 s−2
during the initial three cycles of oxidation at 950 °C and extensive
weight loss thereafter with further thermal cycling.
(ii) The di ﬀusion Al 3Ti coating provides e ﬀective long-term oxidation
resistance by the formation of protective Al 2O3-rich surface oxide
scale. The coated samples exhibit steady weight gain over the en-
tire 500 h of cyclic oxidation and overall low K p values in the range
of 6– 7x1 0 −7 mg2 cm−4 s−2.
(iii) Oxidation for 500 h causes greater deterioration in the compres-
sion yield properties in the bare alloy. The yield strength of the
oxidized bare alloy evaluated at room temperature, 800 and 950 °C
deteriorates to a greater extent by about 100, 140 and 70 MPa,
respectively, as compared to that of the unoxidized bare alloy.
(iv) The brittle di ﬀusion Al
3Ti coating is not detrimental to the com-
pression strength property of the alloy. The deterioration in the
compression yield strength of the coated alloy due to prior oxi-
dation exposure is signi ﬁcantly lower, i.e. by at least 50%, than
that of the corresponding oxidized bare alloy.
(v) The coating provides dual bene ﬁts in providing e ﬀective long-term
oxidation resistance and limiting the oxidation-induced dete-
rioration in strength of the alloy.
(vi) The Al
3Ti coating does not cause signi ﬁcant deterioration in the
room temperature tensile strength of the γ-TiAl alloy and the ex-
tent of decrease in the fracture strength remains within 10 –15% as
compared to that of the uncoated alloy.
