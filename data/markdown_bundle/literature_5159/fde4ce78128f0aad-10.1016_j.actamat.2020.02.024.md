Abstract 
Single-phase face -centered cubic multi -principal-element alloys (fcc MPEAs) comprising 
transition metal elements Cr, Mn, Fe, Co or Ni generally show strong temperature dependence of 
stacking fault energy (SFE) of { 111} plane, the lower temperature the lower SFE. Through 
uniaxial tension of a non -equiatomic Cr 26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy at low temperature, we 
observed nanoscale microstructures including stacking faults bands, nano-twins, hexagonal-close 
packed (hcp) phase bands and amorphous bands . The formation and interactions of stacking

faults, twins and amorphous bands are cha racterized at different strain levels, and thermal 
stability of these nanostructures are investigated by subsequent tempering. We highlight the 
formation of extensive nanoscale amorphous bands and their much higher thermal stability above 
650 ºC than nano-twins and hcp bands. Amorphous bands ensure the enhanced strength and good 
ductility of high temperature tempered Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy. Amorphous bands can 
plastically co -deform with matrix. The interfaces between amorphous band and fcc matrix 
provide not only strong barriers for dislocation motion , strengthening materials, but also natural 
sinks of dislocations, disrupting stress concentrations and delaying decohesion and fracture 
initiation. Our results demonstrate that e ngineering amorphous ba nds could be an efficient 
strategy in remaining enhanced mechanical properties of fcc MPEAs at high temperature.

1. Introduction 
Over the past decade, multi-principal-element alloys (MPEAs) that are composed of f our 
or more elements in an equiatomic or near -equiatomic composition have attracted extensive 
attentions due to their excellent mechanical properties enabling them to act as promising 
structural mat erials [1-6]. Depending on the constituent principal elements, MPEAs mainly 
exhibit three types of crystal structures, i.e. face-centered cubic (fcc), body-centered cubic (bcc), 
and hexagonal close -packed ( hcp) structures [3, 7] . Owing to the near-infinite compositional 
space and adjustable microstructures via thermomechanical processing, mechanical properties of 
MPEAs including strength/hardness, ductility, toughness, wear resistance, and fatigue resistance 
can be enhanced in terms of one or two or more of them [1-4, 8, 9]. 
Intensive studies have demonstrated that the room-temperature yield strengths have been 
enhanced significantly without sacrificing ductility in single-phase fcc bulk MPEAs with nano-
structures and heterogeneous microstructures . These nano-structures and heterogeneous 
microstructures are generally created by severe plastic deformation (such as conventional cold -
rolling) and then intermediate temperature annealing [10]. The h eterogeneous microstructures

3 
 
comprise nano-twins, gradient nanograins, or recrystallized and non-recrystallized grains that are 
arranged in complex hierarchical structures with a charact eristic dimension spanning from 
submicron scale to micro scale [11-15]. The excellent room-temperature strength-ductility 
synergy in single -phase fcc MPEAs with such complex microstructures is attributed to the 
enhanced strain hardening capacity enabled by synergetic or individual strengthening 
mechanisms associated with deformation-induced nano-twinning and phase transformation, or 
plastic deformation incompatibility between nano -grains [10-15]. Thus, engineering the 
heterogeneous and hierarchical microstructure associated with multiple shear transformation 
bands should be an efficient strategy in enhancing room-temperature mechanical properties of 
MPEAs with low and medium stacking fault energy (SFE). 
Single-phase fcc MPEAs comprising transition metal elements Cr, Mn, Fe, Co and Ni 
generally show strong temperature dependenc e of SFE [16, 17], the lower temperature the lower 
SFE [16]. Correspondingly, plastic deformation can be accommodated by motion of Shockley 
partial dislocations and full dislocations, deformation twin ning, and even fcc hcp phase 
transformation that are dependent on deformation conditions, such as strain extent, strain rate, 
and temperature [18-23]. For example, single -phase fcc, coarse -grained equiatomic 
CrMnFeCoNi and CoCrNi alloys exhibit exceptional com binations of tensile strength and 
ductility ( tensile strength of ~1 GPa as well as the ductility exceeding 60% ), and ultra -high 
fracture toughness ( KJ1C 200 MPa √m) at low temperature of 77 K [18, 20, 21] . Such great 
low-temperature mechanical properties are attributed to the lower SFE at cryogenic temperature 
which promotes extensive activities of Shockley partial dislocations and the formation of higher 
density of stacking faults (SFs) and nano-twins [22, 24-27]. However, high density of SFs and 
nano-twins are lacking of thermal stability because of high energy and easy recovery through the 
glide of Shockley partial dislocations. Therefore, stabilizing these nanostructures or even 
creating other types of nanostructures with enhanced thermal stability are highly demanded. 
Taking the advantage of the SFE temperature dependence, low-temperature severe 
deformation can significantly modify microstructures of single -phase fcc MPEA s with high-
density of shear transformation bands such as nanoscale shear bands, twins and even hcp bands. 
In this work, the non-equiatomic Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy is chosen because it has a very low 
SFE, approximately 3.5 mJ/m 2 [28], which promotes the deformation -induced nano-twining and

martensitic transformation. Significantly, we fabricate a nano -structured non-equiatomic 
Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy with nanoscale amorphous bands in addition to stacking fault bands, 
nano-twins, and hcp phase bands, by uniaxial tensile deformation at low temperatures . We 
characterize the evolution of these transformation bands under different strain levels to 
understand their formation and interactions . We also investigate t he thermal stability of these 
nano-structured bands by subsequent tempering. We highlight the formation of extensive 
nanoscale amorphous bands and their much higher thermal stability as compared with nano -
twins and hcp bands, as well as the role of amorphous bands in ensuring the enhanced strength 
and good ductility of non-equiatomic Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy after high temperature 
tempering. This is partially due to the fact that the interfaces between relatively stable 
amorphous band s and fcc matrix provide strong barriers for dislocation motion. Our results 
demonstrate that engineering such a hierarchical microstructure with amorphous bands could be 
an efficient strategy in remaining enhanced mechanical propertie s of fcc MPEAs at high 
temperatures. 
 
2. Experimental procedures 
Alloy ingots with nominal composition Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 (at.%) were prepared by arc-
melting under an argon atmosphere, subsequently homogenized in vacuum at 1200 ºC for 48 h 
and then cooled in the furnace. The homogenized ingots were heated at 1100 ºC for 0.5 h, and 
immediately hot-rolled to the sheets with a thickness of 8 mm, then cold -rolled to 2.4 mm in 
thickness with a reduction of 70%. Finally, the cold-rolled sheets were annealed at 1100 ºC for 1 
h, followed by air cooling. 
Flat dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with a gauge length of 18 mm, thickness of 1.5 mm 
and width of 3 mm were machined from the annealed sheet by electric discharge machining with 
their longitudinal axes p arallel to the rolling direction of the sheet. For low-temperature tensile 
testing, t he sample s were placed in an environmental chamber , and the liquid nitrogen 
vaporization is utilized to decrease the testing temperature. When the temperature was decreased 
to -180 ºC , the sample was held in environmental chamber with cold gas for ~15 min before 
straining. The tensile samples were firstly pre -strained on an INSTRON -8801 instrument at a

5 
 
crosshead speed of 1 mm/min to engineering strains of 5%, 15%, 2 8%, 36%, 46% and 75% at a 
low temperature of -180 ºC , respectively, and then unloaded. We then characterize d 
microstructures of pre -strained samples. In addition, t he 36% pre-strained samples were further 
tempered at different temperatures in the range of 500-700 ºC for 1 h followed by water 
quenching. The pre -strained and tempered samples were ground through 1200-grit SiC paper , 
and further stretched at room temperature with an engineering strain rate of 10-3 s-1 until fracture. 
Microstructure characterizations were performed by optical microscopy (OM), electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and transmission electron microscop y (TEM) coupled with 
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) . The TEM bright -filed (BF)and high -resolution TEM 
(HRTEM) imaging were performed in JEM 2100F TEM operated at 200 kV. The high/low-angle 
annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF/LAADF-STEM) imaging and STEM -EDS were 
conducted in an aberration -corrected microscope (FEI Titan Themis) a t an acceleration voltage 
of 300 kV. For HAADF imaging, a probe semi -convergence angle of 17 mrad and inner and 
outer semi -collection angles rangi ng from 73 to 350 mrad were utilized. For LAADF -STEM 
imaging, a probe semi-convergence angle of 17 mrad and inner and outer semi-collection angles 
from 14 to 63 mrad were used. Specimens for TEM observation were extracted from the gauge 
section of deformed specimens and then thinned using mechanical grinding followed by double -
jet electro-polishing in a solution of 6 % perchloric acid, 35 % n-butyl alcohol and 59 % alcohol 
at -30 ºC and an applied voltage of 30 V. Specimens for TEM observation were also made by 
grinding and polishing followed by low energy (3.5 keV) ion-milling. In order to avoid oxidation, 
TEM samples were kept in a vacuum container for less than one day before performing TEM 
observation. OM specimens were initially ground to 5000 -grit SiC paper and, subsequently, 
polished down to 1.5 μm diamond polishing compoun d, and etched with a solution that is 
composing of supersaturated copper sulfate, hydrochloric acid and alcohol (1 mg: 5 ml: 5 ml). 
The samples for EBSD analysis were prepared by mechanical grinding and polishing.

In addition to nanoscale SFs bands, twins and hcp bands, other type s of shear 
transformation bands, such as shear bands and amorphous bands , are observed . In the 28% pre-
strained sample, Fig. 5a shows ultra-high-density of dislocations that are stored in shear bands,

suggesting highly concentrated plastic flow in shear bands. Shear bands have the width ranging 
from 50 nm to 500 nm . Based on c rystallographic analysis, we find that shear bands do not 
develop along the low indexed planes. These shear bands have a certain angle of ~19° relative to 
the (002) plane of the fcc matrix , as identified in Fig. 5a and 5b. The HRTEM image of a shear 
band in Fig. 5c shows that the contrast of shear band is much darker than that of surrounding fcc

dislocation accumulations. Surprisingly, some of shear bands transform into amorphous bands, 
as demonstrated in TEM-BF images of Fig. 5b and corresponding HRTEM image of Fig. 5 d. 
Under the TEM-BF imaging condition at low indexed zone axes, crystalline shear bands exhibit 
dark contrast, while amorphous bands display bright contrast. The amorphous structure in Fig. 5b 
is further proved by the characteristic maze -like pattern in the HRTEM image (Fig. 5d) and 
diffuse diffraction halo in the corresponding FFT pattern (inset of Fig. 5d ). To eliminate the 
possibility of preparation artefacts, the TEM samples were additionally prepared by gentle ion-
milling and the observed microstructures are identical (Supplementary Fig. S1), which indicates 
that the amorphous phase was not formed due to sample preparation. 
With increasing tensile pre -strain, more shear bands transform into amorphous bands. The 
TEM-BF images ( Fig. 6a and 6b) taken at [ ̅ ] zone axis demonstrate the numerous bright

3.3. Thermomechanical properties of pre-strained alloys 
TEM characterizations clearly demonstrate that multiple deformation bands accommodate 
plastic deformation of the Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy when subjected to tensile deformation at -
180 ºC . These deformation modes are competing with each other during deformation process. 
These nanoscale bands and their intersections will also act as barriers for dislocation motion, 
significantly increasing the strength of materials. 
Fig. 10a compares the representative room -temperature engineering stress -strain curves of 
the 15% -46% pre -strained samples. The corresponding true stress -strain curves and the strain 
hardening curves of the pre -strained samples ar e presented in Fig. 10b. The inset in Fig. 10a 
shows a plan-view OM image of a typical dog -bone shaped tensile sample after pre -straining. 
Compared with the initial annealed sample (annealing at 1100 ºC for 1 h) prior to pre -straining, 
which possesses a ve ry low yield strength (YS) of 0.23 GPa and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) 
of 0.62 GPa, but an excellent elongation to fracture (EL) of 70%, pre-strained samples at 
different strains show an increased YS and UTS but a reduction in ductility and strain hardening 
rate (Fig. 10b). The 46% pre-strained sample displays a very high YS of 1.0 GPa and UTS of 1.1 
GPa, while a low EL of 10.7%. However, the 36% pre -strained sample exhibit a good 
combination of strength and ductility (YS = 0.9 GPa, UTS = 0.94 GPa, and EL = 25%), which is 
much better than that of various single-phase fcc CrMnFeCoNi alloys [2, 18, 34].

The enhanced strength after pre -straining is attributed to the deformation -induced nano -
structures including twins , hcp lamellae and amorphous bands and their intersections . These 
shear transformation bands provide extensive extra interfaces th at impede the dislocation 
motions, leading to an increase in strength. As summarized in Fig. 10 e, the spacing of both 
adjacent twin boundaries/hcp phase interfaces and ACIs decrease with increasing pre -strain, 
which decreases the mean free path of dislocations and thus gradually enhances the yield 
strength. In the 36% pre-strained sample, we identified the deformed microstructure of more than 
50 grains by using TEM. Approximately 56% of grains are abundant in amorphous bands, while 
other grains mainly contain twins and/or hcp bands ( Fig. 10f ). These nano-spaced twin 
boundaries, fcc phase interfaces and ACIs synergistically contribute to enhanced yield strength 
of 36% pre-strained sample through impeding dislocation mot ions, increasing from 0.23 GPa to 
0.90 GPa . We further investigate thermal stability of the deformation -induced nano -structures 
and their influence on mechanical properties. The 36% pre -strained sample are then subjected to 
tempering at different temperatu res of 500 -700 ºC for 1 h. The engineering stress -strain curves 
measured at room temperature are compared in Fig. 10c. After tempering at 500 -650 ºC for 1 h, 
the YS of the 36% pre -strained sample slightly decreases to 0.78 -0.86 GPa, while the UTS and 
EL remain nearly unchanged, stabilizing at 0.9 GPa and 25%, respectively. This means that the 
mechanical properties of pre -strained samples can remain stable up to 650 ºC. In addition, the 
subsequently tempering gradually recover the strain hardening capacity ( Fig. 10d), 
accompanying with a slight decrease in the yield strength ( Fig. 10c). When tempering at 700 ºC 
for 1 h, YS and UTS decrease remarkably to 0.58 GPa and 0.76 GPa, but the ductility is 
improved to 41% (Fig. 10c). It is noted that the 700 ºC tempered sample still exhibits a high YS 
of 0.58 GPa, which is two times higher than that of the initial annealing sample. 
A slight decrease in strength upon tempering is speculated to be correlated to the 
disappearance of nano-twins and hcp bands, while the remained high strength as compared with 
initial annealed sample is ascribed to the nanoscale amorphous bands strengthening. We further 
investigate the thermal stability of these nanostructures by TEM for 36%-pre-strained samples

stable at 500 ºC. At tempering temperature of 700 ºC, TEM -BF image and SAED pattern in Fig. 
11a show only a small number of residual dislocations, but nano -twins are not detected. 
Surprisingly, extensive nanoscale amorphous bands are retained, as shown in Fig. 1 1b. The 
corresponding HRTEM image of Fig. 11c confirms the amorphous structure of bright bands. It 
should be noted that we do not detect any hcp phase in the 500 -700 ºC tempered samples, thus 
hcp phases are thermally unstable at such temperature ranges. This is consistent with the 
previous experiments and simulations, i.e, CrMnFeCoNi alloys exhibit therma lly stable hcp 
phase below 340 K while fcc phase at high temperatures [35, 36]. As summarized in Fig. 10f, 
700 ºC of tempering leads to the disappear of twins/hcp bands while remains most of the 
amorphous bands, which causes a decrease in yield strength from 0.9 GPa to 0.58 GPa ( Fig. 10c). 
Owing to strengthening effect of amorphous bands, the 700 ºC -tampered sample still exhibits 
much higher yield strength than that of initial 1100 ºC -annealed sample. More details on 
amorphous bands will be discussed in Section 4.

4. Discussion 
The most surprising observation is numerous nanoscale amorphous bands, which has not 
been reported previously in MPEAs upon tensile deformation. When the pre-strained samples are

16 
 
exposed to tempering at 700 ºC for 1 h, twins and hcp bands disappear, lead ing to a slight 
decrease in yield strength but an increase in ductility. However, amorphous bands are survived 
after tempering at 700 ºC . They provide effective barriers for dislocation motion and nano -twin 
propagation, resulting in considerable strengthening effect. In our opinion, the much better 
combination of yield strength and ductility can be achieved through further optim izing the pre -
straining and tempering. In what follows, we discuss amorphization mechanisms and 
strengthening effect of amorphous bands. 
4.1. Amorphization mechanisms 
The chemical composition of the amorphous bands is compared with that of the fcc matrix 
by using STEM -EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 12a1-a6) and line scan ( Fig. 12b1-b2). No 
detectable composition difference between amorphous bands and fcc matrix is measured. The 
elemental mapping shows that all elements exhibit homogeneous distribution in both amorphous 
bands and fcc phase matrix. The STEM -EDS line scan across the ACI demonstrates that the 
average chemical compositions in both amorphous bands and fcc matrix are measured to be Cr -
25 at.%, Mn -19 at.%, Fe -21 at.%, Co -20 at.% and Ni -15 at.%, as shown in Fig. 12b2. The 
chemical composition analysis indicates that the formation of the amorphous phase is not due to 
a deformation-induced chemical reaction or oxides. Therefore, the crystal -to-amorphous 
transition in present alloy can be considered as a polymorphic solid-state amorphization (SSA) . 
The polymorphic SSA is widely observed in almost every category of crystalline material s, 
including intermetallic compounds, ceramics and covalent pure elements [37-39]. 
Various mechanical processes can partially or fully amorphize crystalline materials, 
including high-pressure treatments, severe plastic deformation, mechanical alloying, irradiation 
with ions or electrons, inter -diffusion in multiple layers, hydrogen absorption, or decompression 
of solids [37, 40-47]. From the thermodynamic driving force perspective, amorphization starts 
from the massive displacement of atoms into metastable positions and occurs when the free 
energy of the crystalline phase is higher than that of the amorphous phase [48-50]. From the 
kinetic hindrance perspective, the formation of metastable amorphous phase requires the kinetic 
hindrances which block the formation of more stable equilibrium crystalline phase [48]. It should 
be noted that deformation-induced SSA under stresses can take place in the elastic regime at

## Page 18

17 
 
extremely high strain rate, or in the plastic stage at severe strain [40]. At an extremely high strain 
rate, there is no enough time for activation of plastic deformation modes such as dislocation slips 
or even the faster twinning mode . Thus, the large elastic strains promote the crystalline phase 
mechanically unstable due to the loss of shear rigidity, which leads to amorphization [51-53]. 
In addition, w hen the crystalline phase is stressed to an ultra -high strain, severe plastic 
deformation generates and stores high -density of defects (such as point defects, dislocations, 
twins, etc.), which results in amorphization when the free energy of the crystalline phase is 
increased to a point higher than that of amorphous p hase [48]. As described above, the 
similarities in terms of the orientation between amorphous bands and shear bands indicate that 
deformation-induced amorphization in current alloy upon tensile deformation at low temperature 
may be due to significant dislocation accumulations in side shear bands. When dislocation 
accumulation in a constrained shear bands raises the free energy of original fcc phase to a point 
higher than that of amorphous phase, the energy difference drives amorphization. This 
hypothesis is examined by characterizing the amorphous bands via LAADF imaging. As shown 
in Fig. 7b , the amorphous bands appear much darker (negative strain contrast) than the 
surrounding fcc matrix, suggesting that the severe strain induced by dislocation accumulations in 
shear bands are relieved by amorphization.

amorphous bands in this non-equiatomic Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 alloy may be related to low SFE. 
The SFE of this alloy (approximately 3.5 mJ/m 2 [28]) is much lower than that of other 
CrMnFeCoNi systems, such as 20 mJ/m2 of equiatomic CrMnFeCoNi [16]. The much lower SFE 
promotes the formation of LCs, twins and hcp bands. These defects introduce numerous barriers 
to block the motion of dislocations. Thus, shear banding happens as an alternative deformation 
mode. The significant dislocation accumulation inside shear bands results in amorphization. 
4.2. The roles of deformation-induced amorphous bands 
To understand the role of nanoscale amorphous bands on the mechanical response of the 
nanostructured alloy, the 700 ºC tempered sample is then deformed to different strains at room 
temperature. This sample contains extensive nanoscale amorphous bands prior to tensile testing , 
but deformation nano-twins and hcp phase bands are unlikely survived. 
Fig. 14a shows the deformation microstructures at a strain of 5%. At this stage, the 
deformation mode is primarily dominated by the motion of dislocations . One slip band is 
connected to two adjacent ACIs, which implies that, in addition to grain boundaries (GBs), ACIs 
can act as sources for dislocation nucleation. With increasing strain to 41%, more dislocations 
nucleate from ACIs and glide into surrounding fcc matrix. The subsequent dislocation 
interactions lead to heavy dislocation storages and pileups in fcc matrix, rather than at ACIs, as 
shown in Fig. 14b. The lack of dislocation pileups near ACIs suggests that dis locations around 
the ACIs may be attracted and then annihilated in amorphous bands. This is consistent with the 
previous results in crystalline copper/copper -zirconium glass nano -laminates, which 
demonstrates that ACIs act as not only sources for dislocation nucleation but also natural sinks of

## Page 22

21 
 
dislocations, according to molecular dynamics simulations and experiments [54, 55] . Fig. 1 4c 
shows the formation of many deformation nano -twins in fcc matrix. It is clea rly seen that b oth 
ends of nano -twins terminate at the ACIs, indicating that Shockley partials nucleate from ACIs 
and propagate in the fcc matrix, and then are captured by the other ACIs, leaving behind twins or 
SFs. The HRTEM images of Fig. 14d and e clearly reveal that SFs and nano-twins are blocked 
by ACIs. Both the dislocations and nano -twins are not able to penetrate amorphous bands . 
Owing to the high -density of ACIs, it is believed that ACIs and amorphous bands play a crucial 
role (as the GB strengthening mechanism) in enhancing yield strength of our pre -strained and 
tempered samples. 
Another striking feature of amorphous bands is that they can be bended to plastically co -
deform with the fcc matrix during deformation , as shown in Fig. 14b. HRTEM observations of 
pre-strained samples after tension to fracture at room temperature reveal that nanoscale 
amorphous bands do not display shear bands or nano-cracks, indicating the homogeneous plastic 
flow in the amorphous bands by clusters of atoms that undergo cooperative shear displacements 
(shear transformation zones, STZs). The lack of shear banding in the nanoscale amorphous bands 
may be due to fact that the size of stressed amorphous region is insufficient for STZs to develop 
into mature shear bands [56, 57]. It has also been reported that nanoscale metallic glasses are 
often exhibit large ductility upon tensile loading at room temperature, without shear banding. In 
contrast to GBs or crystalline-crystalline interfaces (CCIs) where stress/strain concentrations 
could build up during deformation due to the slip/shear transfer incompatibility near the GBs and 
CCIs [58], slip transfer at the ACIs is more easy and efficient in reducing stress/strain 
concentrations because the STZs in the amorphous materials are omnidirectional. This can delay 
decohesion and fracture initiation at the ACIs, and therefore improve ductility . The critical 
importance of dislocation slip mechanisms modulated by nanoscale metallic glass in sustaining 
the good ductility has been revealed by molecular dynamics simulations, which demonstrated 
that large stress concentrations at the ACIs can be d isrupted and dissolved by the amorphous 
layers [54]. The deformation-driven amorphization may provide a new promising design strategy 
for single -phase fcc MPEAs with the aim of improving yield strength while remaining good 
ductility.

5. Conclusion 
Multiple shear transformation bands, such as SFs bands, twins, hcp lamellae, shear bands 
and amorphous bands, can be generated in a bulk non-equiatomic fcc-structured 
Cr26Mn20Fe20Co20Ni14 MPEA via low -temperature tensile deformation. The formation and 
intersection of these nanoscale bands not only accommodate p lastic deformation but also act as 
strong barriers for dislocation motion, strengthening materials. The thermal stability of nano -
structures is investigated by subsequent tempering and TEM characterizations. Results show that 
low-temperature deformation-induced nanoscale amorphous bands exhibit much higher thermal 
stability than other bands. 
The formation of extensive nanoscale amorphous bands is attributed to the significant 
dislocation accumulation in a constrained region inside shear bands, which raises the free energy 
of original fcc phase to a point higher than that of amorphous phase, then the energy difference 
drives amorphization. Introducing nanoscale amorphous bands can achieve three key benefits: (i) 
ACI hardening (ACIs not only act as hig h-capacity sources for dislocations nucleation but also 
barriers for dislocation motion); (ii) large stress concentrations at the ACIs can be d isrupted and 
dissolved by the amorphous bands (ACIs act as natural sinks of dislocations, averting dislocation 
pileups at ACIs ), which delay decohesion and fracture initiation at the ACIs; (iii) high thermal

## Page 24

23 
 
stability of amorphous bands enables to increase strain hardening capacity through the tempering 
at relatively higher temperatures without sacrificing the high y ield strength. In our opinion, the 
much better combination of yield strength and ductility can be achieved through optimizing the 
low-temperature deformation and tempering. Our work presents a novel design strategy for 
developing high-strength and high -ductility fcc-structured MPEAs via introducing deformation -
induced nanoscale amorphous bands.
