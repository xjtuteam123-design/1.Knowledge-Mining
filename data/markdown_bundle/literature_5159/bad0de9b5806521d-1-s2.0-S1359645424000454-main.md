ABSTRACT 
To support the development of refractory complex, concentrated alloys (RCCAs), a clear understanding of the 
effect of temperature on strength is needed. Body-centered cubic (BCC) refractory metals and dilute refractory 
alloys show a strong temperature dependence of yield stress ( σ
y
) at low and high temperatures, with a relatively 
temperature-independent regime in between. RCCAs may introduce important changes in deformation and 
strengthening mechanisms, so it is not clear if RCCAs will show the same thermal dependencies. The objective of 
this work is to answer the question, “ Is the temperature dependence of strength similar or different for RCCAs 
compared to BCC refractory elements and dilute refractory alloys ? ” We evaluate σ
y 
vs. temperature for 61 RCCAs by 
analyzing data curated from the literature. We find that σ
y 
increases progressively from refractory BCC elements, 
to dilute alloys, to single- and multi-phase RCCAs. Single-phase RCCAs show the same three thermal regimes, but 
the stresses are higher and the intermediate plateau is shorter and is sometimes steeper. The thermal dependence 
of σ
y 
in multi-phase RCCAs shows more substantial differences. Six factors that contribute to the higher σ
y 
of 
RCCAs are discussed: (i) higher solute concentration; (ii) dispersion in atomic sizes; (iii) the shear modulus 
magnitude; (iv) the type of principal elements; (v) the solvus temperature of predicted secondary phases; and (vi) 
microstructure. Based on the present observations and analyses, suggestions for future research are made, 
especially regarding improved models for the effect of temperature on strength. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory complex, concentrated alloys (RCCAs) were conceived 
and developed as a new class of high-temperature structural materials, 
and are the first alloy family to use the high-entropy concept beyond the 
founding palette of 3d transition metals [1] . High-entropy alloys (HEAs) 
and complex, concentrated alloys (CCAs) based on 3d transition ele -
ments often have face-centered cubic (FCC), single-phase microstruc -
tures that expand the large austenite phase field (also known as the 
‘gamma loop ’ ) in the Fe-Ni-Cr system. Similar electronegativities, 
similar atom sizes and low mixing enthalpies of the 3d transition metals 
favor extended solid solutions in these alloys. By contrast, the Group IV, 
V and VI refractory elements in RCCAs display a larger range in both 
electronegativities and atom sizes. Refractory elements in Group V and 
VI are all body-centered cubic (BCC) with extensive mutual solid 
solubility, and the Group IV refractory elements are hexagonal 
close-packed (HCP) at room temperature but BCC at higher tempera -
tures. As a result, RCCAs and refractory HEAs (RHEAs) often display 
single-phase BCC microstructures, but other phases such as disordered 
HCP and the ordered intermetallic B2 and Laves phases can also form [ 2 , 
3 ]. Scientific efforts on RCCAs have increased rapidly – the number of 
publications has grown by nearly 60% each year for the past seven years. 
Many properties and mechanisms have been studied in RCCAs, and 
development as high-temperature structural materials remains a pri -
mary motivation. 
The temperature dependence of strength in BCC metals and alloys is 
not straightforward, and the degree to which strength decreases with 
increasing temperature depends on a range of features. Alloy melting 
temperature, dissolution temperature of second phases in multi-phase 
alloys, and the temperature dependence of elastic properties of the

The yield stress decreases only modestly with increasing temperature in 
this range, perhaps due to the weak temperature dependence of features 
that influence strength and deformation, such as the shear modulus ( μ ) 
and the dislocation Burgers vector, b [ 17 , 18 ]. 
The influence of temperature on the deformation and strength of BCC 
metals and alloys has been an active field of research for many decades – 
an introduction to this extensive literature can be found in selected re -
views [ 5 , 19 , 20 ]. This large body of work provides a foundation to un -
derstand the influence of temperature on the strength of RCCAs. 
However, RCCAs have important differences from BCC elements and 
dilute alloys that may give different results. The main constituent ele -
ments in RCCAs, called principal elements, are sufficiently concentrated 
so that solute-solute interactions become important and cannot be 
ignored as in dilute or ideal solutions. Further, RCCAs are complex – they 
contain 3 or more principal elements. Together, these RCCA character -
istics may introduce important differences in deformation and 
strengthening mechanisms compared to BCC elements and dilute BCC 
alloys. For example, the relative mobility of screw and edge dislocations 
are much different in some RCCAs compared to traditional BCC mate -
rials [ 8 , 21 , 22 ], and new strengthening models have been required to 
accurately predict the strength of CCAs [ 8 , 9 , 23 , 24 ]. 
As a result, it is uncertain if the temperature dependence of strength 
in RCCAs follows the same trends that have been established for BCC 
elements and dilute alloys. Early datasets of strength vs temperature 
were insufficient to establish the thermal dependence of strength in 
RCCAs as a class of materials, but increasing availability of data now 
makes it feasible to consider this more broadly. The objective of this 
paper is to answer the question: “ Is the temperature dependence of strength 
similar or different for RCCAs as a class of materials compared to BCC re -
fractory elements and dilute, BCC refractory alloys? ” To answer this 
question, we evaluate σ
y 
vs. temperature for a wide range of RCCAs by 
curating and analyzing a large dataset collected from the literature and 
then analyze and discuss the observed relationships based on the alloy 
composition, microstructure and thermodynamic and elastic properties. 
From this work, general features and trends for the temperature- 
dependent strength of RCCAs are established. The new information 
from these analyses can be used to guide the future development of 
RCCAs for high temperature structural applications. 
2. Approach 
Yield strength ( σ
y
) as a function of temperature was collected from 
the literature for refractory elements, commercial refractory alloys, and 
both single- and multi-phase RCCAs. Since the objective of this study is 
to explore the behavior of BCC materials, only RCCAs with a single- 
phase BCC microstructure, or multi-phase RCCAs with a dominant 
BCC phase, are included. A minimum second-phase volume fraction of 
5% was used to indicate a multi-phase alloy, and no consideration was 
given to the distribution of additional phases. The objective is to study 
the influence of temperature on σ
y
, and so datasets that minimize 
extrinsic effects are used. Recrystallized or annealed materials are 
preferred to minimize the influence of a wrought microstructure on σ
y
, 
and materials with low interstitial content are favored. Complete and 
consistent datasets with a sufficient number of test temperatures to 
establish the behavior across the three thermal regimes of BCC materials 
are sought. Many RCCA datasets include only three or four test tem -
peratures – this may be inadequate to illustrate the trends and transi -
tions across the three different thermal regimes. As a result, none of the 
RCCA datasets with only three temperatures are plotted in the following 
analyses, and only a select number of multi-phase RCCAs (where data -
sets with a larger number of test temperatures are limited) with four 
temperatures are plotted. For single-phase RCCAs, only datasets with six 
or more test temperatures are plotted. All datasets for refractory ele -
ments and commercial alloys include six or more temperatures. 
Although not plotted, datasets with fewer test temperatures are never -
theless used to evaluate details of the trends observed. 
A total of 61 unique RCCAs are obtained from the literature search. 
In all cases, the data are for compressive loading. Melting ( T
m

alloy is an important consideration and is specified in the present 
database. Single-phase and multi-phase alloys are analyzed separately 
and compared with each other. Many alloys characterized as single- 
phase in the cited studies are predicted to be multi-phase by CAL -
PHAD, and may ultimately be multi-phase after long-term annealing, 
equilibrium conditions. These are treated as single-phase in the present 
analysis, and the reported mechanical properties may correspond to a 
supersaturated, metastable single BCC phase. In almost every case, only 
a single strength measurement is made at each temperature. In the few 
cases where multiple measurements are available for the same alloy and 
process condition, the average uncertainty in stress measurements is 
± 8.5%. This is consistent with experience in the literature [25] .

Deformation in BCC alloys is intrinsically complex due to the 
extended nature of screw dislocation cores. Deformation may include 
one or more of four potential mechanisms: kink nucleation; kink prop -
agation; stress-driven jog dragging; and diffusion-assisted collapse and 
motion of jogs [ 6 , 7 , 11 , 66 ]. A range of additional deformation and 
strengthening mechanisms can include cross-slip, climb, dislocation 
multiplication and dislocation-dislocation interactions. A range of in -
teractions may exist between the RCCA principal elements and each of 
these mechanisms, giving a wide range of alloying effects. Further, 
multi-phase RCCAs introduce additional features that influence the 
temperature dependence of strength. Six factors that may influence the 
strength of RCCAs are considered below. 
4.1.1. Total solute concentration 
Classical solid solution hardening theories show an explicit influence 
of solute concentration on strength in dilute alloys [ 67 , 68 ]. Consistent 
with this expectation, the six commercial alloys in this study show a 
systematic increase in σ
y 
with total solute concentration ( Fig. 2 ). 
Extending this analysis to RCCAs is not straightforward – the total solute 
concentration is not obvious and may be as high as 100%. This is 
consistent with the ‘average atom ’ model, where each principal element 
is considered in turn to be a solute in a solvent comprised of an ‘average ’ 
atom given by a weighted average of all the atoms in the alloy [ 69 ]. 
Here, we use a more conservative method, where the solvent is taken as 
the most concentrated element and the total solute concentration is the 
sum of the remaining concentrations. Mo, Nb, V and W are used as a 
baseline at 0 at.% total solute concentration. Only elements, commercial 
alloys and the single-phase RCCAs shown in Fig. 4 are analyzed. To 
reduce temperature effects, differences in testing strain rates and initial 
microstructural conditions (recovered vs recrystallized), we analyze 
strengths at the upper end of the intermediate plateau ( σ
hi
). 
With one exception (NbTaTi), RCCA strengths range from 504 to 778 
MPa with total solute concentrations of 66.7 – 80%. These strengths 
range from about 25% higher than the strongest commercial alloy (WC- 
3009) to about 100% higher. Combined with σ
hi 
values for the refractory 
elements and commercial alloys in this study, these values are broadly 
consistent with a power law dependence on total solute concentration. A 
square root dependence of strength on solute concentration was initially 
found for dilute alloys [ 67 ], and it was later shown that a power law 
exponent of 2 / 3 was supported by a broader range of data [ 68 ]. Power 
law exponents of ½ and 2 / 3 give equally good consistency with the 
present data, though neither gives a compelling fit. The wide spread in 
σ
y 
and σ
y 
μ ( T ) values for RCCAs with similar total solute concentrations 
supports the concern that additional features must be considered. This 
simple analysis does not consider chemistry – the identities of both the 
‘solvent ’ and solute elements are expected to have an important influ -
ence on the degree of strengthening. The number of principal elements is 
also sometimes considered as a contributing factor. These topics are 
discussed in a later subsection. 
The data for NbTaTi are significantly below the common trend. A 
detailed modeling effort correctly predicts the strength of NbTaTi from

predicted strength include similar atomic volumes, which give low 
strengthening of edge dislocations, and low interaction energies be -
tween solutes and screw dislocations. The dispersion in atomic sizes is

4.1.3. The effect of shear modulus 
The magnitude of μ ( T ) has an obvious, first-level effect on σ
y
. 
Comparing Fig. 4 b,c shows that the relative ranking of σ
y

after normalizing by μ ( T ) . Careful inspection shows that RCCAs with low 
normalized strengths ( Fig. 4 c) can nevertheless have higher absolute σ
y 
values ( Fig. 4 b) when they contain principal elements with high μ ( T ) , 
such as Cr, Mo and/or W. Combined with significant flexibility in the 
constitution of RCCAs, using principal elements with high μ ( T ) gives a 
simple, easily predictable approach to increasing σ
y

Two separate bands of normalized strengths are suggested by Fig. 4 c, 
but evaluation of the complete set of RCCAs from Table 3 shows a 
continuous band of normalized intermediate strengths as described 
earlier. There are also possible inaccuracies associated with the ROM 
approximation of μ ( T ) . Al and Cr each have only one electron in their 
outer shells (3p
1 
for Al and 4s
1 
for Cr), which can form hybridized co -
valent bonds with other elements. As a result, Al forms complex inter -
metallic phases with all of the RCCA principal elements, and Cr forms 
Laves phases with Hf, Nb, Ta, Ti and Zr. These covalent bonds are 
generally shorter and more stable, and may be stiffer than the metallic 
bonding of the pure elements [ 72 ]. Even at concentrations too dilute to 
form intermetallic phases, these elements may produce some degree of 
chemical short-range order (CSRO) in solid solution alloys. CSRO can 
create a higher number of these stiffer bonds than anticipated for 
random solutions. As a result, μ ( T ) may be higher for solid solutions with 
Al and/or Cr than is predicted by a simple rule-of-mixtures approach. 
Further, twelve of the 14 RCCAs with the highest σ
y
/ μ ( T ) also have 
≥ 40% Group IV elements. The Group IV elements (Ti, Zr, Hf) all have an 
allotropic HCP to BCC transformation. The shear moduli for the BCC 
structures are much lower than for the HCP form – the RT shear modulus 
for BCC Hf is 81% of the HCP value, and the BCC shear moduli are less 
than 50% of the HCP values for both Ti and Zr [14] . The effect of these 
elements on the shear moduli of RCCAs may be different from that 
predicted by a rule-of-mixtures. Specifically, the presence of BCC ele -
ments from Groups V and VI has a profound effect on the Group IV el -
ements, coaxing the latter to adopt a BCC structure with significantly 
lower shear moduli. This is independent of a rule-of-mixtures effect, and 
it is possible that the HCP elements may have a reciprocal influence on 
either the magnitude or the temperature dependence of the intrinsic 
elastic properties of the Group V and Group VI BCC elements. Such an 
effect is conceivable but is not included in this analysis. This may 
introduce an additional uncertainty in the normalized strengths of 
RCCAs with high concentrations of Group IV elements. It remains to be 
seen if the RCCAs in the upper band are unusually strong, or if these 
appear to be artificially high because the rule-of-mixtures μ ( T ) values 
used here for normalization are too small. Additional experimental data 
on the elastic properties of RCCAs are suggested for further research.

4.1.4. Influence of the number and type of principal elements 
Additional factors may influence the broad range in RCCA proper -
ties, including the number of principal elements and the general types of 
principal elements. Only three ternary RCCAs are plotted in Fig. 4 c 
(MoNbTi, NbTaTi and WC-3009), and all fall in the lower band. How -
ever, the total dataset ( Table 3 ) contains seven different ternary single- 
phase RCCAs, and one of these (NbTiZr) falls in the upper band. RCCAs 
with both four and five elements populate both bands, and there seems 
to be no consistent trend when considering equimolar and non- 
equimolar alloys. Of course, the concentrations of principal elements 
are also an important consideration. Nevertheless, this analysis suggests 
that the number and concentrations of principal elements by themselves 
are not sufficient to determine the relative strength of an RCCA. 
The specific types of principal elements must also be considered. 
Each solute species exerts a distinct resisting force on dislocations 
through dislocation-solute interaction energies. These interaction en -
ergies depend on the identity of the solute as well as the principal ele -
ments that make up the RCCA, so the number of interaction energies that 
must be considered is large. Interaction energies are typically deter -
mined via ab initio methods or high-quality interatomic potentials, 
making an analysis of broad trends across many different RCCAs diffi -
cult. As a result, a detailed analysis is beyond the scope of this discus -
sion. Instead, trends are sought to identify the general types of principal 
elements that dominate the upper and lower bands of normalized 
strengths in Fig. 4 c. To frame this analysis, we consider elements that are 
not typical of Group V and Group VI BCC refractory elements. We focus 
on Group IV elements (Ti, Zr, Hf), which are HCP over a broad range of 
test temperatures considered here, and on the compound-forming ele -
ments, Al and Cr. 
As mentioned above, Al and Cr are compound formers that may 
produce some degree of covalent bonding and CSRO, even in single- 
phase RCCAs. This can contribute to strengthening by providing an 
additional barrier to dislocation motion. In support of this, RCCAs with 
Al and/or Cr are all in the upper half of normalized strengths in Fig. 4 c, 
and five of the eight strongest single-phase RCCAs in this study contain 
Al when ranked by σ
y
/ μ ( T ) . Group IV elements also have an important 
influence on the strength of BCC alloys, and Zr, Ti and Hf are commonly 
used to strengthen commercial refractory alloys. As broad trends, 13 of 
17 RCCAs in the upper band have ≥ 40 at.% Group IV elements and only 
three of 17 such RCCAs are in the lower band. Of the four upper-band 
RCCAs with < 40 at.% Group IV elements, three have Al and/or 
Cr + Nb + Ti. The last upper-band RCCA with < 40 at.% Group IV ele -
ments, NbTaTiV, has a high dispersion in atomic sizes, supporting higher 
strength.

4.1.7. Summary: RCCA strengthening mechanisms 
Six specific postulates are discussed above to explore the origin of 
strengthening in RCCAs. A positive correlation is seen in each case, 
along with scatter that can be significant. Further, exceptions are found 
for each comparison. It is likely that a more complete understanding 
requires consideration of two or more of these six concepts simulta -
neously. As an example, two RCCAs known for exceptional high tem -
perature strength (MoNbTaVW and MoNbTaW) have no Group IV 
elements, but they have high solute concentrations, high μ ( T ) values, 
and two of the three highest dispersions in atomic volume, δ V . Most of 
these effects are already included in state-of-the-art strength models for 
BCC RCCAs [ 8 , 9 ], but the influence of CSRO and nascent phase in -
stabilities in RCCAs that have not been fully equilibrated remain open 
issues. Studies to develop quantitative connections between these fac -
tors is suggested for future work. More extensive experimental mea -
surements of RCCA elastic properties are also needed. As a reminder, all 
of the RCCA data have been collected in compression, and so some of 
these RCCAs are likely to be brittle in tension at RT.

5. Summary 
The yield stress ( σ
y
) in body-centered cubic (BCC) refractory ele -
ments and dilute alloys shows three regimes: a strong thermal depen -
dence of σ
y 
is observed at low and high temperatures and σ
y 
is relatively 
insensitive to temperature between these regions. Single-phase complex, 
concentrated refractory alloys (RCCAs) show the same three regimes, 
but at stresses that are almost always higher, and the intermediate 
plateau extends over a narrower range of temperatures. Multi-phase 
RCCAs show several important differences from the other refractory 
materials in this study. They have an intermediate plateau and a strong 
decrease in σ
y 
at high-temperatures, but a distinct low-temperature 
regime is seen for only one RCCA in this dataset. The multi-phase 
RCCAs in this study have no data below RT and a rather large temper -
ature increment between RT and the next highest test temperature, 
making it difficult to resolve the nature of the σ
y 
thermal dependence at 
low temperatures. The transition from the intermediate to the high- 
temperature regions occurs at a lower absolute temperature and a 
lower fraction of the liquidus temperature, T
liquid
. These result from the 
generally lower T
liquid 
values for multi-phase compared to single-phase 
RCCAs and from the dissolution of strengthening phases at the solvus 
temperature, T
solvus
. Finally, multi-phase RCCAs appear to show a rela -
tively temperature-insensitive σ
y 
at the highest test temperatures. 
Normalizations by T
liquid 
and the temperature-adjusted shear 
modulus ( μ ( T ) ) provide useful insights. The transition temperatures 
from low-to-intermediate behavior ( T
lo
) and from the intermediate-to- 
high-temperature response ( T
hi
) are more clearly aligned after normal -
izing test temperatures by the appropriate T
liquid 
values. This normali -
zation is less useful for multi-phase RCCAs due to the complicating 
influence of T
solvus
. A significant compression of the data is achieved 
when simultaneously normalizing test temperature by T
liquid 
and σ
y 
by 
μ ( T ) . This dual normalization more clearly displays the three thermal 
regions and the transitions between them. It also clearly separates ele -
ments, commercial alloys and single-phase RCCAs. Dual normalization 
is not provided for multi-phase RCCAs, since the method used to 
approximate μ ( T ) (rule-of-mixtures of elemental shear moduli) is not 
appropriate for multi-phase alloys. 
A detailed analysis of the extent and slopes of the intermediate 
regime is provided for refractory elements, commercial refractory al -
loys, and single-phase RCCAs. Plotting the normalized yield stress, σ
y
/ 
μ ( T ) , against T / T
liquid 
shows that the slope of the intermediate plateau is 
zero within experimental error for two BCC refractory elements (Mo and 
W), two commercial alloys (TZM and WC-3009) and three single-phase 
RCCAs (MoNbTaVW, MoNbTaW and NbTaTi). This supports earlier 
suggestions that the main effect of temperature on σ
y 
in the intermediate 
regime is to reduce μ ( T ) for many refractory elements and alloys. The 
remaining commercial alloys and single-phase RCCAs have slopes that 
are clearly higher than zero, showing that some additional thermal 
dependence operates. The mechanism that underlies this behavior is not 
known. Current strengthening models do not accurately capture this 
intermediate behavior, especially, the presence of a distinct transition 
from a strong thermal dependence at low temperatures to a significantly 
reduced thermal dependence that gives a constant rate of decrease in σ
y 
over an extended temperature range. 
An important result of the present work is to quantify the degree to 
which commercial refractory alloys are stronger than the BCC elements 
upon which they are based, and the degree to which single-phase RCCAs 
are stronger than commercial alloys. Multi-phase RCCAs are the stron -
gest of the materials studied here at low and intermediate temperatures 
but are weaker at high test temperatures, due to the fact that they have 
not specifically been designed with high precipitate solvus tempera -
tures. Six different mechanisms for the higher strengths of RCCAs are 
introduced and discussed. These include: (i) total solute concentration; 
(ii) dispersion in atomic sizes; (iii) the magnitude of the shear modulus; 
(iv) the type of principal elements; (v) the solvus temperature of addi -
tional, kinetically restricted low temperature phases; and (vi) micro -
structure. All six mechanisms are important for single-phase materials 
and the last emphasizes the size, volume fraction, and distribution of 
secondary phases, and is particularly important for multi-phase RCCAs. 
As suggestions for future work, more experimental measurements of 
strength in small temperature intervals are needed to better define the 
low-temperature stress dependence of single-phase and multi-phase 
RCCAs, and to better define the transition between these two regimes. 
Revised strength models are suggested to better represent the interme -
diate regime of single-phase RCCAs. A better understanding of the inter- 
relationships between the six strengthening contributions may provide a 
stronger foundation for the development of RCCAs with a good balance 
of properties. Finally, experimental measurements of elastic properties 
and further analysis of the unique characteristics of diffusion in highly 
concentrated refractories is needed.
