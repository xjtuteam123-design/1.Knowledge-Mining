Abstract
A group ofTi 60Cu14Ni12Sn4M10 (M=Nb, Ta, Mo) alloys was prepared using arc melting and copper mold casting. The as-
prepared alloys have a composite microstructure containing a micrometer-sized dendritic b-Ti(M) phase dispersed in a
nanocrystalline matrix. These new alloys exhibit a low Young’s modulus in the range of59–103 GPa, and a high yield strength of
1037–1755 MPa, together with large plastic strains. The combination of high strength and low elastic modulus offers potential
advantages in biomedical applications.

1. Introduction
Since stainless steel was ﬁrst used as an implant
material in surgery in the early 1930s, a number of
metallic materials such as Vitallium and other Co-base
alloys, and Ti or Ti-base alloys, have been developed for
biomedical applications [1]. With a continuously in-
creasing population in the aged group, large demands of
metallic biomaterials are expected for many years to
come. New biomaterials that are much safer and of
much higher quality, in terms ofboth biomedical and
mechanical properties, are therefore of interest. Re-
search and development ofnew metallic biomaterials, in
particular, are currently active at a fast pace worldwide.
In recent years, Ti-base alloys have received much
attention due to their excellent biocompatibility, low
density, excellent corrosion resistance and good balance
ofmechanical properties [1,2]. By either modifying the
available Ti-base alloys or exploring new compositions,
one can achieve better performance of the Ti-base alloys
in terms oftheir biomedical and mechanical properties
or workability [1]. To improve workability, some strong
b-phase forming elements such as Nb, Ta, Mo, and Zr,
and some b-phase improving elements such as Fe, Cr, V,
Al, and Sn, are widely used in biomedical Ti-base alloys
[1]. Due to the high plasticity ofthe b-phase structure,
the b-phase-type Ti-alloys can be easily shaped or
deformed by thermomechanical processing or even at
room temperature. The low elastic modulus ofthe b-
type Ti-alloys meets the demands ofthe implants [1,2].
The low rigidity of the implants is considered effective
for promoting bone healing and remodeling because
they should imitate the elastic modulus ofcortical bone
[3]. In addition to the low elastic modulus, a high
strength, especially a high fatigue strength and long life
are required for these Ti-base alloys. Therefore, some
alloying elements such as Cu, Co, Ni, Si are used to
strengthen the alloys. However, caution should be
exercised when alloying elements are added. For
example, V exhibits high cytotoxicity [4,5]. Al may
induce senile dementia[4,5]. Ni is also considered having
some bio-toxicity [6].
In general, a biomedical Ti-base alloy should have (1)
a reasonably low density; (2) little or no cytotoxic metals
in its composition; (3) a high strength and long fatigue

2. Experimental
The composition ofTi-Cu-Ni-Sn-Nb(Ta, Mo) was
selected based on the design principles for bulk
nanostructured composites [7]. The refractory metals,
Nb, Ta and Mo are used for forming micrometer-sized
b-phase precipitates that contributes to the ductility of
the alloy. Cu and Ni are added to promote
the nanocrystalline microstructure that contributes to
the high strength ofthe materials. Sn can improve the
formation of the b-phase, and helps to form the
nanocrystalline microstructure as well. We discovered
that the composition Ti
60Cu14Ni12Sn4Nb(Ta, Mo) 10
leads to a good combination ofnanostructured phase
and dendritic b-phase, and a good balance ofstrength,
modulus, and plasticity.
The alloys were prepared by arc melting the
mixture ofthe pure elements on a water-cooled copper
hearth in argon atmosphere. Ingots with dimensions
60 mm/C215 mm/C212 mm were obtained. Alternatively,
copper mold casting was also used for preparing cast
cylinders with 2 and 3 mm diameters. The microstruc-
tures and the phases ofthe prepared ingots were
characterized by using a JEOL-JSM6400 scanning
electron microscope (SEM) with an electron microprobe
analysis unit, and a JEM-2010 electron transmission
microscope equipped with a UHR pole-piece at 200 kV
accelerating voltage, as well as by using a Siemens D
5000 X-ray diffraction (XRD) facility with CuK a
radiation. Rectangular samples 3 mm /C23m m/C26m m
were machined for compressive tests using an Instron
8562 testing machine at a strain rate of1 /C210
/C04/s at
room temperature.

3.2. Mechanical properties of the alloys
For as-arc-melted alloys, the stress–strain curves show
an elastic deformation stage followed by a long plastic
deformation stage with signiﬁcant work-hardening after
yielding, as shown in Fig. 5 . From the ﬁgure, one
observes coexisting high strength and large plasticity.
The Mo-containing alloy exhibits a high yield strength
of1169 MPa, but its Young’s modulus is relatively large
at 103 GPa. The plastic strain reaches 18.37%. The Nb-
containing alloy exhibits a yield strength of1052 MPa
and the lowest Young’s modulus of59 GPa. The plastic
strain reaches as high as 21.34%. Such a combination of
properties is attractive for biomedical applications. The
Ta-containing alloy exhibits a yield strength of
1037 MPa and a Young’s modulus of71 GPa. The
plastic strain reaches 16.5%.
For the as-cast alloys, the yield strength is signiﬁ-
cantly improved due to the ﬁner grain sizes formed at
the relative high cooling rate during the casting. But, the
plasticity is markedly reduced. The stress–strain curves
for the as-cast 3 and 2 mm diameter cylinders of the
Ti
60Cu14Ni12Sn4Ta10 alloy are also shown in Fig. 5.A
smaller diameter implies a higher cooling rate during
solidiﬁcation [9]. The 3 mm diameter samples exhibit
1527 MPa yield strength and 73 GPa Young’s modulus,
as well as 5.98% plastic strain. At an increased cooling
rate, the 2 mm diameter samples have a yield strength of
1755 MPa and 4.65% plastic strain, i.e. are stronger but
less ductile. The Young’s modulus of69 GPa is close to
that of3 mm diameter samples and as-arc-melted alloys,
revealing that the Young’s modulus is insensitive to the
cooling rate. On the other hand, an annealing treatment
at 973 K for 10 min caused a signiﬁcant increase in
Young’s modulus for the as-cast Ti
60Cu14Ni12Sn4Ta10
alloy, as shown in Fig. 5 . The plasticity is severely
decreased. This indicates that the mechanical properties
ofthe present alloys may be adjusted by subsequent heat
treatment. For an easy comparison, all the compressive
test data for our alloys are listed in Table 1.
Since cast pores can form in the alloys[10], the tensile
plasticity, which is very sensitive to the cast ﬂaws, will be
lower than that observed in compression tests. This was
conﬁrmed in preliminary tensile tests, which showed that
the plastic strains are reduced in comparison with the values
in Table 1. In applications under large tensile stresses, the
casting methods for the present alloys need to be improved
to avoid the cast defects. More work is needed in this
direction and is beyond the scope ofthis report.

additions ofCu-Ni-Sn in the Ti alloy [13] can induce the

ture, which contributes to the high strength ofthe alloy,
in accord with the well-known Hall–Patch relationship.
On the other hand, the formation of the nanostructure
also depends on the cooling rate applied during
solidiﬁcation. Arc melting on a water-cooled copper
hearth results in an eutectic microstructure with grain
sizes in the 200–300 nm range. Copper mold casting,
however, reduces the size down to the scale of30–70 nm
because a higher cooling rate is achieved by this method
[9]. A smaller grain size corresponds to a higher yield
strength (Table 1).
Nanoscale grains alone may be prone to localized
shear banding deformation [14–18]. The micrometer-
sized dendritic b-Ti(M) phase was therefore introduced.
The strong b-phase formers, e.g. Nb, Ta and Mo,
promote the formation of dendritic b-Ti(M) solid
solution at the temperature between liquidus and
solidus. The dendrites disperse in the subsequently
solidiﬁed nanostructured eutectic microstructure. The
size and the volume fraction of the dendrites, which
affect the strength and the plasticity of the alloys [10],
depend on the composition ofthe alloy and the cooling
rate during solidiﬁcation ( Figs. 1(c) and (d) ). EDX
analysis indicates that the b-Ti(M) solid solution mainly
contains Ti, M (=Nb, or Ta, or Mo) and low amount of
Sn (which conﬁrms the contribution ofSn to the
formation of the b-Ti(M) solid solution). XRD patterns
show strong peaks corresponding to the dendritic b-
phase (Fig. 2). From the strongest [1 1 0] peaks, one can
calculate the lattice parameter for the b-Ti(M) solid
solution, a ¼ 0:3273 nm for both the Nb- and Ta-
containing alloys, and a ¼ 0:3197 nm for the Mo-
containing alloy. The difference in lattice parameters is
attributed to the different atom radius of Nb, Ta and
Mo. The metallic radius ofMo is f ar smaller than Nb
and Ta, resulting in a smaller lattice parameter for theb-
Ti(M) solid solution and leading to a signiﬁcant shift of
the XRD peaks to high angles (Fig. 2).
4.2. Desired mechanical properties for possible
biomedical applications
In terms ofthe desirable mechanical properties f or
biomedical use, the advantages ofthe present alloys are
their combination ofhigh yield strength and low
Young’s modulus. The high strength may improve the
capacity ofthe material against permanent shape
change, which would beneﬁt the patient when these
materials are considered for implant or dental uses. The
low elastic modulus is very beneﬁcial for the use as
implants because an elastic modulus around 60 GPa is
much more close to that ofthe bone than f or other
available Ti-base alloys [1,2]. Table 2 lists some ofthe
Ti-based alloys for comparison. Some of these alloys are
currently in biomedical use. The present alloys (espe-
cially the Nb- and Ta-containing alloys) are superior in
both yield strength and Young’s modulus. On the other
hand, biomedical use also requires good fatigue proper-
ties, high corrosion resistance, and a density as low as

It should be noted that the present Ti-based alloys

microstructure can be tailored by controlling the

5. Conclusions
With proper combination ofTi-Cu-Ni-Sn strong
nanocrystalline alloys can be directly obtained by arc
melting and copper mold casting. With addition of
refractory elements, Nb, Ta and Mo, a micrometer-sized
dendritic b-phase-nanostructured matrix composite has
been achieved. The dendritic b-Ti(M) solid solution acts
as a reinforced phase stabilizing the deformation of the
nanostructure, so as to achieve a very good balance
between high strength and large plasticity for the alloys.
The new Ti-based alloys exhibit a yield strength as
high as 1755 MPa with a plastic strain up to 21%. The
very low elastic modulus ofthe Nb- and Ta-containing
alloys (the Young’s modulus is 59–73 GPa) adds another
desirable property. Such combined properties, not easily
achievable in previous Ti alloys, make these new Ti
alloys promising candidates as biomedical materials. Ti
alloys based on this work, therefore, deserve further
studies to exploit their advantages and alleviate their
drawbacks in biomedical applications.
