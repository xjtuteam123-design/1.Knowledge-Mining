Abstract
The hot compression behavior and tensile strength after compression of a new Ni–Co-based superalloy produced using elec-
tron beam smelting layered (EBSL) solidification technology were investigated. Isothermal compression tests were performed 
at temperatures of 1120 ℃ and 1150 ℃, with strain rates of 1  s−1 and 0.01  s−1, reaching a true strain of 0.51. Tensile strength 
was evaluated at room temperature. The results revealed that this EBSL technology accelerates dynamic recrystallization 
(DRX), without compromising the strength of alloy. A significant correlation between the volume fraction of DRX and the 
strain rate was observed, with higher fractions at lower strain rates, leading to higher tensile strength. Additionally, at the 
same strain rate, the specimens compressed at 1120  ℃ exhibited higher tensile strength due to undissolved γ ′ precipitates. 
After solution and aging heat treatment, the alloy maintained high tensile strength. The results suggested that the EBSL 
Ni–Co-based superalloy offers excellent prospects for practical applications.
Keywords Ni–Co-based superalloy · Hot deformation · Electron beam smelting · Tensile strength
1 Introduction
Aeroengine performance requirements continue to rise 
with advances in aeronautical technology [ 1–3]. Turbine 
disc plays a critical role as key components that bear high 
temperatures in aeroengines, which significantly impacts 
the thrust-to-weight ratio of the engine [4 –6]. In recent 
years, high-alloy, difficult-to-deform Ni-based superalloys 
have garnered attention due to their elevated yield strength, 
oxidation resistance, and fatigue performance [7 –9]. 
These superalloys benefit from a synergy of precipitation 
strengthening, solution strengthening, and grain boundary 
strengthening.
However, when pursuit of enhanced heat resistance 
brings challenges. The addition of an extensive alloying 
elements can lead to segregation during the solidification 
process, giving rise to the precipitation of complex, unde-
sired phases that can adversely affect processability [10, 11]. 
The complex precipitations, including η and eutectic (γ + γ′), 
are consequence of non-uniform elements distribution in 
Ni–Co-based superalloys [12]. Furthermore, a high weight 
fraction of γ′ precipitates limits the hot processing window 
and increases the difficulty of forging [6, 13]. High-strength 
Ni-based superalloys, like U720Li and GH4151, possess 
superior temperature bearing capacity [14– 17]. However, 
they exhibit shortcomings when subjected to long-term 
exposure to elevated temperatures, including the formation 
of harmful TCP phases, limiting their hot-forming properties 
and service life.
Advanced preparation methods have been explored to 
address the deformation challenges posed by these alloys 
[18, 19]. For instance, magnetorheological pressure forming 
based on subregional flexible-die control has been proposed, 
enabling the manipulation of magnetorheological fluids

ogy has shown promise. EBSL technology can improve the 
segregation of the alloy, resulting from the faster cooling 
rates and solidifying the material layer by layer [10, 21]. 
Moreover, EBSL-alloys exhibit favorable hot compression

signifying improved processability. The layered structure

to finer equiaxed grains and dislocation accumulation, which 
in turn, promotes dynamic recrystallization. In general, the 
technology of EBSL is optimistic for the difficult-to-deform 
superalloys to cope with the challenges which have been 
mentioned above.
However, the EBSL process may lead to the formation 
of weak interfaces between solidification layers, which can 
impact the alloy’s performance. An efficient hot-working 
path for metal materials results in the best performance [23, 
24]. Consequently, this work examined the EBSL Ni–Co-
based superalloy subjected to simulated hot compression 
deformation to analyze the evolution of solidification layer 
interfaces during compression and evaluated their bonding 
strength, providing valuable insights for potential engineer-
ing applications.
2 Experimental
2.1 Material
The experimental materials were prepared by the EBSL 
solidification technology. The parameters and process of 
EBSL technology can refer to the Ref. [21]. The chemical 
composition of the EBSL Ni–Co-based superalloy (EBSL-
alloy) is detailed in Table  1. To ensure a suitable micro-
structure prior to deformation, the alloy was homogenized 
at 1180 °C for 8 h. According to our previous work [6], the 
γ′ solvus temperature of this Ni–Co-based superalloy was 
determined to be about 1141 °C. Therefore, two deforma-
tion temperatures, 1120 °C (γ ′ sub-solvus temperature) and 
1150 °C (γ′ super-solvus temperature), were selected for to 
investigate the effect of hot compression deformation tem-
perature on microstructure.
2.2 Hot Compression and Tensile Tests
The schematic depiction of the hot compression and room 
temperature (RT) tensile test are illustrated in Fig.  1. Hot

specimens were heated to 1120 °C and 1150 °C at a heating

perature gradients. Subsequently, the specimens were com-
pressed to an engineering strain of 40% (true strain is 0.51) 
at two different strain rates: 1  s−1 and 0.01  s−1. Following 
deformation, specimens were water quenched to preserve

3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Initial Microstructures
The initial microstructures of the homogenized heat-treated 
EBSL-alloy, as seen in Fig. 2a–c, consists mainly of coarse γ 
grain and some large size annealing twins (Fig. 2a). Delami-
nation phenomenon can be observed in low-power micro -
structure image. A significant feature is the dense interface 
between two adjacent layers, where the annealing twin 
grew into. Flower-like γ′ phases and a small amount of M C 
carbides are present within the grains (Fig.  2b, c), and the 
amount of MC carbide is notably lower than that in Ni–Co-
based superalloys prepared by vacuum induction melting 
(VIM-alloy) (Fig.  2d). The formation of MC carbides is 
due to the segregation of Ti element during the solidifica-
tion. This disparity arises from the quick cooling rate in the 
EBSL-alloy, preventing Ti element segregation and MC car-
bide formation. Thus, the EBSL-alloy exhibits significantly 
lower segregation compared to VIM-alloy.

alloys were water quenched after soaking at 1120 °C and

3.2 Hot Compression Behavior
True stress–strain curves of the alloys deformed to a true 
strain of 0.51 at 1120 °C and 1150 °C with strain rates of 
1  s−1 and 0.01  s−1, are depicted in Fig.  4. During the ini -
tial stage of deformation, the flow stress increased rapidly 
due to work hardening, driven by a rapid rise in dislocation 
density. As deformation progressed, flow stresses increased 
more gradually until reaching maximal values, at which 
point dynamic softening mechanisms counteracted work 
hardening behavior.
Flow stresses exhibited different pattern at low and high 
strain rates, with high peak stresses at higher strain rates 
for the same temperature. This indicates that the strain rate 
significantly influences microstructural evolution during hot 
compression. At low strain rate (0.01  s−1), flow stress rap-
idly increased to peak levels, with clear signs of dynamic 
softening as the strain continued to increase. At high strain 
rate (1  s−1), the work hardening stage was similar to 0.01  s−1 
strain rate, the difference is, then the flow stresses gradu-
ally increased up to a true strain of 0.35. Beyond this point, 
dynamic softening became more pronounced. Additionally, 
for the same strain rate, the flow stresses at 1120 °C were 
higher than those at 1150 °C, primarily due to undissolved 
γ′ phases at 1120 °C.
It is generally accepted that the maximum stress values 
increased with higher strain rate and lower deformation 
temperature. There are several factors contributing to this 
phenomenon. Firstly, an increase in strain rate means a

strain rates of 0.01  s−1 and 1  s−1 at 1120 °C and 1150 °C are

strain rates. At high strain rate, DRX grains mainly distribute 
along GBs discontinuously. The study also differentiated the 
low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2°–10°), the medium

along initial grain boundaries at low strain rate is in line 
with previous research trends [6, 32, 33]. Furthermore, when

At a high temperature, the slip systems in the alloy become 
more readily activated, make it more likely for dislocations 
to move toward GBs, resulting in increased formation of 
HAGB (black lines in Fig. 5e–h).
The deformation mechanisms for various superalloys 
deformed at γ ′ sub- or super-solvus temperatures are gen-
erally similar. Discontinuous dynamic recrystallization 
(DDRX) and continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) 
occurred simultaneously, regardless of whether the defor -
mation is at γ′ sub- or super-solvus temperatures. However, 
their relative dominance may vary under different conditions 
[25–27, 29, 34, 35].
In this work, the DRX mechanism of the EBSL-alloy 
deformed at γ ′ sub- or super-solvus temperatures was 
explored, under both low and high strain rates. Figure  5i–p 
displays the orientation gradients along the GBs (the lines 
marked A1-D1) and across the grain interior (the lines 
marked A2-D2). The point-to-origin presents cumulative 
misorientations, while point-to-point indicates local mis-
orientation. With a low strain rate, it can be seen that the 
orientation gradients along GBs consistently exceed those 
within the grain interior, regardless of whether the defor -
mation occurs at γ′ sub- or super-solvus temperatures. This 
suggests that DDRX is the dominate DRX mechanism under 
conditions of low strain rate. However, the situation is differ-
ent when deforming at γ′ sub- and super-solvus temperatures 
with a high strain rate. At γ ′ sub-solvus temperature, the 
orientation gradient along GBs exceeds 12°, consistent with 
the characteristic of DDRX. At γ′ super-solvus temperature, 
the orientation gradients along GBs and across the grain 
interior are reversed. This indicates that CDRX occurred 
frequently, as supported by the presence of individual or 
clustered grains within the original grain.
Kumar et al. [36] attributed the differences in grain size 
at various deformation temperatures, with the same strain 
rate, to several factors. Firstly, marginal grain growth at high 
temperature exposure before hot deformation can lead to 
increased DRX grain size. Secondly, fine DRX grains at a 
lower deformation temperature are a result of the pinning 
effect on GBs caused by the undissolved γ′ particles. Finally, 
materials deformed at high temperature with low strain rates 
(< 0.001  s−1) tend to deform superplastically through GBs 
sliding rather than dislocation slip. This is typically associ-
ated with marginal grain growth. In this work, the EBSL-
alloy was heat treated at 1180 °C, and the deformation tem-
perature has little effect on the initial grain size. Therefore, 
the first and third reasons for difference in grain size at 
various deformation temperatures are not applicable. The 
coarser DRX grains in the alloy deformed at γ′ super-solvus 
temperature are primarily due to the absence of the pinning 
effect by the γ′ phase.
Cui et al. [22] have demonstrated that the DRX process 
during hot deformation of EBSL-alloy can be accelerated 
due to its unique layer structure. The deformation activa-
tion energy required for the EBSL-alloy was lower than the 
superalloy prepared by conventional casting process. How-
ever, it is important to note that good processability does not 
necessarily guarantee favorable service performances, and 
the bonding strength of the solidification layer interfaces 
needs to be further evaluated. Therefore, the tensile proper-
ties of EBSL-alloy after hot compression would be verified 
in the following.
3.4 Tensile Properties of the EBSL‑Alloy
3.4.1 Tensile Properties After Hot Compression
Tensile specimens from four deformation conditions, 
namely, 1120 °C-1  s−1 (1120-1), 1120 °C-0.01  s−1 (1120-
001), 1150 °C-1  s−1 (1150-1), and 1150 °C-0.01  s−1 (1150-
001), were machined from the center of the deformed 
samples. To assess the tensile strength of the EBSL-alloy 
before and after deformation, a sample without compression 
deformation was employed as the control. The stress–strain 
curves of the alloy tested at RT are plotted in Fig. 6. Notably, 
the tensile strength of all the deformed specimens exceeded 
that of undeformed specimen, indicating that the EBSL-
alloy retained its strength and stability after hot compres-
sion. Moreover, specimens deformed with strain rate of 
0.01  s−1 exhibited higher ultimate tensile strength compared 
to those deformed with a strain rate of 1  s−1. This suggests 
that the EBSL-alloy possessed a higher degree of DRX at a 
strain rate of 0.01  s−1, resulting in finer grains and increased 
strength. Furthermore, at the same strain rate, tensile prop-
erties of specimens deformed at γ ′ sub-solvus temperature 
(1120 ℃) were superior to those deformed at γ′ super-solvus 
temperature (1150  ℃). This distinction is primarily attrib-
uted to the presence of undissolved γ′ precipitates at 1120 ℃. 
As shown in Fig. 3a, the alloy contained a significant number 
of large sized γ′ precipitates, which exhibited greater elastic 
strain compare to γ matrix. As a result, the alloys deformed 
at 1120 ℃ exhibited higher strength and elongation.

the fracture for the 1120-001 and 1150-001 specimens are 
shown in Fig.  7. Firstly, the fracture locations of the two 
specimens were similar, both occurring adjacent to the edge 
of loading section. This proximity to the loading section’s 
edge suggests that the strength of the layer interface was suf-

3.4.2 Tensile Properties After Heat‑Treatments
Solution and aging heat-treatment are essential processes 
for precipitation strengthened superalloys [37, 38]. Con-
sequently, these compressed specimens underwent solu-
tion and aging heat-treatments as follows: 1100  ℃/4 h-oil 
cooling + 650 ℃/24 h-air cooling + 760  ℃/12 h-air cool-
ing. The morphologies of γ ′ precipitates in the heat-treated 
specimens are shown in Fig.  8. Comparing to compressed 
specimens (Fig.  3), the coarse γ ′ precipitates in the 1120-
001 specimen became more rounded, displaying a distinct 
bimodal distribution of γ ′ precipitates. Similarly, the 1150-
001 specimen also exhibited a bimodal distribution of γ ′ 
precipitates after heat treatments, but the size of coarse γ ′ 
precipitates (approximately 400 nm) in the 1150-001 speci-
men was smaller than that in the 1120-001 specimen (around 
500 nm). The area fraction of coarse γ′ precipitates in both 
specimens was approximately 32%–35%.
The RT stress–strain curves of the heat-treated 1120-001 
and 1150-001 specimens are presented in Fig.  9. Notably, 
the strength of the heat-treated specimens was significantly 
higher. Moreover, the yield strength and ultimate tensile 
strength of the teat-treated specimens were comparable, 
measuring around 1000 MPa and 1300 MPa, respectively. 
Therefore, the EBSL-alloy demonstrated remarkable 
strength after hot deformation at either γ ′ sub- or super-
solvus temperature. When deformed at γ ′ sub-solvus tem-
perature, the alloy exhibited finer grain, which positively 
influenced its mechanical properties.

The slightly lower strength of the EBSL-alloy, as reported in 
this study, is due to differences in microstructure. The C&W 
alloy reported by Zhu et al. [39] consists mainly of equi-

would reduce the strength of the alloy. Therefore, enhanc -
ing the microstructure’s uniformity through appropriate hot 
deformation could further improve the performance of the 
deformed EBSL-alloy. In a word, the solidification layer 
interfaces facilitate the DRX during the hot compression, 
and the finer DRX grains contributed to increased interface 
bonding strength.
Additionally, other researchers are investigating advance-
ments in the melting process to enhance the hot-working 
properties of alloys. For example, Tang et al. [43] designed 
a new γ/γ′ Ni-based superalloy for additive manufacturing 
process using computational approaches. This alloy not only 
exhibited excellent mechanical properties but also demon-
strated favorable processability. Zhou et al. [34] fabricated 
a new Ni–Co-based superalloy using vacuum induction 
melting and directional solidification remelting technology. 
Their results indicated that the columnar crystal structure 
improved the recrystallization of the alloy and enhanced its 
hot deformation performance. In this work, electron beam 
smelting layered solidification technology is the combine of 
additive manufacturing with electron beam smelting. There-
fore, the EBSL-alloy not only benefits from fast solidifica-
tion speed and low segregation similar to additive manufac-
turing but also features microstructure characteristics similar 
to directional solidification, signifying the promising pros-
pects of this EBSL solidification technology.
4 Conclusions
In conclusion, the study investigated the hot compression 
behavior and tensile properties of a Ni–Co-based superalloy 
produced using electron beam smelting layered solidification 
technology. Key findings and conclusions include:
1. The alloy’s deformation resistance was influenced by 
deformation temperature and strain rate. Low deforma-
tion resistance was observed in specimens with high 
deformation temperature and low strain rate. The vol-
ume fraction of DRX was correlated with strain rate, 
with specimens deformed at lower strain rates exhibiting 
a higher DRX fraction. High deformation temperature 
led to larger DRX grains.
2. Tensile strength after hot compression was influenced 
by strain rate and deformation temperature. Specimens 
deformed at lower strain rates exhibited higher tensile 
strength. Deformation at γ ′ sub-solvus temperature 
(1120 °C) resulted in higher tensile strength due to the 
presence of undissolved γ′ precipitates.
3. After solution and aging heat treatments, the alloy exhib-
ited outstanding tensile strength, which was significantly 
higher than the strength of compressed specimens. The 
alloy’s tensile strength was competitive with that of 
other superalloys.
4. The study emphasized the positive effect of layer inter -
faces in promoting DRX during hot compression, while 
also highlighting the alloy’s potential for advanced 
applications.
