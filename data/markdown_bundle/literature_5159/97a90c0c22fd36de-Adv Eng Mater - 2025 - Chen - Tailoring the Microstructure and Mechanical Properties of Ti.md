Yao Chen, Jiaqi Yu, Shirui Hao, Qi Wang, Gang Qin,* and Ruirun Chen*
1. Introduction
High-temperature alloys, particularly Ni-based superalloys, which
are widely utilized as structural materials in aerospace applica-
tions, have been the subject of extensive research by material
scientists.[1– 3] However, due to their inherently high density
(>8gc m /C03) and operating temperatures approaching their
melting points, the further development and optimization of
traditional high-temperature alloys face signiﬁcant limitations.[4– 6]
The high entropy alloys (HEAs), ﬁrst proposed by Ye in 2004,
overcame the limitations of traditional alloys regarding elemental
composition.[7– 10] Comprising multiple principal elements, HEAs
signiﬁcantly expand the design space for metal materials. They
exhibit superior properties, including high strength, hardness,
wear resistance, and corrosion resistance, which have garnered
considerable attention compared to conventional alloys. [11– 19]
To bolster the elevated-temperature mechanical characteristics
of HEAs, extensive research has been undertaken. A notable exam-
ple is the work of Senkov and colleagues in 2010, who engineered
a single-phase body centered cubic (BCC) HEA incorporating
elements with high melting points, includ-
ing W, Nb, Mo, Ta, and V. The resulting
alloy demonstrated superior attributes in
comparison to conventional nickel-based
superalloys, which prompted the introduc-
tion of the concept of refractory HEAs. [20]
Subsequently, with the ongoing advance-
ment of research, a system of refractory
HEAs was developed, typically characterized
by a BCC crystal structure. Some of these
alloys also contain intermetallic phases
such as the Laves, μ, σ, and B2 phases.[21– 29]
The initially developed refractory HEAs,
containing elements such as Ta, W, and
Hf, exhibit considerable strength at both
room and high temperatures; however, their
density is typically high ( >10 g cm/C03). In
2013, Senkov et al. developed a series of

2. Experimental Section
2.1. Material Preparation
In the current study, Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx (x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7,
0.9, molar ratios) and Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y (y = 0.5, 1.0,
molar ratios) alloy ingots were prepared by vacuum nonconsum-
able arc melting using high purity ( >99.95%) Mo, Nb, Ti, V, and
Zr metal particles and graphite powder; polysilicon particles;
and SiC powder as raw materials. Each ingot had been melted
at least ﬁve times to make the composition uniform.
Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx alloys were abbreviated as C0.1, C0.3,
C0.5, C0.7, and C0.9 according to the x value, while alloys with
y = 0.5 and 1.0 in Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y alloys were referred
to as A0.5 and A1 respectively. The speci ﬁc compositions of all
alloys are listed in Table 1 . In order to study the in ﬂuence
of doping methods, Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y alloys were pre-
pared using two different alloy raw materials for the introduc-
tion of C and Si elements. Based on the different methods of
adding C and Si, A0.5 and A1 alloys were divided into two dis-
tinct subsets. The alloys prepared using graphite powder and
silicon particles to introduce C and Si elements separately were
termed Spr A0.5 and Spr A1, while those that introduced
nonmetallic elements simultaneously through SiC particles
were termed Smt A0.5 and Smt A1.
2.2. Experimental Method
To analyze the phase composition of the alloys, an X-ray diffraction
diffractometer (XRD, Panalytical Empyrean, Cu – Kα,2 0 k V ,
8° min/C01) was used to identify the diffraction peaks of the polished
alloys. The microstructure and element distribution of the alloys
were analyzed using backscattered electron (BSE) mode and
the energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) function of a scanning
electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss, Merlin Compact). Additionally,
the EDS function of an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA,
JEOL, JXA-8230) was employed to determine the elemental
composition of each phase in the alloys. The compressive
stress-strain curve at room temperature (RT) was measured using
an electronic universal testing machine (Instron, 5569) with
cylindrical samples having a diameter of 4 mm and a height of
6 mm. For each alloy, at least three specimens were tested at
an initial strain rate of 1 /C210/C03 s/C01. The fracture morphology
was observed using the secondary electron mode of a SEM.
The densities of all alloys were measured using the
Archimedes drainage method.

of content: Zr, Ti, Nb, V, and Mo, which corresponds to the order
of the mixing enthalpy between these elements and carbon from
low to high. As the carbon content in the alloys increases, the
carbide phase becomes the predominant phase. Due to their high
melting points, Mo and Nb elements form dendrites close to the
primary carbide phase, causing the Zr element to segregate
between the dendrites. During the cooling process, a secondary
Zr-rich carbide phase precipitates between the dendrites, which
is represented by the strip-like carbide phase in the ﬁgure.
Overall, the addition of C element has a signi ﬁcant impact on
the microstructure of the microalloyed material. When the
content of C element is low, BCC columnar dendrites precipitate
ﬁrst, and high melting point Mo and Nb elements tend to
agglomerate in the dendrites. Zr element is enriched between
the dendrites, and then a layered eutectic structure of BCC
and MC is formed between the dendrites. After the increase
of C element content, blocky MC carbide phase precipitates ﬁrst,
followed by the formation of Nb- and Mo-rich BCC dendrites
around the MC phase, which grow into equiaxed shapes.
At the same time, Zr element is enriched between dendrites,
and ﬁnally, due to the dissolution of C element, strip-shaped
secondary MC carbide phase is formed between dendrites.
3.1.2. Mechanical Properties and Fracture Behavior
The compressive engineering stress-strain curve at RT, the
compressive true stress-strain curve at 800 °C, and the relevant
properties of Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx (x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9)
HEAs are shown in Figure 3 . As seen in Figure 3c, the yield
strength increases with the increase in carbon content, rising
from 1304 MPa for the C0.1 alloy to 1490 MPa for the C0.9 alloy,
which represents an increase of ≈14.3%. At the same time, the
plasticity of the alloys decreases with increasing carbon content,
from 28.9% for the C0.1 alloy to 12.2% for the C0.9 alloy. When
compressed at 800 °C, the yield strength of the alloys also
increases with carbon content, rising from 586 MPa for the
C0.1 alloy to 823 MPa for the C0.9 alloy, a 40.4% increase.
However, with the addition of carbon, the density of the alloys
increases from 6.41 g cm /C03 for the C0.1 alloy to 7.26 g cm /C03
for the C0.9 alloy. This is likely that the high density of the
carbide phase due to the increased proportion of Nb further
elevates the alloys ’ density.
The changes in strength and plasticity of the alloys are primar-
ily related to the morphology, distribution, and content of the
carbides within the alloy. Since carbides are hard phases at both
room and high temperatures, they hinder dislocation movement
and increase the energy required for deformation during the
alloy’s deformation process. The increase in carbide content
and size signi ﬁcantly enhances the RT and high-temperature
strength of the alloys, acting as a secondary phase strengthening
mechanism. However, the carbide phase is also inherently
brittle, which can reduce the alloys ’ plasticity. The network struc-
ture formed by the aggregation and growth of the carbide phase
increases the interface area between the carbide phase and the
BCC matrix during the deformation process. Due to the uncoor-
dinated deformation, cracks form at the interface, signi ﬁcantly
reducing the alloys ’ plasticity. Similarly, the solid solution of
carbon atoms in the alloy, due to the atomic radius difference
between carbon and other alloying elements, leads to a solid
solution strengthening effect. This enhances the alloys ’ strength
but also reduces its plasticity.
To further analyze the fracture behavior of the alloys, the
compression fracture was observed using a SEM. Figure 4 shows
the RT compression fracture morphology of Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx
(x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9) HEAs, obtained through SEM in
secondary electron mode. As seen in the ﬁgure, the fracture
morphology of the alloys changes signi ﬁcantly with increasing
carbon content. As shown in Figure 4a, the C0.1 alloy exhibits
distinct dimples and river patterns, characteristic of a typical
quasicleavage fracture. In Figure 4b, the number of dimples in
the C0.3 alloy decreases, and the degree of depression on the
fracture surface diminishes, indicating a reduction in the alloys ’
plasticity. The C0.5 to C0.9 alloys exhibit typical cleavage fracture
characteristics, with distinct cleavage steps and planes visible on
the fracture surface. As shown in Figure 4c, the fracture surface of
the C0.5 alloy is ﬂatter, indicating a decrease in the alloys’ plastic-
ity. The fracture surfaces of the C0.7 to C0.9 alloys exhibit a
consistently smooth texture, a characteristic indicative of
diminished ductility. Concurrently, the prominence of the
cleavage step features intensi ﬁes, as vividly depicted in

card #65-0332), but with a 2θ angle that is 1°– 4° larger than that of
ZrC. The M5Si3 silicide phase structure is closer to the Zr 3Ti2Si3

content of Zr, which has a larger atomic radius, in the interme-

Nb, which have slightly smaller atomic radii. This substitution
results in a larger 2θ angle for the intermetallic compound phase.
From a thermodynamic perspective, the formation of these two
phases can be attributed to the low mixing enthalpy between the
C/Si elements and the Zr/Ti/Nb elements. Since the mixing
enthalpy between C and Si is /C039 kJ mol/C01, the formation of
the SiC phase in the alloys is hindered, making it dif ﬁcult
for the SiC phase to form. As the value of y increases from
0.5 to 1, the diffraction peaks of each phase gradually shift to
higher 2 θ angles. This indicates that the lattice parameters of
the alloys ’ BCC matrix, carbides, and silicide phases decrease

a small amount of solid solution of C and Si elements, which
have smaller atomic radii, in the BCC matrix also contributes
to the decrease in lattice parameters. For the intermetallic
compound phase, the increase in C and Si content leads to
the formation of more carbides and silicides with Ti and Nb ele-
ments, which have smaller atomic radii, while reducing the pro-
portion of Zr elements. This results in a decrease in the lattice
constant. Furthermore, it can be observed from the XRD patterns
that different addition methods of C and Si elements do not lead
to any signiﬁcant changes in the phase composition of the alloys.
The SiC particles essentially react completely with the metal and
do not remain in the alloys. The XRD diffraction patterns of the
alloys with the same composition are nearly identical.
The microstructure of Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y (y = 0.5, 1.0)

morphology, 2) a silicide phase forming lamellar eutectic struc-

preferentially distributed along BCC phase boundaries.

its distribution is continuous. The dendrites grow on the edge
of the block carbides, and the silicide eutectic phase is distributed
between the dendrites, accompanied by the strip secondary
carbide phase. When y = 1, the proportion of BCC solid solution
phase decreases, and the proportion of silicide eutectic phase is
relatively high, with the phases being interconnected, while the
dark carbide phase is distributed. For alloys with the same
composition, the microstructure of the alloys with the simulta-
neous addition of SiC is signi ﬁcantly ﬁner compared to the alloys
with only C and Si. Since the BCC dendrite morphology in the
A0.5 alloys is relatively intact, the dendrite spacing of the BCC
phase in A0.5 alloys was calculated using the intercept method.
The average spacing of BCC dendrites in the Smt A0.5 alloy is

alloys, the structure is too complex to be explained solely by BCC
dendrites, but it can still be observed that the volume of BCC
clusters in the Smt A1 alloy is signi ﬁcantly smaller than in
the Spr A1 alloy. This difference is likely due to the fact that
SiC particles, compared to graphite, do not easily form
signiﬁcant C element segregation during melting. This charac-
teristic of SiC is bene ﬁcial for the dispersion and re ﬁnement of
carbides during solidi ﬁcation, which results in smaller

domains. 4) Finally, during the cooling stage, secondary MC
carbide precipitation occurs within the Zr-enriched regions as a
consequence of decreasing carbon solubility in the supersaturated
BCC matrix.

3.2.2. Mechanical Properties and Fracture Behavior
Figure 8 presents the compressive engineering stress-strain curve
at RT, the compressive true stress-strain curve at 800°C, and the
relevant properties of Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y (y = 0.5, 1.0) refrac-
tory HEAs. The alloy composition, along with the incorporation of C
and Si elements, signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the mechanical properties
of the alloys. As shown in Figure 8c, with the increase in C and Si
content, the yield strength and compressive strength of the alloys at
both RT and high-temperature increase considerably, while the plas-
ticity decreases. For the Spr A0.5 and Smt A0.5 alloys with the same
composition, there is little difference in yield strength during the RT
compression test. However, the Smt A0.5 alloy, which directly incor-
porates SiC, demonstrates higher compressive strength and plastic-
ity. Similarly, for the Smt A1 and Spr A1 alloys, the Smt A1 alloy also
exhibits better compressive strength and plasticity. However, when
compressed at 800 °C, the properties of the Smt A0.5 and Smt A1
alloys are lower than those of the Spr A0.5 and Spr A1 alloys,
particularly in terms of fracture strain. The Smt A1 alloy exhibits
a high yield strength of 1933 MPa at RT and maintains 797 MPa
at 800 °C.
In Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)y (y = 0.5, 1.0) alloys, as y increases
from 0.5 to 1, the content of hard carbides and silicides in the
alloys rises with the increasing concentration of C and Si
elements. This leads to a more pronounced strengthening effect
of the second phase. At the same time, the aggregation and
growth of carbides and silicides form a network structure, which
enhances the brittleness of the alloys both at RT and at high
temperature. The difference in strength between Smt and Spr
alloys with the same composition can be attributed to the struc-
tural re ﬁnement of the alloys. The relatively ﬁner structure of
Smt A0.5 and Smt A1 alloys provides more interfaces, which
hinders dislocation movement during deformation at RT,
thereby enhancing the overall mechanical properties of the
alloys. When deformed at high temperatures, the interfaces
soften and become defects in the deformation process, reducing
the alloy ’s high-temperature deformability. In terms of density,
alloys with the same composition are similar, but the densities of
A1 alloys are lower than those of A0.5 alloys, likely due to the
lower density of the silicide phase.

4. Conclusions
In summary, a series of Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx (x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5,
0.7, 0.9) and Ti 1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6(SiC)x (x = 0.5, 1) lightweight
refractory HEAs were prepared, and their microstructure and
mechanical properties were studied. The conclusions are as
follows: 1) The microstructure of the Ti1.5NbZrV0.4Mo0.6Cx alloys
consists of a BCC-solid solution and carbide phases. As the
C content increases, the carbide phase gradually transitions from
lamellar to granular form and grows in size, while the BCC
dendrites change from a columnar to an equiaxed morphology.
The carbide phase is rich in Zr, while the BCC dendrites are
enriched with Nb and Mo elements. 2) The addition of C signi ﬁ-
cantly enhances both the RT and high temperature strength of
the alloys. For the alloys from C0.1 to C0.9, the yield strength at
RT increased from 1304 to 1490 MPa, re ﬂecting a 14.3%
increase. At 800 °C, the yield strength increased from 586 to
823 MPa, marking a 40.4% improvement. Additionally, the
density rose from 6.41 to 7.26 g cm /C03. The fracture mode of
the alloys progressively changed from quasicleavage fracture
to cleavage fracture as the C content increased. 3) The simulta-
neous addition of C and Si elements results in the formation of
the BCC phase, M 5Si3 silicide phase, and MC carbide phase in
the alloys. The microstructure consists of primary block carbide
phase, equiaxed dendritic BCC phase, lamellar BCC and silicide
eutectic structure, and strip secondary carbide phase. The alloys
with individual additions of C and Si elements exhibit a ﬁner
microstructure. 4) With the increase in C and Si content, both
the RT and high temperature strength of the alloys are improved.
The SYS increased from 224 MPa g /C01 cm3 (RT) and
86 MPa g/C01 cm3 (800 °C) for Smt A0.5 to 297 MPa g /C01 cm3 (RT)
and 123 MPa g /C01 cm3 (800 °C) for Smt A1, respectively. These
results provide valuable insights for the design and development
of lightweight refractory HEAs with high speci ﬁc strength.
