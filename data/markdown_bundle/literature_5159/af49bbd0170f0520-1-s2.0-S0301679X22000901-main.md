ABSTRACT 
This review paper presents the state of art for sustainable machining of nickel based super alloys using coated 
and uncoated tools. The paper focusses on the aspect such as tool wear and cutting forces specifically. Apart from 
this, the paper includes state of art for the modern techniques to overcome the problems related to the 
machinability of such high strength alloys i.e., through use of surface modified tools. Future research to make 
these alloy more accessible without any problems related to their machining can be focussed on developing tools 
which can withstand elevated temperature without the use of cooling or lubrication methods. 
1. Introduction 
Machinability refers to how easily a material can be machined to the 
desired geometry to serve the intended function at a reasonable cost. 
Machinability is frequently associated with the properties of the work -
piece; however, the ease of machining is also influenced by other factors 
such as the rigidity of the cutting tool and the cutting environment. 
During the metal cutting operation large amount of heat is generated at 
numerous contact zones, affecting the machining output in terms of 
surface texture, product accuracy, and tool stability, as well as the 
economic and environmental outlook [1 – 3] . To minimise this heat 
various cooling methods i.e. flood cooling, minimum quantity lubrica -
tion, cryogenic cooling etc. have been adopted in the past [4 – 6] . The use 
of flood cooling method in the current era is unecological and uneco -
nomical owing to significant wastage of natural resources [2,7,8] . While 
the utilization of near dry machining or minimum quantity lubrication 
technique also imposes challenges if carried out with metal working 
fluids (MWF). The use of the MWF during the machining operation 
causes various kinds of pollution either in air or water [9,10] . The use of 
these fluids also creates issues related to their disposal, health, and 
increased cost of recycling. Various problems associated with the use of 
MWF are shown in Fig. 1 . 
It has also been reported that the use of cutting fluids accounts for 
16% of the machining cost while the tooling cost is limited to only 4% 
[11] . Hence it is necessary to incorporate the aspects of sustainability in 
the machining process. Drastic changes should be brought to the in -
dustrial processes so as to limit the environmental problems which affect 
the nature and human life directly [12] . 
In order to overcome these problems in manufacturing, a triadic 
solution based on the three important aspects of ecology, economy, and 
equity has been provided as shown in Fig. 2 . The main focus of this is to 
improve the social, economic, and environmental aspects simulta -
neously so as to create a better ecological system [13] . The evolution of 
this framework can be divided into three stages: (1) solutions that focus 
on economic and technical viability, (2) solutions that include envi -
ronmental, energetic, and functional efficiency [14] , and (3) solutions 
that achieve a 3E unified approach by including social criteria. 
As a result, industrial processes and manufacturing engineering in 
general demand more comprehensive design and supervision (with so -
cial and environmental requirements). New frameworks must be 
developed that are oriented toward achieving sustainable goals with the 
least amount of complexity. All this will structure sustainability infor -
mation such that it may be implemented into present production pro -
cesses without jeopardising its technical and economic viability. This 
can be accomplished by including the machining processes long-term 
requirements in the design, modelling, simulation, optimization, and 
assessment task [13] .

Sustainability in metal cutting can be achieved by varying the cutting 
tool conditions or by changing the cutting environment. The various 
emerging techniques for the sustainable metal cutting are focused on. 
(i) Dry cutting using coated or uncoated tools, which limits the usage 
of any kind of cutting fluid [3,16] 
(ii) Minimum Quantity Lubrication, which limits the amount of 
cutting fluid by restricting its flow in the cutting zone [4,17 – 19] . 
(iii) Cryogenic cooling, where liquid nitrogen is generally utilized to 
reduce the temperature in the cutting zone so as to enhance the 
tool life [20] . 
Apart from this, modification of the uncoated and coated tools (ge -
ometry or surface modification) can also be done to enhance their per -
formance during machining operations [21,22] . The modified tools 
reduces the cutting forces and power requirement and also eliminates 
the usage of any kind of MWF [3] . 
The application of these sustainable techniques and adoption of 
advanced technologies will minimize the ecological imbalance and will 
help overcoming the economic challenges. As the growing population 
and increasing demand for the resources has raised serious concern 
about the capacity of natural ecosystem [23] . Half of the world ’ s total 
energy demand is accounted by the industrial sector which is anticipated 
to rise by 40% by 2030, from 175 quadrillion British thermal units (BTU) 
in 2006 – 246 quadrillion BTU in 2030 [24] . As a result, the requirement 
for sustainable manufacturing is of paramount importance to meet 
future energy needs while also conserving natural resources, minimising 
health risks, and ensuring profitability in today ’ s competitive 
environment. 
Hence in this connection, this review paper presents the current state 
of art for sustainable machining of nickel-based superalloys. Along with 
this, it focusses on recent progress in the field of machining to overcome 
the lacunas of traditional cooling and lubrication techniques. The paper 
provides insight about surface modification techniques that can be 
adopted to enhance cutting tool life and achieve desired machinability 
of high strength materials. Furthermore, the paper critically analyses the 
conducted studies and their outcomes to implement the most suited 
indices of sustainability. 
The knowledge creation from this study and future research in the 
mentioned research areas (surface engineering and surface modifica -
tion) will benefit a multitude of disciplines from design & engineering to 
materials synthesis and sustainable development.

2.1. Dry machining 
Dry machining is defined as the process which does not involve the 
usage of any type of cutting fluid so as to protect the environment as well 
as to reduce the process cost. The main advantages of the process were 
listed by Costes et al. [27] as. 
• No air or water pollution, which reduces the danger to skin, and 
respiratory damage, 
• Reduction in energy as well as the cost associated with the cleaning 
of lubricant residues on the machined components, 
• Reduction in energy and disposal costs associated with the lubricant 
residues over the evacuated chips.

2.2. Minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) 
Minimum quantity lubrication or generally referred as MQL is a 
sustainable technique which utilizes very less amount of fluid for the 
lubrication and cooling purposes. It is a better alternative to flood 
cooling which is imposing severe health and environmental problems. 
The process is also referred as near dry machining process (NDM). The 
term “ minimum quantity lubrication ” was first coined by Weck and 
Koch in the year 1993 related to the lubrication of the bearings. How -
ever, in metal cutting the process was first utilized during grinding 
operation and later on to other machining operations [4,45,46] . The 
flow rate during the process is approximately 10,000 times lesser in 
comparison to the flood cooling and its value varies from 50 to 
500 ml/hr [5,47] . 
The use of coatings over the cutting tools sustainably reduces their 
wear and the same has been studied in the previous studies during the 
high speed machining of various nickel based, titanium based alloys [48, 
49] . MQL technique is still generally considered as the better alternative 
of the dry machining process during the high speed machining where a 
significant amount of heat generation takes place. The process also helps 
in avoiding clogging of the chips during dry machining operations as 
well as lowers the temperatures, which has been reported by many re -
searchers as a major problem in the past. The generation of high tem -
peratures not only affects the tool life but also causes work hardening 
effect during the machining of nickel based superalloys [8,50] . 
The MQL system maybe external and internal depending upon the 
usage and requirement [45] . But the most commonly used one is the 
external system where the mixture of the compressed air and the oil is 
applied onto the cutting zone with the help of an external nozzle. Fig. 8 
represents an external MQL system for turning operations. The air 
pressure and the flow of the fluid can be regulated utilizing the provided 
regulators. 
For the MQL process, various kinds of cutting fluids can be utilized 
but it is necessary to note that not every fluid can be utilized for the 
purpose. The selection of these fluids is to be done on the basis of health 
and environmental risks. The fluids containing zinc or chlorine addi -
tives, fluids containing benzopyrene are considered harmful. The fluids 
for the MQL process should be biodegradable, stable, and should possess 
high lubricity [45,47] . So, considering these aspects vegetable oils and 
synthetic esters are considered as the best alternatives to the existing 
metal working fluids [52,53] . 
2.2.1. Vegetable oils 
Vegetable oils possess various distinct advantages over other lubri -
cation fluids and hence considered to be one of the potential fluid al -
ternatives over MWFs. A few of their distinct properties has been 
discussed by various researchers. 
(i) Vegetable oils are nontoxic and renewable. The pH value of the 
vegetable oil emulsions varies from 8.5 to 10. pH level for the 
emulsions should not be too low or too high it leads to waste 
disposal problems. The process associated with their refining and 
processing generates much less pollution. They are also widely 
used for lubrication purposes [54,55] . 
(ii) These oils are biodegradable as the glycerine ester in their 
structure can be easily hydrolysed while the unsaturated bonds in 
the ester group chain are prone to attack by microorganism 
causing bio oxidation (shown in Fig. 9 ) [56] . 
(iii) The high lubricating properties of the vegetable oils owing to 
their basic structure and chemical composition also makes them 
as a viable option. The molecules of the vegetable oil form an 
adsorption film on the surface which influences wear and friction 
properties [19,57] . 
(iv) Last but not the least, the high flash point of the vegetable oils 
makes them less prone to fire hazard and also results in reduced 
smoke formation during the high-speed machining operation 
where high amount of heat is generated. Flash point of the 
vegetable oil emulsions varies from 217
◦
to 235
◦
C [58] . 
The basic structure of the vegetable oil consist of triglycerides 
glycerol molecules. Apart from this the structure of the vegetable oils 
also contains long chains of fatty acids attached by means of ester 
linkage at hydroxyls groups as shown in the Fig. 10 . 
Fatty acids in vegetable oils and fats have chain lengths ranging from 
4 to 26 carbon atoms. Lauric acid (C12:0), stearic acid (C18:0), palmitic 
acid (C16:0), oleic acid (C18:1), linoleic acid (C18:2), and linolenic acid

2.2.3. 2.3. Cryogenic cooling 
The name "Kayos" comes from the Greek language and in English it 
means "cold." As a result, cryogenics entails working in a cold environ -
ment. Cryogenic is a material whose temperature is below 150 
◦
C. The 
Cryogenic Society of America considers temperatures below 120 K to be 
cryogenic temperatures [69] . When this material is used to cool down or 
limit the temperature during the machining process, it is referred as 
cryogenic cooling. The process is very much similar to wet machining, 
however, here instead of some liquids, gases are utilized. The cryogen is 
applied at the cutting interface or at the point of contact between 
tool-workpiece tribopair so as to keep the temperature below the soft -
ening temperature of the cutting tool. 
Though many gases have a temperature below 150 
◦
C, liquid ni -
trogen is preferred over any other gas owing to its non-toxicity. The 
boiling point and melting of the liquid nitrogen also play a significant 
role in its selection over other cryogenics. The effectiveness of the liquid 
nitrogen during the machining process is already discussed by various 
researchers in the past [70 – 73] . During the machining operation, the 
evaporation of cryogenic leads to the formation of cushion at chip-tool 
interface which improves the machinability of work material [52] . 
Cryogenic cooling is an effective technique only if the proper selection of

3. Machining using surface modified cutting tools 
The challenges imposed by the use of nano-fluids, MWF and cryo -
genic raised the need for the use of tools which does not require cooling 
or lubrication methods. Such tools can be developed by surface and sub- 
surface modification through polishing, laser texturing, micro blasting, 
etching, annealing etc. The use of polishing, etching and other heart 
treatment methods for the surface modification works well for material 
with less hardness or those material which does not undergo hardening 
at elevated temperatures. In the recent times, micro blasting and laser 
texturing is gaining popularity among the researchers owing to the low 
associated with these process as well as their high effectiveness and 
efficiency during the dry machining of high strength materials 
[99 – 105] . 
3.1. Machining using mechanically treated cutting tools 
Micro abrasive blasting is a surface treatment technique that has 
shown its effectiveness over the PVD films in enhancing their perfor -
mance during intermittent and continuous cutting operations. The 
technique helps in accessing all the benefits of the coatings by changing 
the surface and sub-surface properties. The use of the micro abrasive 
blasting can be done before as well as after the coating deposition 
depending upon the requirements and applications. The technique can 
also be utilized for changing certain geometrical aspects of the tools like 
cutting edge radius. 
3.1.1. Effect of micro abrasive blasting pre-treatment on machining of high 
strength materials 
Micro abrasive blasting of the cemented carbide inserts affects the 
various physical and mechanical characteristics of the cutting tool. The 
variation in these characteristics depends upon various process param -
eters. So proper selection of these parameters plays an important role 
during the process. The process is generally carried out using alumina 
and zirconia abrasives [100,106 – 108] . However, in certain cases, 
different kinds of abrasive materials are also used for blasting purposes. 
Fern ´andez-Abia et al. [106] carried out blasting on carbide cutting in -
serts using glass microspheres for a duration of 30 s which leads to good 
superficial finish and induces compressive residual stresses on the sur -
face and sub-surface layers. But during the scanning electron micro -
scopy, it is observed that the micro blasting using glass microspheres 
causes craters on the tool surface (which are small depressions and 
inter-granular hollows on the surface due to impingement of the glass 
microspheres used in the process) which in turn reduces the efficacy of 
the tool). It is also reported that the use of micro blasting treatment 
removes the cobalt binder from the surface thereby decreasing the 
adhesion between the coating and the substrate. On the contrary, in a 
study by Bouzakis et al. [109] it is revealed that micro blasting at high 
pressure (around 2 bar) with alumina abrasives of size less than 90 µ m 
not only help in better adhesion of the coatings with the substrate 
through mechanical binding but also lowered the surface roughness 
values significantly. During the process, the mean spacing between the 
surface profiles decreased, contributing to the enhancement in the 
interlocking of the coating-substrate. The capacity of the adhesion due 
to blasting was measured by these values of roughness, where low value 
corresponds to better adhesion. 
The micro blasting surface treatment does not only have an edge over 
the non-treated samples but also in comparison to the polishing surface 
treatment [110] . The process helps in the generation of numerous small 
peaks removing the ductile cobalt from the upper surface which is 
necessary for mechanical binding. The surface treatment only removes 
the bad adherent and relatively soft material which causes poor adhe -
sion between the coating and the substrate [111] . The amount of 
deformation due to micro blasting depends on various parameters and 
improper selection of them will not yield the desired results. It was re -
ported that the low pressure micro blasted tools did not perform well in

ground/micro blasted (G/mb) samples. This improvement in the film

films by the reduction of tungsten carbide regions and enlargement of

This increased adhesiveness of the films can also be judged on the 
basis of the surface energies which can be determined by measuring the 
contact angles. Micro abrasive blasting of the surface increases the 
surface energies which in turn enhances the adhesion between the 
substrate and the coating [116] . 
The study of the physical and mechanical characteristics of the micro

studies there is no agreement in the extent of benefits acquired out of 
such micro blasting [106,109] .

4.1. Future perspective 
In the modern-day world where we are facing so much environ -
mental issues, the surface modification techniques to improve the cut -
ting tool performance are the best possible alternative to any of the 
above-mentioned technique. Efforts can be made further to enhance 
their capability and effectiveness during the dry machining operation so 
that lubrication and cooling requirements can be eliminated. Future 
studies can be extended in following areas: 
(i) Utilization of laser texturing and micro abrasive blasting on tools 
to enhance their performance: Laser texturing on the tool rake 
surface helps in enhancing its performance by reducing the wear 
owing to change in chip flow direction, and proper penetration of 
fluid. The utilization of this technique with micro abrasive 
blasting will be significantly effective in enhancing the cutting 
tool performance. 
(ii) Development of advanced coating deposition techniques: The 
commercial PVD techniques utilized to deposit nitride-based 
coatings on cutting tools have various lacunas i.e., coating sur -
face defects, poor adhesion, and low ionisation rates. New 
coating deposition techniques can be developed to enhance the 
performance of the cutting tools and overcome the existing 
problems.
