1. Introduction
The magnesium-rare-earth (Mg-RE) alloy, recognized for its
heat resistance, exhibits commendable elevated-temperature
properties, indicating a promising future for its use in high-
temperature settings (Ref 1-3). Traditional Mg-RE alloys are
reﬁned by zirconium (Zr) alloying (Ref 4, 5). However, the Zr-
reﬁned alloy exhibits inadequate thermal stability, and the
reﬁnement of the grains does not lead to better performance
under high-temperature conditions (Ref 6). The incorporation
of Aluminium (Al) into Mg-RE alloys elicits a grain reﬁnement
effect analogous to that achieved with Zr. Meanwhile, intro-
ducing thermally stable secondary phases with different
morphologies signiﬁcantly enhances the alloy’s elevated-tem-
perature mechanical performance and creep resistance (Ref 7-
9). Hence, the Al-reﬁned Mg-RE alloy emerges as a promising
candidate for novel heat-resistant magnesium alloy applications
(Ref 10).
Utilizing cost-efﬁcient manganese (Mn) alloying represents
a practical approach to augmenting the high-temperature
performance of Mg-RE alloys. Xu et al. (Ref 11) substituted
Mn for Zr in the Mg-Gd-Y -Zn-Zr alloy, discovering that
including Mn reduced the (Gd + Y) concentration in the
secondary phase, thereby fostering precipitation strengthening.
As a result, the density of precipitated phases in Mn-added
alloys is signiﬁcantly higher than in Zr-added alloys, signiﬁ-
cantly improving their elevated-temperature properties. Includ-
ing Mn in the peak-aged alloy results in a creep rate reaching
up to 2.4 9 10
/C08 s/C01 (250 /C176C/150 MPa). Furthermore,
incorporating Mn in Mg-RE alloys augments the alloy’s
elevated-temperature performance by introducing thermally
stable secondary phases. Celikin et al. (Ref 12, 13) found that
Mg-RE-Mn will dynamically precipitate a large number of
nano-sized a-Mn particles under high-temperature creep (175-
200 /C176C/40-50 MPa). These a-Mn particles play a crucial role in
immobilizing dislocations and impeding their movement,
enhancing the alloy’s creep resistance. Zhu et al. (Ref 14)
found that adding Mn to the die-cast Mg-4Al-3La alloy can
introduce thermally stable nano-sized Al-Mn particles during
high-temperature creep, thereby signiﬁcantly improving the
creep resistance of the alloy. At 175 /C176C/75 MPa, the minimum
creep rate of the alloy with 0.32 wt. % Mn (3.0 9 10
/C010 s/C01)
was reduced by more than three orders of magnitude compared
with the Mg-4Al-3La alloy without Mn addition (5.9 9 10/C07
s/C01). Therefore, adding Mn to the cast Al-reﬁned Mg-RE alloy
is expected to improve its creep resistance further.
Previous studies have found that Al-reﬁned Mg-Y alloys
have excellent creep resistance (Ref 10). The current research
delves into the inﬂuence of varying amounts of Mn (0, 0.3, 0.6,
0.9 wt.%) on the microstructural characteristics, elevated-
temperature mechanical behavior, and creep behavior of Al-
reﬁned Mg-RE alloys. Furthermore, it elucidates the funda-
mental mechanisms responsible for enhancing creep properties

2. Experimental Procedures
Utilizing magnesium ( > 99.99 wt.%), aluminum ( > 99.99
wt.%), and master alloys containing Mg-30 wt.% Y and Mg-10
wt.% Mn, a series of Mg-8.44Y -0.9Al-xMn (x = 0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9
wt.%) alloys were prepared. The aforementioned raw materials
were introduced into a resistance furnace, and the melting
temperature was raised to 750 /C176C. Protective gas, a blend of SF
6
and CO2 with a volume ratio of 1 : 99, was infused throughout
the melting stage. Upon complete melting, the alloy is cast into
a metal mold that has been preheated to 200 /C176C. The precise
chemical composition of the alloy was determined using
inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, as outlined in
Table 1.
Following the mechanical polishing process, the specimens
underwent etching with a picric acid solution made from a
blend of ethanol of 35 mL, deionized water of 10 mL, acetic
acid of 4 mL, and picric acid of 5 g. The polarized
microstructure of the alloy was analyzed using an optical
microscope (OM, DSX1000), and the grain sizes were
quantitatively assessed utilizing the linear intercept method
for precise measurement. The phase composition was analyzed
using an x-ray diffractometer (XRD, X’Pert Pro) running at 40
kV with Cu Ka radiation, scanning from 10-80 /C176at 4/C176/min. The
alloy’s microstructure and fracture features were further studied
with scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Apreo C). The
density of possible effective nucleation particles in the grains
was counted using the point recording method. The internal
phase microstructures were analyzed using transmission elec-
tron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X). For TEM analysis,
samples were ﬁrst mechanically ground to about 40 microme-
tres before ion milling at 20 lA current and a 4 /C176ion gun angle.
Utilizing Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) to assess the
alloy’s orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) and the distri-
bution of orientation differences between the Al
2Y phase and
the a-Mg matrix. The alloy’s OIM and kernel average
misorientation (KAM) following creep testing were also
characterized. For EBSD sample preparation, an electrolytic
polishing technique was employed with an electrolyte solution
consisting of hydrochloric acid of 12 mL, nitric acid of 8 mL,
and ethanol of 100 mL. The polishing operation was performed
under the following optimized parameters: voltage of 23 V ,
current of 5.1 A, and electrolysis duration of 30 s.
Tensile testing at ambient and elevated temperatures was
conducted using the MTS E44.304 electronic universal testing
apparatus. The specimens for tensile testing were prepared into
a lath shape, with dimensions of 15 9 3 9 2m m
3 for the
gauge length, and were subjected to a tensile rate of 1 mm/min
at ambient temperature. For high-temperature tests, the spec-
imens were preheated within a resistance furnace for a duration
of 10 minutes prior to testing, with a tensile rate of 0.25 mm/
min. The tensile creep testing was executed on the RDL100
creep testing rig. For the creep test, the specimens were
prepared as ﬂat samples, possessing a gauge length of 30 mm
and a cross-sectional area measuring 8 9 5m m
2. The creep
tests were performed under 300 /C176C/50 MPa. Temperature
regulation during the creep test was managed by a thermocou-
ple, ensuring a precision of ± 1 /C176C.
3. Result and Discussion
3.1 Microstructure
Examination of Fig. 1(a) and (e) reveals that the alloy
without Mn exhibits the smallest grains (73.2 ± 2.9 lm),
characterized by equiaxed dendritic structures. Increments in
Mn content are correlated with a progressive enlargement of the
alloy’s grains (Fig. 1b-d, f-h). At a Mn concentration of 0.9
wt.%, the alloy’s grain morphology shifts to coarse equiaxed
dendrites, with the average grain size increasing to 140.5 ± 7.5
lm, as shown in Fig. 1(d) and (h). This observation indicates
that the incorporation of Mn leads to grain coarsening. Analysis
of Fig. 2 indicates that the without Mn alloy comprises a-Mg,
Mg
24Y5, and Al 2Y phases. The alloy adding Mn is still
composed of the above phases, and no new diffraction peak is
observed. Therefore, no new phase is produced after adding
Mn.
Figure 3 presents the SEM micrographs of the alloys
containing varying amounts of Mn. In the without Mn alloy
depicted in Fig. 3(a) and (b), four distinct morphologies of
secondary phases are identiﬁed: a network phase, particulate
phase, acicular phase, and lamellar phase. The network
morphology corresponds to the Mg
24Y5 phase, while both the
particulate and acicular forms are attributed to the Al 2Y phase.
The lamellar phase is identiﬁed as LPSO/SFs (Ref 10). The
absence of the characteristic diffraction peak for the LPSO/SFs
phase in the XRD pattern (Fig. 2) is due to its scarcity. Upon
Mn incorporation, the alloy retains these phase morphologies.
However, with increasing Mn content, there is a slight increase
in the particulate Al
2Y phase, whereas the other secondary
phases’ quantities remain unchanged (Fig. 3c-h).
An Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was
performed on the alloy containing 0.6 wt.% Mn to elucidate the
chemical composition of the alloy’s phases after Mn incorpo-
ration, as depicted in Fig. 4. Examination of Fig. 4(a) and (b)
reveals an enrichment of Y elements at the grain boundaries.
The network phase (Mark 1) is predominantly constituted by
Mg and Y , which, combined with XRD data, is identiﬁed as the
Mg
24Y5 phase (Fig. 4c). The particulate phase (Mark 2) and the
acicular phase (Mark 3) are primarily composed of Al, Y , and
Mn, with an approximate atomic ratio of 2 : 1 for Al, Mn and Y ,
as shown in Fig. 4(d) and (e), conﬁrming these phases as Al 2Y

2Y nucleation particles

the number of effective Al 2Y nucleation particles decreases,
resulting in a count of 44/mm 2 in the 0.9 wt.% Mn alloy.
Hence, the observed enlargement of the alloy’s grain size is
attributed to the decrease in nucleation particles as the Mn
content increases.

3.2 Mechanical Property
Figure 8 presents the engineering stress-strain curves for the
alloys containing varying amounts of Mn. At ambient temper-
ature, the without Mn alloy exhibits superior mechanical
properties, with ultimate tensile strength (UTS), yield strength
(YS), and elongation (EL) recorded at 216.9 ± 4.2 MPa,
150.2 ± 3.4 MPa, and 6.9 ± 0.3%, respectively. As the Mn
content increases, the alloy’s mechanical properties deteriorate.
At an Mn concent of 0.9 wt.%, the respective values for UTS,
YS, and EL are reduced to 197.6 ± 3.8 MPa, 147.1 ± 2.9
MPa, and 5.3 ± 0.3%. Elevated temperatures lead to a
diminution in the strength of the alloys, accompanied by a
marked enhancement in elongation. At 300 /C176C, the alloy
without Mn exhibits the least favorable mechanical properties,
with UTS, YS, and EL dropping to 158.6 ± 3.4 MPa,
111.2 ± 2.2 MPa, and 32.4 ± 1.8%, respectively. Alloys
incorporating Mn demonstrate good mechanical properties at
high temperatures, with the alloy containing 0.6 wt.% Mn
exhibiting UTS, YS, and EL of 165.7 ± 3.5 MPa, 111.8 ± 2.2
MPa, and 39.2 ± 2.1%, respectively.

The alloy without Mn exhibits the most favourable mechan-
ical attributes at ambient temperature, primarily attributed to the
smaller grain size. The Hall-Petch relationship encapsulates the
interplay between grain size and yield strength (Ref 16),
stipulating that a reduction in grain size results in increased
yield strength. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the alloy without Mn
addition has the smallest grain size, recorded at 73.2 ± 2.9 lm.
The abundance of grain boundaries serves as an effective
barrier to dislocation movement, ultimately enhancing the
alloy’s strength (Ref 17, 18). The incorporation of Mn induces
grain coarsening, which in turn diminishes the mechanical
properties. At elevated temperatures, the alloy without Mn
addition exhibits reduced mechanical performance. This degra-
dation is attributed to the increase of grain boundaries as weak
areas at high temperatures, which leads to a decrease in the
mechanical properties of the alloy (Ref 19). Conversely, adding
Mn results in grain coarsening and reducing grain boundaries,
paradoxically enhancing the alloy’s mechanical properties at
high temperatures. Furthermore, the Mn atom, with an atomic
radius of 0.124 nm that is smaller than the Mg atom’s radius of
0.16 nm, can curtail the rate of solute diffusion, thereby
reinforcing the Mg matrix and augmenting the alloy’s strength
and ductility at high temperatures (Ref 11).
3.3 Creep Property
As illustrated in Figure 11(a), the alloys were subjected to a
creep tensile test at 300 /C176C and 50 MPa for a duration of 100
hours. The tested alloys did not break throughout this period
and were in the steady-state creep stage (second stage). The
Mn-free alloy demonstrated the poorest creep resistance.
Nevertheless, a gradual improvement in creep resistance was
observed with an increase in Mn content. Fig. 11(b) delineates
the alloy without Mn displayed a minimum creep rate of
1.31 9 10
-8 s-1. Upon increasing the Mn content, there was a
corresponding decrease in the minimum creep rate. Notably, at
a Mn concentration of 0.9 wt.%, the minimum creep rate was
reduced to 2.42 9 10-9 s-1, signifying a reduction by approx-
imately an order of magnitude in comparison to the alloy
without Mn. These ﬁndings illustrate that incorporating Mn
into the alloy signiﬁcantly improves its resistance to creep.

creep. The alloy without Mn exhibits the smallest grain size,
with some grains showing a color gradient, indicating that some
substructured grain structures with different orientations are
formed inside the grains, which is caused by plastic deforma-
tion during creep (Fig. 12a). In contrast, the addition of Mn
leads to an increase in grain size and a uniform color within

RE alloys involves grain boundary migration, and dislocation
climb (Ref 22, 23). At elevated temperatures, grain boundaries
become areas of weakness, and the presence of ﬁner grains
leads to an increased number of grain boundaries. This increase
can intensify grain boundary slip, which in turn diminishes the
alloy’s high-temperature performance (Ref 19, 24). Introducing
Mn into the alloy leads to grain coarsening, thereby efﬁciently
hindering grain boundary migration (Ref 25). Furthermore,

4. Conclusion
(1) The alloy without Mn addition exhibits the ﬁnest grain
size, averaging at 73.2 ± 2.9 lm. Upon augmentation
of Mn content, a gradual enlargement in the alloy’s
grain size is observed. Speciﬁcally, at a Mn content of
0.9 wt.%, the average grain size rises to 140.5 ± 7.5
lm.
(2) The alloy devoid of Mn addition comprises Mg
24Y5,
Al2Y , and LPSO/SFs phases. The phase morphology re-
mains unaltered upon Mn addition, yet a minor fraction
of Mn is incorporated into the Al 2Y phase. This phe-
nomenon results in an augmented orientation disparity
between the Al
2Y phase and the a-Mg matrix crystal
plane, concomitantly reducing the count of effective
Al2Y nucleation particles.
(3) At ambient temperature, the mechanical properties of
the alloy deteriorate gradually with increasing Mn con-
tent. Speciﬁcally, the alloy without Mn exhibits excep-
tional mechanical properties, with UTS of 216.9 ± 4.2
MPa, YS of 150.2 ± 3.4 MPa, and EL of 6.9 ± 0.3%.
However, at elevated temperatures, the 0.6 wt.% Mn al-
loy exhibits optimal mechanical properties due to its
superior grain thermal stability, attaining UTS of
165.7 ± 3.5 MPa, YS of 111.8 ± 2.2 MPa, and EL of
39.2 ± 2.1%.
(4) At 300 /C176C and 50 MPa, the creep properties of the alloy
is progressively enhanced as the Mn content augments.
In comparison to the Mn-free alloy (1.31 9 10
/C08 s/C01),
the 0.9 wt.% Mn alloy (2.42 9 10/C09 s/C01) demonstrates
a minimum creep rate that is decreased by approxi-
