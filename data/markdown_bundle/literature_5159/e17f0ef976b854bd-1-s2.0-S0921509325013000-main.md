1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), as a novel alloy system comprising of 
multiple principal elements, have garnered significant attention due to 
their exceptional mechanical properties [ 1 – 4 ]. Refractory HEAs 
(RHEAs), which contains high-melting-point elements (e.g., W, Nb, Zr) 
demonstrate promising applications in aerospace and nuclear engi -
neering [ 5 – 7 ]. However, their practical engineering applications are 
severely limited by inherent drawbacks including high density (typically 
> 8 g/cm
3
) and room-temperature brittleness (fracture strain < 10 %) 
[ 8 – 10 ]. Recent studies reveal that incorporating lightweight elements 
(e.g., Al, Ti, V) can effectively reduce the density of the alloys while 
preserving a certain plastic deformation capability [ 11 , 12 ]. Neverthe -
less, lightweight refractory HEAs (LRHEAs) still face two critical chal -
lenges at room temperature: (i) relatively low specific strength and (ii) 
inevitable deterioration of the plasticity during strengthening processes, 
failing to achieve ideal strength-ductility synergy [ 13 – 16 ]. For instance, 
Ti
3

870 MPa with plasticity markedly below 20 % [ 7 ]. The yield strength of 
TiZrV

plasticity decreases to 14.8 % due to the addition of Al [ 5 ].
Recent advances have focused on microstructural engineering stra -
tegies to achieve concurrent optimization of yield strength and plas -
ticity. For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that a remarkable synergy 
between yield strength (~1.4 GPa) and plasticity ( > 20 %) can be ach -
ieved by adjusting dislocation movement through nano-precipitates 
[ 12 ]. Jin et al. discovered that a simultaneously enhancement of 
strength and ductility in TiZrNbTaAl HEAs can be obtained by the 
transformation between A2 and B2 induced by a precise control of Al 
content [ 17 ]. Furthermore, V has been proven to improve the solid so -
lution stability and reduce the ductile-brittle transition temperature in 
TiNbZr systems, thereby expanding the room-temperature plasticity 
window [ 16 ]. These findings suggest that regulation of the microstruc -
ture by alloying is an effective way to improve the strength and plasticity 
of the LRHEAs [ 5 , 18 ]. In this context, Si, as a typical alloying element 
with both low density and high reactivity, demonstrates significant 
potential in the regulation of the mechanical properties of LRHEAs. On 
one hand, the density of the alloy after Si doping will be significantly 
reduced due to the extremely low density of Si. On the other hand, a 
substantial second-phase strengthening effect can be obtained because

Fig. 2 presents the backscattered electron (BSE) micrographs illus -

shown in Fig. 2a – c, and e, all three alloys exhibit a typical coexistence of 
columnar primary and secondary dendrites, where dendritic refinement 
occurs as Si content increases. To visually demonstrate the relationship 
between the size of the dendrites and the content of Si, we conducted 
statistical analysis on the length of primary and secondary dendrites in 
Table 1 . For Si0 alloy, the average length of the primary dendrites is 
approximately 500 μ m, and that of the secondary dendrites is about 150 
μ m. After adding 1 at.% Si, the primary dendrites of the alloy were 
shortened to approximately 150 μ m, and the length of the secondary 
dendrites was reduced to about 60 μ m. When Si content further in -
creases to 2 at.%, the primary dendrites in Si2 alloy shrink to about 80 
μ m, while the secondary dendrites further refined to approximately 30 
μ m. Furthermore, from the local magnification images of Fig. 2 a – c and e 
(as presented in Fig. 2b – d and f), it can be seen that no obvious structural 
features can be detected in the inter-dendritic regions of Si0 alloy, while 
rod-like structure appeared in these regions of Si1 alloy. Further 
increasing the content of Si to 2 at.%, rod-like structures evolved into a 
coexistence of lamellar and rod-like structures ( Fig. 2 g), which are 
embedded within a light gray network structure (confirmed as BCC 
structure in the following section). According to the findings reported by 
Guo and Xu, it can be known that the inter-dendritic regions of Si1 and 
Si2 alloys are eutectic structures [ 19 , 20 ].

The observations presented in the previous section unambiguously 
show that: (i) the doping of Si not only refines the grain size of the alloy 
but also induces the formation of submicron-scale silicide at the grain 
boundaries; (ii) after Si doping, the characteristic strength of the alloy is 
significantly enhanced, while its plasticity is somewhat reduced. This 
can be attributed to the synergistic effect of fine-grain strengthening and 
second-phase strengthening. On one hand, the addition of Si signifi -
cantly reduces the average grain size of the alloys, leading to a Hall- 
Petch-type strengthening effect [ 24 , 41 ]. Grain boundaries, acting as 
dislocation slip barriers and energy storage interfaces, not only enhance 
yield strength but also improve deformation uniformity and ductility [ 1 ,
42 ]. Additionally, smaller grains shorten the dislocation free path, 
further suppressing macroscopic stress concentration and contributing 
to uniform plastic flow behavior. This results in a simultaneous increase 
in the specific strength and plasticity. On the other hand, the B2 
coherent precipitated phases with a size of 3–7 nm are stably distributed 
in the BCC matrix and maintains good compatibility with the matrix due 
to its low mismatch interface. Such fine coherent precipitates can 
effectively pin dislocations and significantly increase the critical shear 
stress, delay the occurrence of Orowan bypass, and thereby enhance the 
yield strength of the alloy [ 43 ]. In addition, the introduction of Si also 
induces the precipitation of submicron-scale silicide at the grain 
boundaries, which hinders the slip of dislocations and further 
strengthening the alloy [ 44 ]. However, high-volume fractions of hard 
and brittle silicide phases also result in the stress concentration and 
crack initiation, leading to an increase in cleavage and tearing edges at 
the fracture surfaces ( Fig. 8 ) [ 34 ]. Furthermore, silicide can passivate 
the dislocation sources, weaken work hardening capabilities, and ulti -
mately deteriorate the plasticity. In a word, the synergistic interaction 
between these two mechanisms enables the alloy to maintain favorable 
deformability alongside remarkable strength enhancement.

good ductility. This work indicates that moderate Si doping is an 
effective strategy for developing high-performance LRHEAs.
