Refractory high-entropy superalloys (RHESAs) with a coherent BCC/B2 microstructure, analogous to the γ / γ ′ 
structure in Ni-based superalloys, are regarded as promising candidates to surpass Ni-based superalloys due to 
their high melting points and exceptional creep resistance. However, the inherent brittleness and high- 
temperature instability of the B2 phase limit their practical application. This study focuses on the

1. Introduction
Among light-weight refractory high-entropy alloys (LRHEAs) [ 1 , 2 ], a 
class exhibiting a unique BCC/B2 coherent microstructure analogous to 
the γ / γ ′ structure in Ni-based superalloys is termed refractory 
high-entropy superalloys (RHESAs) [ 3 – 5 ]. These alloys are considered 
promising candidates to surpass Ni-based superalloys due to their 
inherently high melting points and the creep resistance imparted by 
their disordered/ordered (semi-)coherent interfaces [ 6 – 9 ].
Initial RHESAs, such as AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr and Al0.5NbTa0.8 -
Ti1.5V0.2Zr, exhibited minimal room-temperature ductility due to the 
inherent brittleness of the hard-deformable B2 phase [ 10 , 11 ]. Conse -
quently, significant efforts have been devoted to mitigating this 
brittleness. For instance, V. Soni et al. [ 12 ] observed a "phase inversion" 
phenomenon in Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr after isothermal annealing at 
873 K for 120 h. In this process, BCC precipitations diffuse and constrict 
the continuous B2 channels, transforming the original continuous B2 
into submicron-sized spherical B2 precipitates, thereby significantly 
enhancing compressive ductility at room temperature. Adopting alloy -
ing design to improve the deformability of B2 is also an effective way to 
improve plasticity, such as using Ti element for B2 toughening treatment 
[ 13 ].
Furthermore, the stability of the ordered B2 superlattice phase is 
critical for the high-temperature performance of these alloys [ 14 , 15 ]. 
However, practical investigations reveal that this alloy system is prone 
to destabilization and decomposition at elevated temperatures, leading

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 presents the TEM results of the ST sample. The TEM bright- 
field (BF) image in Fig. 1 (a) reveals a clean microstructure devoid of 
significant secondary phases. However, the selected-area electron 
diffraction (SAED) pattern acquired along the [011] zone axis ( Fig. 1 (b)) 
exhibits faint superlattice reflections indicative of the B2 phase. To 
amplify this feature, the diffraction intensity profile along the line 
connecting the (000) and ( 200) reflections in Fig. 1 (b) was plotted (see 
Fig. 1 (c)). This profile clearly shows a distinct intensity peak corre -
sponding to the ( 100) superlattice reflection, confirming the presence 
of the B2 phase. To directly visualize the morphology of the B2 phase, 
the TEM dark-field (DF) imaging was performed using the superlattice 
reflection circled by the green dashed line in Fig. 1 (b). The DF image 
shown in Fig. 1 (d) demonstrates that the B2 phase exists as diffuse 
nanodomains with sizes below 5 nm in the ST sample. High-resolution 
TEM (HRTEM) imaging along the [001] zone axis is shown in Fig. 1
(e). An inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) image was generated using 
the superlattice reflections from this HRTEM image, yielding the result 
presented in Fig. 1 (f). As highlighted by the white dashed box, the IFFT 
image confirms that the B2 phase exists as nanodomains embedded 
within the BCC matrix, forming a nanoscale dual-phase structure.
The formation of this microstructure includes two processes: spino -
dal decomposition and ordering [ 3 , 17 ]. Initially, the thermodynami -
cally unstable BCC parent phase undergoes spontaneous spinodal 
decomposition, leading to its separation into two phases with distinct 
compositions but identical crystal structures. Once the critical compo -
sition for ordering is reached, one of these phases undergoes an ordering 
reaction, transforming into B2 domains.

are enriched in the B2 phase but depleted in the Al – Zr phase. As 
demonstrated in the study by Vishal Soni et al. [ 19 , 20 ], the presence of 
BCC-stabilizing elements such as Nb and Ta is essential for the formation 
of the B2 structure. Consequently, the expulsion of these elements from 
the B2 phase destabilizes it and promotes the formation of the Al – Zr 
intermetallic phase.
The transition from the B2 to the Al – Zr phase involves both 
elemental diffusion redistribution and a displacive transformation 
mechanism. Initially, the B2 phase undergoes compositional adjustment 
through atomic exchange with the BCC matrix and elemental redistri -
bution, during which BCC-stabilizing elements such as Ti, Nb, and Ta are 
expelled, thereby enriching Al and Zr, which can be attributed to the 
lower formation energy and higher thermodynamic stability of the Al – Zr 
phase under the given temperature and compositional conditions. From 
a chemical perspective, the strong interaction between Al and Zr also 
promotes their clustering and facilitates the formation of specific 
intermetallic compounds [ 21 ]. When the concentration of these 
BCC-stabilizing elements in the B2 phase decreases below a critical level, 
the stability of the B2 phase is reduced, triggering local atomic rear -
rangements [ 22 ]. The core of this process involves the periodic collapse 
and contraction of the (222) B2 plane along the [111] direction, where 
adjacent atomic planes merge to form the new basal plane of the hex -
agonal structure [ 16 ]. This mechanism enables low lattice mismatch at 
the interface between the B2 and Al – Zr phases.
In the quenched sample, the B2 phase exists as nanoscale clusters due 
to spinodal decomposition of the metastable high-temperature BCC 
parent phase and subsequent B2 ordering. The rapid cooling rate results 
in the development of short-wavelength concentration waves, leading to 
the formation of extremely fine B2 domains. During subsequent aging, 
the spinodal structure continues to coarsen, increasing in both wave -
length and amplitude, and eventually evolves into a nanoscale inter -
connected (IC) architecture characterized by an interwoven network of 
B2 phase and BCC phase [ 17 ]. As aging proceeds, elemental 
redistribution intensifies, resulting in increased lattice mismatch be -
tween the two phases. The rejection of Ta from the B2 channels drives its 
inward diffusion, facilitating the formation of Ta-rich BCC cubic pre -
cipitates. When the depletion of Nb and Ta in the B2 phase reaches a 
critical threshold, the B2 phase becomes unstable under the given con -
ditions and undergoes periodic displacive transformation, ultimately 
transforming into an Al – Zr intermetallic compound. This transition 
originates from lattice displacements within the B2 matrix, accompa -
nied by significant depletion of Ti, Nb, and Ta, and concurrent enrich -
ment of Al and Zr. The entire microstructure evolution is shown in Fig. 5 . 
These findings provide a clearer understanding of the phase evolution in 
BCC/B2-structured RHESAs, enabling more precise control of elemental 
redistribution to enhance the high-temperature stability of the B2 phase 
in future alloy design.

Recent studies suggest that dislocations can be activated within the B2 
phase during deformation [ 24 , 25 ]. Following the dissociation of 
super-dislocations in the B2 phase, the resulting superpartial disloca -
tions can transmit across the coherent interface into the BCC phase. 
Utilizing their parallel slip systems (e.g., {110} slip planes and < 111 >
slip directions), these dislocations continue to glide. The preferential 
transmission of edge dislocations provides the primary pathway for 
overall plastic deformation, while the "anchoring effect" of screw dis -
locations may slightly suppress transmission efficiency.
Consequently, in the 60h-aged sample, dislocations can glide across 
the interface, accommodating plastic deformation without sacrificing 
ductility. Compared to the ST sample, the larger B2 domains in the 60h- 
aged condition offer greater resistance to dislocation transmission across 
interfaces, resulting in a higher strain hardening rate. In contrast, the 
150h-aged sample suffers from degradation of the B2 phase and the 
precipitation of Al-Zr intermetallic compounds. These compounds 
disrupt the coherent interfaces and impede dislocation slip transfer. This 
obstruction hinders dislocation multiplication during plastic deforma -
tion, leading to the observed reduction in the strain hardening rate [ 26 ].

4. Conclusion
This study elucidates the intrinsic phase evolution and elemental 
diffusion mechanisms in BCC/B2-structured RHESAs, clarifying the 
critical influence of B2 phase stability on mechanical properties, and 
providing a theoretical foundation for optimizing the high-temperature 
stability of RHESAs. 
1. B2 Phase Evolution: The B2 nanodomains in the ST condition origi -
nate from the synergistic interplay of spinodal decomposition and 
ordering. Upon prolonged aging, the B2 phase progressively coarsens 
from nanodomains into an interconnected network structure. After 
60 h of aging, a well-defined coherent BCC/B2 microstructure 
(analogous to the γ / γ ′ structure) develops, accompanied by the pre -
cipitation of cuboidal Ta-rich phases.
2. Al-Zr Intermetallic Formation: During 150 h of aging, the B2 phase 
degrades directly to form Al-Zr intermetallic compounds. These 
compounds exhibit a characteristic serrated morphology, growing 
outward from elongated B2 precursors rather than nucleating anew. 
This process involves significant enrichment of Al and Zr coupled 
with depletion of Ti, Nb, and Ta at the reaction front, providing 
direct evidence for the origin of the Al-Zr phase.
3. Mechanical Behavior and Deformation Mechanisms: The 60h-aged 
sample exhibits a high strain hardening rate, attributed to the 
coherent BCC/B2 interfaces enabling efficient dislocation slip 
transfer. In contrast, the Al-Zr intermetallics in the 150h-aged sample 
disrupt the coherent interfaces, hindering dislocation slip transfer 
and consequently reducing the strain hardening rate. Notably, both 
strength and ductility maintain stability despite these microstruc -
tural changes.
