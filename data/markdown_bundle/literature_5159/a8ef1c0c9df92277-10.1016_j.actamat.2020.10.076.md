Abstract 
Coherent precipitation of cuboidal γ' phase is an efficient strategy to improve high temperature 
properties in nickel-based alloys. The present work aims to introduce th is strengthening mechanism 
into copper alloys to solve the issue of deficient heat resistance. The results shown that a series of 
cuboidal γ' coherent precipitation-strengthened Cu 100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys were obtained in the 
(Ni+Al) content range of 20 at. % to 50 at. %. These alloys are shown to exhibit excellent

environments. In further research es, continued efforts will be required in the multi -component 
design of Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys to improve the comprehensive performance.

1. Introduction 
Copper alloys have been extensively employed in electric power, chemical engineering, 
metallurgy, and aerospace applications owing to their excellent electrical and thermal 
conductivity [1-3]. With the rapid development of industry, there is an increasing demand for 
the use of copper alloys in high -temperature, high -load environments. However, the heating 
resistance of ordinary copper alloys is poor and cannot meet the requirements for high 
temperature linings, special electric contact materials and electrode material s for resistance 
welding, etc. [4, 5]. Improvement of mechanical properties of copper alloys is mainly achieved 
through the strengthening by dispersed nano-precipitates, such as Cu–Cr, Cu–Cr–Zr and Cu–Be 
alloys, etc. [6-9], resulting in excellent room-temperature (RT) strength. However, the softening 
temperature of such copper alloys hardly exceeds 600 °C because of the excessive coarsening of 
nanoparticles. The addition of high melting point oxides (e.g., Al2O3) remarkably increases the 
softening temperature of copper alloys (up to ~900 °C) [10, 11] , however, this approach 
significantly reduce s the plasticity and introduce s other disadvantages , such as non-coherent 
interfaces, the generation of side reaction inclusions via side reactions, and contamination of 
with impurity elements. Therefore, there is an urgent demand for innovative approaches to 
enhance the heat resistance of copper alloys. 
Nickel-based alloys are known to have superior high-temperature (HT) properties, achieving a 
temperature bearing capacity of approximately 90% of the melting point of Ni, which is attributed 
to the coherent precipitation of the γ' phase (Ni 3Al, Pm3̅m) in the γ phase ( Fm3̅m) [12, 13]. The γ' 
phase is a hard intermetallic compound with a high melting point . Because of the precipitation of 
this phase, the strengthening mechanism s of nickel -based alloy s mainly include coherency

strengthening, modulus mismatch strengthening and order strengthening. Thus, the movement of 
dislocations is effectively obstructed by the coherent stress field and anti -phase boundary (APB) 
energy [14, 15]. Cu and Ni have identical structures (fcc) and similar lattice constants ( aCu=0.3615 
nm, aNi=0.3524 nm). Previous studies have confirmed that coherent γ' phase precipitates can be 
generated in Cu–Ni–Al alloys [16, 17]. Our previous work [18] also demonstrated that coherent γ' 
phase strengthened Cu50Ni37.5Al12.5 (at.%) alloy has a high softening temperature (the differences 
between the γ' and γ phases .are shown in Tab. S1 ). Therefore, it is anticipated that the 
strengthening model of nickel -based alloys can be used for the Cu –Ni–Al alloys for the 
development of new heat resistance copper alloys. 
According to the Cu–Ni–Al ternary phase diagram (Fig.S1) [19], once the Al content is higher 
than the Ni content , Cu-Al-Ni alloys (also known as nickel-aluminum bronze (NAB)) can be 
obtained, where the main strengthening phase is martensite β phase with shape memory effect 
(based on Cu3Al) and κ phases, of which κ I, κII, κIV phases (based on Fe3Al) are rich in iron and κ III 
phase (based on NiAl) is rich in nickel [20-22]. The typical NAB CuAl10Fe5Ni5 has high tensile 
strength, good wear resistance , and excellent corrosion resistance, and is widely used for marine 
propellers and pumps [20, 23] . However, its HT properties are deficient. Previous work s have 
shown that the mechanical properties greatly degrade at temperatures beyond 773K because of the 
decomposition of the β and κ phase [20, 24, 25] . When the Ni content is high than the Al content, 
ultra-high strength Cu–Ni–Al alloys are obtained, and their dominant strengthening effect is derived 
from the coherent precipitation of the nano-spherical γ' phase [26]. In addition, discontinuously 
precipitated γ' or NiAl phase would appear in the grain boundary in the different alloy composition 
and heating treat process [27, 28] . Recent research es on Cu–Ni–Al alloys has focused on the

Cu87Ni10Al3 (wt. %) alloy or optimiz ed alloys based on this composition . These alloy exhibits an 
ultimate tensile strength above 1000 MPa after deformation processing, as well as good wear and 
corrosion resistance, are used in bearings, gears and propellers [27, 28]. However, to date, there has 
been little research on the microstructure and HT properties of Cu–Ni–Al alloys. Only the ultimate 
tensile strength at 500 °C of Cu90Ni7.5Al2.5 (at. %) alloy has been reported, and it is about 300 MPa 
[29]. The shape of the γ' phase gradually transforms from spherical to cuboidal during long time 
aging at 550 °C because of the variation of the lattice mismatch degree between the γ and γ' phase 
(δγ-γ′) [30], which is consistent with the microstructural evolution of nickel-based alloys. In addition, 
nickel-based alloys can possess remarkable RT or HT properties if the size (dγ′) and volume fraction 
(fγ′) of the cuboidal γ' phase is ~500 nm and 60% to 75%, respectively . [31-34]. These studies 
indicate that the HT resistance of Cu–Ni–Al alloy is expected to improve; however, further work in 
this area has not yet been undertaken. 
In present study, to explore the feasibility of coherent precipitation of cuboidal γ' phase to 
improve HT resistance of Cu alloys, a series of Cu–Ni–Al alloys with the chemical formula 
Cux[Ni3Al] will be prepared. The Ni:Al atomic ratio fixed at 3:1, to insure precipitation of the γ' 
phase, and the volume fraction ratio between the γ and γ' phase was adjusted by modifying x (x=4, 5, 
7, 8, 9, 12, 16 and 24). The microstructure, conductivity, tensile strength and hardness were 
investigated in detail, and the heating resistance was characterized through investigation of the 
softening temperature, melting point and variable temperature resistivity ; in addition , the 
strengthening and conduction mechanisms were evaluated and discussed. Finally, the application 
prospects of these alloys were clearly elucidated. The results achieved here are both technically and 
scientifically important to the accelerated development of heat resistant copper alloys.

The entire heat treatment process is executed in an argon gas atmosphere using an OTF -1200X

XRD analysis was performed on bulk and powder samples to evaluate the variation of δγ–γ′ and 
fγ. For the bulk samples, a stable coherency interface state between the γ and γ′ phase can be 
remained, and the diffraction peak position is accurate. However, the diffraction peak intensity 
exists deviation due to clear preferred orientation. The use of powder sample s can essentially 
eliminate preferred orientation, ensuring precise analysis of the diffraction peak intensity. However, 
the coherency interface is destroyed, which would produce a small disturbance of the peak position. 
Fig. 4 and Fig. S5 present XRD patterns of the bulk and powder samples, respectively. For the bulk 
samples, the (100) diffraction peak of the γ′ phase was detected and became stronger with 
increasing (Ni+Al) content, indicating that the amount of the γ′ phase increas ed. Additionally, the 
(200) preferred orientation of the γ phase was observed. In contrast, for the powder samples (Fig.

is shown in Fig. S4 and S6 ), and the results are presented in Tab. S2. The values of aγ, aγ′ and fγ′

In the Cu 100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, the fγ′ shows an upward trend as (Ni+Al) content increasing, 
from 16% to 61%. For δγ-γ′, it can be determined as the following formula: 
δγ-γ′= (aγ – aγ′)/aγ (1) 
The values of aγ and aγ′ are shown in Fig.5, with a reducing trend observed with increasing 
(Ni+Al) content. The values of aγ or aγ′ are dominated by both the atomic radii of the solutes and the 
interaction between elements, however, the latter ha s a greater effect. The mixing enthalp ies 
between elements are ΔHCu-Ni=4 kJ/mol, ΔHAl-Cu=-1 kJ/mol and ΔHAl-Ni=-22 kJ/mol [36]. A stronger 
interaction is reflected by a negative mixing enthalpy, resulting in shrinkage of the lattice, while the 
opposite effect is existed in a positive mixing enthalpy. The atomic radii of Cu, Ni and Al are 1.28 
Å, 1.25 Å and 1.43 Å, respectively [37]. In the γ′ phase, the positions of Ni atoms can be occupied 
by the dissolution of Cu, weakening the interaction between Ni and Al , in addition, the atomic radii 
of Cu is larger than that of Ni, inducing expansion of the lattice of the γ′ phase. Therefore, aγ′ of the 
Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys are higher than that of the pure γ′ phase. With increasing (Ni+Al) content, 
the amount of Cu in the γ′ phase decreased; hence, aγ′ decreased. A similar trend has shown in Cu –
Ni–Al ternary equilibrium phase diagram [19], indicating that Cu can dissolve in the γ′ phase 
finitely and that the dissolution amount changes with variation of alloy composition. 
When the Ni and Al with negative mixing enthalpy are co-dissolved in the γ phase, Al tends to 
neighbor with Ni, causing shrinkage of the lattice of the γ phase. With increasing (Ni+Al) content, 
the interaction between Ni and Al became more significant; therefore, aγ of the Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y 
alloys exhibit a declin ing trend, with all of the values lower than that of pure Cu [38, 39]. Alloy

systems with similar mixing enthalpy situation s have been widely reported for high stability 
barrierless films [40-42]. 
After elucidat ing the variation of the lattice parameters of the γ and γ′ phases, δγ-γ′ was 
determined using the above formula, and the results are presented in Fig.5. In the Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y

applicable to Cu alloys but serves as a useful reference [43]. The minimum value of δγ–γ′ is observed 
for Cu85.71(Ni3/4Al1/4)14.29 alloy and the shape is spherical; the shape of the precipitates remained in 
the transition stage from spherical to cuboidal as δγ–γ′ increased. Moreover, for the alloys containing 
a high (Ni+Al) content, the splitting behavior of the γ′ phase is observed ; this behavior is a pure 
directed diffusion process with strong elastic constraint rather than mechanical fracture [44, 45] . 
Therefore, a sufficient driving force is required for this process to occur. A previous study [46] 
demonstrated that two factors need to be satisfied according to micro-elasticity theory. 
(1) |Δ*|(m2J-1)≥0.2, where |Δ*| is defined as the ratio of δγ-γ′/γs, γs is surface energy density, and 
the value is 0.014 Jm-2; 
(2) dγ′ greater than or equal to critical size, of 120 nm. 
In Cu100-y(Ni+Al)y alloys, all of |Δ*| are larger than 0.2, and dγ′ exceeds 120 nm after (Ni+Al)

is found in Cu75(Ni3/4Al1/4)25 alloy with the dγ′ (117 nm) close to the critical size.

3.3 Mechanical properties 
In aged Cu 100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, the microstructure containing the cuboidal γ′ and γ phase s 
can be maintained over a wide composition range. However, compositional variation will result in a 
change of the volume fraction ( fγ′) and size (dγ′) of the precipitated γ′ phase and the solute content 
dissolved in the γ phase, leading to different strengthening effect s and thus modulating the 
mechanical properties of the alloys. Fig. 7(a) presents the engineering stress –engineering strain 
curves, and Fig. 7(b) presents the ultimate tensile strength ( σUTS), yield strength ( σYS), and 
elongation (δ) as a function of (Ni+Al) content for the alloys. With increasing (Ni+Al) content, fγ′ 
and dγ′ increased, σUTS gradually increased from ~586 to ~781 MPa, σYS monotonically improved 
from ~412 to ~741 MPa , and δ decreased from 10.8% to 1.7%. Similar phenomena have been 
observed for cuboidal γ′ phase strengthened Ni-based alloys. As the principal strengthening phase, 
the γ′ phase provides a high yield strength for Ni -based alloys [47]. However, the phase is hard and 
brittle, inducing poor grain -boundary cohesion and intergranular fracture, leading to insufficient 
ductility and deterioration with increasing fγ′ [48].

3.4 Heat resistance 
In the current work , the heat resistance of aged Cu 100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys are evaluated based 
on two factors: the softening temperature and the mechanical properties after annealing at different 
temperatures for 1 h. The softening temperature is a critical factor for eval uating the heat resistance 
of copper alloys. The specific procedure for the softening temperature measurement was as follows: 
the alloys were annealed at different temperatures for 1 h and cooled to room temperature by air 
cooling. Then , the hardness of the annealed alloys was measured; the sample was considered 
softened when the hardness was reduced to 80% of the RT hardness, and the corresponding 
temperature was recorded as the softening temperature [18, 49] . Fig. 8 presents the hardness –
temperature curves of the aged Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys. When the (Ni+Al) content was greater 
than 25 at.%, the hardness showed an upward trend during the entire heating process, and the values 
remained above 80% of the RT hardness invariably, indicating that the softening temperature of

σtotal=σ0 + Δσss + Δσp (2)

as well as the gradual increment of fγ′ and dγ′, which promotes solution or precipitation 
strengthening and the latter is more remarkably.

4.2 Analysis of resistivity with changing temperature 
In the above analysis, the RT and HT mechanical properties and strengthening mechanism of 
the Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys were analyzed in detail; however, this work was insufficient. For 
copper alloys, the hardness and conductivity are two important performance indicators ; therefore,

## Page 27

an exploration of conductivity at high temperature is also critical. The m icrostructural results 
revealed that a γ+γ′ dual-phase structure remained in the Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys. Thus, 
clarification of which phase determines the variation of the resistivity, namely, determination of the 
conductive body, is a prerequisite for systematic analysis of the temperature behavior of the 
resistivity. 
4.2.1 Determination of conductive body 
In previous research related to the resistivity of Cu alloys, where only the size of the 
precipitated phase was of the same order of magnitude as the electron wavelength (the order of 
angstroms), the resistivity could be increased through the scattering effect of precipitate s [53]. 
However, in the current work, both the size of the γ′ phase and the distance between γ′ phases (from 
tens to hundreds of nanometers) are larger than the electron wavelength; therefore, the conductivity 
of the alloys is mainly determined by the γ phase, and the effect of the γ′ phase on the conductivity 
can be ignored theoretically. 
The resistivity of the pure Cu and Ni 3Al phase s differ by one order of magnitude, being 
1.7×10−8 and (28.1 −51.3) × 10 −8 Ωm, respectively [54, 55] ; namely, a conducting path with 
relatively lower resistance can be selected for the movement of electrons in Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y 
alloys. However, the resistivity of the two phases is altered by the dissolution of solutes; therefore, 
the conductivity distinction of the two phases must be analyzed. 
In AFM surface current mode, the precis ely polished surface of the alloys is scanned with 
constant bias voltage, and then , the current between the probe and surface is determined to obtain 
the surface current distribution. Fig. 12(a) presents the surface current mapping for 
Cu75(Ni3/4Al1/4)25 at a bias voltage of 0.9 V . The blue and red area s corresponding to the γ and γ′

## Page 28

27 
 
 
phases can be observed, and their current s were 15.55 and 0.76 nA, respectively. Greater current 
indicates better conductivity. Based on the bias voltage and current of the two phases, the 
resistances were estimated, revealing a difference of two orders of magnitude, which indicates that 
the γ phase is still a conductive body. In addition, the red area s include large cuboidal and small 
granular phases, attributed to the precipitation of the spherical γ′ phase during low -temperature 
aging. The spherical γ′ phase with a size of approximately 13 nm precipitated in th e γ matrix 
between the adjacent cuboidal γ′ phases and was also completely coherent with the γ matrix, the 
size of which was much greater than the electron wavelength, as confirmed by the high-resolution 
TEM (HRTEM) image and corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns (Fig. 12(b)) of the 
spherical γ′ phase. It should be noted that AFM was used to characterize the surface conditions but 
lacks spatial resolution. Accordingly, Cu66.67(Ni3/4Al1/4)30.33 was further subjected to 3D 
reconstruction of the γ′ and γ phases using FIB-SEM (Fig. 13), which revealed two distributions of 
the γ′ phase (cuboidal and spherical); however, their presence did not damage the connectivity of 
the matrix. Therefore, combin ing the theoretical analysis and measured result s, the γ phase still 
remains a conductive body in Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, namely a Cu solid solution containing Ni 
and Al. The solutes drastically affect the electrical resistivity of the host metal as they cause lattice 
perturbations, modif y the lattice -vibrational spectrum and electronic band structure, and alter the 
Fermi energy and electron density of states , resulting in the relatively low conductivity of 
Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys.

4.2.2 Temperature behavior of resistivity 
In Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, γ phases remain conductive body, being solid solution structure 
which compose of pure Cu matrix ( solvent) and the dissolution of Ni and Al (solutes). Therefore, 
according to Nordheim’s rule, the resistivity of γ phase ( ργ) can be divided to two parts: the

## Page 30

29 
 
 
resistivity of pure Cu ρ0 and the resistivity of solutes (related to content of solutes c); and that can 
be indicated as follows [56]: 
ργ= ρ0+Ac(1-Bc) (3) 
where A and B are constant, c is content of solutes. 
With increasing temperature, the resistivity variation of a solid solution with high 
concentration not only depends on the effect of temperature on the resistivity of the solvent but 
also on the increase of resistivity with changing solute content [53]. The intrinsic resistivity ρ0 
(pure Cu) is derived from ideal lattice vibration, which can be altered by changing the temperature, 
and if further considering the lattice thermal expansion, the relationship between ρ0 and T can be 
expressed as follows [57]: 
ρ0 = a0+ a1T + a2T2 (a0, a1 and a2 are constant, respectivity) ( 4) 
The resistivity of solutes should be unchanged if the solute content remains steady during the 
heating process, with Ac(1−Bc), and appears almost temperature independent; however, c can vary 
with temperature. In Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, the dissolution of the γ′ phase can cause an increase 
of solute content in the γ phase, resulting in improvement of the resistivity of the γ phase. 
According to ternary phase diagram of Cu–Ni–Al alloys with different temperatures, the c is 
continuously variable as the temperature gradually increasing, if allowing cT approximately, the 
increment of resistivity due to solutes Ac(1-Bc) also can be considered in direct proportion to a′1T + 
a′2T2 (a′1 and a′2 are constant), which is expressed as the following formula: 
ργ = b0+ b1T + b2T2 (b0 , b1 and b2 are constant, respectivity) ( 5) 
Therefore, the resistivity vs. temperature plot can be fitted using the formula above, indicated 
by the dotted lines in Fig. 14. The fitting results are summarized in Table 3. With increasing (Ni+Al)

## Page 31

content, the value s of b0, b1, and b2 increase monotonically, indicating both enhancement of the 
values and the increasing slope of ργ resulting from severe lattice distortion and the increase of the 
solute content. 
At high temperature, because of the variation of defects, the measured and fitted results differ 
(the gap between the solid and dotted lines in Fig. 14) [57]. Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys are free from 
phase transition and deformation, and the dislocation density also does not substantially change. 
Therefore, the difference is mainly related to the formation of vacancies at HT, as previously 
reported for many alloys and pure metals [58, 59] . Moreover, the starting point of the deviation 
between the measured (solid line) and fitted (dotted line) curve s corresponds to the initial 
temperature ( T0) of substantial vacancy formation and characterizes the thermal stability 
optimization of Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys. With increasing (Ni+Al) content, more solutes are 
dissolved into the γ phase, strengthening the interaction between elements ; therefore, the thermal 
stability of alloys is improved and the initial temperature T0 is delayed, indicating that the formation 
of vacancies is inhibited to a certain extent. Notably, in Cu85.71(Ni3/4Al1/4)14.29 alloy, the complete 
dissolution of Ni and Al at HT , resulting in that the increment of resistivity of the alloy causing by 
re-dissolution vanished, therefore, the resistivity slightly decreased after 1000 K (as indicated by the 
red circle in Fig. 14). 
In summary, the temperature-dependent resistivity is closely associated with the solute content 
in the γ phase, resulting in two contradictory effects. On the one hand, because of the dissolution of 
solutes, the resistivity of the γ phase improve s, and the rising slope of ργ shows a slight increase 
during the heating process. On the other hand, at HT, the re -dissolution of solutes with strong 
interaction can effectively inhibit the vacancy formation to a certain extent, resulting in

## Page 32

improvement of the thermal stability of the resistivity. Therefore, in future research, 
multi-component design of Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys is necessary to promote the stability of the γ′ 
phase and purify the γ phase to ensure reasonable conductivity. In addition, under the premise of 
having little effect on the conductivity, suitable refractory elements can be added with the strong 
interaction between elements restraining vacancy formation to optimize the thermal stability of the 
resistivity at HT [40-42].

4.3 Insight on application prospects of Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys strengthened by γ′ phase 
To evaluate the application prospect s of Cu100−y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, other heat-resistant copper 
alloys were selected for reference, as shown in Fig. 15. I n current heat-resistant Cu alloys, high 
melting point oxide strengthened Cu alloys (e.g., Al2O3-strengthened Cu alloys) have the highest 
softening temperature , close to 1273 K. However, high-melting-point oxides significantly reduce 
the plasticity and can introduce other disadvantages such as non-coherent interfaces, the generation 
of inclusions from side reactions, and contamination with impurity elements, which reduce the 
processability of the alloys. Therefore, the amoun t of oxide addition is limited, and the hardness of 
Al2O3-strenghtened Cu alloys does not exceed 160HV . For CuAlFeNi alloys, the softening 
temperature can reach 923 K; however, their conductivity does not exceed 10.3%IACS, and their 
hardness values are in the range of 170–220 HV . Cu–Be alloys exhibit excellent hardness and a 
certain conductivity ; however, the softening temperature only reaches 823 K. As typical 
high-strength and high-conductivity Cu alloy s, Cu–Cr–Zr alloys possess outstanding conduct ivity 
and hardness; however, the softening temperature also remains at 823 K. After adding Mg, Si, or Co, 
the softening temperature slightly increases but not above 923 K. The softening temperature s of 
other copper alloys, such as Cu –Cd, Cu–Zn, Cu–Sn, Cu–Fe, and Cu–Mg alloys, are not higher than 
823 K. In summary, facing the increasing demand for heat-resistant copper alloys , there are few 
copper alloys with softening temperature s higher than 873 K. The main reason for low heat

resistance of current Cu alloy is the coarsening or dissolution of nano -precipitated phase at ~873K. 
In addition, discontinuous precipitates are easily formed at the grain boundaries due to violent 
solute diffusion at HT, which also is unfavorable to the mechanical properties of t he alloy. In 
summary, facing the increasing demand for heat resistance copper alloys , there are few copper

5 Conclusions

## Page 36

35 
 
 
In order to explore the influence of γ′ phase coherent-precipitation on the improvement of heat 
resistance of Cu alloys , the microstructure, the RT or HT properties and application prospect of 
aged Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys have been analyzed and evaluated in detail. The following 
conclusions could be drawn: 
The microstructure of the aged alloys consists of γ and γ′ phase s which keeps coherent 
relationship between them, and the two phases are not pure but dissolved with other alloy elements . 
With increase of (Ni+Al) content, the shape of γ′ phase s transform from spherical to cuboidal, 
relating to variation of δγ-γ′. Accordingly, due to the solution and precipitation strengthening , the 
hardness, the yield strength and melting point increase as (Ni+Al) content increases, the maximums 
are 304 HV, 781MPa and 1508 K, respectively. 
At high temperature , the alloys show three different softening behavior with variation of 
(Ni+Al) content. As the (Ni+Al) content more than 25 at.% , the alloys are hardened at HT and 
softening temperature of that exceeds 1273 K, resulting in the sharp reduction of dγ' after splitting of 
γ' phase . The hardness and yield strength of Cu80(Ni3/4Al1/4)20 alloy decreases slightly with the 
temperature rising and softening temperature of that equal to 1273K. Wh ile the (Ni+Al) content is 
less than 20 at.%, the re-dissolution of γ′ phase causes the degradation of heating resistance but their 
softening temperature is still higher than 873 K. 
In Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys, γ phases as conductive body, existing dissolution of Ni and Al, 
which results in the relatively low conductivity of the alloys. Under high temperature, the content of 
solutes in γ phase can induce the enhancement of rising slope of ργ due to severe lattice distortion, 
but the advantage is that it restrains the formation of vacancies in a certain extent and improves 
resistivity thermal stability.

## Page 37

36 
 
 
Comparing to other heat resistance Cu commercial alloys , cuboidal γ′ phase precipitation 
strengthened Cu100-y(Ni3/4Al1/4)y alloys have outstanding advantages in terms of hardness, softening 
temperature and resistivity thermal stability, showing a great potential under HT loads and service 
environments. The work provides some effective basis for multi-component design of heat 
resistance copper alloys.
