Abstract
This study investigates the microstructural evolution and tensile properties of a new nickel-iron-based high-temperature 
alloy, HT700R, after solution treatment and ageing treatment. The results show that the yield strength of the aged alloy is 
higher, while the plasticity and toughness of the solid solution-treated alloy are better. Notably, solid solution alloy yield 
strength is lower at tensile temperatures lower than 600 °C. Still, when the temperature reaches 650 °C, solid solution alloy 
yield strength will increase rapidly due to the precipitation of γ′ phase. In addition, the tensile deformation mechanism at 
the critical temperature inflexion point (600 °C) of the HT700R alloy was investigated by tensile tests, SEM and EBSD, and 
TEM techniques, and the tensile properties, dislocation substructure and grain orientation evolution were analyzed.
Keywords Nickel-based superalloy · Tensile behaviour · Deformation characteristics · Ageing heat-treatment
1 Introduction
With the increasing demand for electricity and the continu-
ous promotion of energy conservation and emission reduc-
tion policies, countries have been carrying out research work 
on ultra-supercritical power generation technology, includ-
ing the improvement of steam parameters (pressure and tem-
perature) of power station boilers to effectively increase the 
utilization rate of fossil energy and reduce the emission of 
pollutants. The development of high-parameter coal-fired 
units has become the mainstream in thermal power genera-
tion [1–3]. With the successful application of 600 ℃ ultra-
supercritical thermal power unit technology, the research 
focus of scholars at home and abroad has shifted to higher 
parameter units. The construction of 630-650 ℃ and 700 
℃ levels of ultra-supercritical coal-fired units has become 
an essential goal of coal-fired thermal power units in the 
next stage. Ultra-supercritical units are used to use T/P91, 
T/P92, SAVE12, and other martensitic heat-resistant steel 
as the primary materials [4 –6]. However, due to the lack of 
steam oxidation resistance and durability in the 650 ℃ use 
conditions, ferritic heat-resistant steel is no longer suitable 
for higher parameters of the ultra-supercritical unit [7 –10].
For this reason, domestic and foreign scientists have 
researched and developed nickel-based high-temperature 
alloys [11–13]. Nickel-based high-temperature alloys with 
nickel as the main element, with the addition of iron, cobalt, 
chromium and other components, have a high enduring 
strength, high-temperature corrosion resistance, resistance 
to thermal corrosion and other advantages [14, 15]. In for-
eign countries, research institutions have developed nickel-
based high-temperature alloys, including Inconel740H, 
Nimonic263, CCA617 and Haynes282 [16]. In contrast, the 
domestically produced nickel-iron-based high-temperature 
alloys contain only a few precious metal elements and have 
lower raw material costs. The Institute of Metals of the Chi-
nese Academy of Sciences and Xi'an Institute of Thermal 
Engineering have developed nickel-iron-based high-temper-
ature alloys such as GH984G and HT700T, which have been

and development of nickel-based high-temperature alloy 
materials, domestic, independent research and development 
of nickel-iron-based high-temperature alloys are usually 
used, such as vacuum melting or vacuum self-consumption 
can be realized to prepare [19]. Hence, the preparation pro-

ultra-supercritical unit turbine rotor faces extreme working 
conditions in service, including high temperature, high pres-
sure, high speed, etc. Therefore, it has an extreme working 
environment, which puts higher requirements on the mate-
rial’s properties, such as high-temperature strength, antioxi-

selecting suitable candidate materials is crucial for the reli-
ability of the turbine rotor of high-parameter coal-fired units. 
Suppose the tensile properties of the material are insuffi-
cient. In that case, it will quickly lead to failure phenomena 
such as tensile deformation and rupture of the component, 
which will seriously affect the unit’s regular operation.
It has been documented in the existing literature that 
different tensile test temperatures can directly affect the 
mechanical properties of nickel-based high-temperature 
alloys and that the tensile behavior of the alloys is closely 
related to their deformation mechanisms [21– 24]. In their 
study of the K416B nickel-based alloy [25, 26], Jun Xie 
et al. found that the tensile deformation of the alloy at room 
temperature was characterized by dislocation shear or an 
Orowan mechanism through the phase. When the tensile 
test temperature was increased to 800 °C, a high density 
of dislocation entanglement was formed within the alloy 
matrix, which acted as deformation strengthening. As the 
temperature rises, the dislocation density within the alloy 
matrix increases, leading to a decrease in strength. Zhong 
et al. [27] conducted tensile tests on GH2984 high-temper -
ature alloy from room temperature to 1073 K (800 ℃) after 
solid solution treatment and ageing treatment. The results 
showed that yield and tensile strength decreased gradu-
ally from room temperature to 700 °C, then significantly 
reduced at higher temperatures. Through-crystalline tough-
ness fracture was observed at room temperature, mixed 
through-crystalline and along-crystalline fracture at inter -
mediate temperatures (650 °C to 700 °C), and toughness 
fracture of the toughness of the ligament fossa at tempera-
tures higher than 700 °C. The results showed a significant 
decrease in yield strength and tensile strength at room 
temperature, followed by a substantial reduction in tensile 
strength at higher temperatures. The main deformation 
mechanisms include dislocation slip and shearing of the 
γ′ phase at room temperature, bypassing of the γ′ particles 
by the Orowan mechanism at intermediate temperatures 
(650 ℃ to 700 ℃ ), and dislocation rearrangement due to 
dynamically recovered recrystallization above 750 ℃ . It 
was found that a sawtooth-shaped rheological phenomenon 
appeared on some of its stress-strain curves, and a PLC 
shear deformation zone was found to exist on the surface 
of the tensile fracture. Jin Dan et al. [28] conducted tensile 
experiments for Haynes230 nickel-based alloy at different 
temperatures. They used the electron backscattering dif-
fraction (EBSD) technique to study the micro-mechanism 
of the tensile deformation behaviour of the material at high 
temperatures. The EBSD results show that a large number 
of deformation twins appeared in the organization near 
the fracture at room temperature, and the grain orienta-
tion was dominated; the orientation characteristics of the 
organization near the fracture were more evident at 650 
℃, and the bending deformation of the grain boundaries 
occurred, which increased the resistance to the disloca-
tion movement; with the increase of temperature, dynamic 
recrystallization grains appeared in the intergranular and 
intragranular areas near the fracture.
HT700R is a new generation of nickel-iron-based high-
temperature alloy intended for use in 700 °C ultra-super -
critical units. However, the research and application of this 
alloy are still in its infancy, and its tensile properties and 
tensile fracture mechanism under different heat treatment 
conditions have not been reported. Therefore, investigating 
the properties and deformation characteristics of this new 
nickel-iron-based high-temperature alloy by using charac-
terization techniques such as SEM, EBSD, and TEM can 
provide detailed information on the crystal structure, grain 
boundary properties, dislocation slip, and phase transition 
behaviour and can reveal its plastic deformation mecha-
nism. This is innovative for understanding the mechani-
cal behaviour of materials and optimizing material design, 
which helps improve materials’ performance and reliability. 
Compared to nickel-based high-temperature alloys, nickel-
iron-based high-temperature alloys offer advantages such 
as low cost, good machinability, oxidation resistance, heat 
fatigue resistance and creep resistance. This makes them 
promise many high-temperature applications such as aero-
space, energy, and chemicals. This study helps to promote 
the construction of A-USC ultra-supercritical power genera-
tion technology, which is of great significance in realizing 
the improvement of energy utilization efficiency, reducing 
carbon and pollutant emissions, promoting the transforma-
tion of energy structure, and promoting technological inno-
vation and industrial development.

2.2 Test Methods
After heat treatment, the alloy test bar is mechanically pro-
cessed to obtain a cylindrical tensile specimen with a mark-
ing distance of 25 mm. A marking diameter of 5 mm, and 
the tensile test is completed on a microcomputer-controlled 
electronic universal testing machine of the model E45.105 
concerning the GB/T 228.1-2010 "Tensile Test of Metallic 
Materials" standard, as shown in Fig.  2a. Figure  2b shows 
the distance marking machine used for the standard speci-
men of the tensile test. During the tensile test, three thermo-
couples were placed along the specimen marking distance, 
and the temperature change was kept within ± 2 °C. Before 
performing the tensile tests other than at room temperature, 
the samples were heated at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, and 
then held at the corresponding temperatures (100 °C, 200 
°C, 300 °C, 400 °C, 500 °C, 600 °C, 650 °C, 700 °C, 750 
°C) for 20 min with a strain rate of 2.5x10 -4 s-1. Figure  2c 
shows the macroscopic morphology after stretching at dif-
ferent temperatures.
X-ray diffractometers (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE) 
were used to examine the physical phase composition of 
HT700R alloy in solid solution and ageing states, respec-
tively. The surface morphology of the tensile fracture of the 
alloy was observed using a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, Zeiss Sigma-HD), followed by the preparation of 
sub-surface straight cuts of the tensile fracture and com-
parative analysis of the evolution of the grain orientation 
of the alloy in conjunction with an electron backscattering 
analyzer (EBSD) attached to the scanning electron micro-
scope. Samples were set in resin, ground to 2000# using SiC 
sandpaper, and polished with flannel for 5-10 min. A mixed 
solution of 12.5 mL HCI+2.5 mlHNO3+15 mlC3H8O3 was 
used to etch the alloy straight-cut samples for 5~10 min, 
and the behaviour of the sub-surface matrix tissue structural 
transformation during the deformation process was observed 
using backscatter imaging. The TEM samples for the tensile 
tests were cut perpendicular to the stress axis from the test 
samples, and 0.5 mm thick samples were cut in the area to 
be observed and mechanically ground to 50~100 μm, and 
electrolyzed in a mixed solution of 45 % CH3COOH+45 % 
 C6HO2+10 % HClO4 under the condition of (-20 °C, 30 V) 
for double-jet thinning down to 100 nm. The samples were 
analyzed using a Tecnai F20 transmission electron micro -
scope (TEM) to investigate the microstructure evolution of 
the grains during tensile fracture of the alloy.
3 Results
3.1 Microstructure of alloys
The phase transition behavior of the HT700R nickel-iron-
based high-temperature alloy can be described by the phase 
diagram in Fig.  3a. In the temperature range from 900 °C 
to 1300 °C, the alloy consists mainly of the γ phase. As the 
temperature drops below 900 °C, the γ' phase and MC-type 
carbides form gradually. As the temperature drops further 
to 700 °C, the precipitation of the γ' phase increases and 
is accompanied by the formation of M23C6-type carbides. 
Nucleation and growth of σ-phase may occur when drop-
ping below 600 °C. As shown in Fig.  3b, the diffraction 
peaks from the matrix γ-phase of HT700R alloy measured 
by X-ray diffraction (XRD) after solution treatment and age-
ing treatment indicate that the alloy has a single austenite-
phase matrix organization during both solution and ageing 
treatment. It is difficult to identify the second phases, such as 
γ' phase, MC-type carbide, and M23C6-type carbide, because 
the second phases, such as MC and M23C6 have small sizes 
and relatively low volume fractions. In contrast, the γ' phase 
has a superlattice crystal structure, and its diffraction peaks 
overlap with those of the matrix.

forging, solution treatment and ageing treatment are shown 
in Fig. 4a, b and c, respectively. It is known that the grains of 
the alloy after forging will undergo plastic deformation and 
refinement, and a clear grain boundary interface is gener -
ated between adjacent grains, and the grain granular defor -
mation characteristics are more obvious; the organization

3.2 Tensile properties of alloys
According to the tensile test results at different temperatures 
shown in Fig.  5a, b, it was found that the tensile and yield 
strengths of the solid solution alloys decreased gradually 
during the gradual increase in tensile temperature. At 600 ℃, 
the solid solution alloy’s tensile strength and yield strength 
reached the lowest point, which was 485 MPa and 161 MPa, 
respectively. As the temperature continued to increase, 
the alloy’s tensile strength and yield strength suddenly 
increased. The alloy’s tensile and yield strength reached 554 
MPa and 425 MPa, respectively, at 750 ℃ . The elongation 
of the solid solution alloy in the room temperature to 700 ℃ 
temperature range was between 45.84-59.44%. It decreased 
with the temperature increase and was 33.28% at 750 ℃. 
The curves of section elongation and section shrinkage of 
the ageing state alloys in the temperature interval from room 
temperature to 700 °C show significant fluctuations. Fig. 5a, 
b show that the tensile and yield strengths of the ageing 
alloys are higher than those of the solid solution alloys at 
different temperatures, with the tensile strength showing 
a gradual decrease from room temperature to 750 ℃ , and 
the yield strength not fluctuating much with the increase 
of temperature from room temperature to 750 ℃. When the 
tensile test temperature exceeds 600 ℃ , the yield strength 
of the ageing state alloy increases gradually. Still, when the 
tensile test temperature reaches 750 ℃, the yield strength of 
the ageing state alloy drops to 637 Mpa. As shown in Fig. 5c 
and d, the elongation and sectional shrinkage change of the 
solid solution alloy and ageing state alloy in the 700 ℃-750 
℃ appear to have apparent fluctuation, which is because, in 
the medium temperature condition, the High-temperature 
alloys are prone to brittle fracture, mainly due to the interac-
tion of alloying elements with grain boundaries leading to 
grain boundary embrittlement [29– 31].

3.5 Profile and EBSD analysis of alloys after tensile 
fracture
As shown in Fig.  8a, the microscopic images of the longi-
tudinal section of the alloy in the solid solution state after 
tensile testing at 600 °C show the presence of holes inside 
the grains, and cracks along the grain are not observed. This 
indicates that the slip capacity inside the grains is better in 
the solid solution state. As shown in Fig. 8b, the microscopic 
images of the longitudinal section of the ageing state alloy 
after tensile fracture at 600 ℃ show many cracks extend-
ing along the grain boundaries. This indicates that the slip 
capacity near the grain boundaries is poor in the ageing state, 
leading to crack extension along the grain boundaries. In 
contrast, the grain boundary strength of nickel-based alloys 
is generally higher than their intragranular strength [ 40], 
which further suggests that the yield strengths of solid solu-
tion alloys are lower than those of ageing state alloys. The 
tensile fracture profile of the solid solution alloy at 750 °C 
shows the presence of microcracks extending along the grain 
boundaries, which suggests that the grain boundaries play an 
important role in crack guidance(see Fig.  8c); the fracture 
profile of the aged HT700R alloy after tensile stretching at 
750 °C shows more pronounced cracks extending along the 
grain boundaries, and no perforation fracture characteristics 
are seen(see Fig. 8d).
The subsurface layers of the tensile fracture specimens at 
600 °C and 750 °C were analyzed by EBSD. Figure 9a shows 
the alloy’s IPF and antipodal plots in solid solution state 
after tensile testing at 600 °C. Significant grain deformation 
and grain boundary slip occurred along the RD direction, 
and a 〈111〉//RD filament weave configuration was formed, 
as shown by the antipodal plots. Figure 9b shows the IPF and 
antipodal plots of the ageing state alloy after tensile testing 
at 600°C. No formation of〈111〉//RD filamentary structure is 
seen along the RD direction. As shown in Fig.  9c, the solid 
solution alloy formed a 〈111〉//RD filament weave configura-
tion after 750 °C tensile testing. As shown in Fig. 9d, no new 
weave formation was observed after 750 °C tensile testing 
of the ageing state alloy. Combined with the tensile fracture 
morphology (see Figs.  7c, d, 8 c, and d), it was found that 
the fracture characteristics of both solid solution alloys and 
ageing alloys after tensile fracture were dominated by along-
crystalline fracture. In contrast, the longitudinal micrographs 
of the solid solution alloys and the ageing alloys after tensile 
fracture at 750 ℃ showed many intergranular cracks, con-
sistent with the results of the tensile tests.

3.6 Microstructure of HT700R alloy after tensile 
fracture (TEM)
The TEM images of the HT700R alloy after stretching at 600 
°C and 750 °C are shown in Fig.  11. As shown in Fig. 11a, 
the solid solution alloy undergoes PLC effect after tensile 
fracture at 600 °C. The dynamic interaction between the 
moving dislocations in the crystal and the forest disloca-
tions and solute atoms [32, 33] leads to the increase of dis-
location density and the change of lattice constant in the 
crystal, which causes the change of lattice curvature and the 
uneven stress distribution in the crystal. Finally, it causes the 
plastic deformation and fracture of the alloy. As shown in 
Fig. 11b, after the tensile fracture of the aged alloy at 600 
°C, the interaction between the dislocation and the γ′ phase 
was observed under TEM, which was dominated by the APB 
mechanism, that is, the spherical particles of the γ′ phase 
were cut, accompanied by a small number of dislocation 
loops. As shown in Fig.  11c and d, when the temperature 
rises to 750 °C, the TEM morphology of the solid solution 
alloy and the ageing alloy is similar; the grain boundaries 
in the alloy are visible, and many dislocation loops can be 
seen. Therefore, the dislocation motion of the alloy is mainly 
dominated by the Orowan mechanism. That is, the disloca-
tion motion crosses the γ′ phase [33], and there is no phe-
nomenon of cutting the γ′ phase spherical particles. Due to 
the dislocation ring distribution density of the aged alloy at 
750 °C being more significant than that of the solid solution 
alloy, the high-density dislocation tangles make the defor -
mation strengthening effect [31]. Therefore, the aged alloy’s 
tensile strength/yield strength at 750 °C is higher than that 
of the solid solution alloy.
4 Discussions
During stretching at low to medium temperatures, the defor-
mation mechanisms mainly include dislocations forming 
loops or bowing out and cutting into phase boundaries to 
form laminar dislocations or anti-phase boundaries (APB) 
[22–24, 27]. As the temperature increases, dislocation entan-
glement, dislocation cross-slip migration, and dislocation 
climb phenomena are observed in the alloys.The deforma-
tion behaviour of HT700R alloy in tensile tests is closely 
temperature-dependent. After tensile fracture at 600 °C, 
dislocations accumulate and slip in the grains, forming 
shear bands in the solid solution state alloy (See Fig.  11a). 
In the ageing state alloys, some dislocations and dislocation 
buildup can be observed within the grains. Still, in general, 
the grain boundaries remain relatively straightforward and 
intact. Observation of the tensile fracture profile at 600 ℃ 
reveals that the fracture surfaces of the solid solution alloys 
exhibit prominent characteristics of through-grain ductile 
fracture, with significant plastic deformation and necking

The number of γ′ phases within the alloy organization 
at the beginning of the tensile experiments was low for 
solid solution alloys. However, when the high-temperature 
tensile experiments at 600 ℃ were conducted, although a 
small amount of γ′ phase was found to precipitate at the 
end of the experiments, it could not be effectively pinned 
within the alloy grains. At high temperatures, the activity 
of atoms such as C and Cr is extensive, which is con-
ducive to forming a certain amount of solute atom gas 
clusters near the movable dislocations. If the solute-atom 
gas clusters can effectively pin the movable dislocations, 
the stress peaks will jump upward on the stress-strain 
curves; on the contrary, if the solute-atom gas clusters 
cannot be effectively pinned, the stress peaks will show 
a downward decreasing trend (see Fig.  6a and b). As 
plastic deformation proceeds, the density of movable 
dislocations increases. Still, their motion rate gradually 
decreases, increasing the chance of interaction between 
solute atomic gas clusters and movable dislocations. Even-
tually, this interaction leads to a maximum stress drop 
that exceeds the initial stress drop. When the tensile tem-
perature is increased to 750 °C, the solute atom diffusion 
rate is further accelerated due to the higher temperature, 
resulting in the inability to form effective solute atom gas 
clusters, and the interaction of all dislocations with sol-
ute atoms is weakened, so that the PLC effect no longer 
occurs at high temperatures. A related study of GH2984 
alloy [27, 48] found that the alloy’s yield strength was 
temperature-independent up to 700 °C after tensile testing 
in the temperature range from room temperature to 800 °C 
and decreased significantly at higher temperatures. The 
deformation mechanism shifted from anti-phase bound-
ary shear to stacked-layer staggered shear to precipitated-
phase bypass through the Orowan cycle. In a study by 
Yan et al. [48] it was found that after prolonged ageing 
treatment, the strength of GH2984 alloy decreased sig-
nificantly during long-term ageing from 700 °C to 750 °C. 
Increasing the ageing temperature accelerated the growth 
of precipitated phases, leading to a decrease in the strength 
of the alloy. Higher ageing temperatures further promoted 
a reduction in the critical stress for dynamic recrystalliza-
tion of the alloy.
After ageing treatment, a certain amount of γ′ phases 
are precipitated in the high-temperature alloys, and these 
γ′ phases have a hindering effect on the movement of 
dislocations, which attenuates the slip motion of disloca -
tions. At high temperatures of 600 ℃ and 750 ℃, the rein-
forcing effect of γ′ phase is more significant, which makes 
the slip of dislocations more difficult. When the deforma-
tion temperature reaches 750 ℃, the increase in the activa -
tion energy of diffusion makes it easier for the disloca-
tions to pass through the precipitated phase and enrich at 
the grain boundaries, which eventually react with each 
other to form low-angle grain boundaries. Transmission 
electron microscopy observations confirm the presence 
of many entangled dislocations that form low-angle grain 
boundaries, eventually leading to the formation of sub-
grains (see Fig.  14a and b). The significantly smaller size 
of these subgrains and the lower density of dislocations 
within the grains than the initial grains suggest that they 
act as continuous dynamic recrystallization nucleation 
sites during the alloy’s deformation. As the deforma-
tion continues, these subgrains gradually transform into 
recrystallized grains with significant angular grain bound-
aries. Previous studies have shown [49, 50] that alloys 
with low stacking dislocation energies (e.g., Fe-Ni alloys) 
usually undergo continuous dynamic recrystallization and 
form small-angle grain boundaries through dislocation 
reactions during deformation, which eventually evolve 
into large-angle grain boundaries along with the constant 
accumulation of dislocations at the grain boundaries. As 
a result, dislocations cannot slip effectively during the 
stretching process, thus preventing the PLC effect from 
occurring.

5 Conclusion
Tensile tests were carried out on the nickel-iron-based 
high-temperature alloy HT700R from room temperature 
up to 750 °C. The main conclusions concerning the defor -
mation behavior and fracture characteristics derived from 
this study are as follows:
(1) The yield and tensile strengths of the solution-treated 
HT700R alloy gradually decreased with increasing 
temperature, but the yield and tensile strengths sud-
denly increased after 600 ℃ . For the aging-treated 
alloy, the yield strength and tensile strength maintain a 
decreasing trend with the increase in temperature.
(2) After tensile fracture at 600 ℃, in solid solution alloys, 
dense screw-like dislocations are formed during 
stretching, and regions of enrichment of solute atoms 
are also found. After tensile fracture at 600 ℃ in the 
ageing state alloy, some dislocations and dislocation 
stacking were observed inside the grains. Still, the 
grain boundaries remained relatively straightforward 
and intact overall, and phase shearing with γ′ was the 
primary deformation mechanism.
(3) After tensile fracture at 750 °C, the TEM morphol -
ogy of the solid solution alloys and ageing alloys are 
relatively similar, the grain boundaries in the alloys are 
visible, and sawtooth dislocations and dislocation rings 
can be seen in both alloys. The Orowan mechanism, 
i.e. mainly dominates the dislocation motion of solid 
solution alloys. The dislocation motion crosses the γ′ 
phase, while dynamic recrystallization defeats ageing 
alloys’ dislocation motion.
(4) Combined with the results of post-tensile fracture 
EBSD analysis at 600 ℃, the solid solution alloys have 
better sliding ability near grain boundaries than the 
aged state and the solid solution alloys exhibit higher 
GOS values during stretching at 600 ℃, which indicates 
severe lattice distortion and higher dislocation density, 
leading to crack extension along the intercrystalline.
