ABSTRACT
In this work, processing of molybdenum and tungsten by Selective Laser Melting (SLM) is analyzed. The study
reveals the impact of the oxygen content of the powder, the process atmosphere and the temperature of the
substrate plate on the structural and mechanical properties of the processed material. For clarifying the causes
and mechanisms for the formation of defects in molybdenum and tungsten processed by SLM, the samples were
examined by x-ray, scanning and transmission electron microscopy including elemental distribution maps and
crystallographic analyses by electron backscatter di ﬀraction. Impurities, mainly oxygen, were identi ﬁed as cause
for the predominant defect structure comprising cracks and residual porosity. During processing, oxygen in the
form of molybdenum/tungsten oxide, segregates at the grain boundaries, thereby inducing hot cracking. This is
due to the lower melting point of the eutectic compared to the matrix phase. Moreover, the oxygen impurities
were found to weaken the grain boundaries and thus increasing the risk for cold cracking and leading to a higher
Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature (DBTT). Subsequently, the combination of cracks through hot cracking
at planar solidi ﬁed grain boundaries and cold cracking along weakened grain boundaries during rapid cooling
from the melting point creates the crack network generally found in molybdenum and tungsten processed by
SLM. Also a substrate plate temperature of 1000 °C does not prevent the formation of cracks in tungsten caused
by oxygen segregations.
1. State of the art and challenges
The refractory metals molybdenum and tungsten are characterized
by a high melting point, a low coe ﬃcient of thermal expansion, high
electrical and thermal conductivities as well as a high corrosion re-
sistance against many molten metals. Based on this unique combination
of those properties molybdenum and tungsten are used in a wide range
of applications in engineering, electronics and medical technology.
For these metals the 3D printing industry analyst SmarTech
Publishing issued a market forecast, which sees AM technology as a
potentially disruptive processing method, which will unlock new po-
tential for molybdenum and tungsten products [ 1]. But before this can
happen still some technological barriers must be overcome.
Commercially pure molybdenum and tungsten are prone to defects
when processed in a molten state [ 2]. In fusion welding [ 3] as well as in
Selective Laser Melting (SLM), molybdenum and tungsten are a ﬄicted
by cracks and porosity [ 4–12]. Most research activities in additive
manufacturing (AM) of refractory metals focus on tungsten and only a
few publications address molybdenum. But the defects found in addi-
tively produced molybdenum and tungsten are very similar. Faidel
et al. [ 4] as well as Bajaj et al. [ 5] reveal for di ﬀerent SLM-equipment
and powder grades that a laser power of up to 200 W is insu ﬃcient for
processing molybdenum when applying 30 μm powder layers because
of delamination porosity. Similar results were obtained for tungsten by
Zhou et al. [ 6].
Studies with a SLM-equipment having a 400 W laser show that de-
lamination defects are thus avoidable, but relative densities do not
exceed 96.5% and cracks were still present [ 7]. Only for the combi-
nation of a high laser power and plasma spheroidized powder a density
of 99.1% for molybdenum [ 8] and 98.5% for tungsten [ 9] were
achieved.

direction were observed by Wang et al. [ 7,8] both for molybdenum and

Another approach to decrease thermal stresses was taken by

94.4% when using equipment with 200 ppmv oxygen in the process
atmosphere instead of < 50 ppmv. A shortcoming here, however, is
that although the same energy densities were used, the laser spot dia-
meter and maximum laser power di ﬀered for the machines compared.
The in ﬂuence of oxygen can also be concluded from experiments
conducted by Kurzynowski et al. [ 10]. In this study planetary milling of
the tungsten powder was applied. Milling was not done in an inert or
reducing atmosphere, which inherently leads to a higher oxygen con-
tent of the powder, resulting in a maximum relative density of the built
sample of only 88%. The same set of parameters showed in other stu-
dies between 96% and 98% relative density with plasma spheroidized
powder [ 7,9].
Despite all e ﬀorts of parameter and scanning strategy variations, it
was not possible to achieve fully dense and crack-free molybdenum or
tungsten parts. Eﬀorts to clarify the mechanism of cracking and residual
porosity were taken by Wang et al. [ 12] and Vrancken et al. [ 13]. In the
latter study, high speed camera imaging of producing a molten track by
laser melting in a tungsten plate with SLM parameters were conducted
for studying the formation of cracks. Cracks appeared in regular dis-
tances behind the molten zone and the authors assumed that cold
cracking occurs when temperature falls below DBTT. However, in this
experiment a commercially pure tungsten plate was used, which typi-
cally has an oxygen content of around 5 μg/g, which is much lower than
the oxygen content of tungsten powder (100 –1000 μg/g). Wang et al.
[12] proposed that the cracks are initiated by nanopores formed by
evaporated and segregated oxides along the solidi ﬁcation
front and thus
along grain boundaries.
Although a reduction of the speci ﬁc crack length was achieved by
various research groups, the still prevailing crack structure and wea-
kened grain boundaries of state of the art SLM molybdenum and
tungsten lead to insu ﬃcient tensile and ﬂexural strength with no
plasticity and thus marginal fracture toughness of fabricated parts.
This paper shows results of SLM samples produced with di ﬀerent
oxygen contents in the process atmosphere and powders. Moreover, the
eﬀect of heating the substrate plate to 1000 °C is compared with results,
where no heating was applied. Based on the ﬁndings the defect me-
chanisms in molybdenum and tungsten processed by SLM will be
discussed.
2. Materials and methods
Molybdenum and tungsten powders used in this study were pro-
duced by plasma spheroidization. The particle size fraction was
10–45 μm for the molybdenum powder and 5– 25 μm for the tungsten
powder. The metallic purities both of molybdenum and tungsten
powder was higher than 99.9%. The particle size distribution was
measured by a MALVERN 2000 laser scattering particle size analyzer.
The inﬂuence of aging on the powder chemistry and process results was
evaluated for molybdenum powder by storing it in plastic powder
containers for 6 months. The carbon, oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen
contents of the powders were measured by combustion method (Leco
CS-230) and carrier gas hot extraction (Leco TC-500), respectively.
Morphology of particles and fracture surfaces were examined with a FEI
(Hillsboro, USA) Quanta 3D 200 and a JEOL (Tokyo, Japan) JSM-7610F
Schottky Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM).
Elemental distribution was measured by Energy Dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDX) with an Element EDS System by EDAX, Ametek
(Pennsylvania, USA). Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) images
and characterizations were conducted by FELMI Graz with a FEI Titan 3
G2 60-300 (elemental analyzation by FEI Chemi STEM, Bruker Super-X
detector) and FEI Tecnai F20. The microstructure was further in-
vestigated by electron backscatter di ﬀraction (EBSD) using a Zeiss Ultra
55 plus. 3D X-ray microscope (XRM) images were taken with an Xradia
520 by Zeiss (Jena, Germany) with a voxel size of 1 μm, an acceleration
voltage of 160 kV and a power of 10 W.
The SLM experiments were performed using a customized, lab –scale
SLM machine equipped with a 400 W continuous Nd:YAG ﬁber laser
and substrate plate heating. A line energy input of 0.67 J/mm, 100 μm
hatch distance, 30 μm layer thickness and scanning rotations between
adjacent layers of 90° and 67° were applied for the displayed results.

4. Discussion of defect formation mechanisms
Molybdenum and tungsten do not form a passivating layer and
continuously pick up oxygen when stored under air or in contact with
moisture. Recent studies [ 14] showed the e ﬀect of oxidation for an
Invar 36 alloy by storage in an unregulated atmosphere for one year by
comparing in-situ the defect generation during SLM through high speed
x-ray imaging. An increase in oxygen contamination resulted in an in-
creased spatter formation and an alteration of the Marangoni convec-
tion from an inward centrifugal to an outward centripetal ﬂow and thus
an initiation of pore nucleation, stabilization and growth. For mo-
lybdenum and tungsten all these observations are transferable due to
their low melting oxides with high evaporation pressure.
According to our results, an increased oxygen level in the powder or
equipment leads to a more pronounced crack structure in the material
built up by SLM. Provided su ﬃcient oxygen is present, molybdenum/
tungsten and oxygen form a ﬁlm of eutectic liquid between the already
solidiﬁed molybdenum/tungsten grains, which can cause hot cracking.
The eutectic can be identi ﬁed in SEM pictures (see Fig. 7)i nd i ﬀerent
morphologies according to the local concentration ratio between re-
fractory metal and its oxide. When the amount of eutectic liquid is low
enough, it is assumed that the liquid sheet between grains is not stable
anymore, will break up according to Plateau-Raileigh and coagulate to
droplets.
During cooling from solidus temperature, thermal stresses will arise.
These stresses can be compensated by plastic deformation as long as the
molybdenum or tungsten is still above DBTT and strength of grain
boundaries is greater than force needed to initiate and move disloca-
tions around the crack tip. Produced in a conventional powder me-
tallurgical process DBTT of stress relieved and ﬁne grained mo-
lybdenum would be around 300 °C [ 29] and grain boundary strength is
high due to minimal content of impurities. DBTT of the two discussed
refractory metals should be positively in ﬂuenced when processed by
SLM due to the introduction of a high number of dislocations [ 28] and a
ﬁne grain structure [ 28] due to the fast cooling after laser melting. Yet,
the most important in ﬂuence on DBTT plays the oxygen precipitated at
high-angle grain boundaries resulting in cracking. The embrittlement of
grain boundaries by oxygen is well described in [ 17–27]. Due to the
high amounts of oxygen introduced by the powder surface, an increased
defect size and a raised DBTT emerges during the SLM process, which
inhibits the production of molybdenum and tungsten with mechanical
properties as got with the conventional process route pressing/sin-
tering/deformation.
When altering the scanning direction from layer to layer by 67°
within SLM of molybdenum and tungsten it results in an increase of
intertwined grain boundaries and thus improves crack growth re-
sistance. As already discussed, this is due to a better-distorted heat
gradient, better interlocking of grain boundaries, and thus less match
between metallographic preferred directions and heat gradients for
epitaxial grain growth. The heat gradient, in general, can be reduced by
heating the substrate plate, which thus results in lower induced thermal
stresses. But even with a special equipment with heating of the sub-
strate plate up to 1000 °C, oxygen levels in the process atmosphere
below 20 ppmv and a su ﬃcient laser power, molybdenum and tungsten

5. Conclusions
The refractory metals tungsten and molybdenum show almost no
solubility for oxygen at ambient temperatures as well as a very low
critical defect size below their DBTT. Oxygen impurities in the powder
and the process atmosphere lead to an increased oxygen content in the
material processed by SLM. The oxygen is segregated as molybdenum
or tungsten oxide at the grain boundaries leading to hot or cold cracks
depending on the amount of the segregated phase. Thereby, defects
above the critical size for failure remain in the built part. In addition the
oxygen contamination leads to porosity in the built parts. This can be
explained by an altered Marangoni convection and wetting behavior as
described in [ 6,14]. Therefore, impurities have to be either eliminated
or controlled, e.g. by alloying or avoiding any contact of the powder
with oxidizing atmosphere throughout the complete process route. The
paper of Kaserer et al. of the same research groups titled “Fully dense
and crack free Molybdenum manufactured by Selective Laser Melting
through alloying with carbon ” [30] published in the same special issue
of the International Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials
(2019) reveals how the negative e ﬀects caused by oxygen can be pre-
vented by alloying with carbon for achieving completely dense and
crack free parts in the SLM of molybdenum and tungsten.
