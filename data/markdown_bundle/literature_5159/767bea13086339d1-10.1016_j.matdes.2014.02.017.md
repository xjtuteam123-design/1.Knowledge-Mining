abstract
The Ti–30Zr–5Al–3V (wt.%, TZAV-30) alloy having good mechanical properties is a potential structural
material to apply in the aerospace industry. The microstructure and mechanical properties of ZTAV-30
alloy underwent various annealing heat treatments were investigated. The specimens annealed from
500 to 800 /C176C are composed of
a and b two phases. No compound is detected in specimens annealed
in that temperature range. The microstructure of annealed specimens is characterized as a typical basket-
weave microstructure. Three microstructural parameters, thickness of plate
a phase, relative fraction of b
phase and aspect ratio of a grains, were measured in those annealed specimens. As the alloy annealed in
the range from 500 to 800 /C176C, the average thickness of plate a grains increases with the increasing
annealing temperature from 500 to 700 /C176C but decreases while annealed at 800 /C176C. The fraction of
retained b phase increases with annealing temperature. And the aspect ratio of plate a grains decreases
ﬁrstly but increases while the annealing temperature is higher than 700 /C176C. As the variation of those three
microstructural parameters, the strength of examined alloy varies from 1269 to 1355 MPa for tensile
strength and from 1101 to 1190 MPa for yield strength, inversely, the elongation changes in the range
from 12.7% to 8.4%. The strengthening and toughening mechanism of the TZAV-30 alloy with basket-
weave microstructure is also discussed in this paper.

1. Introduction
Titanium (Ti) and Ti alloys show attractive high speciﬁc
strength ( r/q), excellent erosion resistance in many aggressive
environments, and low density nearly half of steel and nickel al-
loys. Those excellent physicochemical properties are the key fac-
tors for the successful application in aviation and chemical
engineering industries [1]. Furthermore, applications in other
ﬁelds, such as medical, seafaring, sports, transportation and other
industries, are increasing in stages.
However, the development of new high strength Ti-based alloys
has long been the focus of materials research. Alloying is one of the
most common and effective method to develop new alloys with
excellent properties [2,3]. Ti has two allotropic phases, namely,
hexagonal close packed (hcp) crystal structural a phase and
body-centered cubic (bcc) structural b phase. The low temperature
a phase will transform into high temperature b phase at tempera-
ture over the phase transition temperature, 885 /C176C for pure Ti.
Apart from those two basic phases, some intermediate martensitic
phases such as a0, a00 and x phases will present while Ti–Ti alloys
rapid cool from b phase temperature zone. According to effects of
alloying elements on phase structure and transition of metal Ti,
suitable a stabilizer and b stabilizer can be added into Ti alloys
to develop new Ti alloys with high strength [4–6]. Elements zirco-
nium (Zr) and Ti belong to the same group in the element periodic
table and show similar physicochemical properties. Lots of investi-
gations have been showed that the effects of Zr on the microstruc-
ture and properties of pure Ti and Ti alloys are obviously.
Investigating the structure and properties of Ti–Zr or Zr–Ti binary
alloys, Ho [7] and Hsu [8,9] showed that the phase composition
and mechanical properties of those binary alloys varied markedly
with the change of Zr content. Investigations on multi-component
Ti alloys also showed signiﬁcant effects of Zr on microstructure and
properties. Zhou [10] investigated the effect of Zr content on struc-
ture and properties of ternary Ti–Nb–Zr alloys and showed that the
strength was improved but the plasticity signiﬁcantly decreased
with the increasing of Zr content. Investigating the inﬂuence of
Zr on structure, mechanical and thermal properties of Ti–Al–N
based coating, Chen [11] noted that alloying with Zr leads to high
hardness values through promoting the formation of cubic do-
mains but retarding the formation of stable wurtzite AlN during
thermal annealing. Recently, new Ti–Zr–Al–V (TZAV) series alloys
[12–15] with ultra-high strength were developed, among which

Heat treatment, apart from alloying, is another common and
effective method to adjust and control structure and properties
of alloys [16]. Various microstructures such as equiaxed, widman-
stattern, basketweave, and duplex, can be obtained for Ti alloys
through adjusting heat treatment processes. According previous
reports, those various microstructures have signiﬁcant inﬂuence
on mechanical properties. Equiaxed microstructure results in good
plasticity and high fatigue strength, Widmanstattern microstruc-
ture brings about high fracture toughness, excellent creep and fa-
tigue crack propagation resistance, and Ti alloys with duplex
structure have the mixed advantages of equiaxed and widmanstat-
tern structural alloys [17,18]. Thus, investigations of structure and
properties of alloys during various heat treatments have great pro-
moting effect on industrial application. In this paper, the structure
and mechanical properties of the ZTAV-30 alloy after various
annealing heat treatments were investigated and the strengthen-
ing mechanism was also discussed.
2. Experimental procedure
Sponge Zr (Zr + Hf P 99.5 wt.%), sponge Ti (99.7 wt.%), indus-
trial pure Al (99.5 wt.%), and vanadium (V) (99.9 wt.%) were used
to prepare alloy with the following composition in weight percent-
age: Ti–30Zr–5Al–3V. The alloy was melted by ZHT-001 type vac-
uum consumable electro-arc furnace followed by breakdown at
1050 /C176C and heat forging at 950 /C176C successively. The detailed pre-
paring procedure was showed in Ref. [19]. Specimens with size
10 mm /C2 10 mm /C2 70 mm (width /C2 height /C2 length) were sec-
tioned as heat-treated specimens. Heat treatments were per-
formed in a SK-G06143 tubular vacuum heat treatment furnace
with a protective argon atmosphere. The annealing treatments
were performed at 500, 600, 700 or 800 /C176C for 1 h. Bone-shaped
plate specimens with a 21 mm gauge length, 3 mm gauge width,
and 2 mm gauge thickness were prepared for tensile tests.
Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to test the tran-
sition temperatures using heating rate 10 /C176C/min adopting the stan-
dard of ASTM: F2004–05(2010). Phase structures were examined by
X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu K a radiation (ASTM: D5380-93).
Optical microscopy (OM) was used for microstructural analysis.
Tensile tests were conducted on an Instron mechanical testing sys-
tem at room temperature, and the strain rate was 5 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 in
the rate range from 10 /C0 3 to 10 /C0 4 s/C0 1 according to the ISO 6892-
1:2009 [20]. The strain throughout the entire testing process was
monitored with an extensometer 12.5 mm in gauge length.
The mean thickness of plate a phase is the average values of
those incising the diagonals of the OM micrographs (ASTM:
E112-12). The volume fraction of the retained b phase was tested
by the point counting method (ASTM: E562-11). The detail process
is that an OM micrograph was covered by a suitable grid, and the
ratio of the number of grid points located at the b phase to the total
number of grid points is regarded as the volume fraction of the re-
tained b phase.
To avoid occasional results, all tests on each specimen were per-
formed at least three times. The data points signed in ﬁgures are
the average values. The error bars in ﬁgures show the relative devi-
ations between those three repetitive results and the average
value.
3. Results and discussions
Fig. 1 shows the DSC curve near the a + b two-phase tempera-
ture zone of the TZAV-30 alloy. According to the DSC curve, the
onset and end temperatures of a to b transformation are 739 and
846 /C176C, respectively. It is namely that the a phase in TZAV-30 alloy
is a stable phase below 739 /C176C. Other non-equilibrium phases such
as b, a0, a00 and x phase will transform into the stable a phase while
holding at below that onset transition temperature. As the holding
temperature increases to or over 739 /C176C, the a phase is no longer
the stable phase and will transform into b phase gradually. while
the temperature is over 846 /C176C, the a phase will completely trans-
form into the b phase.
Fig. 2 shows the XRD results of specimens exhibiting the phase
analysis of the examined alloy annealed at various temperatures.
The XRD patterns of all specimens in Fig. 2 (a) show main peaks
of the a phase and the clear (2 0 0) peak near 2 h = 54.2/C176of the b
phase [21–23]. No other peak was detected in those XRD patterns.
So, it is easy to believe that all specimens in the examined condi-
tions are wholly composed of the a phase and b phase. Fig. 2 (a)
also shows an obvious variation of the relative peak intensity in
various annealed specimens. According to the theory of XRD and
the previous result [24], the relative content of each phase in mul-
ti-phase alloy has the similar trend with the relative peak intensity
of each phase in the same XRD pattern. Although the XRD is a
semiquantitative method to calculate relative phase fraction, it
can be used to reveal the trend of each phase fraction in the same
specimen. Thus, the trend of peak intensity ratio Ib/Ia in various an-
nealed specimens can indicates the trend of relative phase content.
Here Ib is a sign of (1 1 0) peak intensity of b phase and Ia is a sign of
ð10/C2211Þ peak intensity of a phase. The trend line of peak intensity
ratio ( Ib/Ia) vs. annealing temperature is showed in Fig. 2 (b). The
Ib/Ia value in the initial forged specimen is about 0.7. After anneal-
ing heat treatment at 500 /C176C, that ratio decreases to about 0.35. The
decreasing of Ib/Ia ratio indicates that the volume fraction of re-
tained b phase in the specimen annealed at 500 /C176C is lower than
that in the forged specimen. It is note that the forged specimen
is cooled in air from the forged temperature (in the range of b
phase temperature zone). So the transformation of b phase to a
phase should be incomplete and some b phases are retained. The
retained b phases will continue to transform into a phase in the
next annealing process when annealing temperature is below the
onset of transition temperature. Thus, the relative fraction of re-
tained b phase in specimen annealed at 500 /C176C is lower than that
in the forged specimen. As the annealing temperature increases
in the range from 500 to 800 /C176C, the retained b phase increases
gradually. The Ib/Ia ratio of 800 /C176C annealed specimen is even high-
er than that ratio of forged specimen. Phase transformation theory
shows that the phase transformation driving force decreases with
the increasing of temperature [25]. Reduced driving force is unfa-
vorable for phase transformation. Thus, the transformation of the

Fig. 3 shows the microstructure of specimens annealed at vari-
ous temperatures. The microstructure of each annealed specimen
is characterized as a typical basketweave microstructure of a and
b two phases featured by such a structure where the plate a grains
are interwoven and separates the b phase. Although specimens an-
nealed at various temperatures have the same basketweave micro-
structures, the grain size, phase fraction and other microstructural
parameters in each specimen vary with annealing temperature.
The character of a grains in the forged specimen are spindly in
Fig. 3 (a). After annealed at 500 /C176C, the thickness of a grains in-
creased showed as Fig. 3 (b). As the annealing temperature in-
creases to 600 /C176C, lots of short-bar shaped a grains present in the
Fig 3 (c). As the annealing temperature further increases, the a
grains grow and the amount of short-bar shaped grain decreases
gradually in Fig. 3 (d) and (e). To show the relationship between
microstructure and annealing temperature more clearly, the trends
of microstructural parameters such as average thickness and as-
pect ratio of the plate a grains with annealing temperature are
showed in Fig. 4. The mean thickness of plate a grains in annealed
specimens increases with the annealing temperature while the
holding temperature is below onset of transition temperature
(739 /C176C). However, an opposite trend presents in the 800 /C176C an-
nealed specimen. It is note that grain size of new phase in annealed
specimen is greatly dependent on growth rate during phase transi-
tion. Meanwhile, the transition of b to
a phase belongs to the dif-
fusion transformation. Thus, the mean thickness of plate a grains
depends on the diffusion velocity of alloying element. The diffusion
velocity increases as annealing temperature. So, the mean thick-
ness of plate a grains increases as annealing temperature below
onset of transition temperature. On the contrary, the transforma-
tion will be from a phase into b phase while the annealing
temperature is in the range from onset to end of transition temper-
ature. Thus, the mean thickness of a grains in specimen annealed
at 800 /C176C is smaller than that annealed at 700 /C176C. Fig. 4 also shows
the trend line of average aspect ratio of plate a grains vs. annealing
temperature. The average aspect ratio of a grains in the original
forged specimen is about 15. After annealing treatment, that aspect
ratio decreases. As the specimen annealed at 500 /C176C, the average
aspect ratio is about 10. As the annealing temperature increases,
that ratio decreases continuously. Lots of short-bar shaped a grains
present after the specimen annealed at 600 /C176C. As the annealing
temperature further increases to 700 /C176C, the average aspect ratio
increases slightly again. It is well know that the growth process
of plate a grains is a diffusion process. The growth rate of plate
grains in lengthwise direction should be rapid compared with that
rate in thickness direction. However, the growth of plate a grain in
the lengthwise direction is hampered by other a plates according
to Fig. 3(a), but the growth in the thickness direction is free. Thus,
the aspect ratio of a grains should tend to decrease during next
annealing treatment at low temperature such as 500–600 /C176C. As
the annealing temperature increases over the onset of transition
temperature, the dissolution of portion a grains and increased dif-
fusion velocity of elements increase the growth rate in lengthwise
direction. So, the average aspect ratio of a grains increases again.
Fig. 5 shows the tensile test results of specimens forged and/or
annealed at various temperatures. The stress–strain curves and
variations of mechanical properties with annealed temperature
are showed in Fig. 5 (a) and (b), respectively. According to tensile
test results, the mechanical properties of the examined alloy were
greatly dependent on annealing temperature. The yield strength,
tensile strength and elongation of the forged specimen are
1097 MPa, 1258 MPa, and 8.4%, respectively. After annealing at
500 /C176C, values of those mechanical properties are 1190 MPa,
1355 MPa and 8.4%, respectively. As the annealing temperature in-
creases, strength decreases but elongation increases continuously.
While annealed at 700 /C176C, the specimen has the lowest strength of
1101 MPa for yield strength and 1269 MPa for tensile strength, and
highest elongation of 12.7%. However, as a further increase of
annealing temperature, this approach inverted at 800 /C176C. The yield
strength, tensile strength and elongation of the specimen annealed
at 800 /C176C are 1121 MPa, 1327 MPa and 12.2%, respectively. On the

4. Conclusions

ties of the ZTAV-30 alloy have been investigated. The main conclu-
sions are as follows:

(1) The ZTAlV-30 alloy is composed of two phases, namely, a
phase and b phase, while specimens anneal from 500 to
800 /C176C. The relative b phase in annealed specimens increases
with the increasing of annealing temperature.
(2) The annealed specimens are mainly characterized as typical
basketweave microstructure. The average thickness of plate
a grains in specimens increases with the annealing temper-
ature below onset of transition temperature, but decreases
when annealing temperature increases to 800 /C176C.
(3) The strength of forged specimen increases from 1258 to
1355 MPa for tensile strength and from 1097 to 1190 MPa
for yield strength after annealing at 500 /C176C. However, the
strength decreases and ductility increases with the increas-
ing of annealing temperature from 500 to 700 /C176C but this
approach inverted at 800 /C176C. Both phase fraction and thick-
ness can obviously affect mechanical properties. As the b
phase fraction and average thickness of plate a grains
decrease, the strength of the examined alloy increases but
elongation decreases.
