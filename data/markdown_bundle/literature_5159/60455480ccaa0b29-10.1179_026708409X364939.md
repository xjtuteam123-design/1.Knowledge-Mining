Mechanical and microstructural
characterisations of NiCrCoAlYTa coatings on
superalloys deposited by detonation gun
technique
S. Kamal, R. Jayaganthan * and S. Prakash
The microstructure and mechanical properties of detonation gun sprayed NiCoCrAlYTa alloy
coatings on the selected superalloys were investigated. The coatings were characterised in
relation to thickness, porosity, microhardness, microstructure, compositions, and phases across
by using the techniques such as optical microscopy, X-ray diffraction, field emission scanning
electron microscopy/energy dispersive analysis and X-ray mapping. The formation of spherical or
elliptical shaped dendritic splats was observed in the as sprayed coatings on the superalloys. The
splat morphology of the coatings is due to the deposition and re-solidification of successive
molten or semimolten powder particles. The coatings showed a bond strength of 50 MPa and the
surface roughness (arithmetic average height) of the coatings was found to be in the range of
6?25–7?48 mm. The porosity and microhardness of the coatings were less than 0 ?48% and 385–
748 Hv respectively. The XRD analysis of the coatings showed the phases c-Ni or c9-Ni
3Al and b-
NiAl inter metallic as the minor phase.
Keywords: Superalloys, Detonation gun coating, characterisation, NiCrCoAlYTa
Introduction
Nickel base superalloys, by virtue of their excellent high
temperature properties coupled with corrosion resis-
tance, have been widely used as gas turbine engine
components.
1 In today’s gas turbines, the emphasis is on
saving energy and reducing the amounts of pollutants
emitted; this can be attained only by designing alloys
with higher melting points and the capability to retain
mechanical integrity at increased temperatures. To meet
these demands, the development of nickel based super-
alloys (NBSAs) has reached its limit, as the alloys are
operating at critical temperatures close to their melting
points and further increase in the operating tempera-
tures would result in dissolution of the strengthening
phases and even melting.
2 Structural alloys have been
developed for optimal mechanical properties 3 and coat-
ings have been developed to provide environmental
resistance.
4,5 Superalloy components in the hottest
sections of gas turbines need the application of overlay
coatings like NiCoCrAlY compositions in order to resist
effectively the environmental attack by hot gases and
deposits.
6 NiCoCrAlY alloys are widely used for high
temperature applications due to their outstanding
property proﬁle with good ductility, high strength up
to 700 uC and excellent oxidation resistance.
7–10
MCrAlY coatings are among the most important
protective coating materials applied to counteract hot
corrosion and high temperature. They are capable of
developing a thermodynamically stable, slow growing
and adherent surface oxide layer.
11 For instance,
NiCoCrAlY alloys are used as corrosion resistant
coatings applied on various superalloy components
and also serve as bond layers for thermal barrier
coatings (TBC) on superalloy substrates.
12 Sidhu
et al.13,14 have characterised NiCrAlY coatings on boiler
tube steels in order to provide some database regarding
their engineering applications. Various researchers have
suggested the use of MCrAlY (M is Ni and/or Co)
overlays and Pt-aluminide diffusion coatings as typical
bond coats to protect the superalloy against oxidation
and to reduce the mismatch stresses between the top
coat and the superalloy.
15–20 In the late 1960s, the Pratt
and Whitney Company initiated a program to develop
coatings with compositions nominally independent of
substrates, and with capabilities for tailoring to the wide
range of requirements of gas turbine applications.
21
Detonation gun spraying is characterised by the spray-
ing of particles that are accelerated by the detonation
wave and impact on the substrate surface at a high
velocity of 800–1200 m s
21,22–24 which is higher than
that of high velocity oxyfuel spraying (HVOF) (about
550–850 m s 21).25 As a result, higher particle velocity
during deposition provides a minimum decomposition

28,29 The study on detonation gun sprayed

of this paper is to characterise the D-gun sprayed
NiCoCrAlYTa alloy coatings on superalloys in relation
to microstructure, porosity, coating thickness, phase
formation and microhardness by using the combined
techniques of the optical microscope, X-ray diffraction
(XRD) technique, Field emission scanning electron
microscopy/energy dispersive analysis (FE-SEM/
EDAX) and X-ray mapping to provide an experimental
basis so as to enlarge the applications of NiCoCrAlYTa
coatings.
Experimental procedure
Development of coatings
Substrate material and coating powder
The nickel and iron based superalloy substrate materials
selected for this study, namely superni 75, superni 718
and superfer 800H, were provided by Mishra Dhatu
Nigam Limited, Hyderabad (India) in the rolled sheet
form. The nominal chemical compositions of the
substrate materials are reported in Table 1. Specimens
with dimensions of approximately 20 61565 mm were
cut from the alloy sheets, polished using emery papers of
220, 400, 600 grit sizes and subsequently on 1/0, 2/0, 3/0
and 4/0 grades, samples were degreased with acetone
and subsequently grit blasted with alumina powders
before deposition of the coating by the D-gun process. A
commercially available NiCoCrAlYTa [particle size: 5–
37 mm, shape: spheroidal, gas atomised, Sulzer metco
(Amdry 997)] powder with the chemical composition of
Ni–23Co–20Cr–8?5Al–4Ta–0?6Y is shown in Fig. 1.
Detonation gun coating
The D-gun process was used to apply coatings on the
superalloys at SVX Powder M Surface Engineering Pvt.
Ltd, New Delhi (India). Standard spray parameters were
designed by the above ﬁrm for depositing this coating.
All the process parameters, including the spray distance,
were kept constant throughout the coating process. The
spraying parameters were the oxygen/acetylene ﬂow rate
(Ratio)—1 : 1 ?2, carrier gas ﬂow rate (N
2)—1 ?82
(m3 h21), frequency—3 shots s 21, spot size diameter—
20 mm and spraying distance from the nozzle—165 mm.
Characterisation of coatings
The coated samples were wheel cloth polished and then
analysed using an optical microscope and a ﬁeld
emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, FEI,
Quanta 200F company) with EDAX Genesis software
attached for characterising the surface morphology,
cross-sectional morphology and compositional analysis
(X-ray mapping) of the coatings. The XRD analysis of
the coated samples was carried out using a Bruker AXS
D-8 Advance diffractometer for the phase identiﬁcation.
The porosity of the coatings was measured along its
cross-section with an image analyser using the Zeiss
Axiovert 200 MAT inverted optical microscope ﬁtted
with imaging software Zeiss Axiovision Release 4 ?1,
(Germany) software, which was developed based on
ASTM B276. The magniﬁcation was chosen such that
the coating microstructure image covers the screen and
allows the resolution of the voids that contributes
notably to the total porosity area percentage. The
process of selecting the appropriate range of black
values was carried out methodically to ensure that only
voids were selected. About 20 separate locations were
selected to avoid the overlap between two locations and
determine the area percent porosity. To identify the
cross-sectional details, the samples were cut in cross-
section using a Buehler low speed diamond cutter
(ISOMET) 1000 precision saw and then they were hot
mounted in transoptic powder and subjected to mirror
polishing using emery papers of 220, 400,600 grit sizes
and subsequently on 1/0, 2/0, 3/0 and 4/0 grades. Finally,

Bond strength of as sprayed D-gun coating
The macro photograph of the fractured surface of a
coated specimen after pulling apart in the tensile test
machine is shown in Fig. 3. The coating failed at the

Surface roughness ( Ra) of as sprayed coating
The coating surface was very rough in the as coated
condition due to the presence of unmelted particles and
the roughness (Ra) was found to be in the range of 6?25–
7?48 mm. The centreline average (CLA) method was
used to obtain the Ra values.
Microhardness of coatings
The microhardness data for the coatings are plotted in

microhardness of all the substrates is observed near the
coating/substrate interface.

Discussion
The NiCoCrAlYTa coating on superalloys by D-gun
exhibits a dense and adherent microstructure. It can be
observed that coatings possess porosity, unmelted and
semimelted particles and oxide inclusions (Fig. 2). The
thin contrast stringers which appear in the microstruc-
ture are presumably the oxides that are believed to be
formed due to the oxidation of inﬂight particles. Because
the D-gun entrapped the air during spraying and
micropores acted as the shelters for the residual air,
29
it can be deduced that oxidation took place on the
surface of the powder droplets during their ﬂight in D-
gun spraying. The resulting Al
2O3 was then entrapped
into the coating and sandwiched between the splats after
deposition.

amount of entrapped air, particle temperature and the
nature and turbulence of detonation ﬂame. The quantity
of air entrained in the D-gun depends strongly on the jet
velocity. Deshpande et al.
32 proposed that, during
inﬂight oxidation, a layer of oxide is formed on the

Surface roughness, which is one of the important
parameters of the D-gun sprayed NiCoCrAlYTa coat-
ings, needs to be measured for its better performance in
high temperature applications. For example, in thermal
power plants, the coatings employed on the boiler tube
components are subjected to solid particle erosion and
therefore it is essential to ensure the erosion resistance of
the coatings with respect to their surface features such as
surface roughness, adhesive bond strength and mor-
phology of the grain, etc. It may be mentioned that
higher surface roughness of the coated component
would result in the higher erosion rate; contradictory
to this statement is the beneﬁcial effect of surface
roughness, as reported by Fu et al.
42 and Li et al. ,43 in
enhancing fretting fatigue life. During fretting, hard
oxide debris formed at the contact surface may cause
severe abrasion. However, on a rough surface, the wear
debris may escape from the contact area into the
adjacent depressions instead of causing abrasion
damage.
The roughness of the as sprayed NiCoCrAlYTa
coating, in the present investigation, is higher than the
HVOF sprayed NiCoCrAlYTa coating. The roughness
of the coatings affects the adhesion strength of YSZ top
coats in thermal barrier coating systems as reported by
Lima and Guilemany.
44 Microhardness, which is one of
the important mechanical properties of the coatings,
needs to be measured to ensure its reliability in actual
applications.45 The microhardness of D-gun sprayed
coatings has been found to be very high as compared to
the superalloy substrates (Fig. 4). Some variations in
hardness values in the coating may be due to the
presence of micropores, oxide inclusions, unmelted and
partially melted particles. Near the coating/substrate
interface, a slightly higher value of hardness is found as
compared to the bare substrates. This increased hard-
ness value near the coating/substrate interface might be
due to the work hardening effect of the sandblasting of
the substrates before the coating process as reported by
the authors’ institute research group.
46–50 The hardening
of substrate superalloys may also be partly attributed to
the high speed impact of the coating particles during D-
gun spraying. Similar phenomena have also been
observed in the authors’ previous publications. 51,52
Further, the higher values of the hardness of the
coatings as compared to the substrate alloys may be
partly contributed by the high density and cohesive
strength of the individual splats as a result of the high
impact velocity of the coating particles as reported by
various researchers.
53–55 The high hardness values found
in the coating could also be due to the severe plastic
deformation undergone by partially unmolten material
upon impact owing to the high impact velocity of the
sprayed particles. Ahmed and Hadﬁeld analysed the
rolling contact fatigue performance of thermally sprayed
(WC-12%Co coatings) rolling steel elements and
reported that the performance of high velocity oxy fuel
and D-gun sprayed coatings were superior to that of the
plasma sprayed coatings due to their inherent properties
such as low porosity, high hardness and compressive
residual stresses.

Similar phases have also been reported by Raffaitin

Tribomet, chemical vapour deposition and plasma

Conclusions
NiCoCrAlYTa (Amdry 997)) coatings were deposited
on Ni and Fe based superalloys by the D-gun thermal
spray process.
The surface morphology of the as prayed coating on
all three substrates depicts the formation of unmelted
particles in the form of globular dendritic structure. The
diameter of the dentritic agglomerates is larger than the
original powder particles.
The XRD analysis revealed the presence of c-Ni or c9-
Ni
3Al as the major phase and b-NiAl inter metallic as
the minor phase.
The bond strength of the coating is 50 MPa and the
surface roughness of the coating was found to be in the
range of 6 ?25–7?48 mm.
Microhardness measurements revealed that the coat-
ing microhardness varies throughout its section. The
microhardness of the coating is found to be in the range
of 385–748 Hv.
The thickness of the coatings ranges from 220–250 mm
and it showed a good adhesion with all the substrates
due to the absence of cracks and gaps at the interfaces.
The measured porosity values are less than 0 ?48%
