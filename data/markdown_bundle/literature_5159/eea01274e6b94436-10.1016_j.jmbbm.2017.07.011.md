Abstract 
In this study, a new series of Ti -Ta-Zr-Nb alloys (Ti -38.3Ta-22Zr-8.1Nb, Ti -38.9Ta-25Zr-
5Nb, Ti-39.5Ta-28Zr-2.5Nb, designated TTZN -1, TTZN-2, TTZN-3, respectively) with high 
elastic strain and high mechanical strength have been developed as alternatives to 
conventional orthopedic implant materials. The TTZN alloys have been designed using the 
electronic parameters of the alloying elements, combined with the approaches of the electron -
to-atom ratio (e/a) and molybdenum equivalence (Moeq). The microstructure, mechani cal 
properties and cytocompatibility of the as -cast TTZN alloys were investigated via optical 
microscopy, compression tests and in vitro assessment using osteoblast cells. X -ray 
diffraction analysis has revealed that all the TTZN alloys are compris ed of a single β phase,

2 
 
however, transmission electron microscopy observations revealed that ω and β phase s co-
existed in the microstructure. The compression strains of the TTZN alloys range from 22% to 
36% and the compression strength from 1787 to 1807 MPa. The tensile Young’s modulus, 
elastic strain and yield strength are 73.12 ± 4.43, 74.98 ± 2.19 and 76.62 ± 2.38 (GPa); 1.57 ± 
0.27, 1.25 ± 0.27 and 1.29 ± 0.16 (%); and 1107.42 ± 144.68, 932.11 ± 195.22 and 953.58 ± 
120.76 MPa for TTZN -1, TTZN-2 and TTZN -3, respectively. Further, all the TTZN alloys 
exhibit excellent cytocompatibility as their cell adhesion density is higher than that of CP -Ti. 
This study demonstrates that these TTZN alloys can be anticipated to be promising candidate 
for orthopedic implant materials due to their high mechanical strength and high elastic strain.

1. Introduction 
Inarguably, the rapid increase in the number of elderly people worldwide increases the 
demand for replacement of dysfunctional hard tissues with orthopedic implants (e.g. artificial

## Page 4

3 
 
hip joint and knee joint) (Ehtemam-Haghighi et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2008). Kurtz et al. (Kurtz 
et al., 2007 ) reported that in the United States, to the end of 2030, the total number of hip 
prosthesis and knee arthroplasty surgeries was estimated to increase by 174% reaching 
572,000, and by 673% reaching 3.48 million procedures, respectively. Furthermore, between 
2005 and 2030, total hip prosthesis and knee arthroplasty revision surgeries were projected to 
increase by 137%, and 601%, respectively (Kurtz et al., 2007). 
It is well known that metallic materials, which present the oldest history compared to other 
kinds of materials, e.g., ceramics and polymers, in terms of usage for biomedical purposes, 
exhibit more reasonable mechanical properties over ceramics and polymer s (Niinomi, 2002). 
Among the metallic biomaterials, stainless steels and Co -Cr alloys are accepted to cause 
allergic reactions in consequence of the released metal ions (e.g. Co and Ni ions) in body fluid 
(Biant et al., 2010; Granchi et al., 2008; Rushing et al., 2007; Zhubrak and Bar-David, 2014), 
thereby requiring revision surgeries in order to circumvent allergic reactions. For orthopaedic 
implant applications, allergic reactions are esti mated to occur between 1% and 5% of the 
patients (Zhubrak and Bar-David, 2014). The potential release of Ni, Co, Cr, and Fe ions from 
stainless steel and Co -Cr alloys presents a serious risk (e.g. carcinogenic effect) to human 
health (Okazaki and Gotoh, 2005). 
Ti alloys, which are an indispensable material group in the biomedical industry due to their 
high specific strength and corrosion resistance, are also used extensively in the aerospace 
(Peters et al., 2003 ), marine (Candel et al., 2010 ), automotive (Moxson et al., 2000 ), and 
sports equipment (Moxson and Froes, 2001 ) industries. Ti alloys are preferred as orthopedic 
implant materials due to their excellent biocompatibility feature, high corrosion resist ance, 
fatigue strength, and relatively low Young’s modulus. The fact remains that, CP -Ti and Ti 
alloys are superior to other metallic materials in terms of mechanical, physical and biological 
performances, thus making them the most preferred metallic mater ials for biomedical

## Page 5

4 
 
applications (Niinomi, 1998 ; Rack and Qazi, 2006 ). In recent years, there has been a great 
effort on designing new Ti alloys composed of biologically compatible alloying (e.g. Nb, Zr 
and Ta) elements with reasonable mechanical properties (Biesiekierski et al., 2017 ; 
Biesiekierski et al., 2014; Kuroda et al., 1998; Ozaki et al., 2004; Ozan et al., 2015; Zhang et 
al., 2011). 
Titanium alloys can be classified according to their microstruc tures present at room 
temperature as α, near α, α+β, metastable β, and stable β alloys (Polmear, 1995). It is well 
known that α+β titanium alloys have higher yield strength and Young's modulus than those of 
β titanium alloys. CP -Ti and Ti alloys used as orthopedic implant materials have many 
advantages over other metallic implant materials. The long -term, problem-free use of 
orthopedic implants depends on high mechanical strength, as well as low Young’s modulus 
(Abdel-Hady Gepreel and Niinomi, 2013 ). The reason for preferring implant materials with 
low Young's modulus in biomedical applications is to minimize the stress shielding effect 
caused by the Young’s modulus mismatch between the implant mate rial and adjacent bone -
tissue. An implant material, which is more rigid than that of the adjacent bone tissue, is forced 
to carry great part of the mechanical loads, thus reducing bone remodeling and leading to 
implant loosening, progressively. 
Another iss ue in relation to orthopedic implants is their magnetic susceptibility. An 
orthopedic implant material with high magnetic susceptibility will cause artefacts during 
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Shafiei et al., 2003 ). Some orthopedic implant materials 
that possess the required mechanical properties may exhibit inadequate biocompatibility or 
inadequate MRI compatibility, such as SS and Co-Cr alloys (Kondo et al., 2011; Shafiei et al., 
2003). Notably, magnet ic susceptibility of Co -Cr alloys is approximately 7.5 times than that 
of Ti (Chinzei et al., 1999 ). Under such circumstances, we were motivated to carry out this 
study in order to develop new beta Ti alloys that are be able to overcome the drawbacks of

conventional orthopedic implant materials. Three new beta titanium alloys have been 
designed using the biocompatible elements Ti, Ta, Zr and Nb (Li et al., 2010a ) and the 
microstructure, mechanical properties and cytocompatibility of these materials have been 
examined with regard to orthopedic implant applications. The effects of different 
concentrations of Ta, Zr and Nb on the microstructure, mechanical properties and 
cytocompatibility of the TTZN alloys were investigated. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Alloy design 
Three Ti-Ta-Zr-Nb (TTZN) alloys have been designed via the d-electron alloy design method 
(Morinaga et al., 1988 ), combined with the electron-to-atom ratio ( (e/a)ratio) (Boyer et al., 
1994) and molybdenum equivalence (Mo eq) approaches (Bania, 1994), aiming to develop a 
new orthopedic implant material with enhanced biocompatibility, biomechanical 
compatibilities and MRI compatibility . The nominal compositions by weight percent (wt.% ) 
of the designed TTZN alloys are Ti-38.3Ta-22Zr-8.1Nb (TTZN-1), Ti -38.9Ta-25Zr-5Nb 
(TTZN-2) and Ti-39.5Ta-28Zr-2.5Nb (TTZN-3). The niobium ( Nb) and tantalum (Ta) were 
selected since they are isomorphous beta stabilizing alloying elements (Henry et al., 1995 ). 
The effect of zirconium (Zr) on binary titanium alloys is considered to be that of a neutral or 
weak beta -stabilizing element (Abdel-Hady et al., 2006) ; however, Zr is considered a beta 
stabilizer in multi-element titanium alloys containing Nb and/or Ta (Abdel-Hady et al., 2006). 
The use of a trial -error method for the design of Ti alloys leads to time consuming and non -
economical production processes (Laheurte et al., 2010 ). D-electron alloy design method , 
developed by Morinaga et al. (Morinaga et al., 1988), is an effective approach in designing Ti

2.2. Materials preparation 
The TTZN alloys were fabricated with the cold-crucible levitation melting (CCLM) method 
using high purity Ti, Nb, Ta and Zr elemental metals. This method has the advantage of

## Page 8

7 
 
melting metals and alloys without contact between the melt and the melting crucible and it is 
particularly suitable for melting metals with large melting point difference s (Matsugi et al., 
2010). Ingots were remelted five times in order to guarantee chemical homogeneity. Ti metal 
with granule sizes of 3-20 mm, Zr metal with granule sizes of 3-15 mm and Nb-Ti metallic 
wire with diameters of 1.5-3 mm were used as raw material s, and this choice of raw material 
sizes was determined in order to provide effective melting in the production of alloys via the 
casting method. 
Disc-shaped samples with 8 mm diameter and 2 mm thickness , for phase analysis, 
microstructure examination and micro -hardness measurements , were machined using 
electrical discharge machining (EDM) from cast ingots. The disc samples were ground using 
SiC grinding papers up to 4000 grit and then polished via colloid al silica and H2O2 mixture 
solution. Subsequently the samples for microstructure examination and micro -hardness 
measurements were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath for 5 min using ethanol and mounted , and 
the samples for phase analysis were polished and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath without 
mounting. Phase analysis of the alloy samples was carried out via a Bruker D8 Advance X -
Ray diffractometer (XRD). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observa tion, the 
samples were first cut into 0.3 mm thick discs through electrical discharge machining (EDM) 
and mechanically ground to a thickness of about 80 μm. The TEM foils were then electro-
polished in a solution consisted of 6% perchloric acid, 35% n-butyl alcohol and 59% 
methanol through Twinjet (Fischione Model 120). TEM o bservation were carried out on a 
JEOLJEM 2000FX system at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. 
2.3. Mechanical testing 
Micro-hardness measurements were carried out on the polished surfaces of the disc samples 
via processes numbered E384 -11e1 in the ASTM standard (ASTM, 2011). During the micro -

## Page 9

8 
 
hardness measurements, a load of 300 g was applied for 15 seconds. Eleven different 
measurements were carried out for each sample group and the minimum and maximum 
micro-hardness values were not taken into consideration when calculating the average values 
for micro-hardness. Samples polished for microstructure observation were etched using Kroll 
solution (distilled water 100 ml, nitric acid 5 ml, hydroflu oric acid 3 ml) and examined via 
optical microscopy. 
Both tensile and compression tests were performed to measure the mechanical properties of 
the TTZN alloy samples. Tensile samples with gauge section dimensions of 8 mm × 2 mm × 1 
mm were cut via EDM. Both surfaces of the tensile samples were ground using 200, 320, 
1200, 2400 grit SiC grinding papers, progressive ly. Samples were tensile -tested at a cross -
head speed of 0.5 mm/min at room temperature and tensile stress -strain curves were recorded 
via a video extensometer. Yield strength, Young's modulus, modulus of resilience and elastic 
strain values of the alloys were calculated using the data acquired from tensile test ing with 
five repetitions. Young’s modulus was determined by calculating the slope of the linear 
section of the stress-strain curves for the TTZN alloys. The area under the elastic deformation 
region in the stress -strain curves was calculated as the modulus of resilience of the alloys. 
Fracture surfaces of the samples were examined via sca nning electron microscopy (SEM) 
after tensile testing. Cylindrical samples with dimensions of Ф 5×8 mm for compression tests 
were cut via EDM with parallel end surfaces. C ompressive yield strength, compressive 
strength and compression strain values were cal culated using the data obtained from the 
compression tests. The compression tests were carried out using a materials testing system at 
0.5 mm/ min cross -head speed at room temperature in accordance with the processes 
numbered E9−09 in the ASTM standard (ASTM, 2009).

The cytocompatibility of the TTZN alloy samples w as assessed using disc samples with

were seeded at 5 x 10 3 cell density per well following the placement of the samples in to the

carried out for the preparation of the cell -seeded TTZN samples for confocal and SEM 
analysis. The samples were fixed in 3.9% glutaraldehyde for 1 hour for sterilization. 
Dehydration of the cells w as carried out through sequential washing of the samples in 60%, 
70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol solutions for 10 min at each step. Hexamethyldisilazane 
was used for the chemical drying of the samples and gold -coating was performed for SEM

4.1. Microstructure of the TTZN alloys

Figure 1 shows the optical micrographs of the TTZN alloys. It is well known that subgrain 
formation typically occurs during recovery of work -hardened alloys. However, the as -cast 
TTZN alloys exhibited subgrains (Fig. 1). Hynowska et al. (Hynowska et al., 2012 ) reported 
similar research results in their study on an as -cast Ti –39.3Nb–13.3Zr–10.7Ta alloy which 
exhibited sub-grains with peaks of only a β phase in its XRD pattern. It is assumed that the 
compositional differences in the microstructure may lead to the formation of such sub -grains 
in as -cast β Ti alloys . The formation of subgrains in as -cast β Ti alloys have also been 
reported by other researchers (Biesiekierski et al., 2014 ; Sarkar et al., 2013 ; Zhao et al., 
2000). It can be seen that the primary grain size of the TTZN alloys varies between 200 and 
300 μm. The XRD patterns of the TTZN alloys are given in Fig. 2 . It can be seen that all the 
TTZN alloys exhibit the peaks of a β phase. Typical electron diffraction patterns and TEM 
micrographs from TTZN-3 are given in Fig. 3. Through TEM analysis, the particle size of the 
ω phase existed in TTZN -3 was evaluated to be from 3 nm to 8 nm. The XRD patterns of 
TTZN alloys showed only a β phase; however, TEM observation s revealed that ω and β 
phases co-existed in the microstructure. The e lectron diffraction patterns of the ω and β 
phases are shown in Fig . 3(b). Nanoparticles of ω phase, with a size ≥ 5 nm, may cause a 
significant increase in the mechanical strength of Ti alloys while causing the material to be 
brittle under tensile loading (Lin et al., 2016; Ping, 2014). In the XRD analysis, the absence of 
the reflection of the ω phase is probably due to the small size of the ω phase. 
4.2. Mechanical properties of the TTZN alloys 
The micro -hardness values of the TTZN alloys and some other commonly used orthopedic 
implant materials are listed in Fig. 4. It can be seen that TTZN-3 and TTZN-2 show similar 
micro-hardness and it is significantly higher than that of TTZN -1. The micro-hardness values 
of TTZN-1, TTZN-2 and TTZN-3 are 433.4 ± 7.9 HV, 422.5 ± 9.6 HV and 364.6 ± 3.3 HV, 
respectively. In comparison, the micro-hardness values are 134 HV, 172 HV, 238 HV and 318

HV for CP-Ti (Bertrand et al., 2010), 316L SS (Muley et al., 2016), MP35N (a nickel-cobalt-
chromium-molybdenum alloy ) (Ishmaku and Han, 2001 ) and Ni-Ti (Man et al., 2006 ), 
respectively. Thus the micro -hardness values of TTZN-1, TTZN -2 and TTZN-3 alloys are 
substantially higher than those of commonly used orthopedic implant materials. 
Figure 5 shows the compression stress-strain curves for the TTZN alloys . The compressive 
yield strength, compressive strength and compressive strain values of the alloys are given in 
Fig. 6. The compressive yield strength of TTZN-1, TTZN-2 and TTZN-3 has been determined 
as 1317.42 ± 7.24 MPa, 1578.41 ± 11.01 MPa and 1602.83 ± 3.72 MPa, respectively. It can 
be seen that the se alloys are ordered TTZN-3 > TTZN-2 > TTZN-1 with regard s to 
compressive yield strength. Taking into consideration that high compressive yield strength of 
TTZN alloys, ranging from 1317.42 ± 7.24 MPa, to 1602.83 ± 3.72 MPa, compared to 550 
MPa for CP-Ti (grade-4) (Boyer et al., 1994), is obtained under as -cast condition, i.e., in the 
absence of thermo -mechanical treatment, it is of importance that TTZN alloys can be 
considered suitable candidate materials for prospective orthopedic implant applications. 
The compressive strength values have been determined as 1787.19 ± 49.37 MPa, 1797.98 ± 
24.27 MPa and 1807.25 ± 4.89 MPa , and the compressive strain values calculated as 35.72% 
± 1.97, 25.80% ± 4.94 and 22.03% ± 0.04 for TTZN-1, TTZN-2 and TTZN-3, respectively. It 
can be seen that TTZN-1, with the highest compressive strain among the se TTZN alloys, 
shows the lowest compressive strength and compressive yield strength. It is worth noting that 
TTZN alloys present relatively high compressive strength, compared to 1400 -1550 MPa for 
MP35N (Henry, 2009 ). Fig. 7 shows the images of the TTZN samples after compression 
testing. As indicated by the white arrows in Fig. 7, shear band formation at an angle of 45° 
along the sample longitudinal axis is observed in the TTZN samples similar to those of BMGs 
tested under c ompression (Qu et al., 2015 ; Zhang et al., 2010 ). Shear band formation le d to 
the failure of the TTZN samples through crack nucleation and subsequent fracture. The

TTZN-1 samples did not split into two pieces after the compression tests, as seen in Fig. 7 (a,

Figure 8 shows the tensile stress-strain curves for the TTZN alloys. Regarding the tensile 
testing of brittle materials, i t should be noted that rupture strength coincides with tensile 
strength and yield strength (Askeland and Fulay, 2009 ). As can be seen in Fig. 8, rupture 
occurred at the maximum load, where the rupture strength, tensile strength and yield strength 
were all the same, which indicates that the TTZN alloys are brittle during tensile test ing. The 
brittle feature can be explained by the fact that und er the applied tensile stresses, the ω 
nanoparticles, which might be clustered considerably at the grain boundaries, may cause 
stress concentration, thus resulting in brittle fracturing of the TTZN alloys. The ductile feature 
of the TTZN alloys under compression loading is ascribed to the propogation behavior of the 
cracks, ie., cracks prone to remain closed under compression stress . Therefore, the TTZN 
alloys exhibit high mechanical strength and even ductile behavior under compressive loading. 
The high strength of the TTZN alloys is mainly due to the combined effect of solid -solution 
strengthening and precipitating strengthening of the ω nano-particles. 
The yield strength, Young's modulus, elastic strain and modulus of resilience for the TTZN 
alloys are given in Table 2. Considering the yield strength, elastic strain and modulus of 
resilience, the TTZN alloys are in descending order: TTZN-1 > TTZN-3 > TTZN-2. The yield 
strength values are 1107.42 ± 144.68 MPa, 932.11 ± 195.22 MPa and 953.58 ± 120.76 MPa 
for TTZN -1, TTZN -2 and TTZN -3, respectively . The ability of a material to release the 
energy absorbed via loading during elastic deformation on the removal of loading is defined 
as resilience, and is measured by the modulus of resilience or elastic energy (Callister, 2007). 
Yield strength is accepted as the elastic limit (Callister, 2007) in calculating the modulus of 
resilience values of TTZN allo ys. As shown in Fig. 8, TTZN-1 exhibits the highest elastic 
strain among the TTZN alloys, with an elastic strain of 1.57 ± 0.27 (%) . TTZN-1 presents a

targeted applications . The microstructure of TTZN alloys could be changed by means of

4.3. Cytocompatibility of the TTZN alloys 
The i n vitro cytocompatibility of the TTZN alloys w as assessed using SaOS2 cells. The 
surfaces of the TTZN alloy discs were seeded with the se osteoblast-like cells and examined 
with regards to the cell morphologies after 24 h cell culturing. Fig. 10 shows the SEM images 
at different magnifications for the cell-seeded TTZN alloys after cell culturing for 24 h. It can 
be seen that the SaOS2 cells attached to the surfaces and started proliferati ng after culturing 
for 24 h, showing polygonal shapes with numerous filopodia extending over the TTZN alloys. 
Since filopodia formation in cells is an indication of strong cell adhesion and healthy cell 
growth (Li et al., 2012 ), it can be deduced that the TTZN alloys are suited to cell attachment 
and growth. Confocal microscope images of SaOS2 cells on the TTZN alloys after cell culture 
for 7 days are shown in Fig. 11. As can be seen from Fig. 11a-c, a high density of SaOS2 cells 
with blue oval nuclei and elongated red actin filaments are observed, showing healthy growth 
on the surfaces of the TTZN alloys. It is also observed that the SaOS2 cells attached to the 
TTZN alloy surfaces show no significant differences with respect to their morphology. Fig. 12 
shows the cell adhesion density for the TTZN alloys and CP -Ti after cell culturing for 7 days 
in a bar diagram. It can be seen that the cell adhesion density values for TTZN-2 and TTZN-3 
are similar, and slightly higher than th ose of TTZN -1 and CP -Ti. In general, there is no

5. Conclusions 
In this study, three new Ti-Ta-Zr-Nb alloys , namely Ti-38.3Ta-22Zr-8.1Nb (TTZN-1), Ti -
38.9Ta-25Zr-5Nb (TTZN-2) and Ti-39.5Ta-28Zr-2.5Nb (TTZN-3), have been designed and 
fabricated with the cold-crucible levitation melting (CCLM) method . The microstructure, 
mechanical properties and cytocompatibility of the TTZN alloys have been comparatively 
investigated. The main conclusions are as follows: 
1) All three TTZN alloys exhibit peaks belonging to a β phase as determine d via XRD 
analysis, however, TEM observation s revealed that ω and β phase s co-existed in the 
microstructure. 
2) Compression yield strength and compression strength of the TTZN alloys vary from 1317 
to 1603 MPa, and from 1787 to 1807 MPa, respectively. The TTZN-1 alloy shows the highest 
compressive strain and the lowest compressive strength and compressive yield strength 
among the TTZN alloys. The micro-hardness values are 364.6 ± 3.3 HV, 422.5 ± 9.6 HV and 
433.4 ± 7.9 for TTZN-1, TTZN-2 and TTZN -3, respectively. It is worth noting that the high 
compression strength of the se TTZN alloys, obtained in the absence of thermo -mechanical 
treatment, offers high potential for prospective orthopedic implant applications. 
3) The tensile yield strength, elastic strain and Young’s modulus values are 1107.42 ± 144.68 
MPa, 932.11 ± 195.22 MPa and 953.58 ± 120.76 MPa ; 1.57 ± 0.27 (%) , 1.25 ± 0.27 (%) and 
1.29 ± 0.16 (%); and 73.12 ± 4.43 GPa, 74.98 ± 2.19 GPa and 76.62 ± 2.38 GPa , for TTZN-1, 
TTZN-2 and TTZN -3, respectivel y. The modulus of resilience of the TTZN alloys varie s

high mechanical strength and modulus of resilience of the TTZN alloys will enable the design 
of implants with high flexibility, which will be benefi cial in relation to a reduced stress 
shielding phenomenon. 
4) These TTZN alloys show excellent cytocompatibility with a cell adhesion density higher 
than that of CP-Ti. 
5) The TTZN alloys can be anticipated as candidate orthopedic implant materials that could 
be used in the future because of their promising mechanical properties and excellent 
cytocompatibility.
