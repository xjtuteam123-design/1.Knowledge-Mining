Highlights 
- The effect of Cr on the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC alloy is investigated. 
- Substitution of 10 at. % Cr hardly changes the microstructure of the alloy. 
- Cr addition significantly enhance the oxidation resistance of the alloy at 800 °C. 
 
Abstract 
The e ffect of Cr addition on the oxidation behavio ur of multiphase Mo–28Ti–14Si–6C–6B 
(at. %) alloy composed of Mo solid solution, Mo 5SiB2, Ti 5Si3, and TiC phase s was investigated. 
Replacement of 10 at. % Cr for Mo hardly changed the microstructure of the alloy and significantly 
enhanced its oxidation resistance at 800 °C. At 1100 °C, the Cr-added MoSiBTiC alloy showed less 
weight loss than the Cr-free base alloy. However, Cr addition had little effect on the substrate reduction, 
particularly for longer oxidation periods, due to the high volatility of Cr oxide at higher temperatures. 
 
Keywords 
A. alloy; A. intermetallics; B. weight loss; C. high temperature corrosion; C. oxidation

1. Introduction 
Heat engines are essential for harvesting energy from fossil fuel s and convert ing it into 
electrical power and/or propulsion. Fluid dynamics shows that i ncreasing the operating temperature 
and pressure of heat engines is key to enhanc ing their energy -conversion efficiency [1,2] and 
consequently, reducing emissions of CO2 gas. However, the heat-resistance performance of component 
materials of heat engines , that is Ni -based si ngle crystal superalloys, imposes limitations on the ir 
operating temperature and pressure. Therefore, there has been a great need for novel ultrahigh -
temperature materials that can be applied at higher temperature and pressure in the hot section of gas 
turbines. 
Because of their excellent high -temperature strength, Mo–Si–B-based alloys composed of Mo 
solid solution (Mo ss), A15-type Mo 3Si, and D8 l-type Mo 5SiB2 (so-called T 2) phases have attracted 
considerable attention as promising candidates for use as ultrahigh-temperature materials [3–6]. 
Improvement in fracture toughness has been the most crucial issue pertaining to ultrahigh-temperature 
structural applications of ternary Mo–Si–B alloys. In our recent study [7], we have found that the 
addition of a certain amount of TiC improves Moss continuity in the microstructures of cast and heat-
treated Moss–Mo5SiB2-based alloys. As a result, the room -temperature fracture toughness of a TiC -
added Moss–Mo5SiB2 alloy produced by casting was enhanced to approximately 15.2 MPa(m)1/2 at a 
composition of 65Mo–5Si–10B–10Ti–10C (at. %) after heat treatment [8]. Furthermore, it has been 
reported that the MoSiBTiC alloy show s excellent ultrahigh-temperature tensile cree p strength at 
1400–1600 °C [9]. Consequently, the so-called 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy is one of the most 
promising candidates for use as an ultrahigh-temperature material. 
Improving the oxidation resistance of Moss-Mo5SiB2 and Moss–Mo3Si–Mo5SiB2 ternary alloys 
is another critical issue . Yoshimi et al. investigated the oxidation behavio ur of near -monolithic 
Mo5SiB2 [10]. They reported that Mo 5SiB2 shows relatively good oxidation resistance at and below 
1200 °C, and an absence of pesting (catastrophic oxidation) in the temperature range 700 –1000 °C. 
However, Mo3Si has poor oxidation resistance [11,12], implying that this phase does not operate as an 
oxidation-resistant agent in the Mo ss–Mo3Si–Mo5SiB2 system. Because Mo5SiB2 is only the phase

operating as an oxidation-resistant agent, it is apparent that Moss–Mo3Si–Mo5SiB2 ternary alloys will 
not show good oxidation resistance over a wide temperature range [13–15], and that the same is also 
true for MoSiBTiC alloy [16]. Increasing the Si content might be an effective way to improve the 
oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC-based alloys; however, it is suggested from the Mo–Si–B ternary 
phase diagram [17] that merely increasing the Si content promotes the formation of Mo 3Si at the 
expense of equilibrated Moss, not markedly improving the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC-based 
alloys. 
First-principles calculations conducted by Sakidja et al. [18,19] indicated that addition of a large 
amount of Ti should change the phase equilibria from Moss–Mo3Si–Mo5SiB2 to Moss–Mo5Si3-
Mo5SiB2 by destabilizing A15-type Mo3Si and stabilizing D8m-type Mo5Si3. These results inspired the 
idea that the addition of Ti might produce a high Si concentration in the MoSiBTiC system while 
leaving a certain volume fraction of Mo ss in the microstructure. Heilmaier and co -workers [20–22] 
studied the microstructure of the Mo-Ti-Si-B quaternary system experimentally. Unfortunately, they 
did not observe the formation of D8 m-type Mo5Si3, but instead D88-type Ti5Si3 was formed in high-
Ti-content Mo-Ti-Si-B alloys. They also showed that high-Ti-content Mo-Ti-Si-B alloys exhibit good 
creep strength and high oxidation resistance at high temperature s [20–22]. Hatakeyama et al. [23] 
found that an increase in the Ti concentration of MoSiBTiC alloys changes the phase equilibrium from 
Moss-Mo3Si-Mo5SiB2-TiC to Moss-Ti5Si3-Mo5SiB2-TiC; they also found that this change improves the 
intermediate-temperature oxidation to a certain extent though it was still insufficient. Burk et al. [24] 
reported that the addition of a large amount of Cr can significantly improve the intermediate -
temperature oxidation resistance of Mo-9Si-8B (at. %). Therefore, the addition of Cr should improve 
the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC alloys in the intermediate temperature range. 
On the basis of the background described above, we investigated the effects of Cr on the 
microstructural evolution and oxidation resistance of a Ti5Si3-containing MoSiBTiC alloy in this study, 
focusing on concerns about pesting in the intermediate temperature range. Oxidation behaviour was 
examined at 800 °C and 1100 °C. 800 °C is just in the intermediate temperature range, where oxidation 
often becomes catastrophic by pesting. 1100 °C is just above the intermediate temperature range, but

2. Experimental Procedure 
Mo–28Ti–14Si–6C-6B (at. %) alloy (base alloy) and Mo–10Cr–28Ti–14Si–6C-6B (at. %) alloy 
(10Cr alloy) w ere prepared from pure Mo (99.99 mass%), Cr (99.9 mass%), Ti (99.95 mass%), Si 
(99.99 mass%), TiC (99 mass%), and MoB (99 mass%) powders by a conventional arc -melting 
technique in a water-cooled copper crucible under an argon atmosphere. The powders were carefully 
weighed, mixed, and cold-pressed before melting. Button ingots of the alloy were subjected to several 
cycles of melting and physical inversion to ensure compositional homogeneity, and the resulting as-
cast samples were heat treated in an argon atmosphere at 1800 °C for 24 hours for the base alloy and 
at 1600 °C for 24 hours for the 10Cr alloy. The densities of the heat-treated alloys were measured at 
room temperature by the Archimedean method. Pure Mo, Mo–20Ti, Mo–40Ti, Mo–20Cr, and Mo–
20Cr–20Ti (at. %) were also prepared in the same manner and homogenized at 1600 °C for 24 hours. 
For comparison purposes in the oxidation test, 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy was also prepared by 
the method reported by Miyamoto et al. [7]. 
Constituent phases of the as -cast and heat -treated alloys were identified by X -ray diffraction 
(XRD) with Cu-Kα radiation. The concentrations of Mo, Cr, Ti, and Si in the heat-treated alloys were 
analysed semiquantitatively for each phase by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)–scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM). Boron and carbon were not measured in this study because of the poor 
reliability of EDS-SEM for these elements. The microstructures of the as-cast and heat-treated alloys 
were observed by SEM in the back-scattered electron (BSE) mode. All images were recorded in the 
plane parallel to the direction of solidification, so that the upper and lower sides of the microstructures 
corresponded to the top and bottom of the ingots, respectively. The volume fraction of each phase in 
the heat-treated alloys was determined by the electron backscatter diffraction pattern (EBSD) –

orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) technique. The sample surface for EBSD–OIM analysis was 
finished by vibratory polishing with a diamond slurry of average particle diameter 40 nm. 
To evaluate the oxidation resistance of the alloys, coupon -shaped specimens measuring 
4 × 3 × 0.7 mm3 were fabricated from the centr es of the heat -treated ingots by electrodischarge 
machining. Both the 4 × 3 mm2 faces of these coupons were polished with SiC paper (up to #1500) to 
remove the heat-damaged layer to finally produce 4 × 3 × 0.5 mm3 coupons. After polishing, these 
were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone and dried in air. Oxidation tests were performed isothermally 
at 800 °C and 1100 °C for up to 100 hours in a thermogravimetric analyser. To ensure homogeneous 
oxidation, the coupon was mounted on a cylindrical platinum mesh. During heating at a rate of 
20 °C/min, the apparatus was purged with protective argon gas (flow rate: 40 mL/min). Once the 
testing temperature was reached, the argon flow was maintained for 30 minutes to ensure homogeneity 
of the furnace atmosphere, and then high purity oxygen gas was introduced at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. 
After testing, the oxygen flow was stopped and the sample was cooled to 30 0 °C at a rate of 
−20 °C/min under an atmosphere of flowing argon; this was followed by a furnace cooling. Oxidation 
kinetics curves were obtained by plotting the specific mass change against the testing time. The 
oxidized surfaces were analysed by XRD using Cu-Kα radiation. The oxidized coupons were mounted 
vertically in resin and their cross-sections after polishing were observed by SEM in the BSE mode, 
and the oxide scale was characterized by means of EDS. The th icknesses of the oxide scale and 
substrate were measured on the BSE images of the cross-sections by the linear intercept method. The 
thickness of oxide layer is defined as follows: (Thickness of oxide scale) = [(Thickness of oxidized 
coupon) – (Average thickness of substrate)]/2. The substrate reduction was determined by considering 
the thickness of the coupon before testing as follows: (Substrate reduction) = [(Thickness of coupon 
before testing) – (Average thickness of substrate)] /2.

3.2 Oxidation behaviour at 800 °C 
The oxidation kinetics curves of the base alloy and 10Cr alloy obtained at 800 °C are shown in 
Fig. 6; the results for the 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy are also shown for comparison. All the alloys 
exhibited a continuous weight loss , mainly due to the evaporation of volatile MoO3. At this 
temperature, the 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy showed a catastrophic weight loss, and the 0.5-mm-
thick coupons were fully oxidized within a couple of hours. The base alloy also showed an almost 
linear weight loss for several hours during the initial stage. Later, the weight-loss rate suddenly 
flattened, as shown by the dashed line in the figure. However, the coupon after testing was covered 
with porous oxide, possibly as a result of the evaporation of volatile MoO3, indicating that the base 
alloy does not form a protective oxide scale. Cross-sectional observation of the base alloy revealed 
that the substrate was completely oxidized within about eight hours, corresponding to the end of the 
linear weight-loss region, and only the Ti5Si3 phase partially remained inside the oxide, as shown in 
Fig. 7. However, the samples maintained their original shapes after exposure. Therefore, the plateau 
after the rapid weight loss from the beginning is interpreted as that Mo in the specimen had evaporated 
completely. 
On the other hand, the 10Cr alloy showed a parabolic weight loss curve and much better oxidation 
resistance than other samples, with a weight loss of about −5 mg/cm2 even after 100 hours of exposure. 
The parabolic oxidation rate constant (kp) was determined as kp = 5.3 × 10–3 g2·m–4·s–1. However, it

oxide, but also weight gains due to the formation of other oxides such as Ti and/or Cr oxides. 
Figure 8 shows (a) the BSE image of a cross-section of the 10Cr alloy oxidized at 800 °C for 100

The upper sides of these figures correspond to the surfaces of the oxidized coupons. The thickness of

3.3 Oxidation behaviour at 1100 °C 
Oxidation curves for the 1st generation MoSiBTiC, base, and 10Cr alloys obtained at 1100 °C 
are shown in Fig. 9. At 1100 °C, the oxidation of the 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy was catastrophic, 
as at 80 0 °C, and the coupon became completely oxidized in less than five hours. The base alloy 
showed a rapid weight loss in an initial oxidation stage lasting several minutes. The weight-loss rate 
then suddenly decreased but remained linear. The 10Cr alloy showed a much smaller initial rapid 
weight loss, followed by a slow weight loss up to 100 hours. 
Figure 10 shows BSE images of the cross-sections of the base alloy oxidized at 1100 °C for (a) 
eight hours and (b) 100 hours, and for the 10Cr alloy oxidized for (c) eight hours and (d) 100 hours. 
As previously reported [16,23], the Ti5Si3 phase remained in the oxide scale after exposure for eight 
hours. However, after oxidation for 100 hours , the Ti5Si3 phase in the oxide scale completely 
disappeared, and a thick oxide scale, with a thickness of about 150 μm, was formed for both alloys.

The progress in the reduction in the substrate thickness and the commensurate increase in

4. Discussion 
Strategies for improving the oxidation resistance of Mo–Si–B-based alloys consisting of Mo ss 
and silicides have focused mainly on the rapid coverage of the Moss phase by the formation of fluid 
and protective borosilicate [10]. For example, Rioult et al. investigated the relationship between the 
oxidation resistance and the microstructural size scale of ternary Mo–Si–B alloys [11], demonstrating 
that a finer microstructure produces better oxidation resistance due to advantages in immediately 
covering the Moss phase with amorphous borosilicate from adjacent silicide phases or of easily 
exposing the underlying oxidation-resistant silicides by the evaporation of Mo ss. Sossaman et al. 
[27,28] reported that addition of minor amounts of Fe increase the fluidity of the silicate and is 
effective in improving the oxidation resistance of Mo–Si–B alloys. Unfortunately, these improvements 
are only achieved at temperatures above 1000 °C. Furthermore, we cannot ignore oxidation resistance

at intermediate temperatures below 1000 °C. 
The XRD peaks of Moss phase in the as-cast 10Cr alloy are apparently broadened in Fig. 1. After 
heat treatment at 160 0 °C for 24 hours , however, the peak width became sharper, indicating that 
compositional inhomogeneity of Mo with Cr was well resolved during the heat treatment. In addition, 
the Moss peaks of the heat-treated 10Cr alloy shifted to a greater angle due to the presence of a high 
concentration of Cr solute. The homogenization and element-distribution behaviour observed in the 
BSE images and SEM-EDS were in good agreement with the results of XRD. The volume fractions 
of the Moss, T2, Ti5Si3, and TiC phases and their morphologies were not greatly changed by Cr. Cr is 
in same group of the Periodic Table as Mo and it forms a continuous solid solution with Mo at high 
temperatures [29]. This might be the reason why the addition of Cr to the Mo–Si–B–Ti–C system does 
not change the constituent phases or their volume fractions. In the 10Cr alloy, however, coarsening of 
the microstructure was significant during heat treatment at 1600 °C, which is 200 °C lower than the 
corresponding temperature for the base alloy. This means that the addition of a high concentration of 
Cr to the MoSiBTiC alloy enhances diffusivity at high temperature s; therefore, careful attention to 
heat-treatment condition s is needed to obtain a desir ed microstructure. Furthermore, creep 
performance controlled by diffusion could be degraded by Cr even though the element should act as a 
strong solid solution hardener of Moss [30]. 
At 80 0 °C, the 1st generation MoSiBTiC alloy and the base alloy exhibited catastrophic 
oxidation behaviours, and the 0.5-mm-thick coupons of these alloys were completely oxidized within 
ten hours. Therefore, increasing the Si content to 14% is ineffective in improving the oxidation 
resistance of MoSiBTiC alloys at 800 °C. The slightly slower rate of weight loss of the base alloy was 
attributed to its lower content of volatile Mo. Under these conditions, whereas oxidation progressed 
across the Moss, T2, and TiC phases, the Ti5Si3 phase was hardly oxidized. As a result, a porous TiO2–
SiO2 duplex phase and an unattacked Ti5Si3 phase were observed in the oxide scale, as shown in Fig. 
7. This porous structure is attributed to insufficient fluidity of the oxides, as well as continuous 
evaporation of MoO3, leading to catastrophic oxidation behaviour.

and Ti5Si3 bulwarks were observed in the oxide scale and in the oxide/substrate interface, respectively. 
Accordingly, the 10Cr alloy showed better oxidation resistance at 800 °C; therefore, the addition of 
Cr is effective in improving the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC alloys at 80 0 °C. Because the 
oxidation progresses preferentially across the Moss phase among the four phases in this alloy system 
[23], the oxidation rate of the Moss phase significantly influences the oxidation resistance of the entire 
alloy. In other words, the instant removal of the Moss phase promotes exposure of other adjacent phases 
to the oxidation environment and accelerates oxidation. According to the investigation of a simplified 
Moss phase as shown in Fig. 14, from the viewpoint of weight loss rate, whereas Ti addition contributes 
little to the oxidation resistance of Mo, the addition of Cr decreases the oxidation rate of Mo, and the 
co-addition of Ti and Cr was rather helpful in improving the oxidation resistance by suppressing the 
formation or sublimation of the Mo oxide. This means that although the Moss phase in the base alloy 
has little oxidation resistance, the co-addition of Cr to the alloy had a marked effect in reducing the 
oxidation rate of Moss, resulting in the improvement of the oxidation resistance of the alloy at 800 °C. 
Furthermore, the preferential partitioning of Cr to the Moss phase was fortunate in this situation. In 
addition, because both the rate of mass change and the rate of substrate reduction of the 10Cr alloy 
follow a parabolic rate law at 80 0 °C, the expected rate-controlling process for the 10Cr alloy is 
diffusion of ox ygen through the oxide scale. After oxidation, only T iO2 was detected, and no 
chromium oxide could be detected on the surface by XRD (not shown here), indicating that Cr oxide 
was not present in the surface of oxide scale. The effect of Cr on the diffusion of oxygen in TiO2 has 
been reported by Mi et al.[31]. According to their literature, when the Cr content is low, Cr dissolves 
in TiO2 as Cr3+ by substituting for Ti4+ and produces oxygen vacancies. These vacancies enhance the 
diffusion of O2–, resulting in faster oxidation. On the other hand, if the content of Cr in TiO2 exceeds 
the solubility limit, Cr oxide separates out from TiO2 and the finely distributed one retards the diffusion 
of oxygen as diffusion-blocking agent. The solubility limit of Cr in TiO2 has been reported to be 7.5 
at. % at above 1400 °C [32]. As shown in Fig. 5, the Ti/Cr ratios in the Moss, T2, and Ti5Si3 phases of 
the 10Cr alloy were approximately 1:1, 5:1, and 7:1, respectively. When Moss in the 10Cr alloy is 
oxidized and Mo evaporates as MoO3, it is considered that the amount of Cr in Ti(Cr)O2 exceeds the

solubility limit. As a result, chromium oxide would separate from Ti(Cr)O2. The idea is supported by 
the result shown in Fig. 15 that not only TiO2 but also Cr2O3 were detected on the oxide scale of Mo-
20Cr-20Ti. This would be the reason why the oxidation resistance of the Moss phase in the 10Cr alloy 
increases. A similar effect occurs through the oxidation of other silicides, and this mechanism also 
plays an important role on the improvement of oxidation resistance at 800 °C. The results shown in 
Fig. 14, that the co-addition of Ti and Cr improve the oxidation resistance of Mo , support this 
hypothesis. 
At 1100 °C, the base alloy showed a much better oxidation resistance than the 1st generation 
MoSiBTiC alloy. This indicates that a higher concentration of Si contributes to protection against 
oxidation at this temperature. The base alloy showed a rapid weight loss and rapid substrate reduction 
during the first hour. Subsequently, whereas the substrate reduction hardly changed, weight loss 
occurred mainly through evaporation of MoO3, and the oxide scale grew in a parabolic manner. From 
a cross-sectional analysis, it was found that Ti5Si3 phase was hardly oxidized whereas other 
surrounding phases were oxidized during the initial stage s of oxidation , as shown in Fig. 10(a). 
Moreover, the progress of oxidation was prevented when the invasion reached the T2 phase because 
the T2 phase has a relatively good oxidation resistance at this temperature as a result of the formation 
of borosilicate [10]. For longer exposure time s, the Ti5Si3 islands gradually shrank and eventually 
disappeared from the region near the surface but the T2 phase appeared to continue to prevent the 
progress of oxidation. These results suggest that the oxidation process of the base alloy can be divided 
into two regions. First, the Moss and TiC phase s are preferentially oxidized from the surface and , 
consequently, rapid weight loss due to the evaporation of MoO3, B2O3, and CO2 occurs, leaving SiO2 
and TiO2. Once the oxidation reaches the elongated T2 phase, the inward growth of the oxide scale is 
interrupted by borosilicate on the T2 phase and the Ti5Si3 phase in the oxide scale begins to oxidize. 
Due to a slower oxidation of Ti5Si3, as well as the smaller concentration of molybdenum in the Ti5Si3 
phase, the mass change in this latter region is generally smaller than that in the former region. Once 
the Ti5Si3 phase is consumed, the T2 starts to be damaged and local repetition of this cycle throughout 
the specimen results in relatively rapid linear weight loss kinetics.

The 10Cr alloy showed a small er initial rapid oxidation step followed by a continuous linear 
weight loss during oxidation. In the initial step, although the surfaces of Ti5Si3 phases showed slight 
damage, they contributed to the oxidation resistance as a bulwark (see Fig. 12). However, the bulwarks 
disappeared on longer exposure (see Figs. 10 and 13). Therefore, a large and continuous Ti5Si3 phase 
is preferred to maximize the lifetime of Ti5Si3, thereby improving the oxidation resistance of the alloy. 
In addition, Cr in the Moss phase contributes to a reduction in the oxidation rate. However, as pointed 
out in Fig. 11, the beneficial effect of Cr addition on the oxidation resistance at 1100 °C occur only in 
the initial stage of oxidation. This is because of the lifetime of the Ti5Si3 bulwarks and the moderate 
volatility of Cr. In addition, as shown in Fig. 13(b), Cr and Ti were detected in different regions in the 
oxide scale. This can be interpreted as the phase separation of Ti(Cr)O2 and Cr2O3 occurred and the 
Cr2O3 clusters largely grew. In this case, the blocking effect of finely-dispersed Cr2O3 reduces, and as 
a result the diffusion flux of O2- goes back to the same level as in Ti(Cr)O 2. This would be another 
reason that little contribution of Cr had achieved at this temperature. 
Because the fracture toughness of Mo –Si–B-based alloys is dominated by ductile -phase 
toughening of the Moss phase, the presence of a high-volume fraction of a continuous Moss phase is 
preferable. However, such a microstructure has inadequate resistance to oxidation [33]. These 
conflicting factors make it difficult to obtain a n optimal balance between fracture toughness and 
oxidation resistance. In this study, the oxidation resistance of MoSiBTiC alloy at 800 °C was shown 
to be improved by the addition of Cr, without a change in phase equilibria. This improvement results 
from the enhanced oxidation resistance of the Moss phase, meaning that we can design MoSiBTiC 
alloys with a higher volume fraction of Moss phase. The Moss phase in MoSiBTiC alloys has been 
recognized as the main phase contribut ing to fracture toughness [8,23]. On the other hand, it is 
considered that Moss phase contributes to oxidation resistance at intermediate temperatures by acting 
as a Cr source when Cr is added to MoSiBTiC alloys. Furthermore, solid-solution hardening of the 
Moss phase by Cr is also expected to occur [30]. Among the constituent phases in MoSiBTiC alloys, 
the TiC phase is considered to contribute to both high-temperature strength [34] and room-temperature 
fracture toughness [8], whereas the T2 phase provides high-temperature strength [7,35] and oxidation

resistance at higher temperatures [10]. The Ti5Si3 phase is also considered to contribute to both high-
temperature strength [23] and oxidation resistance by acting as a Si source. To design multiphase 
metallic composites with sufficient high-temperature strength, fracture tough ness, and oxidation 
resistance as next-generation ultrahigh-temperature materials, it is desirable that the constituent phases 
each contribute at least two properties to the alloy. In this study, we have approached one possible 
solution to the development of an ideal composite material. The effects of Cr on the mechanical 
properties of MoSiBTiC alloy will be investigated in the future. 
 
5. Conclusions 
The effect of the addition of Cr on the microstructure and oxidation resistance of a Ti5Si3-
containing MoSiBTiC alloy was investigated in this work . The addition of Cr did not change the 
constituent phases or the volume fraction s of the Moss, Mo 5SiB2 (T2), Ti5Si3, and TiC phases, but 
nevertheless enhanced microstructural coarsening. The substitution of 10% of Cr for Mo reduces the 
density of the alloy to 7.1 g/cm 3, which is considerably less than that of Ni -based superalloys or 
MoSiBTiC-based alloys. Cr is mainly partitioned into the Moss phase, and this improves the oxidation 
resistance of the Moss phase itself by the fine dispersion of Cr2O3 in Ti(Cr)O2 layer, resulting in the 
oxidation resistance of the alloy with a weight change of only –5 mg/cm2 during 100 hours at 800 °C, 
where catastrophic oxidation typically occurs in Cr-free alloys. Ti5Si3 bulwarks also contributed to 
this good oxidation resistance. However, the positive effect of Cr addition on the oxidation resistance 
cannot be obtained at 1100 °C due to the increase in the volatility of Cr2O3 and the coarsening of Cr2O3 
in Ti(Cr)O 2 layer. This implies that Cr addition has the limited contribution at much higher 
temperatures. These results are supported by comparative experiments with single-phase Mo, Mo–Ti, 
and Mo–Ti–Cr alloys.
