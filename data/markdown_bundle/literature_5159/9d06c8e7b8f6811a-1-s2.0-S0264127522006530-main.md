abstract
A gradient microstructure was successfully fabricated by high-frequency induction quenching treatment
(HFIQT) to improve the mechanical behavior of Ti-6Al-4V alloy in this work. Microstructural evolution
was systematically characterized by electron backscattered diffraction and transmission electron micro-
scopy. The results showed that the gradient microstructure of the alloy after HFIQT and aging varied from
a ﬁne
as lamellae decomposed from a0 -martensite at the surface layer to a bimodal microstructure at the
center. Moreover, the alloy with gradient microstructure presents an optimal strength-ductility synergy.
The tensile test results showed that a best strength plastic product U
T of 12433.1 MPa /C1 %, an ultimate
strength of 1231 MPa and an elongation of 10.1 % were obtained after HFIQT for 5.2 s and aging at
400 /C176C for 6 h. The tensile strength of the alloy aged at 400 /C176C markedly increased with the increase in
soaking time from 5.1 to 5.3 s during HFIQT. Meanwhile, the tensile strength of the alloy slightly
increased with the decrease in aging temperature from 550 to 400 /C176C during HFIQT. Surprisingly, the
elongation of the alloy also increased with decreasing aging temperature. The effects of the gradient
microstructure characteristic on strength and ductility of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy were discussed.

1. Introduction
The titanium industry has been developing rapidly over the past
few decades due to the imperious demands of aerospace [1,2].
Now, titanium alloys are indispensable structural materials in the
aerospace industry and have been widely used in shipbuilding,
chemical industry, medical, and other aspects [3–7]. The Ti-6Al-
4V alloy has become the most popular a + b titanium alloys
because of their excellent performance, e.g., high strength-to-
weight ratio, low density, good biocompatibility, and outstanding
corrosion resistance. These excellent mechanical properties can

microstructures, including equiaxed, lamellar, bimodal, and net
basket microstructures, which have an effect on some important
mechanical properties, can be achieved in Ti-6Al-4V alloy by
changing the thermomechanical processing route [12].
The surface structural integrity of titanium alloys can be dam-
aged, and the surface structure of the stress state can also be changed
with materials in the process of manufacturing, making the failure of
parts often start from surface defects and damage. This phenomenon
leads to the fatigue damage of materials in the subsequent service.
Numerous investigations are conducted to improve the surface
mechanical properties of titanium alloys [13–21]. An ultraﬁne grain
layer treated by surface mechanical attrition treatment is generated
on the surface of commercial pure titanium, resulting in a 60 %
reduction in friction coefﬁcient [13]. Tang et al. studied the surface
performance of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy treated by the cathode-assisted
discharge plasma nitriding process, which improves the wear resis-
tance of Ti-6Al-4V alloy [14]. Jian et al. established the excellent fric-
tion and wear property of medical Ti-6Al-4V alloy subjected to laser
peening [15]. The surface microhardness of the samples treated by
shot peening is markedly improved by 25.7 %, and the corresponding
hardened layer depth is higher than 0.3 mm. Y.G. Liu et al. [16] fab-
ricated a gradient nanostructure in the surface of Ti-6Al-4V alloy via
shot peening, which increases the strength–ductility synergy for Ti-
6Al-4V alloy. P.E. Markovsky et al. [17] studied the inﬂuence of dif-
ferent microstructures on the mechanical properties of
commercial-purity titanium by local rapid heat treatment.
Results indicated that the tensile strength increases by about
40 MPa and about 1.5 times increase for fatigue limit.
Based on the obtained results by many researchers, the surface
strengthening of titanium alloy is mainly through shot peening
and surface mechanical treatments to obtain nanocrystals on the
surface of the material and chemical heat treatment to change the
chemical composition of the work-piece surface to form a gradient
structure for a good combination of strength and ductility [22–25].
As a rapid thermal treatment technology, high-frequency induction
heating has been widely used in the industrial ﬁeld due to its advan-
tage of process controllability, fast heating rate, cleanliness, low
energy consumption, and environmental friendliness. A tempera-
ture gradient can be formed via fast heating. The surface tempera-
ture of the material rises rapidly, whereas the temperature inside
the substrate rises slowly. Thus, different microstructures can be
obtained during high-frequency induction heating, causing a differ-
ence in the material’s surface and internal mechanical properties.
Unfortunately, the application of high-frequency induction heating
in titanium alloys is rarely reported for the material’s surface
strengthening. Therefore, this work aims to balance the strength
and ductility by fabricating a gradient microstructure in the Ti-
6Al-4V titanium alloy subjected to high-frequency induction heat-
ing. Furthermore, the relationship among the microstructure, pro-
cessing parameters, and mechanical properties of Ti-6Al-4V
titanium alloys with gradient structure is established, and the inﬂu-
ence mechanisms of high-frequency induction heating are eluci-
dated and discussed. This work will provide a new idea and
method for improving the strength–ductility synergistic perfor-
mance of the Ti-6Al-4V titanium alloy.
2. Materials and experimental procedures
2.1. Material
The Ti-6Al-4V alloy used in this work was an ingot of
550 mm /C2 227 mm /C2 65 mm provided by the Western Titanium
Industry Co. ltd. Its detailed chemical composition is listed in
Table 1 . The b transus temperature (T
b) of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy
was measured at approximately 985 /C176C by metallographic method.
The initial microstructure of the as-received material is a typical
bimodal structure with approximately 38 % of primary a phase
(ap) and 62 % of the transformed b matrix ( btrans), in which ap
phases and ﬁne a platelets were distributed uniformly, as shown
in Fig. 1 .
2.2. Experimental procedures
The uniaxial tensile samples were machined from the as-
received material by electrical discharge machining. The corre-
sponding size is shown in Fig. 2 . Tensile samples were subjected
to local rapid heat treatment by using high-frequency induction
heating equipment (SYG-10AB, Guangdong Saiyang Intelligent
Equipment Technology Co., ltd) consisting of an induction coil
and induction power supply with output oscillation power of
10 kW and frequency of 600–1100 kHz. The heating duration could
be controlled within 0.1 s, and the heating rate reached up to
400 /C176C/s. Each sample was dropped into a water bath to quench
after high-frequency induction heating. Then, these samples were
aged at 400, 450, 500 and 550 /C176C for 6 h in a vacuum tube furnace
with argon protection and air-cooled. The relevant heat treatment
process is shown in Fig. 3 .
Induction-heated samples were used for tension tests after
removing the oxide layer. Tensile experiments were performed
using an MTS servo-hydraulic testing machine (MTS 810) at a con-
stant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min and room temperature. Two
tensile samples were tested at each heat treatment condition to
ensure accuracy.
Moreover, the microhardness of specimens in cross-section was
tested by the automatic Vickers microhardness tester (HVS-1000)
with load of 100 g and holding time of 10 s. For each sample, the
distance between microhardness is 200 lm.
Metallographic samples were ground with abrasive papers
coded as 320#, 1000#, 2000#, and 3000# and mechanically pol-
ished with Al2O3 powder. Specimens were etched in Kroll’s reagent
(10 % HF, 30 % HNO 3, and 70 % H 2O). The microstructure and frac-
ture surface of the broken specimens were characterized using
ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscopy (SUPRA40) and elec-
tron back-scattered diffraction (EBSD). In addition, the metallo-
graphic disks (thickness, 0.5 mm) were sliced using a wire-
electrode cutting saw along the transverse direction ‘‘A–A”, as
shown in Fig. 4 . Then the specimens were mechanically thinned
to obtain a thickness of approximately 70 lm. The microstructural
characterization of these specimens was carried out using the FEI
Talos F200X transmission electron microscope (TEM) at 200 kV
after ion polishing.

moved to the left with increased aging temperature, which indi-

4.2. Surface-strengthening mechanisms
The top surface, subsurface layer, and central zone of the spec-
imen with gradient microstructures experience equal elongation
during uniaxial tension. It is well known that the bimodal structure
in the central zone has higher ductility and lower strength com-
pared with the full lamellar microstructure in the top surface lay-
ers. The soft bimodal microstructure in the central zone would
yield ﬁrstly whereas the hard lamellar microstructure in the top
surface layer of the specimens maintains elastic deformation under
the tensile test. However, lateral instability is constrained and
quickly stopped because of the interaction between different lay-
ers. A plastic strain gradient is generated during the tension pro-
cess due to the different deformability of the interior and surface
layer. Numerous geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) need
to accumulate at the elastic/plastic interface, building up high back
stress, which is in the opposite direction of the applied stress, to
accommodate this strain gradient [41–43]. The introduction of
GNDs can often enhance the strength and work hardening of mate-
rials [44–46]. The strengthening methods, in addition to the short-
range interaction between GNDs and movable dislocations,
improve the slip resistance of dislocations [47], and the long-
range back stress produced by GNDs increases the slip resistance
of dislocations as well. Besides, back-stress work hardening is ben-
eﬁcial to prevent necking during tensile testing to enhance ductil-
ity [48]. Therefore, the Ti-6Al-4V alloy with gradient
microstructure shows higher strength than the alloy with an initial
microstructure without an observable drop in plasticity.
Additionally, internal phase boundaries in the microstructure
can present obstacles to dislocation motion. On geometrical con-
siderations alone, phase boundaries are expected to provide stron-
ger obstacles to dislocation motion than line or point defects
because the intersection of a surface with a slip plane is a line
rather than a point. Hence, a surface, such as a phase boundary,

4.3. Mechanism for the improvement of tensile strength
On the one hand, dislocations moving along a particular slip
plane cannot directly cross a/b interfaces and accumulate near
the phase boundaries during the plastic deformation of Ti-6Al-4V
alloys. On the other hand, due to the misorientation between the
ﬁne as lamellae, multiple slips to coordinate deformations result
in dislocation delivery. Therefore, the strength of the alloy
improves signiﬁcantly after HFIQT and aging treatment.
The surface layer of the specimen consists of the ﬁne as lamel-
lae decomposed from a0-martensite, and the interior retains many
ap phases after HFIQT and aging at different temperatures ( Fig. 4).
Compared with initial microstructures consisting of ap phases,
increased a/b phase boundaries of gradient microstructures pro-
vide strong obstacles to dislocation motion. Consequently, the ten-
sile strength of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy after HFIQT and aging treatment
is remarkably enhanced.

In this study, YS is closely related to the transfer of dislocation
glide, and whether such a transfer can occur depends predomi-
nantly on whether the stress concentration ( s) generated by the
dislocation pileups near a/b interfaces, which can activate the dis-
location sources in adjacent slip systems. Thus, coordinated
multiple slips are carried out. According to the equation s ¼ n s0,
the stress concentration ( s) depends on the number of dislocation
pileups. The stress concentration increased with with the number
of dislocation pileups nearby a/b interfaces in the lamellar
microstructure. In turn, this number increases as the mean slip dis-
tance (the size of as lamellae) in the lamellar microstructure
increases. By contrast, the dislocation pileups near the a/b inter-
face of small as lamellae causes less s, and the plastic deformation
of adjacent as lamellae can take place only under large applied
stress.
The schematic of the dislocation motion in the lamellar
microstructure of different sizes is shown in Fig. 14. Thus, the alloy
with ﬁne as lamellae after HFIQT and aging at 400 /C176C for 6 h pos-
sesses high strength.
4.4. Mechanism for the improvement of plasticity
The ﬁne as lamellae can improve the strength of the alloy and
enhance its plasticity. On the one hand, due to increased slip sys-
tems in the alloy, the deformation between the ﬁne as lamellae
cannot occur simultaneously and can be well-coordinated. On
the other hand, the strain discrepancy near the phase boundaries
of the ﬁne as lamellae is small, leading to the deformation of mate-
rials being uniform under the same external stress. Thus, the alloy
cannot easily form the crack caused by stress concentration. So,
sustaining a large deformation prior to fracture is possible. There-
fore, the Ti-6Al-4V alloy after HFIQT and aging at 400 /C176C for 6 h
exhibits excellent plasticity.

5. Conclusion

and aging treatment was studied. Several conclusions drawn from
this work could be summarized as follows:
(1) The Ti-6Al-4V alloy fabricated by HFIQT and aging treatment
exhibited a large microhardness gradient, and the
microstructural gradient in the volume fraction of equiaxed
a microstructures was observed from the surface to interior.
The gradient microstructures consisted of full lamellar
microstructure in the top surface and bimodal microstruc-
ture in the center region, which resulted in the decrease in
microhardness of the alloy with increasing distance from
the top surface.

HFIQT for 5.2 s and aging at 400 /C176C for 6 h exhibited excel-
