Development of Nanocrystalline Copper-Refractory Metal Alloys 
 
J. M. Rigsbee 
Department of Materials Science and Engineering 
North Carolina State University 
Raleigh, North Carolina 27612 USA 
mike_rigsbee@ncsu.edu 
 
Keywords: amorphization, composite, copper-refractory metal alloys, ion plating, nanocrystalline, 
partially coherent interphase boundary, physical vapor deposition, precipitation, sputtering 
 
Abstract. Precipitation-strengthened Cu-based alloys have limited use as structural materials at 
high temperatures due to precipitate coarsening and strength loss. We have recently shown that Cu-
refractory metal alloys produced by various physical vapor deposition methods have stable, 
nanocrystalline microstructures and maintain their strength properties even when annealed at 
temperatures as high as 900 C for up to 100 hours. This paper presents discussions of how these 
alloys are processed and the resulting microstructures. X-ray and electron microscopy results will 
be presented to document the phase transformations that occur in these alloys and result in such 
exceptionally stable microstructures. 
 
Introduction 
 
Cu-based alloys are candidate structural materials for new aerospace technologies requiring high 
strength with high thermal and electrical conductivities. Research on co-melted and highly 
plastically deformed Cu-refractory metal composites, which became very active in the mid-1980’s 
partially because of the X-30 National Aero-Space Plane program, successfully produced high 
strength composite materials with microstructures consisting of a fine dispersion of long, ribbon-
like refractory metal particles in a pure Cu matrix [1,2]. However, these same studies proved that 
annealing at 500 C caused coarsening of the refractory metal phase and a corresponding large 
decrease in strength. As evidence of the materials challenges in the X-30 program, 500 C is well 
below the 820 C design temperature for the external skin of the X-30 [3]. It is presumed that the 
refractory metal particle coarsening occurred by diffusion along the extensive Cu-refractory metal 
interfaces. The role of the interphase boundary is critical in coarsening in these systems since the 
solid solubilities of Ta and W in Cu are extremely low. An analysis by Smith of Ta solubility in 
molten Cu showed an equilibrium solubility level of only 88 ppm at 1200 C [4]. 
 Other methods for producing Cu-refractory metal alloys have included mechanical alloying 
using high energy ball milling [5-7], ion beam mixing of multilayers [8] and various forms of 
physical vapor deposition [9-14]. 
 
Ball Milling. Sakurai et al. [5] and Veltl et al. [6] showed that ball milling induced the formation of 
amorphous alloys by solid state reactions between crystalline Cu and Ta powders. Because of the 
large positive heat of mixing, the mixing reaction was driven by the high defect densities created by 
ball milling. Recent work by Ma and co-workers showed that ball milling at temperatures from 
cryogenic to room temperature produced similar microstructures of nanocrystalline Cu and Ta, with 
partial amorphization and with no apparent increase in solubility with decreasing temperature [7]. 
 
Ion Beam Mixing . Gong et al. studied amorphization reactions in ion beam mixed multilayered 
Cu/Ta and Cu/W systems. Experimental studies agreed with the results of molecular dynamics 
modeling: in the Cu+Ta system the amorphization reaction occurred with a critical composition of 
about 30 atomic percent Ta; in the Cu+W system amorphization occurred with W levels between 20 
and 65 atomic percent [8].

Physical Vapor Deposition. An early example of using physical vapor deposition (PVD) to study 
Cu-refractory metal alloys was the work by Nastasi et al. [9] who co-evaporated Ta and Cu to 
produce a series of Ta rich films (45 to 90 percent Ta). These films were found to be amorphous as 
deposited and when subjected to annealing they transformed into tetragonal Ta and FCC Cu phases, 
with the kinetics and temperatures for transformation being a function of composition. 
 
Nanocrystalline Cu-Refractory Metal Studies 
 
A series of studies have been performed under the author’s direction investigating processing,-
structure-property relationships for different Cu-refractory metal systems [10-14]. These studies 
used various forms of PVD to produce the alloys, applied x-ray diffraction and transmission 
electron microscopy for direct observation of the as-deposited and annealed microstructures and 
applied mechanical testing techniques to determine how the strength properties varied with 
microstructure and thermal treatment. Common themes in these studies were to understand; (1) to 
what degree PVD allows creation of chemically and structurally non-equilibrium, nanocrystalline 
microstructures; (2) what factors are critical in determining the stability (prevention of coarsening) 
of the microstructures; and, (3) the effects that annealing for long times (up to 100 hours) at very 
high temperatures (900 C or 0.865 T mp of Cu) have on the alloy strengths. These studies are 
reviewed in the following sections, in chronological order. 
 
Sputter Deposited Cu-Cr. Shin et al. [10] used biased sputter deposition to investigate non-
equilibrium mutual solubility between Cu and Cr. Without substrate bias, the Cu 0.75 Cr 0.25 films 
were equilibrium mixtures of the expected FCC and BCC phases. With application of a -100V 
substrate bias, the Cu 0.75 Cr 0.25 films were single phase FCC. Compared with the Cu-Cr equilibrium 
phase diagram [15], this represents an approximate 1000X increase in solubility. The enhanced 
solubility is attributed to ballistic collisional mixing in the top several atomic layers of the growing 
film caused by the impacting ~20 eV Ar + ions and the ultra-rapid quench rate achieved by PVD 
(~10 14 C/sec). A similar result was found on the Cr-rich side, with a Cu 0.2 Cr 0.8 film being single 
phase BCC when deposited with an -100V applied substrate bias. TEM imaging results for the 
single phase films showed equiaxed grains about 100 nm in size for both composition films. 
Annealing at 300 C produced no apparent changes in the film microstructure. 
 
Ion Plated Cu-Ta. Ion plating is a plasma-assisted physical vapor deposition (PAPVD) process 
that combines e-beam evaporation (the source of deposited material) with continuous bombardment 
of the growing film by medium energy (<100eV) Ar + ions. The argon ions come from a plasma 
generated adjacent to the substrates fixture by application of a large (-1000V) bias on the substrate 
fixture. Ion plating is a commercial process capable of deposition of adherent films over large areas 
(m 2) at high rates (ten’s of microns per hour). 
 Savage et al. [11,12] produced a series of Cu 1-xTa x (x = 0.05-0.2) ion plated alloy films 
using ion plating. The Cu and Ta were evaporated from individual e-beam sources and a -1000V 
bias was applied to the substrate fixture. In the as-deposited films, the second phase consisted of 
discrete Ta particles ranging in size from about 2-20 nm. The matrix consisted of equiaxed Cu 
grains about 100 nm in diameter. Diffraction studies in the TEM determined that the smaller Ta 
particles were FCC and the larger Ta particles were sometimes BCC. After annealing at 
temperatures up to 900 C for as long as 10 hours, no substantial changes occurred in the matrix or 
second phase particle sizes, suggesting that Cu grain boundaries were pinned by the Ta particles. 
 Figure 1 is a weak beam dark field TEM micrograph showing the nanoscale Ta particles in a 
Cu 94 Ta 6 sample annealed at 800 C for 1 hour. The features shown in this micrograph are a dark 
background (the Cu matrix) and the brighter, more strongly diffracting Ta particles. Most Ta 
particles are approximately 3-4 nm in diameter and appear to be randomly distributed. There is also 
a distribution of larger Ta particles, 8-20 nm in size. Electron diffraction revealed the smaller Ta 
particles to be FCC with orientations identical to the matrix Cu grains. This orientation relationship

Sputter Deposited Cu-Ta. Wang et al. [13, 14] studied the microstructures and mechanical 
properties of conventionally (no applied bias) sputter deposited Cu 1-xTa x (x = 6 and 17) alloys. TEM 
analyses of the low and high Ta composition films as-deposited directly onto carbon support films 
revealed two sets of FCC diffraction rings, corresponding to Cu and Ta. The Ta particles were 
randomly distributed and 5 nm or less in size. The Cu matrix was polycrystalline, with the 
approximate grain size being 500 nm for the low Ta alloy and 100 nm for the high Ta alloy. A key 
observation for both the as-deposited and the annealed films was that the FCC Ta particles were 
oriented identically with the Cu matrix, consistent with the prior ion plated Cu-Ta study by Savage 
et al. [11, 12]. 
 XRD for 12 micron thick as-deposited low and high Ta alloy films showed clear FCC Cu 
peaks with no observed peak shift, indicating little Ta in solid solution. There was no evidence in 
the as-deposited films of any Ta XRD peaks (FCC or BCC). After annealing for 1 hour at 900 C, it 
was observed by XRD that the low Ta alloy had clear FCC (no BCC) Ta diffraction peaks and the 
high Ta alloy had clear BCC (no FCC) diffraction peaks. 
 Figure 2 shows is a bright-field TEM micrograph from a vacuum hot-pressed Cu 94 Ta 6 bulk 
sample that was annealed at 900C for 1 hour under a pressure of 35 MPa. The Cu matrix is the 
lighter gray background. The discrete, nanoscale FCC Ta particles are aligned in rows, about 3-5 
nm thick, on uniformly spaced {111} (dominant) and {100} Cu habit planes, with about 10-15 nm 
spacing between parallel Ta particle arrays. Annealing for 10 and 100 hours at 900 C did not 
eliminate or significantly change the FCC structure of the Ta. 
 Annealing the high Ta content alloy at 900 C for 1 hour caused the formation of large BCC 
Ta particles and a major reduction in the amount of FCC Ta particles, as shown by TEM and XRD. 
The 50-150 nm diameter BCC Ta particles were mostly on the Cu grain boundaries. FCC Ta 
particles were found only in the grain interiors. The Cu matrix grains were between 200-500 nm 
after annealing. Annealing 10 and 100 hours at 900 C virtually eliminated FCC Ta particles but did 
not significantly alter the size or distribution of the BCC Ta particles or Cu grains.

Phase Evolution and Stability 
 
The results discussed show that PVD techniques can be used to create microstructures in Cu-
refractory metal alloys systems that differ significantly from those predicted from equilibrium 
thermodynamics. Solid solubilities can be increased several orders of magnitude and phases can be 
produced that would appear to violate the equilibrium phase diagram predictions. Much of these 
results obtain from the fact that PVD techniques allow materials to be created atom-by-atom and 
non-equilibrium microstructures are effectively frozen in place by the ultra-rapid ~10 14 C/sec 
cooling rates achieved by PVD. 
 There remain however several questions that must be addressed concerning how certain of 
the microstructures evolve and why these non-equilibrium microstructures are unusually stable even 
when exposed to very high temperatures for extended times. Specifically, how does the FCC Ta 
phase form and why is it so very stable with no evidence of coarsening or transformation to the 
equilibrium BSS crystal structure. These questions are addressed by figures 3 and 4, which are 
higher magnification images of regions from the same area shown in Figure 2. Figure 3(a) is a 
lattice image of a Ta region surrounded on the top and bottom by the Cu matrix. The broad, dark 
bands along the Ta region are again moiré fringes caused by the interaction of the Cu and Ta 
diffraction spots. The Ta and Cu lattices are identically aligned and the Ta:Cu interphase boundary 
is partially coherent, consisting of a[100] misfit dislocations spaced every 5-6 lattice planes. The 
model of this interface, with the locations of the misfit dislocations identified, is shown in figure 
3(b). It is believed that the partially coherent Ta:Cu interphase boundary, along with the volume

Two important questions, that can be only partially answered at this point, are how and why 
the FCC Ta particles form. It is believed that the Ta particles form as FCC because they nucleate 
and grow epitaxially on the Cu matrix at locations where statistically there is a sufficiently high Ta 
content. In some cases, this occurs during the deposition process when there is sufficient energy in 
the growing surface to allow some diffusion and clustering of Ta. In other cases it appears that Ta 
particles form through a segregation and amorphization reaction. 
 Figure 4 shows an example of amorphous regions that are found throughout the vacuum hot-
pressed Cu 94 Ta 6 sample annealed at 900 C for 1 hour. Following the work of Gong et al. [8], when 
Ta segregation reaches a concentration of about 30 atomic percent, these amorphous regions form. 
With continuing partitioning of the Cu and Ta, regions of essentially pure Ta are created. These 
regions then nucleate expitaxially on the FCC cyrstalline Cu lattice, thus establishing the observed 
orientation relationship between the FCC Ta particles and the Cu matrix. Ta particle nucleation 
within the amorphous regions was observed. In the lower left region of the amorphous region in 
Figure 4, 1-2 nm sized Ta particles can be seen.
