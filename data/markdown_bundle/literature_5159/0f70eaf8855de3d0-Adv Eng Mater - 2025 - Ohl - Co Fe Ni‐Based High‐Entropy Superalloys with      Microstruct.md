1. Introduction
Gas turbine blades and disks, w hich operate at high tempera-
ture, at stress, and in corrosive environments, have long relied
on superalloys based on nickel, iron, and/or cobalt [1] to provide
necessary creep resistance. Nickel-based superalloys have seen
much wider use than Co- or Fe-based superalloys because their
γ/γ 0 microstructure (face-centered cubic matrix with coherent
L12 precipitates) impedes dislocation motion —especially at
high temperature —due to dislocation interactions such as
Kear– Wilsdorf locking. [2– 5] However, the melting points of
Co and Fe are higher than Ni (by 40 and 83 K, respectively),
so Co- or Fe-based superalloys with a γ/γ 0 microstructure
may achieve solidus and liquidus temper-
atures higher than Ni-based superalloys.
In Co-based superalloys with γ/γ 0 micro-
structure, [6,7] the γ 0 phase is based on var-
iations of the following L1 2 phases: Co 3Ti,
Co3(Al,W), Co 3(Nb,Mo), Co 3(Al,V),
Co3(Ti,V), Co 3(Nb,V), or Co 3(Ta,V).[8– 14]
Although these γ 0 compositions have
largely been consider ed distinct subfami-
lies of Co-based superalloys, we hypothe-
size that these subfamilies, together
with L1 2-strengthened high-entropy alloys
(HEA),[15,16] a l lb e l o n gt ot h es a m el a r g e r

Iron-based γ 0-strengthened superalloys,
in addition to the melting point advantage
of Fe compared to Co and Ni, bene ﬁtf r o m
signiﬁcantly reduced cost and supply–chain
challenges.[17] However, Fe is known to
destabilize the γ/γ 0 phase ﬁeld. Commercial
Ni-based superalloys may have high Fe concentrations, most nota-
bly IN718, which contains 19 wt% Fe and is strengthened by γ 0
and γ 00 precipitates;[18–20] however, these alloys have relatively
low γ 0 volume fractions because Fe partitions strongly to the
γ matrix. In most Co-based superalloys, Fe has even more detri-
mental effects, reducing solvus temperature and frequently desta-
bilizing the γ/γ 0 microstructure. [21– 27] Even most so-called
“high-entropy superalloys ” (HESA) typically limit Fe concentra-
tion to below 12 at%. [28– 30] Nevertheless, in our recent work, we
added 18 at% Fe to an existing W-free Co-based superalloy [31,32]
and maintained a γ/γ 0 microstructure after aging for 1000 h at
850 °C. However, additional deleterious phases also precipi-
tated, indicating that this alloy requires further modi ﬁcations
to achieve high γ 0 fractions with no further undesirable phases.
Here, we develop six new Co – Fe– Ni-based superalloys to further
explore this new phase space and demonstrate the possibility of
an equiatomic HESA exhibiting a stable γ/γ 0 microstructure.

2.2. Materials and Experimental Procedures
The six new alloys listed in Table 1 were arc-melted in Ar as
button-shaped ingots (/C25 15 g) using pure elements: Co (99.9þ%),
Ni (99.95%), Al (99.5%), Cr (99.995%), V (99.7%), Ti (99.95%),
Nb (99.8%), Ta (99.95%), and B (95 –97%), purchased from Alfa
Aesar (Tewksbury, MA). The alloys were melted six times, with
a ﬂip between each of remelting to improve composition homoge-
neity. These ingots were encapsulated in evacuated fused silica
tubes, homogenized for 24 h at 1200 °C, and water quenched. Ingot
sections were aged in evacuated fused silica tubes at 850 °C for
durations between 24 and 1000 h, followed by water quenching.
Alloy compositions in Table 1 were veri ﬁed by EDS using pol-
ished cross sections of homogenized specimens in an FEI
Quanta 650 scanning electron microscope (SEM). All aged
specimens were investigated with secondary-electron (SE) and
backscatter-electron (BSE) detectors. Specimens for SEM were
mounted, polished down to 1 μm diamond suspension, and
etched with Carapella ’s reagent.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Transformation Temperatures
Solvus, solidus, and liquidus values for the six alloys are shown in
Figure 1 . Raw DSC data is shown in Figure S1, Supporting
Information. The solvus temperature varies signiﬁcantly, although
the liquidus temperature is nearly the same for all alloys
(1388– 1423 °C). The alloy with the highest solvus temperature
(997 °C) is 30Ni, consistent with this alloy having the highest
amount of refractory elements (1.5% Nb þ 1.5% Ta).
The alloy with the next-highest solvus temperature (928 °C) is
VTiTa, consistent with its second-highest concentration of refrac-
tory elements (1% Ta). Alloys VVV and VTiAl, with no refractory
element additions, both show solvus values below 850 °C;
thus, they do not precipitate γ 0 with the standard 850 °C aging
treatment. However, going from VTiAl to CCA, which changes
γ-forming ratios from 50Co – 18Fe– 12Ni to 26Co –26Fe–26Ni,
improves the solvus temperature by /C25 50 °C, allowing CCA to
be aged at 850 °C. Going from CCA to HEA, which involves
rebalancing γ 0 formers from 4V – 3Ti– 6Al to 4.3V –4.3Ti–4.3Al,
increases the solvus temperature by an additional /C25 20 °C. This
trend—provided that the γ/γ 0 microstructure is not destabilized—
is expected as the concentration of the lowest-melting element (Al)
is decreased.
Figure 2 shows the solvus temperatures of our six alloys
and literature alloys as a function of Fe concentration. For
W-containing literature alloys, there is a clear trend that substitut-
ing Fe for Co reduces the solvus temperature. For our W-free
alloys, the trend is not as clear. Comparing our 30Ni to previous
0Fe8Cr (Co–30Ni– 8Cr– 5Al– 3V– 2Ti– 1.5Nb–1.5Ta, Table 1)[31] with
a solvus temperature of 1031 °C, it is apparent that substituting
18% Fe for Co drops the solvus by /C25 30 °C. However, comparing
30Ni to 18Fe8Cr (Co –12Ni–18Fe–8Cr– 5Al– 3V– 2Ti–1.5Nb– 1.5Ta,
Table 1), [32] with the same Fe concentration (18%), shows that
increasing the Ni concentration from 12% to 30% by Co substitu-
tion reduces the solvus temperature strongly (by >200 °C). Thus,
the effect of adding Fe to Ni – Co-based superalloys seems to
depend on whether Fe is replacing Ni or Co.
Figure 3 plots the solvus temperature as a function of the
Fe/(Fe þ Co) ratio. The solvus stays nearly unchanged from
0Fe4Cr to 30Ni, as the Fe:Co ratio increases strongly, whereas
it drops markedly from 30Ni to HEA and CCA. However, HEA
and CCA have γ 0 formers which are different from 30Ni, so this
drop is unlikely to be caused by the Fe:Co ratio alone. In our pre-
vious alloys (with red circle symbols in Figure 3 [3]), a consistent
increase in solvus temperature is apparent with increasing
Fe/(Co þ Fe) ratio. We previously claimed that doubling the Cr
content from 4 to 8 at% (substituting for Co) led to a small increase
in solvus temperature due to the higher melting point of Cr.
However, Figure 3 seems to indicate that the solvus temperature
for these alloys linearly increases with Fe/(Co þ Fe).
In Figure 4 , we plot the solvus temperature against the
Fe/(Ni þ Fe) ratio. No clear trend is visible, unlike Figure 3.

3.2. Microstructure
The microstructures of the HEA, CCA, 30Ni, and VTiTa alloys,
after 1000 h aging at 850 °C, are shown in Figure 6– 9. Alloys VVV
and VTiAl are not depicted in the main text, as they have a single
γ phase at 850 °C.
Figure 6 shows the grain- and grain-boundary microstructures
of aged HEA. The γ 0 volume fraction is /C25 40%. The alloy is free of
additional precipitates, even at the grain boundaries. Based on
the “rounded cuboidal ” morphology of the precipitates, we esti-
mate lattice mis ﬁt to be between 0.25 and 0.5%. [41] This small
misﬁt is difﬁcult to detect in laboratory XRD, so it should be mea-
sured with synchrotron X-rays or neutron diffraction. The latter
technique is preferable, as Co high absorption of neutrons typi-
cally allows for superlattice peak visibility, which is often below
the noise level of X-rays in similar alloys. [33] Superlattice peak
identiﬁcation is necessary for verifying whether the mis ﬁt is pos-
itive or negative; given that Ni-based superalloys typically have
negative misﬁt, while Co-based superalloys typically have positive
misﬁt, the mis ﬁt sign of HEA (equimolar Co, Ni, and Fe) is not
obvious. Alternatively, the orientation of γ 0 precipitates rafted
under uniaxial stress (compression or tension) can reveal the
sign of the mis ﬁt.[42]
Figure 7 shows aged CCA with γ 0 precipitates which are spher-
ical and not aligned in any crystallographic direction. This is
indicative of either a very high misﬁt (to the point of incoherency)
or a very low mis ﬁt. The latter hypothesis is more likely, as the γ 0
precipitates have coarsened slowly and not merged with neigh-
bors. We expect the mis ﬁt to be between /C0 0.2% and 0.2%, given
the known trend of Fe reducing mis ﬁt when substituted for Co,
and the morphological similarity between these γ 0 precipitates
and those of other low-mis ﬁt alloys. [27]

4. Conclusions and Future Work
A continuous γ/γ 0 phase region extends from a W-free, Fe-free Co-
based superalloy (Co –10Ni– 8Cr– 5Al–3V–2Ti–1.5Nb– 1.5Ta, at%,
from ref. [31]) to a Fe-alloyed Co – Ni superalloys (Co –30Ni–
18Fe– 8Cr– 5Al–3V–2Ti–1.5Nb– 1.5Ta, labeled “30Ni”, Table 1),
to a “high-entropy”, multi-principal-element alloy with equiatomic
γ-a n d γ 0-former concentrations but no Cr (i.e., (Co 0.33Fe0.33
Ni0.33)87(Al0.33Ti0.33V0.33)13, labeled “HEA”,T a b l e1 ) .AC o–Fe– Ni
superalloy with equiatomic γ formers, non-equiatomic
γ 0-formers, and 8% Cr (labeled “CCA”, Table 1) shows additional
precipitation of intermetallic phases: future work will show if this
is due to the Cr addition destabilizing the γ 0 phase or to the “high
entropy effect” stabilizing the γ matrix. Higher Ni:Co ratios are
found to improve γ 0-phase stability; thus, future γ/γ 0 alloys may
be able to tolerate small additions of refractory Nb and Ta
(expected to improve the solvus temperature and coarsening resis-
tance) without precipitation of additional undesirable phases.
As this study has demonstrated the long-term stability of equia-
tomic “high entropy ” γ 0-strengthened alloys, future work is
planned to re ﬁne the microstructure further by 1) increasing γ 0
volume fraction and 2) reducing or eliminating undesired inter-
metallic phases. In particular, we will tune the Ti/Al ratios and
study additional γ 0 formers such as Mo. Additionally, we plan
to study the role of Cr in greater detail.
Modifying from the HEA composition, we plan to follow up
with[47] 1) Al:Ti ratios changing from 1:1 to 2:1 and 1:2, 2) Cr addi-
tions of 4, 8, and 22 at%, substituting for γ formers (Co, Ni, Fe),
and 3) increasing γ 0 formers (Al, Ti, V) to (Al, Ti, V, Nb, Ta, Mo).
Additionally, the low freezing range found by DSC suggests
that these alloys may be prime candidates for additive manufactur-
ing, so we are preparing an additively manufactured version of
alloy HEA.[48]
