ABSTRACT 
Ductilization and suppression of annealing-induced embrittlement have become the critical premises for various 
structural applications of high-strength tungsten materials but have yielded few practical advances. Here, we 
successfully fabricated W – Re and nano-particle doped W-Re-ZrC alloys with a combination of desirable ductility 
and a high tensile strength above 1.3 GPa by powder metallurgy plus hot swaging. Specifically, the W – Re and W- 
Re-ZrC alloys exhibit total elongations of 31.6% and 16.1%, respectively, at room temperature. Upon high- 
temperature annealing at 1600 
◦
C, the W-Re-ZrC alloy still retains a high total elongation of 12.9% and a 
high tensile strength of over 1.3 GPa, unlike the W – Re alloy, which exhibits substantial losses of ductility and 
strength. Microstructure analysis reveals that a large amount of low-angle grain boundaries and high-density 
mobile dislocations are responsible for ductilization. Meanwhile, ZrC nano-particles stabilize low-angle grain 
boundaries and dislocation structures at elevated temperatures, suppressing annealing-induced embrittlement in 
the W-Re-ZrC alloy. The present work provides a promising methodology for manufacturing bulk high-strength 
tungsten materials with favorable room-temperature ductility and high resistance to catastrophic failure during 
long-term service. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten materials have received extensive attention for a set of 
excellent properties, such as outstanding mechanical properties at 
elevated temperatures, high melting points, and high thermal conduc -
tivities, and are now regarded as promising candidates for use in heating 
resistant components in nuclear devices, tools in friction stir welding, 
kinetic energy penetrators, etc. [ 1 – 5 ]. However, high-strength tungsten 
materials are prone to suffering from poor room-temperature ductility 
and catastrophic fracture [ 6 – 8 ], which darkens the outlook for their 
engineering applications. The ductilization of tungsten materials is 
highly desired for advanced industry and has become a long-standing 
pursuit for material scientists. 
Re alloying is a practical pathway to enhance the ductility of tung -
sten materials because of the modification of the dislocation core 
structure and the improved mobility of screw dislocations at low tem -
peratures [ 9 , 10 ]. For instance, the fracture toughness of W – Re alloys 
increases with increasing Re content [ 11 , 12 ], indicating that W – Re al -
loys are more ductile than the un-alloyed tungsten. Re addition alone, 
however, cannot render desirable ductility for tungsten materials 
because most as-melted or as-sintered W – Re alloys still show 
room-temperature brittleness. Thermomechanical processing, thus, has 
been employed to make tungsten materials more ductile. As reported in 
prior works, various thermomechanical processing techniques, e.g., 
rolling [ 13 – 16 ], swaging [ 17 ], forging [ 18 ], high-pressure torsion [ 19 ], 
and equal-channel angular pressing [ 20 , 21 ], can enhance tungsten 
material ductility to different degrees. As an example, low-temperature 
strength-ductility synergy can be achieved in bulk pure tungsten after 
high-energy-rate forging [ 22 ]. Likely, rolling gives rise to a tensile 
elongation of up to 10.0% and a tensile strength as high as 1.6 GPa in the 
electron-beam melted W-24Re alloy [ 23 ]. After proper thermo -
mechanical processing, high-strength W – Re alloys with acceptable 
ductility can withstand strong mechanical and thermal shocks and have 
become the primary materials suitable for the manufacture of tools in

Doping nano-particles that can pin grain boundary migration is a 
feasible strategy for increasing the recrystallization temperature and 
overcoming the annealing-induced brittleness at higher temperatures

ZrC, and HfC, the ZrC nano-particle has been proven to be an effective 
strengthening and stabilizing phase owing to its high melting point

W-Re-ZrC alloy shows a stable microstructure after heating over 1500

as verified by the finding that the particle-doped W – Re alloys have a 
total tensile elongation of less than 5.0% [ 32 ]. Thus, achieving a 
favorable balance between room-temperature ductility and resistance to 
annealing-induced embrittlement has become an inescapable hindrance 
to expanding tungsten material applications to advanced industry. 
In this work, we exploit the superposition of the different contribu -
tions of Re alloying, swaging, and ZrC nano-particle doping to tailor the 
combination of ductility and resistance to annealing-induced embrit -
tlement for tungsten materials simultaneously. The W – Re and W-Re-ZrC 
alloys show the desired tensile ductility and high tensile strength at 
room temperature. Furthermore, the dispersion of ZrC nano-particles 
endows better microstructure stability at high temperatures of up to 
1600 
◦
C, leading to the retention of acceptable ductility in the annealed 
W-Re-ZrC alloy. On these grounds, the ductilization mechanisms of the 
W – Re and W-Re-ZrC alloys without and with annealing are discussed. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Material preparation 
Here we have successfully fabricated the W-25 wt% Re (WRE) and 
W-25 wt% Re-0.3 wt% ZrC (WRZ) alloys via ball milling, sintering, and 
swaging in succession. Pure W powders (~600 nm, purity > 99.0%) and 
Re powders (~45 μ m, purity > 99.5%) were obtained from Xiamen 
Tungsten Co., Ltd, while ZrC powders (~50 nm, purity > 99.0%) were 
purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co., Ltd. Milling of the mixed pow -
ders were carried out in a standard planetary ball mill (WXQM-4L, 
Changsha Tianchuang Powder Technology Co., Ltd., China) with WC/Co 
balls and vials for 4 h. The interior volume of the WC/Co container is 
500 ml, and the diameter of the ball is 8 mm. To avoid oxidation, the 
powder was sealed in the container under an argon-hydrogen mixture 
atmosphere (Ar: H
2 
= 9 : 1, oxygen < 0.1 ppm, H
2
O < 0.1 ppm). The ball- 
to-powder weight ratio is 8:1, and the rotation speed is 240 rpm. After 
milling, the powder compact was cold-pressed at 150 MPa for 2 h. The 
powder compact was sintered under an H
2 
atmosphere at 2200 
◦
C for 8 
h. Note that the sintering temperature used here is in line with prior 
works to obtain dense tungsten materials [ 13 ]. Apart from the slight 
oxidation of ZrC nanoparticles, the majority of ZrC particles are stable at 
this temperature, and can be preserved after sintering at 2200 
◦
C. The 
sintered tungsten alloy rods with a diameter of approximately 22 mm 
were subsequently processed with rotary swaging in the temperature

◦

the normal strain is ~1.6). To evaluate the resistance to 
annealing-induced embrittlement, the swaged WRE and WRZ alloys

◦
C are 
denoted as A15-WRE, A16-WRE, A15-WRZ, and A16-WRZ.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Synergy of the ductilization and the resistance to annealing-induced 
embrittlement 
Making strong tungsten materials ductile is highly desirable; how -
ever, most existing tungsten materials generally suffer from limited 
ductility. Fig. 6 a summarizes the tensile strengths and the total elon -
gations of various W – Re alloys [ 7 , 12 , 23 , 25 , 32 , 35 ]. It can be seen that 
most cases reported in the literature fall under a compromising tensile 
ductility; that is, the total elongation is less than 10.0% (the gray zone in 
Fig. 6 a). For instance, the W-24.5Re-2HfC alloy with a high tensile 
strength of 1413 MPa exhibits a total elongation of merely 6.3% [ 32 ]. To 
the best of our knowledge, only two particular cases, the rolled W-24Re 
[ 23 ] and K-doped W-3Re [ 7 ] alloys, show total elongations of ~10.5% 
and ~19.1%, respectively. Nevertheless, the tensile strength of the 
K-doped W-3Re is 1.2 GPa, which is only at a lower level in tungsten 
materials. The ductilization of high-strength tungsten alloys, thus, has 
only been achieved to a limited extent, and the tensile ductility remains 
to be improved so far. 
In contrast, our WRE and WRZ alloys possess exceptional room- 
temperature ductility. Specifically, the WRE alloy with a tensile 
strength of ~1.3 GPa displays a total elongation as high as ~31.6%, 
which is 1.7 times that of the K-doped W-3Re alloy (where total elon -
gation ~ 19.1%) with a lower strength. With added nano-particles, the 
WRZ alloy still shows a total elongation of ~16.1% on the premise of 
superior tensile strength of ~1.5 GPa; its ductility is better than those of 
most W – Re alloys. It is, therefore, reasonable to believe that our WRE 
and WRZ alloys jump out of the gray region toward the upper right 
corner (i.e., the red region in Fig. 6 a); that is to say, our WRE and WRZ 
alloys outperform the majority of previously reported W-Re-based alloys 
in terms of room-temperature ductility, showing the success in 
ductilization. 
Beating the brittleness induced by annealing is another urgent task 
for prolonging the service lives of tungsten materials under harsh con -
ditions. Unfortunately, most tungsten materials [ 11 , 25 , 26 , 36 ] lose 
room-temperature ductility upon heating at elevated temperatures, as 
displayed in Fig. 6 b (the gray region). As an example, the rolled 
K – W-3Re alloy is brittle at room temperature after annealing at 1100 
◦
C 
[ 25 ]. Even the swaged W-25Re [ 11 ], as the special case, exhibits a low 
tensile ductility of only 4% after annealing. In contrast, our WRE and 
WRZ alloys overcome the brittleness induced by annealing, showing 
high resistance to annealing-induced embrittlement. Especially for the 
WRZ alloy, the tensile elongations are over 10% and 20%, respectively, 
after annealing at 1600 
◦
C and 1500 
◦
C; hence it outperforms traditional 
W-Re-based alloys.

temperature ductility and resistance to annealing-induced embrittle -
ment in our WRE and WRZ alloys, the effects of LAGBs, dislocation

4.2. Role of LAGBs 
One beneficial consequence of deformation (e.g., swaging) is the 
generation of LAGBs in metals. As shown in Fig. 7 a, the number fractions 
of LAGBs in the as-swaged WRE and WRZ alloys are up to ~40% and 
~45%, respectively. Compared with high-angle grain boundaries 
(HAGBs), LAGBs are more effective for transferring plastic strain and 
accommodating deformation in tungsten materials due to the reduced 
requirements for independent slip systems [ 10 , 37 , 38 ]. As verified by the 
Kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps of the zone in the vicinity of 
the fracture surface ( Fig. 7 b and c), our as-swaged WRE and WRZ alloys 
show relatively homogeneous strain distributions under tension. The 
uniform strain distribution is ascribed to the better strain compatibility 
between neighboring grains of LAGBs and their ability to inhibit strain 
localization and crack initiation at GBs [ 10 ], avoiding the brittle inter -
granular fracture that occurs in traditional tungsten materials (see 
Fig. 5 a and b). As a result, high-density LAGBs are of benefit to sup -
pressing plastic instability, enabling desirable tensile ductility at room 
temperature. 
High-temperature heating tends to cause recovery and annihilation 
of defects in metals. For the WRE alloy, the number fraction of LAGBs 
significantly decreases after annealing ( Fig. 7 a). Strain localization thus 
occurs at HAGBs of recrystallized equiaxed grains of the A15-WRE 
specimen (as Fig. 7 d). Strain localization at HAGBs gives rise to a sig -
nificant strain incompatibility and a brittle fracture fashion, which is 
responsible for the marked reduction in tensile elongation of the 
annealed WRE alloys. In contrast, a large number of LAGBs can be 
preserved in the WRZ alloy. The number fraction of LAGBs even slightly 
increases after annealing at 1500 
◦
C for the WRZ alloy. As a result, the 
plastic strain still shows a uniform distribution in the A15-WRZ spec -
imen ( Fig. 7 e), corresponding to the increased ductility. Even after 
annealing at 1600 
◦
C, the number fraction of LAGBs is over 30%, 
allowing for the acceptable room-temperature ductility in the A16-WRZ 
specimen. Therefore, introducing an adequate amount of LAGBs is of 
great importance for the ductilization of tungsten materials, and the 
conservation of LAGBs at elevated temperatures is vital for increasing 
the resistance to annealing-induced embrittlement. 
4.3. Influence of the dislocation structure 
Screw dislocations with limited mobility at low temperatures govern 
the plastic deformation of tungsten materials, causing room- 
temperature brittleness [ 10 , 39 ]. In light of this finding, modifying the 
dislocation structure and increasing the density of dislocations (e.g., 
dislocations with edge and mixed character) with higher mobility is a 
promising way to improve ductility. Extensive works have revealed that 
a modified dislocation structure and increased dislocations can be ach -
ieved in metals via various deformation processes. For instance,

also leads to the generation of a high density of mobile dislocations in

mobile dislocations with edge and mixed character, plasticity can be 
readily carried and accommodated in the as-swaged WRE and WRZ al -

Upon annealing at elevated temperatures, dislocations rearrange and 
annihilate as recovery and recrystallization proceed. As shown in the 
TEM images of the A15-WRE specimen ( Fig. 8 c), the number of dislo -
cations significantly decreases, and the dislocations rearrange to form a 
network. In contrast, the A15-WRZ specimen shows a dislocation 
structure similar to that of the as-swaged counterpart ( Fig. 8 d). As a 
result, the reduced number of mobile dislocations available to accom -
modate deformation causes a significant loss of ductility in the A15-WRE 
specimen, while the preserved high density of dislocations can still play 
a role in sustaining plasticity in the A15-WRZ specimen. With a further 
increase in the annealing temperature, the sharply decreased tensile 
elongation is associated with extinct dislocations in the A16-WRE 
specimen ( Fig. 8 e), while the acceptable tensile ductility is related to 
the remaining dislocations in the A16-WRZ specimen ( Fig. 8 f). Thus, a 
high density of mobile dislocations promotes the ductilization of our 
WRE and WRZ alloys, and the retention of dislocations at high tem -
peratures has a positive impact on the suppression of annealing-induced 
embrittlement. 
4.4. Effect of nano-particles 
Adding nano-particles can effectively strengthen tungsten materials, 
in accordance with the result that the WRZ alloys are stronger than the 
WRE alloys under various conditions ( Fig. 4 ). In general, particles can 
act as stress concentrators and have a detrimental effect on ductility 
[ 41 ]. For the as-swaged WRZ alloy, apart from the majority of 
nano-particles that disperse within the grain interior, there are a small 
number of nano-particles at the GBs ( Fig. 8 b). The nano-particles located 
at GBs cause stress concentrations during straining, which accounts for 
the as-swaged WRZ alloy underperforming the as-swaged WRE alloy in 
terms of tensile ductility ( Fig. 4 ). In addition, nano-particles have a 
slight tendency to agglomerate ( Fig. 8 b), which should also be 
responsible for the reduced ductility of the WRZ specimen in part. 
Nevertheless, nano-particles are also capable of bringing about favor -
able results. First, ZrC nanoparticles can react with oxygen and inhibit 
oxygen segregation at the GBs, as shown in the element mappings 
( Fig. 9 a – d). The suppression of impurity segregation can strengthen GBs 
[ 13 ], which is beneficial for enhancing the ductility of tungsten mate -
rials. Second, the ZrC nano-particles with an average diameter of 
approximately 85 nm ( Fig. 9 e) tend to form a semi-coherent or coherent 
interface with the W matrix ( Fig. 9 f), in line with prior works [ 13 , 42 ]. 
The perfect ZrC/W interface is strong enough to evade the degradation 
of mechanical properties caused by weak particle/matrix interfaces 
[ 43 – 45 ]. Moreover, the semi-coherent or coherent ZrC/W interface can 
give rise to an improved strength without excessively sacrificing 
ductility [ 13 , 46 ], reminiscent of the favorable tensile properties of the 
WRZ specimen. Third, nano-particles can exert a pinning effect on the 
migration of dislocations ( Fig. 8 d and 8 f), leading to enhanced micro -
structure stability at elevated temperatures. The superior thermal sta -
bility enables the retention of LAGBs and mobile dislocations during 
annealing, ensuring a higher resistance to annealing-induced embrit -
tlement for the WRZ alloy. 
It should be pointed out that the present work is only a starting point. 
The swaging processing and the distribution of nano-particles can be 
further optimized to achieve a better balance between the ductilization 
and the resistance to annealing-induced embrittlement of high-strength 
tungsten materials. For example, suppose all the ZrC nano-particles can 
be refined to a size of less than 10 nm and homogeneously dispersed 
within the grain interior. In that case, the ductility of the WRZ alloy is 
expected to be further improved without compromising the high resis -
tance to annealing-induced embrittlement. As such, we believe that our 
work provides a promising methodology for fabricating strong tungsten 
materials with a good combination of exceptional ductility and high 
tolerance to brittleness caused by annealing. 
5. Conclusions 
In this work, W – Re (WRE) and ZrC nano-particle-strengthened W – Re 
(WRZ) alloys have been successfully fabricated via powder metallurgy 
and swaging. Based on the experimental results of room-temperature 
tensile performance, the following main conclusions are obtained:

1. The as-swaged WRE and WRZ alloys each have a typical deformed

2. The as-swaged WRE alloy with a tensile strength of ~1.3 GPa pos -

nano-particles, the tensile ductility decreases to ~16.1% as the 
tensile strength increases to ~1.5 GPa in the WRZ alloy. Upon 
annealing, there is a significant loss of ductility in the WRE alloy, 
while the WRZ alloy can maintain an acceptable tensile ductility of 
~20%. 
3. The generation of LAGBs and mobile dislocations can transfer strain 
and accommodate strain incompatibility more effectively, leading to 
the ductilization of the as-swaged WRE and WRZ alloys. 
4. The retention of LAGBs and mobile dislocations at elevated tem -
peratures stem from the pinning effect provided by nano-particles, 
resulting in higher resistance to annealing-induced embrittlement 
in the WRZ alloy. 
5. This work provides a promising methodology for producing high- 
strength tungsten materials with the synergy of room-temperature 
ductilization and tolerance to embrittlement induced by annealing, 
extending their engineering applications.
