abstract
In this study, Ta was added to an L1 2-type Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy at different levels and into different substitution
sites, substituting for either Ni, Ti or Si. The solubility limits of Ta in the L1 2 phase were 1.9 at%, 5.7 at%
and 1.0 at% when Ta substituted for Ni, Ti and Si, respectively. The lattice parameters in the L1 2 phase
region increased in the order of the Ta(Ni) 4 Ta(Si)4Ta(Ti) quaternary alloys, in which Ta substituted for
Ni, Si and Ti, respectively. The room-temperature hardness in the L1 2 phase region increased linearly
with increasing Ta content, and the increment rate increased in the order of the Ta(Ni) 4Ta(Si)4Ta(Ti)
quaternary alloys. Similarly, the room-temperature 0.2% proof stress as well as the tensile strength in the
L12 phase region increased linearly with increasing Ta content, and the increment rate increased in the
order of the Ta(Ni) 4Ta(Si)4 Ta(Ti) quaternary alloys. High tensile elongation was observed at room
temperature when the microstructures remain in the L1 2 single phase. At high temperatures, a positive
temperature dependence of the hardness as well as the ﬂow strength was observed in the quaternary
alloys. It was also shown that the wear resistance of the quaternary Ta(Ti) alloys was improved and
attributed to plastically induced hardening of the worn surfaces combined with the positive temperature
dependence of the ﬂow strength. The strengthening and hardening resulting from Ta addition was
suggested to be due to the hardening of the solid solution arising from the mis ﬁts in the atomic radius
between Ta and the constituent atoms Ni, Ti or Si.

1. Introduction
L12-type Ni 3Si compounds alloyed with Ti, i.e., Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys
whose constituent elements, Si and Ti, are present in almost equi-
atomic proportions [1], have speci ﬁc mechanical and chemical
properties and are candidates for use in high-temperature struc-
tural or chemical components. For example, the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy
exhibits an increase in ﬂow strength with increasing temperature
[2–4] (a so-called strength anomaly [5,6]), accompanied by high
tensile ductility over a wide range of temperatures [3]. It also
displays excellent corrosion resistance in sodium chloride [7] and
acidic [8] solutions and a superior oxidation resistance in air at
elevated temperatures [9,10]. The high strength or hardness of the
Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy at ambient and intermediate temperatures has
been attributed to the maximization of the solid-solution
strengthening (or hardening) stemming from a mutual solid
solution of Si and Ti [2–4] in addition to the strength anomaly.
Additionally, the high tensile ductility of the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy has
been suggested to result from the intrinsically high grain boundary
cohesion [1 1,12].
Further improvement of the mechanical properties of the
Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy without sacri ﬁcing other properties, such as che-
mical and physical properties, has been attempted by the addition
(at a wt ppm level) of the interstitial atoms B [3,13],C [14] and Be
[14] and the addition (at 1 –2 at%, i.e., below the solid solubility
limit in Ni 3(Si,Ti)) of the transition metals Cr [15,16],M n [15],F e
[15],C o [16],N b [15],M o [16],V [17] and Hf [15,18]. Recently, the
effect of the refractory elements (at 2 at%) Ta [18–20],W [18] and
Re [20] has also been investigated by the present author's group.
Based on the knowledge obtained from these quaternary alloys,
some alloys applicable to wear-resistant materials, corrosion-
resistant materials and high-temperature structural materials
were developed by the present author's group [21]. For example,
Ta-added Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys have been shown to be applicable to
ball-bearing materials functioning under high temperatures and in
corrosive solution and to tools for the friction stir welding (FSW)
of hard materials, such as steel or Ti alloys with a high melting
point and high hardness [21].

have the highest solid solubility in the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy [22],a sT i
had the highest solid solubility in the binary Ni 3Si compound
[1,17]. Therefore, a signi ﬁcant improvement in the mechanical
properties, especially the strength and hardness, could be
achieved by the addition of Ta. However, previous studies were

In this study, Ta was added into the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy in different
content levels and into different substitution sites, namely, the
Ni-, Ti- or Si-sites. The quaternary Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys were arc-melted
and annealed. The microstructures of the quaternary Ni 3(Si,Ti)
alloys were characterized, and their mechanical properties were
evaluated by high-temperature hardness, high-temperature ten-
sile and room-temperature wear testing. It was found that the
microstructure and mechanical properties of the quaternary
Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy were strongly dependent on the Ta substitution
method. In particular, highly hardened (strengthened) alloys were
found in the quaternary Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy in which Ta substituted for
the Ni-site.
2. Experimental procedures
Table 1 lists the chemical compositions of the alloys prepared
in this study. The chemical composition of the base alloy is
Ni79.5Si11. 0Ti9.5 (at%). The prepared alloys are divided into three
groups: in the ﬁrst, second and third groups, Ta was added to the
base alloy in three different ways such that Ta substitutes for Ni, Ti
and Si, respectively. Here, the letters in the parentheses next to the
alloy symbols indicate the substitution sites of Ta, i.e., whether Ta
substitutes for Ni, Ti or Si. Additionally, the subscripted numbers
preceded by the alloy symbols indicate the composition
(expressed by at%) of Ta added to the alloys. In each group, Ta
was added to the base alloy in 1 at% increments. A trace amount of
boron (B) was added to all alloys to suppress the intergranular
fracture [3,13].
The base and quaternary alloys were prepared from starting
raw materials of 99.99 wt% Ni, 99.9999 wt% Si, 99.9 wt% Ti, 99.9
wt% Ta and 99.9 wt% B. The boron content was not included in the
total of 100 at%. Button ingots with a diameter of 50 mm and a
weight of approximately 130 g were prepared by arc melting the
raw pure metals, Ni, Si, Ti, Ta and B, in an argon gas atmosphere
using a non-consumable tungsten electrode on a copper hearth.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructures
Based on the SEM-EPMA results and the metallographical
observations, the solubility limits of Ta in the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy were
approximately 1.9 at%, 5.7 at% and 1.0 at% when Ta substitutes for
Ni, Ti and Si, respectively ( Table 2). Figs. 1–3 show the SEM images
of the microstructures of the alloys in which Ta substituted for Ni,
Ti and Si, correspondingly. In each ﬁgure, representative SEM
images of the alloy with the Ta content below and above the
solubility limit are shown. In the microstructures of the quaternary
alloys 4Ta(Ni), 7Ta(Ti) and 3Ta(Si) with Ta contents above their
solubility limits, particle-like, plate-like and plate-like second-
phase dispersions were observed, respectively. Figs. 4 –7 show
the X-ray diffraction patterns of the base alloy and quaternary
alloys in which Ta substituted for Ni, Ti and Si, respectively. In each
ﬁgure corresponding to the quaternary alloys ( Figs. 5 –7), repre-
sentative patterns of the alloys with Ta contents below and above
the solubility limit are shown. From the XRD results, the second-
phase dispersions observed in the quaternary alloys in which Ta
substituted for Ni, Ti and Si ( Figs. 1 –3) were identi ﬁed as Ni 3Ti,
Ni3Ta and Ni 3Ti, respectively. The EPMA results reveal that the
Ni3Ti second-phase dispersion in the Ta(Ni) alloy contained
approximately 6 at% Ti. Based on the XRD measurements, the
lattice parameters of the L1 2 phase increased linearly with
increasing Ta content regardless of the Ta substitution variant, as
shown in Fig. 8. The rate of the lattice parameter increment in the
L12 phase region increased in the order of Ta(Ni) 4Ta(Si)4Ta(Ti).

3.3. Tensile properties
Fig. 1 1 shows the changes in the room-temperature tensile
properties (0.2% proof stress, tensile strength and tensile elonga-
tion) with Ta content for three quaternary alloys (Ta(Ni), Ta(Ti) and
Ta(Si)) in which Ta substituted for Ni, Ti and Si, respectively. The
0.2% proof stress increased linearly with increasing Ta content in
the L1 2 phase range, similarly to the hardness behavior. The
strengthening rate in this Ta content range was higher in the Ta
(Ni) alloy than in the Ta(Si) and Ta(Ti) alloys, similarly to the
hardening rate behavior shown in Fig. 9. Table 2 summarizes these
values as the parameters de ﬁned by ds0.2/dc. In contrast, the
tensile strength increased linearly with increasing Ta content in
the Ta content range below the solubility limit but rapidly
decreased in the Ta content range above the solubility limit, unlike
the behaviors of the hardness and the 0.2% proof stress. For the
tensile elongation, high tensile elongation (exceeding 30%) was
observed for the quaternary alloys with an L1
2 single-phase
microstructure regardless of the Ta substitution variant. However,
once the solubility limit of Ta is exceeded, the tensile elongation
rapidly decreased to zero. Therefore, it should be emphasized that
the Ta(Ni) alloys in the L1 2 phase range exhibit relatively high
tensile elongation over a wide range of the Ta content despite the
steady increase in their ﬂow strength with increasing Ta content.
Figs. 12–14 show the SEM fractography of the three quaternary
alloys that were tensile-deformed at room temperature. In each
ﬁgure, representative SEM fractography images of the alloy with
Ta contents below and above the solubility limit are shown. It has
been repeatedly observed that the preferable fracture mode of the
base alloy Ni 3(Ni, Ti) at ambient temperature is ductile transgra-
nular fracture with dimple patterns [2–4]. The fracture mode of
the quaternary alloys in the L1 2 phase range (e.g., 5Ta(Ti) and 1Ta
(Si)) was ductile transgranular fracture ( Figs. 13 and 14), although
the mixture mode of the transgranular and intergranular fracture
dominated in the 1Ta(Ni) alloy ( Fig. 12 ). Thus, the high tensile
elongation observed in the quaternary alloys in the L1 2 phase
range appears to be associated with a large amount of energy
dissipated during the branching or lengthening processes of the
micro-cracks. On the other hand, the fracture mode of the
quaternary alloys with Ta contents above the solubility limit
(e.g., 4Ta(Ni), 7Ta(Ti) and 3Ta(Si)) was transgranular if viewed at
low magni ﬁcation. However, if viewed at high magni ﬁcation,
interfacial fracturing between the second-phase particles (or
plates) and the matrix was observed. This result indicates that
the interfacial cohesion between the second-phase particles (or
plates) and the matrix is not very strong, leading to low tensile
elongation values in these alloys.
High-temperature tensile testing was conducted for the Ta(Ti)
alloys with the highest solubility limit of Ta among the three
quaternary alloys. Fig. 15 shows the changes in the 0.2% proof
stress, tensile strength and tensile elongation with temperature for
the base and Ta(Ti) alloys. The Ta(Ti) alloys and the base alloy
showed the strength anomaly [5,6] for the 0.2% proof stress but
not for the tensile strength. The ﬂow strength (the 0.2% proof
stress as well as the tensile strength) increased by the addition of
Ta for all temperatures tested but was similar among the 4Ta(Ti),
5Ta(Ti) and 7Ta(Ti) alloys. On the other hand, the tensile elonga-
tion of the Ta(Ti) alloys in the L1 2 phase region decreased slightly
with increasing Ta content for all temperatures tested. The Ta(Ti)
alloy with a Ta content above the solubility limit resulted in a
signiﬁcant reduction in the tensile elongation. Fig. 16 shows the
SEM fractography of the 5Ta(Ti) and 7Ta(Ti) alloys that were
tensile-deformed at 500 1C. Comparing Fig. 16 with Fig. 13 , the
fractography of the 5Ta(Ti) alloy changed from ductile transgra-
nular fracture to brittle intergranular fracture, which is the
preferred fracture mode of the base alloy deformed at high
temperature [3]. This change is consistent with the change in
the tensile elongation, i.e., the decrease in the tensile elongation,
as observed in Fig. 15. 7Ta(Ti) exhibited a similar fractography to
that at room temperature: interfacial fracturing between the
second-phase particles (or plates) and the matrix was observed.
The second-phase dispersions were also detrimental to the high-
temperature tensile elongation.

3.4. Wear properties
Fig. 17 shows the wear resistance of the Ta(Ti) alloys relative to
the base alloy. The relative wear resistance was de ﬁned as the ratio
of the wear loss of the base alloy to that of the Ta(Ti) alloys. It is
apparent that the relative wear resistance increases with increas-
ing Ta content. The increase in the relative wear resistance is
steeper for Ta contents below the solubility limit than above the
solubility limit. The Vickers hardness before and after the wear
test was measured for the 3Ta(Ti) and 5Ta(Ti) alloys and is plotted
in Fig. 18. A signi ﬁcant wear-induced hardening was observed in
both alloys and appears to be associated with the improvement of
the wear resistance. Corresponding to the relative wear resistance
of the Ta(Ti) alloys, the top and side views shown in Fig. 19 clearly
indicate that the plastic ﬂow on the worn surface of the cylindrical
specimen was strongest in the base alloy, followed by the 4Ta(Ti)
alloy and the weakest in the 7Ta(Ti) alloy, consistent with the
behavior of the relative wear resistance shown in Fig. 17. Fig. 20
shows the SEM images of the worn surfaces of the base, 4Ta(Ti)
and 7Ta(Ti) alloys after the wear test. It appears that the worn
surfaces of the two Ta(Ti) alloys were smoother than that of the
base alloy, accompanied by fewer stuck debris particles along the
sliding direction. Therefore, the improvement of the wear resis-
tance by the Ta addition may be attributed to the signi ﬁcantly
enhanced plastic hardening on the worn surfaces as well as the
positive temperature dependence of the ﬂow strength (hardness).

previous literature, the solid-solution hardening behavior of the B
subgroup elements as well as the transition metals in an L1 2-type
Ni3Al compound in which the additive elements substituted for the Al-

ternary Ni3Al compound, in which Ta substitutes for the Al-site. Even
the “modulus effect” [32,33] and the “temperature effect” cannot
explain this difference, as the former effect is known to be relatively
small. The latter effect, relating to the strength anomaly, may affect the
ambient tensile stress. Generally speaking, the intermetallic alloy is
harder than (ordinary) metallic alloys, and the more complex the
crystal structure is, the harder the intermetallic alloy. In the case of the
Ta-added Ni3(Si,Ti) alloys with an L12 structure, the hardening effects
due to (1) a large mutual solid solution between Si and Ti and (2) Ta
addition as well as (3) the hardening effect due to the L1 2 crystal
structure appears to result in extremely high hardness values. Further
study is required to understand the solid-solution hardening mechan-
ism operating in the quaternary Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys and to explain the
difference in the degree of the solid-solution hardening between the
Ni3Al and Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy.

5. Conclusion
In this study, Ta was added into the L1 2-type Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy at
different levels and in different substitution roles, substituting for
the Ni-, Ti- or Si-site. The microstructures of the quaternary alloys
were characterized, and their mechanical properties were evalu-
ated by hardness, tensile and wear tests, focusing on the effect of
the substitution method. The following results were obtained from
the present study.
(1) The solubility limits of Ta in the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy were approxi-
mately 1.9 at%, 5.7 at% and 1.0 at% when Ta substitutes for Ni,
Ti and Si, respectively.
(2) The lattice parameters in the L1 2 phase region increased
linearly with increasing Ta content, and their increment rate
increased in the order of the Ta(Ni) 4Ta(Si)4 Ta(Ti) alloys, in
which Ta substituted for Ni, Si and Ti, respectively.
(3) The room-temperature and high-temperature hardness in the
L12 phase region increased linearly with increasing Ta content,
and their increment rate at room temperature increased in the
order of the Ta(Ni) 4 Ta(Si)4Ta(Ti) alloys.
(4) The room-temperature 0.2% proof stress as well as the tensile
strength in the L1 2 phase region increased linearly with
increasing Ta content, and their increment rate increased in
the order of the Ta(Ni) 4 Ta(Si)4Ta(Ti) alloys.
(5) High tensile elongation was observed at room temperature as
long as the microstructures remain in the L1 2 single phase.
However, once exceeding the solubility limit of Ta, the tensile
