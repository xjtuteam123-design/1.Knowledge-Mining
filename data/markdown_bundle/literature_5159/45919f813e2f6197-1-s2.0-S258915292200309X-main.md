ABSTRACT 
In this paper, we develop an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) approach to rapidly predict and explain the 
temperature-dependent yield strength (YS) trends of as-cast multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) based on an 
ensemble of support vector regression (eSVR) models. Post-hoc interpretability of trained eSVR models based on 
SHapley Additive exPlanations, k -means clustering and Ceteris-Paribus profile plots reveal trends that explain the 
variation in YS as a function of temperature, constituent elements, and phase. According to our XAI algorithm, 
MPEAs with the highest room temperature YS should occur in multi-phase refractory metal based six-component 
systems, whose microstructure is made up of secondary phase precipitates within the body-centered cubic (BCC) 
matrix. The propensity for secondary phase formation is promoted by the optimal concentrations of Al and/or Si 
alloying additions. Even in MPEAs that form in the face-centered cubic structure, secondary phases are identified 
to be important for higher YS at room temperature. We uncover two design criteria that favor high room- 
temperature YS: ( i ) Choose compositions with equiatomic or near-equiatomic concentrations and ( ii ) Choose 
alloying elements with an optimal number of p -electrons in the valence configuration. High temperature YS 
trends associated with both single-phase and multi-phase BCC-based MPEAs are dominated by the temperature 
descriptor, which obfuscates the interpretation of other input variables. 
1. Introduction 
Crystalline high entropy alloys (HEAs), first reported in 2004, have 
attracted a great deal of attention in the recent years [1,2] . HEAs 
comprise of at least five principal elements in equiatomic proportions 
and can be considered as a subset of the multi-principal element alloys 
(MPEAs) [3] . Both equiatomic and non-equiatomic MPEAs have been 
experimentally found to exhibit desirable properties. One of the key 
reasons behind the excitement in investigating MPEAs is the accessi -
bility to vast chemical and composition space that has enabled previ -
ously less explored alloy design strategies to tailor the multitude of 
properties for a targeted application [4 – 10] . The MPEAs have experi -
mentally shown to exhibit excellent properties such as high hardness, 
high yield strength (YS) at high temperatures, improved thermal sta -
bility without deleterious phase transformations, soft magnetic proper -
ties, and corrosion resistance [11 – 23] . While the single-phase FCC 
structures have reported excellent ductility and fracture toughness at 
both ambient and cryogenic temperatures, they suffer from low YS. On 
the other hand, single-phase BCC structures have excellent YS at high 
temperatures above 1300 K, but have low ductility [24 – 28] . Co-design 
of multiple properties via chemistry, composition and microstructure 
engineering is an active research topic [4,10,29 – 33] . 
Navigation of the vast MPEA search space based on trial-and-error 
experimental synthesis and characterization is not practical. As a 
result, there have been many computational studies focused on devel -
oping novel physics-inspired mechanistic models and data-driven ma -
chine learning (ML) methods to enable rapid prediction of the desired 
properties solely as a function of chemical composition. Although many 
of these approaches lack the fidelity of first principles calculations [34] 
and large-scale atomistic simulations [35] , they achieve the intended 
goal of rapidly down-selecting candidate compositions for targeted 
high-fidelity computations and/or experiments. Several analytical 
models exist for predicting the YS of MPEAs that are based on the solid 
solution strengthening mechanism [36 – 41] . For example, Varvenne

alloying element is a solute embedded in an effective solvent environ -

thermally-activated glide to predict the temperature-dependent YS of 
single-phase FCC and BCC MPEAs. Many of these models require inputs 
that can either come from first principles calculations, molecular dy -
namics, elemental descriptors, or experiments. Despite the meticulous 
details that have gone into developing the mechanistic models, they are 
limited to single-phase MPEAs. This is a short-coming because a vast 
majority of MPEA microstructures have multi-phases, secondary phases 
and/or precipitates [42] that may also contribute to the overall strength 
of the alloy across the temperature range. 
An alternative to mechanistic models is the data-driven ML approach 
to predict the mechanical properties of MPEAs. For instance, Rickman 
et al. constructed a regression model by combining canonical- 
correlation analysis and genetic algorithm to predict the hardness of 
MPEAs using 82 alloy compositions. The authors subsequently validated 
the predicted hardness by fabricating representative alloys. The trained 
models showed a prediction accuracy (estimated as the difference be -
tween predicted and measured hardness) ranging from 100 to 400 
Vicker ’ s hardness value [43] . In another work, Wen et al. trained several 
ML algorithms based on a data set that contained 186 as-cast single- -
phase FCC and BCC HEAs. The authors demonstrated that their trained 
ML models provided superior predictability for the hardness of 
single-phase HEAs as compared to existing physics-based models [44] . 
Another study by Xiong et al. reported construction of two random forest 
models using a training data set of 290 and 71 alloy compositions for the 
prediction of hardness and ultimate tensile strength, respectively. The 
authors then predicted the mechanical properties for CoCrFeNi-X (X =
Ti, V, Mn, Cu, Zr, Nb, or Hf) compositions; some of the predictions were 
also validated by experimental measurements [45] . Several other pub -
lications can also be found in the literature that are based on 
regression-based ML methods to predict the mechanical properties of 
specific alloy systems such as Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Fe-Ni [46] , Al-Cr-Fe-Ni [47] , 
Al-Cr-Fe-Mn-Ni-W [48] , and Nb-Ta-Ti-V systems [49] . 
Recently, ML studies for HEA mechanical properties prediction have 
also explored methods borrowed from explainable artificial intelligence 
(XAI) community, such as the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) 
analysis [50] , for post-hoc interpretation of their model predictions. Kim 
et al. trained a ML model based on the computational data set of ordered 
inorganic compounds for the HEA hardness prediction and computed 
the SHAP values to quantify each variable contribution to the model 
prediction for bulk and shear moduli [51] . Xiong et al. used a data set of 
complex concentrated alloys and analyzed the SHAP values for the 
random forest predictions of hardness and ultimate tensile strength. The 
authors then identified important input features such as average valence 
electron, enthalpy of mixing, and mismatch in boiling temperature in 
governing the mechanical properties [45] . Yang et al. also built a 
hardness prediction model based on the support vector machine method 
[52] . These authors used a data set of 370 compositions that are 
representative of four-, five-, six- and seven-component alloys. They 
identified valence electron concentration, atomic size difference, and 
melting temperature as important descriptors using the SHAP analysis 
[52] . 
While these publications provided meaningful insights into the 
MPEA design beyond what could be accomplished with only black-box 
ML models, our recent XAI work on MPEA phase classification prob -
lem showed that combining SHAP with clustering analysis and then 
visualizing the cluster-averaged Ceteris-Paribus (CP) profile plots can 
uncover trends that can explain the common behavior of similar MPEA 
compositions [53,54] . Our XAI approach is a departure from what is 
explored in the literature [45,51,52] because we go beyond the global 
explanation of top (or important) predictors and focus on identifying 
clusters of MPEA compositions that exhibit similar SHAP values. 
Detailed analysis of clusters then reveal hidden insights about the 
impact of various input variables on the MPEA property of interest. In 
this paper, we demonstrate the promise of integrating black-box ML 
models with SHAP, clustering analysis and CP profile plots to not only 
predict the temperature-dependent YS of as-cast MPEAs, but also explain 
the underlying functional relationships learned by the trained ML 
models. We find that the XAI algorithm sub-divides the entire data set 
into four groups based on the chemistry-composition-temperature-YS 
relationship learned by the trained ML models. Each group shows 
distinct characteristics in terms of phase, constituent elements, tem -
perature regime, and other important input variables. We use this 
analysis to shed light into the key differences in the YS property between 
single-phase and multi-phase MPEAs. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Data set construction 
We constructed our training and testing data sets by reviewing 26 ML 
papers that report the mechanical properties of MPEAs. We found 10 
papers that made the data sets open to public. We then narrowed down 
to three data sets [44,45,55] after excluding the papers that contain 
overlapped data sets or had no information about processing methods. 
Finally, we gravitated towards one data set that was developed by Borg 
et al. because it provided consistent information about MPEA compo -
sitions, processing method, test temperature, phase, and mechanical 
properties [55] . Although Borg et al. data set contained data on multiple 
mechanical properties, we restricted ourselves to the 
temperature-dependent YS of as-cast MPEAs for this work. We further 
refined the data set containing 460 instances by ruling out 12 instances 
with missing values for phase or test temperature; 56 instances with 
multiple YS values at a given test temperature and phase; 27 duplicated 
instances; 11 instances with multiple phase labels at a given temperature 
or an identical phase label at multiple temperatures. This pre-processing 
step resulted in 353 data points that are based on 246 unique MPEA 
compositions. The test temperature distributions in the raw and 
pre-processed data sets are given in Supplementary Section S1. We then 
generated a total of 36 input features to represent each MPEA compo -
sition based on constituent elements and their relative concentrations 
using a custom program inspired by the Magpie package [56] . The list of 
features can be found in Supplementary Section S2. The number of 
features were then further reduced by calculating the pair-wise Pearson 
correlation coefficient (PCC) to remove redundant features [53] . We 
used an absolute PCC (|PCC|) threshold of 0 . 6 to remove strongly sta -
tistically correlated input features. We also explored a tighter threshold 
of 0 . 4, but the results were not satisfactory (the trained models showed 
relatively poor performance on the independent test data set). 
2.2. Ensemble machine learning 
To establish the relationship between the multivariate input vari -
ables and YS, we utilized an ensemble support vector regression (eSVR) 
method that involves building multiple SVR models using the bootstrap 
resampling method [57,58] . The data set was randomly divided into two 
subsets: ( i ) training and ( ii ) testing. We used 80% of the data for training 
and 20% for testing. Each SVR model was built using the methodology 
implemented in the open source e1071 package [59] . We varied the 
number of bootstrap samples as follows: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, and 100. 
We used the non-linear radial basis function kernel and a grid search 
method for tuning hyperparameters. Our SVR models have two hyper -
parameters: ( i ) Cost and ( ii ) γ . We considered 22 (minimum = 0.001 and 
maximum = 600) and 18 (minimum = 0.001 and maximum = 3) 
different values for Cost and γ , respectively. Thus, our grid was made up 
of 22 × 18 = 396 unique hyperparameter combinations. We searched 
the entire grid for every bootstrap sample in order to perform hyper -
parameter optimization. Starting from the training data set, different sub 
data sets were randomly sampled with replacement. Each of the sub data 
set was further split into in-bag and out-of-bag (OOB) samples. We used

2.3. Post-hoc model interpretability and k-means clustering 
The trained eSVR models were further analyzed by post-hoc local 
model interpretation approaches such as the SHAP and CP profile plots, 
as implemented in the DALEX package [61] . The mathematical details of 
SHAP analysis and CP profile plots have been discussed in sufficient 
depth in these references [54,61 – 64] and therefore, we focus only on the 
salient points in this subsection. 
SHAP is a local, model-agnostic explanation method that was 
introduced in cooperative game theory to calculate the contributions of 
players to the total payout [65] . In predictive models, SHAP approxi -
mates the contribution of each input variable to the overall prediction by 
averaging over every possible ordering of input variables. Yet another 
characteristic of SHAP analysis is that it allows one to locally analyze 
individual MPEA composition and quantify the relative importance of 
each input feature in explaining the predicted YS value as learned by the 
eSVR model. Thus, each input feature has a SHAP value contributing to 
the overall prediction. One of the main outcomes from SHAP analysis is 
a new (or transformed) data set, where each MPEA composition is rep -
resented by the SHAP values. We refer to this data set as the SHAP data 
set. 
While the SHAP values provide a detailed account of variable 
importance for each MPEA composition, it does not reveal the functional 
relationship between the input variables and the output property of 
interest. To address this limitation, we used the Ceteris-Paribus (CP) 
profile plots. CP profiles show the conditional expectation of the output 
variable as a function of a particular input variable, under the constraint 
that the values of all other input variables are fixed (akin to What-If 
analysis) [63,66] . The functional dependence between the input 
feature and the predicted response is then visualized in the form of CP 
profiles, where one can glean insights into how the input and output are 
related at a glance (e.g. in a linear, non-linear or more complex pattern). 
Thus, the CP profile analysis helps us to quantify the impact of a given 
input variable on the predictions of a black-box model and provide a 
cursory explanation of the functional form. Similar to the SHAP method, 
we can calculate CP profiles for each individual MPEA composition in the 
data set. Surprisingly, CP profiles are not as popular as SHAP values in 
the MPEA-XAI literature. 
We also performed k -means clustering analysis [67] of the SHAP data 
set to identify similar MPEA compositions. We used the euclidean dis -
tance as the similarity measure in our clustering analysis. Silhouette 
coefficient was used to determine the optimal k -value. Each MPEA 
composition in the SHAP data set was then labeled with a cluster index. 
We then calculated the averaged SHAP value and CP profile data for the 
MEPA compositions in each cluster. Visualization of the averaged 
cluster-specific SHAP and CP profile plots provided an in-depth assess -
ment of the trained black-box eSVR models, revealing hidden insights 
that govern the chemistry-composition-temperature-YS relationship of 
as-cast MPEAs.

3.3. SHAPley values and SHAP data set 
We now focus on the post-hoc model analysis of trained and tested 
eSVR models using the SHAP method. The role of SHAP analysis is to 
estimate the relative contributions from each input variable in 
explaining the YS prediction for each individual MPEA composition in 
the data set. Thus, for each MPEA composition we have a new data 
representation that captures the weighted contribution (in terms of 
SHAP value) of each input variable in explaining the YS prediction. As 
described earlier, we refer to this new data representation as the SHAP 
data set. It is important to note that the SHAP data set is distinctly 
different from the initial data set that was used for training the eSVR 
models. The initial data set was made of elemental descriptors capturing 
uniquely the variation or periodic trends in the MPEA chemical com -
positions. The SHAP data set is a transformed data set that uniquely 
quantifies the impact of each input feature in explaining the predicted 
YS as learned by the eSVR model. Therefore, the SHAP data set encodes 
the chemistry-composition-temperature-YS relationship, which was 
absent in the initial MPEA composition data set.

3.7. Implications in alloy design 
Our central premise in this paper is that predictability and explain -
ability are not mutually exclusive. Especially when we are training ML 
methods using small data, it is critically important to evaluate whether 
the trained models have captured insights that agree with the intuition 
or reproduce some of the known trends. We believe that such a capa -
bility will increase the overall trustworthiness of trained data-driven ML 
models, especially to domain experts who may be not ML experts. In this 
work, in addition to demonstrating that our trained models work well on 
an independent test data set, we have applied novel model diagnostic 
tools to peek inside the black-box models and tease out trends. The 
outcome has key implications in MPEA alloy design. From the 
perspective of XAI approach, several interesting trends were uncovered 
particularly in explaining the room temperature YS trends of single- 
phase and multi-phase MPEAs: ( i ) The inverse or reverse sigmoid func -
tion that explain the temperature-dependence of YS (this is known in the 
literature). ( ii ) A non-linear, negative parabolic trend (open downward) 
between the number of p -electrons in the valence electron configuration 
and the YS. Adding Al and/or Si appears to increase the YS, at the cost of 
losing the single-phase microstructure. This is a direct “ knob ” available 
to tune the alloy composition and tailor YS. Recent experimental work of 
Qi et al. [74] supports this argument. ( iii ) Positive correlation between 
equiatomic or near-equiatomic alloy compositions and room tempera -
ture YS. In addition to demonstrating the power of XAI methods, we 
have also built an interactive web application for alloy design that can 
be accessed at this url: https://adaptivedesign.shinyapps.io/AIRHEAD/ . 
Any interested reader can visit our web app, input an alloy composition 
and immediately obtain a prediction for the YS as a function of chemical 
composition and temperature, along with the associated uncertainties. 
4. Conclusion 
MPEAs have significantly widened the alloy design space when 
compared to traditional alloys. For the MPEAs to be competitive in niche 
structural materials market, we need novel computational approaches 
that permit efficient navigation of vast and unexplored compositions in 
an accelerated manner. Although the MPEA community has embraced 
the promise of data-driven ML methods in composition design, the sheer 
vastness of the search space demands trustworthiness of the ML results. 
In this work, we have demonstrated the promise of two model-agnostic 
post-hoc interpretability methods (SHAP and CP profiles) to rigorously 
analyze the inner workings of an ensemble of black-box SVR models. We 
focused on a problem where the goal was to establish quantitative re -
lationships between chemistry, composition, temperature and YS of 
complex, as-cast MPEAs. The role of microstructural descriptors (e.g. 
grain size) of the as-cast MPEAs were largely ignored or assumed to be 
held constant. High temperature YS trend was dominated by the tem -
perature descriptor, which suggests that we should analyze the low 
temperature and high temperature data independently. This work, in 
conjunction with the growing body of XAI literature in materials infor -
matics research [77 – 79] , provides a compelling motivation for 
employing complementary post-hoc model interpretability methods to 
establish an understanding of the quantitative structure-property re -
lationships learned by trained ML models.
