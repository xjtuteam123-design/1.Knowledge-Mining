Solidiﬁcation behavior and cracking mechanisms of Ru-containing BCC-B2 
superalloys

Many precipitation-strengthened alloys are highly susceptible to cracking during additive manufacturing (AM), 
which often requires compositional adjustments to mitigate. Recently, it has been demonstrated that refractory 
BCC alloys can be precipitation strengthened with ordered Ru-containing B2 precipitates to temperatures 
exceeding 1200 °C. This motivates a study of the potential fabricability of quaternary and quinary refractory 
BCC-B2 alloys by AM using single track laser experiments. Alloys with greater than 9 at.% Ru exhibit crack 
formation upon cooling. Solidiﬁcation cracking was observed in all Zr-containing alloys, which can be mitigated 
by substituting Hf. This cracking is attributed to the strong partitioning of Zr during solidiﬁcation, as revealed by 
characterization of alloy solidus, liquidus, and partition coeﬃcients. Hf5Ru4-Nb63V28 is identiﬁed as a promising 
BCC-B2 composition with a high B2 solvus (>1300 °C) and solidus (1695 °C) along with a high resistance to 
cracking.
Precipitation-strengthened Ni-base alloys have been the leading class 
of materials to meet the demands of high temperature applications [1]. 
High temperature strength and creep resistance are conferred from co-
herent L12 precipitates that possess morphological and thermodynam-
ical stability at operating temperatures, and the FCC matrix provides 
room temperature ductility. Though these alloys have been the predom-
inant choice for aerospace applications up to 1100 °C [2], the inherent 
temperature limit of the melting point of Ni (T𝑚 = 1455 °C) motivates 
the development of superalloys comprised of higher melting point el-
ements [3,4]. Candidate materials to satisfy this temperature advance-
ment, including Fe, 𝛽-stabilized Ti, and refractory elements, like Nb, Mo, 
Ta, and W, that possess a BCC (𝛽) structure, and can be strengthened 
with ordered intermetallics, such as B2 and L21.
To date, a large suite of BCC superalloys have been investigated, and 
potential applications for these superalloys are highly dependent on the 
temperatures at which the precipitates retain morphological and ther-
modynamic stability. Ferritic superalloys, comprised of Fe with NiAl 
B2’s [5,6], and some variants with hierarchical L21 structures [7,8], are 
candidates for energy applications, such as gas turbine components [9]. 
Cr-base superalloys, incorporating a NiAl B2 [10], or a similar hierar-

Increasing the service temperatures of aerospace components, espe-

alloys with thermal stability exceeding 1200 °C. A wide range of BCC-B2 
alloys, based on refractory and precious metals [12,13], have been de-

tory alloy matrices, comprised of Nb, Mo, Ta, and W, have been paired

TiFe [21,22] and (Mo,Cr,Ti,Al) [23–26] to yield a variety of interesting

for creep resistance and high temperature strength [27], many of the 
alloys mentioned face challenges with achieving both precipitate co-

BCC alloys to form coherent precipitation-strengthened microstructures 
with solvi up to 1500 °C [28,4,29,30]. The exceptional thermal stability 
of these alloys motivates further investigation into potential processing 
avenues.

The structural applications demanding precipitation-strengthened 
refractory alloys, such as rocket propulsion systems and turbine engines, 
have beneﬁted from the near-net shape, complex geometries oﬀered by 
additive manufacturing (AM) [31–34]. However, many high strength 
alloys, including Ni-base superalloys and refractory alloys, exhibit crack-
ing during the printing process [35,36]. In Ni-base superalloys, higher 
volume fractions of the strengthening 𝛾′ phase result in greater sus-
ceptibility to cracking during solidiﬁcation and during cooling [37,38]. 
For refractory alloys, ductile to brittle transitions resulting in solid-state 
cracking are an additional consideration [39,40].
Tailoring alloy composition to mitigate crack susceptibility during 
printing is often required for AM [41,42]. Compositional alterations 
to mitigate cracking aim to modify solute segregation and freezing 
range [43–45], the temperature window between solvus and solidus 
temperatures [46,47], and/or lower the ductile-to-brittle transition tem-
perature (DBTT) of the alloy by adding ductile elements, reactive ele-
ments, and/or carbides [48–51]. Informed compositional adjustments 
have relied on alloy design principles from welding or predictive prop-
erty metrics, both of which are in the early stages of development for 
refractory alloys [39,52]. Though Ru additions have been successful for 
the formation of B2 precipitates with high thermal stability, large ad-
ditions of Ru in solid-solution during laser melting can embrittle the 
material and result in higher solid-state crack susceptibility [53,54]. De-
sign rules for AM are needed for Ru-B2 strengthened refractory alloys 
and can be established by better understanding of their solidiﬁcation be-
havior, including their solute segregation during solidiﬁcation, freezing 
range, and cracking susceptibility.
Six representative BCC-B2 alloys possessing coherent B2 precipitates 
were selected from a prior study [28] that focused on fabrication, design 
methodology, and phase characterization [55]. In this alloy class, the B2 
solvus temperature scales directly with the Ru content [28]. The selected 
compositions and their experimentally estimated solvus temperatures 
are provided in Table 1.
Properties relevant to solidiﬁcation, including the solidus, liquidus, 
and elemental partition coeﬃcients, were characterized for selected al-
loys (Hf5Ru4, Hf11Ru9, Zr5Ru4, Zr10Ru9) via melting experiments and 
subsequent electron probe microanalysis (EPMA). The solidus-liquidus 
measurements were performed using the set-up described in reference 
[56]. The technique integrates UV imaging and multispectral pyrometry 
to observe and quantify critical transitions during melting. Specimens 
are heated using a direct current, while UV imaging captures shape 
changes. The emergence of the mushy zone indicates the solidus point, 
while the liquidus point corresponds to the maximum temperature 
recorded once fully liquid. Calibration of this technique was performed 
with C103, W, Nb and Kanthal alloys, which have well-documented 
solidus and liquidus points. The solidus and liquidus temperatures of 
each composition are provided in Fig. 1. Additional 1 hour exposures in 
vacuum were performed on arc-melted buttons in the solutioned condi-
tion at temperatures near or above the measured solidus (1700, 1800 °C) 
to validate the pyrometer measurements. Partial melting was observed 
in compositions annealed at temperatures above their solidus (Supple-
mental Fig. 1), in agreement with the pyrometer measurements.
For both Hf-containing and Zr-containing alloys, the freezing range 
and liquidus decreases with the addition of increasing amounts of B2-
forming elements (Ru, Hf, Zr). Freezing ranges exceeding 450 °C are 
observed for most alloys, with the exception of Hf11Ru9, which has a 
narrow 15 °C freezing range. The microstructures of each sample after 
heating above their liquidus and subsequent solidiﬁcation is shown in 
Fig. 1C–F. The alloys with large freezing ranges (Hf5Ru4, Zr5Ru4, and 
Zr10Ru9) exhibit dendritic microstructures. In all alloys, Nb is enriched 
in the dendrite core, whereas V, Ru, Hf and Zr are enriched in the inter-
dendritic regions (see Supplemental Fig. 2 for EPMA maps). Oxides are 
also are observed in all compositions, with HfO2 in the Hf-containing 
alloys and ZrO2 in the Zr-containing alloys.

To investigate the cracking resistance of the alloys under laser melt-
ing conditions, single track laser experiments were performed on each 
of the substrates using a custom small-scale apparatus, which is de-
scribed in detail in [39]. All alloys were annealed at 1600 °C, other 
than Hf11Ru9 (annealed at 1750 °C for 10 h), for 20 h to achieve a so-
lutioned microstructure before conducting laser melting experiments. 
B2 precipitates were still present in Hf11Ru9, attributed to not reach-
ing the solvus temperature, and Hf2Zr7Ru9, due to precipitation upon 
cooling [28,59]. Eight single track experiments of 6 mm in length were 
conducted on each alloy in a puriﬁed argon environment (<5 ppm O2), 
using laser powers between 250–370 W, scan speeds of 200–800 mm/s, 
and a 80 μm laser spot size. Following laser melting, the surfaces and 
cross-sections of the laser track melts were characterized with scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diﬀraction (EBSD).
The surfaces of laser track melts, performed at 330 W, 800 mm/s 
on each arc-melted and annealed composition are shown in Fig. 2. 
Hf-containing alloys with lower solvus temperatures (Hf2Ru1, Hf5Ru4) 
show no cracks across all tested powers (250, 290, 330, 370 W) and 
scan speeds (200, 800 mm/s). The bright contrast on the edges of Hf5Ru4
track melt is due to the presence of HfO2, which is attributed to the high 
oxygen levels in the original arc-melted button. Laser track experiments 
were repeated on Hf5Ru4 in the as-cast and aged conditions, which also 
produced crack-free microstructures (see Supplemental Fig. 3). Hf11Ru9, 
with the highest solvus temperature of the Hf-containing alloys, shows 
crack defects in all laser track melts (Fig. 2C). These crack defects ex-
hibit a planar morphology, consistent with brittle crack propagation, 
and are attributed to solid-state cracking upon cooling.
Crack defects were observed in all track melts performed on all Zr-
containing alloys, including the quinary Hf2Zr7Ru9. Most cracks were 
positioned along the centerline of the melt with fracture propagation 
along dendritic arms, consistent with solidiﬁcation cracking.

Cross-sections of each track melt conducted at 330 W, 800 mm/s 
are shown in Fig. 3. Nearly all compositions exhibit similar melt pool 
characteristics, with a high proportion of nucleated grains in each melt 
pool. Coarser dendrites are observed at the melt pool boundary, which 
transition to a ﬁner cellular structure during solidiﬁcation. The only ex-
ception is the melt pool of Hf11Ru9, in which case the ﬂuid ﬂow pattern 
during solidiﬁcation is visible through the changing orientations of the 
cells and dendrites. In all cases, ﬁnely dispersed oxides (HfO2, ZrO2) 
are observed at the interdendritic regions due to the partitioning of Hf, 
Zr, and O to these regions [60]. In Hf5Ru4, which contained a high 
volume fraction of HfO2, the melt pool contains multiple populations 
of oxides, including nano-precipitates decorating the interdendritic re-
gions, slightly larger precipitates uniformly distributed throughout the 
melt pool, and a few large oxides that were not fully melted during the 
laser melting process, indicating that the oxides do generally melt dur-
ing the process across this composition space. Critically, no cracks are 
observed within or surrounding those oxides. No B2 precipitates were 
observed in the melt pools in the BSE SEM micrographs.
Solidiﬁcation cracks are observed in the cross-sections of the Zr-
containing alloys (Fig. 3D–F), with segments that appear more planar 
and propagate outside of the melt pool. It is hypothesized that these 
solid-state cracks propagated from the initial solidiﬁcation crack defects 
during cooling at a temperature where there is a ductility debit. Ad-
ditional cracking was observed along grain boundaries decorated with 
deleterious phases, including the aged substrate of Hf5Ru4 and solu-
tioned substrates of Zr10Ru9 and Hf2Zr7Ru9 (see Supplemental Fig. 4). 
The morphology of the crack faces are indicative of partial melting of 
the grain boundary phases, corresponding to liquation cracking. Though 
Hf11Ru9 has a high volume fraction of Hf-rich phases at the grain bound-
ary, (Fig. 3C), no partial melting of grain boundary phases or liquation 
cracking was observed after laser melting.
The distribution of crack defects as a function of laser parameters in 
the alloys with 9 at.% Ru are compared in Fig. 4. FIJI [61] was used 
to threshold and isolate the individual cracks in each track melt image, 
then quantify their lengths and angles for comparison across laser pa-
rameters and compositions. The extent of cracking, represented by the 
normalized crack length, is far more severe in Zr10Ru9 than Hf11Ru9
across nearly all process parameters, attributed to the severity of solid-
iﬁcation cracking in the Zr-containing alloys. Trends in crack severity 
are not apparent with respect to changes in laser process parameters. 
The cracks were further analyzed for their directionality with respect 
to the laser scan direction. Crack defects in Hf11Ru9 are predominantly 
oriented transverse to the scanning direction (90°), similar to solid-state 
cracks in laser-melted tungsten [40]. The bimodal population of angles 
in Zr10Ru9, parallel (0°) and transverse (90°) to the scanning direction, 
indicates that crack defects formed both during solidiﬁcation and cool-
ing, consistent with the morphologies of cracks observed in Fig. 3E–F.
EBSD was performed to investigate the microstructure surrounding 
the solidiﬁcation and solid-state cracks in Hf2Zr7Ru9 and Hf11Ru9, re-
spectively (Fig. 5). In Hf2Zr7Ru9, columnar grains follow the direction 
of maximum thermal gradient and exhibit intragranular misorientation 
along their length. A large grain in the centerline presumably grew from 
the base of the melt pool, with an orientation close to <110>, unlike 
the <100>grains commonly observed in welded and printed FCC mi-
crostructures [62–64].
In contrast, the grain structure in Hf11Ru9 has no columnar grains, 
with no evident signs that grain structure followed the direction of 
maximum thermal gradient. Similar microstructures have formed from 
printing of elemental powder feedstocks [65,66]. It is hypothesized that 
the liquid did not mix suﬃciently before solidiﬁcation, due to the pres-
ence of three chemically distinct phases in the substrate (BCC, B2, and a 
Hf-rich grain boundary phase), leading to a complex solidiﬁcation path-
way.
Interestingly, slip traces are observed in the transverse direction 
across the laser track melt in Hf11Ru9 (Fig. 5D), which extend across 
multiple grains and still maintain a similar orientation. The observation 
of visible slip traces on the laser melted surface indicate surface relief 
from accumulated slip, which likely developed from the high thermo-
mechanical stresses generated from solidiﬁcation and cooling [67,68]. 
Solid-state cracks are also observed in proximity to the slip traces, in-
cluding two directly connected to slip traces (inset of Fig. 5D). However, 
many slip traces are present away from the cracks, suggesting the traces 
were the ﬁrst to form. An accumulation of kernel average misorien-
tation (KAM) is evident near the edges and tips of solid-state cracks, 
especially in the ﬁeld between the two cracks. Recrystallized grains are 
also observed in the regions with high KAM in the area between the two 
solid-state cracks (Fig. 5E–F).

Hf2Zr7Ru9 (Fig. 5A–C) shows crack propagation along the intersection 
of the large centerline grain with the smaller columnar grains, con-
sistent with observations of solidiﬁcation cracking in welds and AM 
components across many alloy systems [69,70]. High KAM values are

The inclusion of Zr in this alloy family leads to extensive solidiﬁca-
tion crack defects. This is consistent with severe elemental partitioning 
and corresponding solidus depression during solidiﬁcation, conﬁrmed 
experimentally by low partition coeﬃcients for Zr and wide freezing 
ranges for Zr-containing alloys (Fig. 1). Solidiﬁcation cracking is mit-
igated by substitution of Hf for Zr, as demonstrated in Hf5Ru4. To be 
amenable to printing, Zr-containing BCC-B2 alloys will require com-
positional tuning to modify the solidiﬁcation pathway and eliminate 
solidiﬁcation cracking. CALPHAD has been a useful methodology to 
calculate solidiﬁcation pathways and determine favorable alloy design 
strategies for Ni superalloys that face similar challenges [42]. To assess 
its utility for this alloy class, a Scheil-Gulliver curve has been computed 
for Hf2Zr7Ru9, using the TCHEA7 database of ThermoCalc [71], and is

The observation of solid-state crack defects in both Hf11Ru9 and 
Zr10Ru9 suggests that 9 at.% Ru in solid solution may be an upper limit, 
beyond which fabricability is compromised by embrittlement. Solid-
state cracks have been observed in laser track melts of refractory alloys 
with 15 at.% Ru [53] and are attributed to debits in ductility during 
cooling [39]. In Hf11Ru9, the ductility debit could be attributed to the 
presence of deleterious grain boundary phases, embrittlement from high 
Ru content, and/or a higher DBTT. Using lower cooling rate AM meth-
ods, such as laser directed energy deposition (L-DED) or electron beam 
melting (EBM), to encourage Ru redistribution to B2 precipitates could 
be a useful strategy to mitigate the cracking problem [72].
Another important consideration is the inﬂuence of interstitial con-
tent on the embrittlement and cracking susceptibility of these alloys. 
Nb and the other refractory elements are highly sensitive to concentra-
tions of interstitial elements, such as O, N, and C. Nb will readily react 
with these elements, even in an environment with insuﬃciently pure 
gases, such as L-PBF, which will increase the strength and decrease the 
ductility of Nb [73]. Additions of elements with higher negative free en-
ergies of formation, like Hf and Zr, lower the solubility of O in the Nb 
matrix and promote the formation of oxide dispersions [74]. Using re-
active elements, such as Hf and Zr, can promote the formation of oxide 
dispersions, which are less detrimental towards ductility and fabrica-
bility than oxygen as an interstitial solute. However, in the context of 
these BCC-B2 alloys, the formation of (Hf,Zr) oxides could reduce the 
eﬀective concentration of those elements to react with Ru to form B2, 
resulting in reduced B2 volume fractions after aging [55]. Strategies to 
promote favorable interstitial uptake will likely be critical for most BCC 
superalloy systems.
Other considerations for the improvement of Ru-B2 alloys include 
mitigation of deleterious grain boundary phases [28], which are dele-
terious for mechanical properties and can lead to liquation cracking. 
Liquation cracking is an issue in some additively manufactured superal-
loys, such as Inconel 718, if Laves phases with lower melting points form 
during AM [75]. Bulk printing eﬀorts would be necessary to investigate 
if these tertiary phases would form during the printing process.
Two key considerations regarding the design of BCC superalloys for 
AM have been clariﬁed. First, the elemental partitioning of solutes, typ-
ically the B2 formers, can lead to solidiﬁcation defects if partitioning 
is severe. CALPHAD approaches can be useful for predicting the solid-
iﬁcation pathway of alloys with more historic database development, 
such as Fe and Ti alloys [76], but improvements are still needed for 
refractory databases. Revisiting the binary phase diagrams for equilib-
rium partition coeﬃcients can also be informative. For example, the 
computed partition coeﬃcient of Zr, at a concentration of 5 at.% in Nb, 
is 0.4 [77], indicating the likelihood of severe partitioning during solid-
iﬁcation. However, evaluating solidus and liquidus surfaces for higher 
order alloys, like refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs), is 
more challenging. A number of Zr-containing RMPEAs have successfully 
been printed without reported crack defects, including a BCC superalloy 
with (Al,Ti,Zr)-B2 [14]. This suggests that alloy design strategies exist to 
modify the solidiﬁcation pathway in Zr-containing superalloys to lower 
their susceptibility to solidiﬁcation cracking.
The second primary consideration is the tolerance of the alloy to 
embrittling solute additions that promote cracking during cooling. Ev-
idence indicates that B2 phases do not precipitate upon cooling from 
laser melting [14,53], indicating that the DBTT and room temperature 
ductility of the alloy in the solutionized condition are critical for process-
ing considerations. Nb is regarded to have a low DBTT with suﬃciently 
low impurity content, and signiﬁcant additions of Hf, Zr, and V are tol-
erated before reaching room temperature [73]. The DBTT of Nb is likely 
to increase with Ru in solution, though further testing is necessary. Sus-
ceptibility to solid-state cracking may be a concern for other refractory 
superalloys containing more brittle elements, such as Cr, Mo, and W 
containing alloys [22,21,20,10]. Rapid experimental assessments, such 
as the methodology presented here, are useful for quick evaluations in 
absence of bulk mechanical property tests, and is intended to facilitate 
the identiﬁcation of alloys that may be amenable to L-PBF.
In summary, the solidiﬁcation behavior of Ru-containing BCC-B2 al-
loys is dependent on the selection and concentration of the B2 forming 
elements (Hf, Zr, Ru). Fundamental solidiﬁcation properties (solidus, 
liquidus, and elemental partition coeﬃcients) were coupled with sin-
gle laser track experiments to elucidate compositional eﬀects on solid-
iﬁcation behavior. Zr partitions strongly during solidiﬁcation, leading 
to extensive solidiﬁcation cracking of Zr-containing alloys under laser 
melting. Solid-state cracking observed at higher Ru contents (9 at.%) 
is attributed to alloy embrittlement when a high level of Ru is incor-
porated in solid solution. Finally, Hf5Ru4-Nb63V28 exhibits favorable 
solidiﬁcation behavior for printing while maintaining a high B2 solvus 
and solidus.
