Additive manufacturing (AM) presents a new design paradigm for the manufacture of engineering ma- 
terials through the layer-by-layer approach combined with welding theory. In the instance of diﬃcult 
to process materials such as tungsten and other refractory metals, AM offers an opportunity for radical 
redesign of critical components for next-generation energy technologies including fusion. In this work, 
electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) is applied to process pure tungsten to study the inﬂuence of 
process parameters on the defect density of the material. An in-situ image analysis algorithm is applied 
to pure tungsten for the ﬁrst time, and is used to visualize the defect structure in AM tungsten. Finally, 
a cracking mechanism for AM tungsten is proposed, and suggestions for suppression of cracks in pure 
tungsten are offered.

1. Introduction 
Fusion power has the potential to produce abundant carbon- 
neutral energy, but many technical challenges currently pre- 
vent commercial production of fusion power. One of the most 
formidable challenges is the design of materials and components 
that can withstand the enormous heat and neutron loads gener- 
ated by the plasma. In next-generation tokamak designs such as 
the Demonstration Power Plant (DEMO), the most extreme heat 
loads will be experienced by the plasma-facing surfaces of the di- 
vertor, which separate colder edge plasma from the hot plasma 
core to allow for removal of fusion waste products and recycling 
of unreacted tritium fuel [1] . These surfaces must withstand peak

requirements of extreme high temperature service, high sputter- 
ing threshold, low tritium retention, and good thermal conductiv- 
ity point to the refractory metals, particularly tungsten, as good 
candidate materials for the plasma-facing surfaces of the divertor. 
Unfortunately, tungsten is a notoriously diﬃcult metal to fabri- 
cate, due to its high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT, 
≈ 0 –600 ◦C [3] ), susceptibility to intragranular cracking [4] , and 
the diﬃculty of joining tungsten parts to other structural materials 
[5] . Traditionally, tungsten components are made via powder met- 
allurgy [4] . Component geometries are therefore necessarily sim- 
ple, and existing divertor designs are limited to what is possible 
to fabricate today. Complex design features such as active cooling 
surfaces or embedded diagnostics might dramatically increase the 
lifetime and effectiveness of these parts [6] , but these designs re- 
main essentially unexplored. 
Meanwhile, metal additive manufacturing processes have 
rapidly matured over the past few years. Additive manufacturing 
makes it possible to fabricate near-net shapes of great complexity, 
and has recently been extended to diﬃcult-to-fabricate materials 
systems such as nickel-base superalloys [7] . Furthermore, the layer- 
by-layer nature of additive manufacturing processes offers a high 
degree of control over the thermal history of the part, and, conse- 
quently, the ability to control the ﬁnal microstructure of the part 
in a site-speciﬁc manner [8] . Additive manufacturing also lends it-

Tungsten is a particularly diﬃcult metal to adapt to additive 
processes, due to the shared similarities of AM processes to fu- 
sion welding. Of fusion welding techniques, the most promising 
for tungsten are those that involve a high-intensity source, a vac- 
uum environment, and a preheating step [11] . The vacuum en- 
vironment is critical in preventing oxidation in the weld metal, 
as oxygen reacts readily with tungsten at elevated temperatures, 
but, upon cooling, segregates to grain boundaries where it pro- 
motes intergranular cracking [12] . Preheating above the DBTT can 
reduce cracking, but may also lead to recrystallization. As tungsten 
typically exhibits satisfactory room-temperature ductility only af- 
ter signiﬁcant cold working, recrystallization and grain growth in 
the heat affected zone pose a problem even in welds that appear 
to be sound [13] . Fine-grained tungsten is more resistant to inter- 
granular crack growth as cracks must follow more tortuous paths 
[3] . Furthermore, some evidence suggests that cold working im- 
proves room-temperature ductility by breaking up and redistribut- 
ing brittle oxides that have segregated to grain boundaries [12] . 
Recrystallized material in the heat affected zone therefore offers 
long, straight crack paths along grain boundaries embrittled by re- 
segregated oxides, resulting in low ductility in the weld even if the 
base metal is ductile [11] . 
Many of the issues observed in fusion welding of tungsten also 
appear in additive manufacturing. Most work to date has focused 
on laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) which typically operates at 
room temperature and in an inert environment, which is problem- 
atic for materials with a ductile-brittle transition and oxygen sen- 
sitivity. The ﬁrst moderately successful mention of laser powder 
bed fusion of tungsten was in 2013, where it was used to produce 
a collimator for SPECT imaging [14] . The authors reported a den- 
sity of 89.9% of theoretical. Later, other researchers focused on im- 
proving density via a double melt strategy [15] , increasing power 
[16–18] , or otherwise changing parameters. Recent works report 
densities above 98% of theoretical [19–21] . However, microcracking 
remains an issue in producing additively manufactured tungsten 
suitable for structural applications. Virtually all W manufactured 
via LPBF shows some degree of cracking, especially along grain 
boundaries. Various authors have suggested mechanisms by which 
this cracking may occur. Guo suggests that cracks are caused by 
stresses arising from high temperature gradients and rapid cool- 
ing [22] . Vrancken et al. [23] used high-speed video to demon- 
strate that cracking in single line melts occurs in a narrow tem- 
perature range (177–377 ◦C) and conclude that cracking is due to 
the presence of high von Mises stresses when the material drops 
below the DBTT. Wang suggests they arise due to coalescence of 
nanopores formed at grain boundaries due to entrapped oxygen 
[24] . Müller et al. have demonstrated that heating the substrate 
to 10 0 0 ◦C can reduce microcracking, but does not eliminate it. 
[25] Alloying [26,27] , especially with tantalum, has been shown to 
reduce cracking behavior [18,24] , but Ta alloys are generally con- 
sidered to be undesirable for nuclear applications due to Tas ready 
transmutation into the radioactive isotope Ta182 and the associ- 
ated large afterheat, which presents safety concerns. No researcher 
has been able to eliminate cracking in LPBF of pure tungsten, and 
some have even suggested that production of crack-free tungsten 
via LPBF is essentially impossible [24] . 
Electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) uses a electron 
beam as the heat source that is controlled through electromagnetic 
lenses and can achieve beam speeds of up to 80 0 0 m/s. Further, 
to prevent the build-up of negative charge from individual powder 
particles, the build area is pre-heated to temperatures to enable 
sintering of the powder particles and dissipation of the charge. 
Fig. 1. As-received plasma-atomized tungsten powder illustrating the spherical 
morphology. 
In the case of brittle refractory metals such as tungsten, the pre- 
heat step allows the entire part to be kept above the brittle-to- 
ductile transition temperature throughout the entire build, poten- 
tially reducing the tendency for the material to crack. Additionally, 
the EB-PBF systems such as the Arcam process operates within a 
controlled vacuum environment to maintain quality of the focused 
electron beam and minimize impacts of material vaporization dur- 
ing melting on the vacuum level. The control is typically achieved 
with bleeding an inert gas such as helium into the chamber in 
a controlled manner. Further, for material sensitive to impurities 
such as pure tungsten, this controlled vacuum environment bene- 
ﬁcial for limiting the exposure to imputries during AM processing. 
Electron beam melting of tungsten is less reported in the liter- 
ature, however, does show promise for eliminating cracking. Yang 
et al. [28] used EB-PBF to produce W samples of nearly full den- 
sity (99.5%). The powder bed was maintained at a temperature of 
about 850 ◦C, well above the DBTT, and while some cracks were re- 
ported, cracking is less severe than in the LPBF samples mentioned 
above. Wright [29] was able to prevent the formation of cracks in 
EB-PBF tungsten with a bed temperature of 10 0 0 ◦C, perhaps due 
to the addition of wafer supports between the start plate and the 
sample. The goal of this work is to study the inﬂuence of process 
parameters on the defect density of pure tungsten and identify a 
process space that may yield fully dense and crack-free material. 
2. Experimental methods 
2.1. Powder characteristics 
Plasma-atomized tungsten powder with a d10 and d90 of 56 
and 95 μm (d50 = 71 μm), respectively, was obtained from Tekna 
Advanced Materials, Inc. (Quebec, Canada). According to the mate- 
rial speciﬁcations by the manufacturer, virgin material has a pu- 
rity is 99.9% W with less than 250 ppm oxygen. The material was 
stored in a sealed inert environment prior to use and then sub- 
sequently kept in the vacuum environment of the Arcam Q10+ 
printer during builds. Illustrated in Fig. 1 is microscopy of the pow- 
der depicting the highly spherical morphology. 
2.2. Sample fabrication 
All prismatic geometries were manufactured using an Arcam 
Q10+ electron beam melting system which during the build pro- 
cess a controlled chamber vacuum level of 10 −3 mbar is main- 
tained through a control bleed of high purity helium into the 
chamber. Build substrates were machined from Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64)

Ti64 was chosen as a substrate material over typically used 304

Beam current during melting was 21 mA for all samples, with

decreasing the beam speed during melting from a speed suitable 
for the start plate material to the ﬁnal beam speed to be used for 
the tungsten part. Focus offset values were varied from 10 mA to 
40 mA, and speed function was varied from 3 to 9. A raster scan 
pattern was used for all builds. ARCAM automatic control functions 
were used during the fabrication of these samples. 
2.3. Characterization 
Upon completion of each build, the part was removed from the 
build chamber, sintered powder was removed, and individual sam- 
ples were removed from the start plate. Samples were sectioned 
using a diamond saw, mounted, and polished using standard met- 
allographic techniques. For the analysis, no etching was performed 
on the EBM tungsten samples. Optical microscopy was performed 
on all samples and used to evaluate build quality. 
2.4. In-situ analysis 
To analyze the quality of the material in an in-situ manner, the 
built-in capability of the Arcam Q10+ to acquire near-infrared (NIR) 
images at the conclusion of melting and after raking of each layer 
was leveraged. These NIR images were analyzed to identify pores 
and combined to create a 3D visualization of the part. The follow- 
ing four step image processing ﬂow was used to automate to the 
detection of anomalies (1) image correction and NIR image map- 
ping of the 2D space of the post-melt and post-rake to ensure 
pixel resolution consistency and accurate localization of anoma- 
lies; (2) correction of temporally dependent emissivity to account 
for spatially asynchronous thermal decays; (3) detection of anoma- 
lies using spatial statistical analytics; and (4) measurement of mor- 
phological properties of anomalies such as center of mass, volume, 
orientation, or eccentricity to determine the likelihood of anomaly 
classiﬁcation. 
The spatial relationship between post-melt and post-rake NIR 
image anomaly detection was used to distinguish between anoma- 
lies caused by topology, swelling, or porosity. Regions with greater 
than 75% probability of being porosity are then measured for their 
center of mass, volume, orientation, and eccentricity to improve 
the probability of porosity identiﬁcation. The ﬁnal calculation of 
density is based on area of porosity and total area of anticipated 
melt region, and is later converted to a volumetric density. 
As described earlier, no effort was made to optimize the pa- 
rameters associated with the turning points function. Therefore, we 
have only considered the center 8.5 mm 8.5 mm of each cube for 
optical density and NIR porosity analysis as described above. 
3. Results 
3.1. Support 
As stated in Section 2.2 , all builds began with an increased 
beam speed at the Ti64 base plate, then gradually decreased beam 
speed until reaching the desired speed. The resulting sample struc- 
ture is shown in Fig. 2 . Beginning at the Ti64 build plate, we ob- 
serve a narrow region of mixed W and Ti64, followed by a porous 
support region, followed by the dense W region of interest. As 
speeds were changed manually, the height of the support region 
varies from about 1.5 mm in Build 3, to 7.5 mm in Build 2. The use 
of higher speeds (and therefore lower energy densities) for the ﬁrst 
few layers of the build was successful in creating a robust interface 
layer between the tungsten part and the base plate. 
3.2. Material quality 
The primary variables explored in this experiment were beam 
speed, focus offset, and preheat temperature. Speed was varied us- 
ing the ARCAM control function Speed Function (SF), and beam 
size was varied using the control function Focus Offset (FO). Beam 
current was maintained at 21 mA and hatch spacing was main- 
tained at 32.5 μm. Build 1 varied actual beam speed from 324 to 
588 mm/s and FO from 20 to 40 mA. Fig. 3 a shows the resulting 
parts in their as-built state, Fig. 3 b shows optical microscopy of 
XZ sections taken near the center of the cuboid (midline). The re- 
sulting variations in energy density and beam size resulted in no- 
ticeable changes in geometric ﬁdelity and porosity. Increasing spot 
size and decreasing speed tend to decrease porosity, but also re- 
sult in the balling of the molten metal on the part surface. Based 
on these results, Build 2 was melted with larger spot sizes and a 
higher build temperature, with slightly higher beam speeds (456–
720 mm/s), and is shown in Fig. 4 . These specimens show im- 
proved geometric ﬁdelity, but higher porosity. Nonetheless, part 3, 
with the largest spot size and slowest beam speed, shows a large 
region of essentially zero porosity at the top of the sample. Build 
3 uses these melt parameters for all parts, and also uses a reduced 
preheat, with both lower current and lower duration during the 
preheat step. This build, shown in Fig. 5 , is relatively dense, but 
shows signiﬁcant vertical microcracking. 
Part density was estimated using the in-situ analysis method 
described in Section 2.4 , and results for Build 2 are given in Fig. 6 . 
Fig. 6 a shows layerwise relative density for each part. Transition to 
the ﬁnal parameter set was complete at 7.58 mm, and this point 
is indicated on the ﬁgure. A distinct change in relative density 
across all parts is visible at this height, and analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) and pairwise analysis indicates that the likelihood of cre- 
ating this density change randomly was small. Fig. 6 b shows an 
XZ section through the full 3D reconstruction for Build 2, part 2, 
compared with an optical micrograph of the same part at a simi- 
lar location. The reconstruction captures the presence of the chim- 
ney pores that form in the support region and propagate upwards. 
The formation of chimney pores in tungsten can be related to the 
dimpled surfaces observed on the surfaces of the tungsten cubes 
similar to that observed in-situ for Ti64 processed through EBM- 
PBF [30] . While in-situ near-IR is a powerful technique, pores be- 
low the pixel resolution of the camera ( ≈ 40 μm) will not detected 
and appear in the reconstructed part. The missing material on the 
left side of the sample is believed to be damage to have occurred 
during sectioning of the sample, and thus does not appear in the 
in-situ data. However, the large pore visible in the in-situ data near 
this region may have contributed to the formation of the hole dur- 
ing sectioning. 
4. Discussion 
Unlike most others who report dense additively manufactured 
tungsten, we have successfully produced material with essentially 
zero cracks, as seen in Build 2, Part 3 ( Fig. 4 b). Possible reasons for 
this difference, as well as a hypothesized mechanism of cracking in 
additively manufactured tungsten, are discussed below.

This article presents the ﬁrst use of in-situ near-infrared layer 
images for 3D build reconstruction and defect detection in refrac-

technique for other metals, including Ti64 [9] , but refractory met-

relatively high emissivity at high temperature [31] , and the emis- 
sivity of unmelted tungsten powder is quite close to that of pores 
in the melted material. As it is the contrast in emissivity that al- 
lows for in-situ detection of pores, this presents a challenge. In 
these experiments, a piece of welding glass (shade number 10 
with a luminous transmittance of 0.0139%) was placed in front of 
the NIR camera lens to attenuate the signal, but pore identiﬁca- 
tion is still somewhat uncertain. Nevertheless, the results shown 
above demonstrate a promising ﬁrst step toward developing an 
in-situ defect detection algorithm for tungsten in spite of these 
diﬃculties. Densities in the region of interest (i.e. not including

An accurate in-situ defect detection method could potentially 
reduce reliance on x-ray computed tomography, a time-intensive 
and expensive procedure which, for high-z materials such as tung- 
sten, is suitable only for thin specimens. In contrast, the in-situ 
method can provide an essentially instantaneous mapping of part 
defects, and its detection volume is limited only by the size of the 
build chamber. Even for simple assays such as density measure- 
ment, the in-situ technique offers advantages over traditional tech- 
niques. Optical measures of density taken from single sections as- 
sume a uniform distribution of defects throughout the part, where 
the in-situ method surveys the entire volume. The Archimedes 
method, He pycnometry, and other volume measurements will not 
accurately capture the density of parts with surface-penetrating 
porosity, while the in-situ method has no such limitation. There- 
fore, in-situ defect detection methods can prove instrumental in 
eﬃcient exploration of parameter spaces for dense refractory ma- 
terials. 
4.2. Cracking 
The fundamental diﬃculty in producing crack-free tungsten is 
that tungsten becomes brittle below some critical temperature, the 
ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT). The value of the 
DBTT is not ﬁxed, but varies considerably (as much as 0–600 ◦C 
[3] ) based on purity and processing history. Oxygen is a particu- 
lar problem: oxygen reacts readily with tungsten at high temper- 
atures, but has very low solubility in solid tungsten. This means 
that oxygen dissolved in the melt pool at high temperatures will 
tend to segregate to grain boundaries, where it can form various 
species of tungsten oxides. These oxides then reduce grain bound- 
ary strength, leaving intergranular fracture as the dominant fail- 
ure mode. Even in the absence of impurities, however, tungsten 
retains a ductile-to-brittle transition. The transition temperature

in BCC metals, namely, that the slip mechanism changes with tem- 
perature. Slip in BCC metals is limited by the generation of kink 
pairs in screw dislocations, which is a thermally activated process. 
At low temperature, slip occurs on a slip system with a high Peirls 
stress because only this system has a low enough activation energy 
barrier to generate the kink pairs needed to make dislocations mo- 
bile. Because the resistance to dislocation motion is so high, frac- 
ture occurs before yielding. At higher temperatures, slip systems 
with lower Peirls stresses but higher kink-pair activation energies 
are able to activate, and plastic deformation can occur before frac- 
ture [36] . 
Examination of crack morphology in those builds which do 
present process-induced cracking suggests a possible mechanism 
of crack formation in additively manufactured tungsten. The in- 
verse pole ﬁgure map in Fig. 8 shows localized orientation gradi- 
ents at crack edges, indicating plastic deformation preceding crack- 
ing. This suggests that these cracks formed in the solid state. A 
solid-state cracking mechanism would be consistent with the ob- 
servations of Vrancken et al. [23] , who captured high-speed video 
of microcracking during laser melting of tungsten and observe that 
cracks appear in a narrow temperature range between 177–377 ◦C 
regardless of beam power and velocity. Additionally, Fig. 9 a shows 
a string of pores that grows along the build direction before be- 
coming a large crack. Such porosity strings are well-known in ad- 
ditive processes [37] , and are associated with non-uniform temper- 
ature distributions in layers above a pore [38] . These cracks also 
seem to be localized in the XY plane: Fig. 9 b shows concentrated 
heavily cracked areas surrounded by mostly uncracked material. 
This suggests a relatively rare crack nucleation event with easy 
propagation along the build (Z) direction, and minimal propagation 
along the in-plane (X and Y) directions. This is consistent with an 
initial microcrack nucleated from the stress concentration associ- 
ated with a pore, then propagated along columnar grain bound- 
aries. In the speciﬁc case of Fig. 7 , the crack may be associated 
with a pore in the porous support region, but in principle any pore 
could be the initiation site for a crack. 
It is clear that ambient temperature plays a signiﬁcant role 
in cracking behavior in additively manufactured tungsten. Müller 
et al. [25] used a selective laser melting system with substrate 
preheating, and showed that increasing build temperature from

Within this range, we see a difference in cracking behavior with 
preheat energies. Fig. 10 shows two builds with identical melt pa- 
rameters but different preheat parameters: the part in Fig. 10 a and 
c used a longer and higher-current preheat step than the part in 
Fig. 10 b and d, resulting in a decrease in total energy deposited 
during the preheat step from 735 to 325 J / mm 2 , and the appear- 
ance of cracks in the lower-preheat part. We therefore conclude 
that the higher preheat energy suppresses cracking, presumably by 
raising the ambient temperature of the build. 
Previous authors have justiﬁed the use of high ambient temper- 
ature during AM processing by suggesting that processing above 
the DBTT will allow for the production of crack-free parts [29] . 
However, examination of the literature reveals that merely build- 
ing above the DBTT is not suﬃcient to eliminate cracking entirely. 
Each of the elevated-temperature builds referenced above, includ- 
ing our work, show cracking in builds performed at ambient tem- 
peratures well above the established range of DBTT of tungsten. 
While the DBTT of W is highly dependent on impurity content, 
grain size, and dislocation density, the reported range of transition 
temperatures is from room temperature to about 600 ◦C [3] . The 
presence of cracks in parts built at temperatures well above the 
DBTT indicates either that thermal stresses are so large they can- 
not be accommodated plastically, or that residual stresses remain- 
ing after the build has cooled to room temperature cause cracking. 
Parameters that reduce stresses are therefore of interest in design- 
ing build processes for crack-free W. Electron beam powder bed 
fusion and high-temperature laser powder bed fusion are typically 
assumed to result in lower residual stresses than low-temperature 
laser processes, probably due to the annealing effect of the heated 
powder bed [39] . Clearly, this annealing is not always adequate to 
reduce residual stresses suﬃciently to prevent cracking, consider- 
ing the number of cracked parts built at elevated temperature. It is 
therefore worth examining what other factors distinguish our un- 
cracked samples from others.

the theoretical volumetric energy required to heat and melt tung- 
sten. Some of this excess energy is conducted away from the melt 
area, but much of it contributes to remelting material in previous 
layers and neighboring beam paths. In other words, our experi- 
ments feature a much larger overlap between neighboring paths 
than other reported results. 
A simple Rosenthal model [40] was used to calculate the size 
of the melt pool for various beam speeds and currents, and then 
compared with the hatch spacing between adjacent line scans. 
Based on these values, we determined that each point is remelted 
approximately 14 times in our experiments, compared to 2 times 
for those of Müller et al. As the beam rasters across the surface, 
each area of material is effectively preheated by each scan of ma- 
terial upstream of the area of interest, and then reheated by each 
scan of material downstream. The effective local ambient temper- 
ature before and after melting is therefore likely to be consider- 
ably higher than the average surface temperature, and the cor- 
responding thermal gyrations would therefore be of lower am- 
plitude than those associated with fewer remelts. These lower- 
amplitude gyrations should in turn reduce thermal gradients and 
cooling rates, inducing lower thermal stresses and resulting in 
lower residual stresses. This might be thought of as analogous to 
dual-beam welding processes, which are well known to result in

Beam overlap may also play an important role in oxygen reten- 
tion in additively manufactured tungsten. Though the EB-PBF pro- 
cess operates at vacuum, some amount of residual oxygen remains, 
and may react with the tungsten part. Wendel [43] has published 
the free energy of formation of various tungsten oxides in the form 
of an Ellingham diagram, and these data show that at an oxygen 
partial pressure of 10 −7 bar (the approximate oxygen level in these 
experiments), WO 3 is stable up to 20 0 0 ◦C. Thus, at temperatures 
below 20 0 0 ◦C, i.e. during the preheat cycle, the tungsten build 
is oxidizing. However, at temperatures above 20 0 0 ◦C, i.e. while 
melting, the tungsten build is reducing. The many remelts associ- 
ated with a large beam should therefore be expected to result in 
reduced oxygen content. Wang et al. [24] have proposed that ex- 
cess oxygen beyond the solubility limit forms nanopores at grain 
boundaries, which then nucleate cracks, and similar mechanisms 
have been observed in powder metallurgy tungsten [44] . This is 
consistent with our observation above that cracks are in some 
cases associated with pores, and suggests that reducing oxygen 
content may be critical in preventing cracks. Farrell et al. [45] ob- 
serve a similar pattern in tungsten welds, where voids at grain 
boundaries coalesce and form cracks at high temperature. (Note 
that while these authors term this ”hot cracking” they do not indi- 
cate that these cracks form during solidiﬁcation. Instead, they seem 
to use the term to refer, quite literally, to cracking that takes place 
at elevated temperature.) 
It is clear that oxygen increases the propensity toward cracking 
in additively manufactured tungsten. Braun et al. [32] deliberately 
varied the oxygen content in their build chamber to study the ef- 
fect on cracking and found that decreasing the oxygen in the en- 
vironment from 800 to 20 ppm signiﬁcantly reduced, but did not 
eliminate, cracking in selective laser melted tungsten. Braun et al. 
identify oxide particles at the grain boundaries in high-oxygen

grain boundaries suﬃciently that thermal stresses generated dur- 
ing melting cause cracks. Speciﬁcally, they argue that the liquid

eutectic leading to hot-cracking. Similarly, Wang et al. [24] propose

still further. These increases in oxygen content may cause cracking 
even in a build performed at elevated temperature. EB-PBF, which 
is usually performed with a larger-diameter powder, may therefore 
be advantageous for processing oxygen-sensitive materials such as 
tungsten. 
5. Conclusions 
Pure tungsten plasma atomized powder feedstock was pro- 
cessed through electron beam powder bed fusion in an Arcam 
Q10+ system. Examining available information on the processing 
space for pure tungsten from available sources, the inﬂuence of 
surface temperature can be seen to impact material density. For 
regions of the fabricated material absent of chimney pores, dense 
and micro-crack free regions were observed. Evidence obtained 
from analyzing cracks that did occur in the EB-PBF material prop- 
agate through a solid state mechanism that could be hypotheti- 
cal suppressed through increasing the ambient temperature of the 
build, which correlates to relaxing stresses during the build and 
oxygen content to suppress the formation of brittle oxides along 
the grain boundaries. Lastly, an in-situ defect detection algorithm 
was applied for detecting porosity within pure tungsten for the 
ﬁrst time.
