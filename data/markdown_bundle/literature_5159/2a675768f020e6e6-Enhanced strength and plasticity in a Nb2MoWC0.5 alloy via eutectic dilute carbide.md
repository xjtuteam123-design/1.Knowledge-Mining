ABSTRACT 
Refractory niobium alloys are widely used in aero-engine combustion chambers and rocket propulsion systems. 
Conventional Nb-based alloys often suffer from insufficient high-temperature strengths or poor room- 
temperature plasticity. Here we report a strategy to develop a novel Nb
2
MoWC
0.5 
niobium alloy with a good 
strength-plasticity synergy by introducing eutectic NbC carbide. Microstructure characterization reveals that the 
as-prepared alloy exhibits a hypoeutectic microstructure, in which low-energy eutectic interface remains stable 
under high-temperature compression up to 1473 K. The stable low-energy metal-carbide interface enables not 
only a superior high-temperature strength ( > 1 GPa at 1473 K and 0.61 GPa at 1673 K) but also possesses good 
room-temperature plasticity (a true plastic strain of 16%) via microcrack tip blunting. The dilute NbC carbide 
with alloying elements has low stacking fault energy and improved high-temperature plastic deformation. This 
strategy provides theoretical guidance for designing next-generation high-performance high-temperature alloys 
to bypass the limitation of alloying elements in conventional refractory alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory alloys with high strength under high temperature and 
high pressure are required in the aerospace, weapons, and equipment 
fields. Nb-based alloys with a high melting point (2467 
◦
C), excellent 
high-temperature strength, good processing, and welding properties, are 
widely used in the manufacture of thrust chambers and nozzle of the 
rocket motor [ 1 , 2 ]. However, at temperatures above 1273 K, the 
strengths of pure Nb decrease substantially [ 1 ], limiting its industrial 
application. To improve the high-temperature strength, refractory 
alloying elements (e.g., W, Mo, Ta, Zr, and Hf) have been added to 
enhance the alloy by solid solution strengthening [ 3 ]. For example, 
WC-3009 alloy (Nb-30Hf-9W, wt.%) exhibited improved yield strength 
of 0.3 GPa at 1473 K [ 4 ] and Nb-30 at.% Mo and Nb-20 at.% W exhibited 
high compressive yield strengths of about 0.36 GPa and 0.31 GPa at 
1573 K, respectively [ 5 ]. However, these single-phase Nb-based alloys 
possess insufficient high-temperature strengths due to a limited addition 
of alloying elements. 
Additional efforts were made by introducing hard intermetallic 
phases (e.g., NbC and Nb
5
Si) into Nb-based alloys to enhance the high- 
temperature strengths, based on the second-phase strengthening [ 6 – 9 ]. 
The small precipitates in the Nb-based alloys act as obstacles to block the 
dislocation motion and remarkably improve the creep strength due to 
their high stability [ 3 ]. Also, a small amount of the second phase gives 
marginal strength enhancement. To bypass the additional limitation and 
optimize Nb-based alloys, Nb – Si alloys with numerous silicide phases 
are synthesized, such as Nb – 10Mo – 10Ti – 18Si and 
Nb – 10Mo – 15W – 10Ti – 18Si alloys, which have demonstrated high 
compressive yield strengths of 0.55 GPa and 0.65 GPa at 1773 K, 
respectively [ 8 , 9 ]. However, the Nb – Si alloys are noted to suffer from 
weak interfaces due to interface misfit between Nb and silicide, which 
results in poor room-temperature plasticity with compressive fracture

2.2. Computational simulations 
The effect of solute atoms on the stacking fault energy (SFE) in NbC 
and its segregation behavior to stacking faults were simulations using 
density functional theory (DFT) with Vienna ab initio simulation pack -
age (VASP) [ 22 ]. The exchange-correction functions were defined with 
the Perdew-Wang (PW91) form of the 
generalized-gradient-approximation (GGA) [ 23 ]. The interaction be -
tween valence electrons and ionic cores was defined using the 
projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [ 24 ]. Through convergence 
tests, until the force on each atom is smaller than 0.01 eV/Å, the 
plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 450 eV, and the Brillouin zone was 
sampled using a k-points mesh sampling of 9 × 9 × 1 in reciprocal space.

deformed carbide. The thin stacking faults were found to originate from 
the phase interface and terminate inside the NbC phase, as depicted by 
white circles in Fig. 9 a, suggesting that the stacking faults generate by 
the passage of partial dislocations from the BCC/NbC interface. Inter -
