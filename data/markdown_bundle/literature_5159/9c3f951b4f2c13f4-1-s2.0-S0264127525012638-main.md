ABSTRACT
Solid-solution strengthened Ni-based superalloys are widely employed in high-temperature applications due to 
their excellent mechanical strength, creep resistance, and microstructural stability. However, prolonged thermal 
exposure induces complex phase transformations and co-evolution among precipitates — including γ ′ phases, 
carbides (MC, M
23
C
6
), and topologically close-packed (TCP) phases — that fundamentally alter deformation 
mechanisms and accelerate performance degradation. Understanding these phase transformation sequences and 
their synergistic interactions is essential for controlling precipitate architectures and achieving multi-property 
optimization. Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of tailoring precipitate characteristics to 
enhance specific properties; however, a systematic framework for coordinating precipitate types, distributions, 
and evolution pathways toward synergistic performance enhancement remains lacking. This review systemati -
cally elucidates the formation mechanisms, phase transformation behaviors, and co-evolution dynamics of key 
precipitates during long-term aging, emphasizing their influence on dislocation interactions and oxidation 
resistance. Furthermore, emerging strategies for precipitate engineering based on phase synergy are summarized, 
establishing microstructure – mechanism – performance correlations that underpin the targeted design of next- 
generation Ni-based superalloys for extreme service environments.
1. Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys are extensively utilized in aerospace, power 
generation, and chemical industries due to their outstanding high- 
temperature strength, oxidation resistance, and corrosion resistance 
[ 1 , 2 ]. Among them, solid-solution strengthened Ni-based superalloys 
have garnered increasing attention for their superior microstructural 
stability and high-temperature mechanical properties. Representative 
alloys such as Inconel 617 and Haynes 230 achieve an optimized balance 
of strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance through multi-element 
solid-solution strengthening in conjunction with controlled precipita -
tion hardening [ 3 , 4 ].
These alloys typically adopt a face-centered cubic (FCC) γ matrix, 
where elements such as Cr, Fe, Al, Ti, Nb, and Mo are added to tune 
phase stability, lattice parameters, and interfacial behaviour [ 5 – 7 ]. 
While their primary strengthening mechanism is derived from solid- 
solution effects, the presence of fine γ ′ precipitates (Ni

interacting with dislocations through semi-coherent interfaces and 
ordered structures. However, during prolonged thermal exposure, γ ′ 
particles may undergo coarsening, morphological changes, or transform 
into η phases, which can reduce their strengthening contribution [ 8 ]. 
More critically, precipitate evolution is rarely an isolated process; it is 
often accompanied by multi-element diffusion and multiphase coupling 
phenomena, which collectively influence the microstructural stability 
and long-term mechanical properties.
For example, MC-type carbides may decompose into M

2.1. Alloy design principles
Typical solid-solution strengthened Ni-based superalloys include 
Inconel 617 (IN617), Inconel 800H, Inconel 800HT, and C-HRA-2. These 
alloys generally contain over a dozen alloying elements, designed 
around three fundamental strategies: 
(1) strengthening the matrix via solid-solution effects,
(2) enhancing high-temperature performance through controlled 
precipitation, and(3) improving service reliability by grain 
boundary (GB) strengthening.
Among these, solid-solution strengthening serves as the foundational 
mechanism in alloy design. By incorporating solute atoms with signifi -
cant atomic size and lattice misfit into the γ matrix, elevated high- 
temperature strength can be achieved. Key solutes include Ni, Co, Fe, 
Cr, Mo, W, Re, and Ru, with their solubility largely governed by atomic 
radius mismatch with Ni. A smaller mismatch generally results in higher 
solubility and a more stable solid solution. For instance, in C-HRA-2 , Cr, 
Co, and Mo remain in solid solution within the matrix, significantly 
enhancing both high-temperature strength and corrosion resistance 
[ 15 ].
However, solid-solution strengthening alone is insufficient to meet 
the stringent requirements for creep resistance and structural stability at 
elevated temperatures.. As such, precipitation hardening becomes a 
critical supplementary mechanism. In solid-solution strengthened 
superalloys, the γ ′ phase (Ni

boron can reduce grain boundary diffusion rates, delay cavity nucle -

relieving local stress concentrations and improving creep resistance 
[ 27 , 28 ]. Nevertheless, excessive B content must be avoided, as it can 
lead to the formation of brittle, plate-like borides at grain boundar -

0.03 wt%, resulting in premature embrittlement and reduced service life

The σ phase can manifest in various morphologies, including intra -

along grain boundaries. These morphologies exert markedly different 
effects on mechanical properties: intragranular needle- or plate-like σ 
precipitates tend to impede dislocation motion and facilitate crack 
initiation, thereby undermining structural stability, while globular σ at 
grain boundaries is relatively benign [ 68 ].
Formation of the σ phase also leads to Cr depletion from the γ matrix, 
reducing solid-solution strength and degrading oxidation and corrosion 
resistance. To enhance high-temperature strength, alloy design often 
involves additions of refractory elements such as Re, W, Mo, and Ta. 
While beneficial for γ ′ strengthening, these elements simultaneously 
increase the risk of TCP phase precipitation — particularly σ — especially 
when Cr content exceeds ~ 25 wt%, significantly accelerating σ for -
mation under long-term exposure [ 69 , 70 ].
The nucleation of σ is governed by interfacial energy and lattice 
matching conditions. It tends to nucleate preferentially at low-energy 
interfaces or crystallographic planes with good structural compati -

In summary, precipitation in solid-solution strengthened Ni-based

complex phase interactions. As heat treatment progresses — particularly

including coarsening, morphological evolution, phase transformation, 
and dissolution – reprecipitation. The following section focuses on these 
precipitation processes and their evolution pathways under thermal

During long-term aging, precipitate evolution alters interactions 
with dislocations, where size variations drive deformation mechanism 
transitions and affect high-temperature performance. When the γ ′ phase 
is fine and uniformly dispersed, strengthening is mainly achieved via 
dislocation shearing. This process involves the formation of antiphase 
boundaries (APBs) or stacking faults, which increase the energy barrier 
for dislocation motion, thereby enhancing both yield strength and creep 
resistance. As the γ ′ particles coarsen, the deformation mechanism shifts 
toward Orowan looping, wherein dislocations bypass the precipitates. 
This transition reduces the obstacle strength against dislocation motion 
and promotes dislocation accumulation in the matrix channels, 
increasing the likelihood of local strain concentration and creep-induced 
damage [ 104 , 105 ].
The relationship between γ ′ precipitate size and deformation mech -
anisms has been validated in Ni-based superalloys, for example in 
powder metallurgy alloys, where weakly coupled shearing dominates 
below ~15 nm, strongly coupled shearing prevails between 15 and 60 
nm, and Orowan looping becomes dominant above 60 nm ( Fig. 8a ) 
[ 107 ]. As γ ′ precipitates coarsen, the increasing resistance to dislocation 
cutting eventually forces dislocations to bypass the precipitates via the 
Orowan mechanism. However, within an intermediate size range, full 
dislocation shearing becomes energetically unfavorable while bypassing 
remains incomplete, giving rise to faulted shearing as a transitional 
mode. TEM observations ( Fig. 8b ) reveal shear-induced stacking faults 
in γ ′ precipitates of this critical size range, providing direct micro -
structural evidence of this size-driven mechanism transition, which is a 
key contributor to performance degradation during long-term high- 
temperature service.

size are also closely linked to fracture behavior, especially under pro -
longed loading or localized strain conditions. As shown in Fig. 9 , large 
grains combined with a high γ ′ volume fraction enhance internal 
strengthening but also increase the susceptibility to grain boundary

intergranular fracture and reducing ductility [ 108 ].

ductility and fracture toughness. These findings underscore that the co- 
evolution of precipitates and grain structure not only governs defor -

high-temperature stability.

5. Optimization of high-temperature performance through 
precipitate behavior control
During long-term thermal exposure at elevated temperatures, the 
precipitation behavior of Ni-based superalloys undergoes significant 
evolution, which is closely coupled with their mechanical and chemical 
properties. Consequently, tailoring precipitation processes to optimize 
microstructures has become a key strategy for enhancing the overall 
high-temperature performance of these alloys. Unlike previous reviews 
that mainly emphasized the role of individual precipitate types, the 
present section highlights the synergistic evolution between γ ′ pre -
cipitates and carbides, and introduces a “ precipitate synergy – multi- 
property linkage ” framework. This framework not only elucidates the 
intrinsic correlations between precipitation behavior and high- 
temperature strength, creep resistance, and oxidation stability, but 
also provides new insights into achieving simultaneous optimization of 
multiple properties.
In these alloys, precise control over precipitation is not only essential 
for improving high-temperature mechanical properties but also plays a 
critical role in modulating dislocation dynamics and deformation 
mechanisms. By systematically designing the size, volume fraction, and 
spatial distribution of γ ′ precipitates, the transition of deformation 
modes from dislocation shearing to Orowan bypassing can be effectively 
delayed, thereby extending the creep strengthening regime and signifi -
cantly enhancing yield strength and creep life.
Studies have shown that a moderate γ ′ volume fraction with fine, 
dispersed distribution enhances dislocation pinning, mitigating local 
strain concentration during creep deformation. When the γ ′ precipitate 
size is controlled within the range of 32 – 118 nm, the alloy exhibits an 
optimal balance between strength and ductility, with peak yield strength 
achieved at approximately 65 nm [ 46 ]. However, while optimizing the 
size and dispersion of a single γ ′ population can achieve a 
strength – ductility balance, further enhancement of creep performance 
requires tailoring multi-scale precipitate distributions. In this context, 
Zhao et al. [ 146 ] developed a dual-modal γ ′ structure in the K4800 alloy 
through a solution plus sub-solution heat treatment process ( Fig. 11 ), 
consisting of primary γ ′
1

1

strengthening, which contributes to extending creep life and improving 
microstructural stability at high temperatures.

strength, microstructural stability, and oxidation resistance. This re -

Despite recent advancements, several critical challenges remain in 
elucidating the coupling mechanisms between precipitate evolution and 
high-temperature performance: 
(1) Limitations in γ′ Phase Research : Current research mainly ad -
dresses γ ′ coarsening during long-term aging, while their 
morphological evolution, hierarchical distribution, and in -
teractions with η , σ phases, and carbides remain insufficiently 
understood. Advanced in-situ and 3D characterization tech -

tomography (APT), are needed to clarify the multiscale co- 
evolution of γ ′ precipitates, including their morphology transi -

structure – property degradation models.
(2) Unclear Mechanisms of Multi-Phase Co-Evolution and 
Interface: Interactions: The co-evolution of various precipitates 
under coupled high-temperature and stress conditions is not yet 
fully understood. The selective influence of special grain 
boundaries (e.g., Σ 3 twin boundaries, low-energy interfaces) on 
precipitation behavior requires further investigation. Moreover, 
the impact of elemental redistribution and interfacial structural 
changes on crack initiation and damage propagation remains to 
be clarified. Future investigations should integrate first-principles 
calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and phase-field 
modeling to predict interfacial energetics, segregation behav -
iors, and multi-phase co-evolution pathways from atomic to 
macroscale. Combining these simulations with advanced multi -
scale characterization will provide a comprehensive framework 
for understanding interface-precipitate interactions.
(3) Lack of Targeted Control Strategies: Although the correlation 
between precipitate behavior and high-temperature mechanical 
properties is well established, performance-driven precipitation 
design remains in its early stages. Achieving synergistic optimi -
zation of strength, creep resistance, and oxidation performance 
requires strategies for tailoring precipitate types, compositions, 
morphologies, and spatial distributions to enhance the coopera -
tive interactions among γ ′ phases, carbides, and TCP phases.

2) Development of performance-oriented predictive models for pre -
