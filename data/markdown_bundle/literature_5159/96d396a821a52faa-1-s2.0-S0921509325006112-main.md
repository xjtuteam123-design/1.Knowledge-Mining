1. Introduction
Traditional titanium alloys are recognized for their high specific 
strength, low density, and ability to withstand high and low tempera -
tures, which makes them well-suited for aerospace applications. How -
ever, they demonstrate poor ductility at room temperature. Meanwhile, 
traditional Ni-based alloys show high service temperatures but con -
strained by the low specific yield strength. Therefore, there is an urgent 
requirement for structural materials that are lighter, stronger, more 
heat-resistant, to further enhance the operational efficiency and lifespan 
of next-generation aerospace components.
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), proposed by Canter and Yeh [ 1 , 2 ], 
display exciting application potential due to their unique effects and 
promising properties [ 3 – 6 ], such as excellent room- and 
high-temperature strength [ 7 , 8 ], thermal stability [ 9 ], oxidation resis -
tance [ 10 ] and corrosion performance [ 11 ]. HEAs containing Cr, Al, and 
Ni elements can form protective oxide layers and enhance corrosion 
resistance [ 12 ]. Additionally, their oxidation and corrosion performance 
can also be influenced by different microstructural features. To develop 
aerospace materials meeting the requirements, researchers have devel -
oped various lightweight HEA (LWHEA) systems, composing of light -
weight elements such as Al, Sc, Ti, Zr along with one or two refractory 
elements like V, Nb, Mo. They usually exhibit higher compressive/ -
tensile yield strength [ 13 – 16 ] than commercial nickel alloys [ 17 , 18 ] and 
titanium alloys [ 19 , 20 ] at room temperature and high temperature, thus 
are considered to be suitable for high-performance applications in the 
aerospace industry in recent years [ 21 , 22 ]. For example, the

interface forefront [ 35 ], leading to a reduction of eutectic reaction rate.

heat, which reduces the undercooling of the alloy and slows down the 
eutectic reaction. Therefore, the eutectic layers at eutectic boundaries 
become thicker [ 33 , 36 , 37 ].
