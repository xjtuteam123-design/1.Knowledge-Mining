ABSTRACT
SS 321 is a potential structural alloy for elevated temperature applications. In this work, austenitic stainless steel 
321 (SS 321) was successfully fabricated via wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM). Microstructural analysis 
of the wrought SS 321 revealed the presence of equiaxed grains. In contrast, the WAAM 321 wall structure 
comprised equiaxed, columnar, and elongated dendrites with ferrite fractions ranging from 3.6 to 5.9 FN. 
Further, the WAAM 321 exhibited better hardness than the wrought alloy. WAAM 321 samples exhibited 
excellent tensile properties compared to the SS 321 wrought alloy, while the tensile strength and ductility 
decreased considerably from room temperature (RT) to elevated temperatures (900 
◦
C). The average tensile 
strength of SS 321 at 900 
◦
C was 108 ± 6 MPa, and the WAAM 321 samples showed higher strength (~135 ± 7 
MPa). The decrease in tensile strength is attributed to the activation of a multi-slip system, resulting in a lower 
resistance to dislocation motion at elevated temperatures. Also, serrated yielding was observed at 600 
◦
C due to 
dynamic strain aging. The fractured surface characteristics align with the ductility trend. This research highlights 
the role of WAAM in manufacturing high-performing structures for elevated temperature environments.
1. Introduction
Stabilized austenitic stainless steel (ASS) grades have a wide spec -
trum of engineering applications in many important industries because 
of their outstanding processability, weldability, excellent tensile and 
corrosion properties at elevated temperatures [ 1 , 2 ]. The suggested 
maximum service temperature for the stabilized ASSs exposed to 
continuous service conditions is < 925 
◦
C [ 3 ]. Due to their excellent 
tensile properties at elevated temperatures (SS 321 has a tensile strength 
of ~320 MPa at 650 
◦
C [ 3 ], stabilized ASSs are preferred in nuclear 
reactor systems, aircraft exhaust systems & heat exchangers, reheater 
tubes, superheaters, boilers, turbine blades, etc., [ 4 , 5 ]. Ti-stabilized SS 
321 is highly preferred for enormous applications in the temperature 
range of 400 – 900 
◦
C as Ti addition restricts chromium carbides ’ pre -
cipitation by forming titanium carbides (TiC) and thereby avoids weld 
decay [ 6 , 7 ]. However, manufacturing complicated geometries via 
traditional processing routes is not achievable at low cost [ 8 , 9 ]. With 
additive manufacturing (AM), there is a unique and promising oppor -
tunity to fabricate near-net-shaped critical components via a 
layer-by-layer deposition strategy with less material wastage [ 10 ]. This 
benefit may result in notable improvements in the weight reduction of 
components, resulting in better system efficiency and production time. 
Compared to arc-based directed energy deposition AM technology, 
electron, and laser-beam-based AM systems have higher equipment 
costs, lower deposition rates, and limited ability to produce large com -
ponents despite having excellent precision and accuracy. In the fabri -
cation industry, arc-based wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) has 
emerged as a potential and versatile technique to manufacture Fe-based 
and Ni-based alloys [ 11 , 12 ]. The gas metal arc welding (GMAW) based 
WAAM has been highly preferred for fabricating medium-to-large parts 
with higher deposition rates and less lead times [ 13 – 15 ]. Excellent 
tensile properties are required when considering WAAM-processed 
components for elevated temperature applications.

321 and SS 347 and has a composition that is exactly the same as SS 
321 ’ s. The filler material has the following chemical composition: Fe-

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 3 a shows the macrostructure of the WAAM 321 wall component, 
devoid of defects such as cracks, fissures, and pores. Fig. 3 b – e presents 
the microstructural morphologies of the wrought SS 321 and WAAM 321 
along the BD (arrow mark indicates the BD) at the bottom, middle, and 
top layers. The wrought SS 321 consists of equiaxed grains along with a 
small amount of ferrite ( < 0.5 FN), and TiC [ 17 , 21 ] is noticed within the 
dominant austenitic matrix as shown in Fig. 3 b. EBSD IPF map shows the 
random orientation of grains, and the average grain size was ~21 μ m. 
Also, the fraction of low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) and high-angle 
grain boundaries (HAGBs) were 3 % and 97 %, respectively. Further, 
Fig. 3 c – e illustrates the microstructural variation along the BD at the 
bottom, middle, and top layers. EBSD IPF maps and micrographs 
confirm the existence of equiaxed, columnar, and elongated dendrites in 
the WAAM 321 wall. The length of the dendrites increased along the BD 
from bottom to top. The SS 321 wall is full of columnar dendrites of 
austenite (shown in white), along with a small amount of residual ferrite 
(shown in black). This confirms the mode of solidification as 
ferritic-austenitic mode (FA mode), where a small amount of retained 
delta-ferrite exists in the austenitic matrix. The formation of residual 
ferrite has been reported in previous works on ASS fabricated via AM 
processes [ 20 , 21 ].
The average grain diameter in the bottom, middle, and top layers 
were ~172 μ m, ~230 μ m, and ~347.5 μ m, respectively. However, a few 
dendrites in WAAM 321 are longer than 2 mm, mostly elongated and 
columnar in shape. From the grain angle boundary maps, the fraction of 
LAGBs and HAGBs in the bottom, middle, and top regions were 53 % & 
47 %, 42 % & 58 %, and 70 % & 30 %, respectively. As heat 
accumulation decreased, the size of columnar dendrites increased [ 25 ]. 
From ferrite measurements, it is clear that the ferrite fraction varies 
between 3.6 and 5.9 FN or % along the BD from bottom to top, i.e., it is 
higher at the bottom layers and lower at the top layers due to the varying 
thermal history and heat accumulation characteristics. According to 
earlier work, a ferrite fraction less than 8 % is equivalent to FN [ 26 ]. The 
variable ferrite fraction influences the WAAM 321 wall ’ s hardness and 
tensile characteristics.
To look into the local heterogeneity of the microstructure along the 
BD, hardness mapping was performed. The wrought SS 321 had an 
average hardness of 174 ± 4 HV. Fig. 4 shows the contour hardness 
maps of the WAAM 321 in the bottom ( Fig. 4 a), middle ( Fig. 4 b), and top 
( Fig. 4 c) regions, respectively. The contour maps clearly highlight the 
significant difference in the hardness values. Soft and hard spots can be 
noticed on the maps, and it does not form any particular pattern. The 
existence of residual delta-ferrite can corroborate the hard spots. The 
average hardness in the bottom, middle, and top regions were 230 ± 5 
HV, 215 ± 6 HV, and 198 ± 9 HV, respectively. The standard deviation 
is higher at the top region than the bottom and middle regions due to the 
slower cooling rate, resulting in elongated dendrites with less ferrite 
fraction. The hardness results are in line with the microstructure and 
ferrite measurements.

4. Conclusion
This study evaluated the microstructure and the relative mechanical 
properties of wrought SS 321 and WAAM 321 from RT to 900 
◦
C. The 
fracture surfaces were analyzed. Also, this work validates the feasibility 
of WAAM technology for fabricating parts for elevated temperature 
environments. The outcomes of the present work can be summarized as 
follows. 
❖ The as-printed WAAM 321 wall was free from defects, and the 
complex thermal history influenced the ferrite fraction along the BD.
❖ The wrought SS 321-BM has a dominant austenitic phase with a 
small fraction of ferrite, while the residual delta ferrite in WAAM 321 
ranged between 3.6 and 5.9 FN along the BD.
❖ EBSD analysis revealed the difference in the dendritic microstructure 
of WAAM 321 specimens along the BD, and the dendrites ’ average 
grain diameter in the bottom, middle, and top layers of SS 321 were 
~172 μ m, ~230 μ m, and ~347.5 μ m, respectively.
❖ The average hardness of WAAM 321 was higher than the SS 321-BM 
and varied along the BD between 189 HV and 235 HV due to the 
varying microstructure and ferrite fraction.
❖ The WAAM 321 specimens showed better mechanical properties at 
RT and elevated temperatures than the wrought SS 321. The average 
UTS of WAAM 321 specimens at 900 
◦
C was close to 135 ± 7 MPa, 
which is higher than the wrought SS 321, having a UTS of 108 ± 6 
MPa.
❖ The fractured surfaces of SS 321 and WAAM 321 specimens revealed 
the existence of dimples and voids, while the dimple size and density 
varied with increasing test temperature. Also, the fracture charac -
teristics changed at 600 and 900 
◦
C.
❖ WAAM technology holds significant potential for the fabrication of 
complex components intended for high-temperature applications 
across various industries.
