abstract
New γ0-strengthened Co-base superalloys show an interesting potential for high temperature applica-
tions. However, protective coatings are needed as for Ni-base superalloys to ensure suf ﬁcient oxidation
and corrosion resistance. Therefore the properties of a commercial coating on a multinary new
γ0-strengthened Co-base superalloy have been studied. Especially the in ﬂuence of the coating process on
the substrate also after long term annealing is discussed. It was found that the highly deformed areas at
the coating – substrate interface indicated by a high local misorientation and caused by the sandblasting
process led to a recrystallization of the interdiffusion zone during the age hardening heat treatment.
A chemical gradient of γ and γ
0 promoting elements was found in the interdiffusion zone causing a
change in hardness as measured by nanoindentation. Depending on the composition two separate
recrystallized regions formed in the interdiffusion zone, one with single phase γ-(Co,Ni) and the other
with a cellular two phase microstructure of discontinuously grown γ and γ0.

1. Introduction
Co-base superalloys with γ/γ0 microstructure have been intro-
duced by Sato in 2006 [1] and much research has been conducted
since then. The alloys have evolved from the simple ternary Co –
Al–W system to more complex alloys with several alloying
elements [2]. However, a drawback is the rather poor intrinsic
oxidation resistance. Systematical research was conducted by
Klein et al. [3,4], which showed that the oxidation resistance can
be improved with additions of Boron and Silicon due to the
formation of a protective Al 2O3 layer. The oxidation behavior was
further improved by adding Chromium which builds Cr 2O3 [5].
Nevertheless, oxidation of Co-base superalloys leads to a depletion
of elements like Al and Cr close to the surface which change the γ/
γ0 microstructure and hence are detrimental to the mechanical
properties [2]. So far, most work was focused on the intrinsic
oxidation behavior of Co-based superalloys. A successful use in the
hottest parts in high-temperature combustion environments,
however, would require a coating on the Co-base superalloys that
provides the necessary resistance against oxidation and corrosion
under the harsh operation conditions. In addition, for the ther-
mally highly loaded parts a ceramic thermal barrier coating is
essential. The appropriate bonding to the substrate is also guar-
anteed by the coating therefore it is often called bond coat (BC) [6].
A well-established bond coat on Ni-based superalloys is of the
MCrAlY type (M ¼Ni and/or Co), which has a high corrosion and
oxidation resistance along with good ductility [7–9]. Recently, a
MCrAlY coating was applied to new γ0 strengthened Co-base alloys
by Vermaak et al. [10]. They found that the oxidation resistance
could be enhanced for a simple Co –Al–W alloy and one with Cr
and Ta additions, although, many refractory rich phases in the
unstable Co-base substrate were found in both alloys. In this work
coating of the more stable Co-base superalloy ERBOCo-0 with a
commercially available CoNiCrAlY bond coat is investigated and
the in ﬂuence of long term exposure at 1000 1C is discussed. The
intrinsic oxidation resistance, phase stability and the mechanical
properties of ERBOCo-0 are enhanced by the alloying additions Ni,
Cr, Si, Ti, and Ta. Characterization of the microstructure was carried
out with scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive
X-ray analysis (EDX) and electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD).
The investigations are focused on the interface between the Co-
base substrate and the MCrAlY bond coat since this region has the
highest difference in chemical composition and diffusion is
expected leading to phase transformations and change in mechan-
ical properties. An established method to investigate mechanical
properties on a local scale is nanoindentation. As shown in Refs.
[1 1,12], hardness and Young's modulus of individual phases on
diffusion coated Ni-base superalloys can be successfully measured.

2. Experimental
The investigated Co-base superalloy ERBOCo-0 was cast as a
single crystal in a Bridgeman process at the chair of Metal Science
and Technology of the FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg. It has a γ/γ0
microstructure and its composition is given in Table 1 . The γ0
solvus temperature is 1 100 1C which has been measured by
differential scanning calorimetry.
Prior to the coating process, the Co-base substrate was solution
heat treated at 1300 1C for 4 h in order to dissolve the γ0 and
further intermetallic phases in the interdendritic region and
homogenize the material. Subsequently, the samples were sand-
blasted to roughen the surface and strip the natural oxide for a
better adhesion of the bond coat. The used CoNiCrAlY protective
bond coat (BC) was sprayed using an Amdry 9954 powder by
Oerlikon Metco Europe GmbH and it was applied at Forschungszen-
trum Jülich by vacuum plasma spraying, using a Sulzer Metco F4
plasma gun. The resulting bond coats have thicknesses of 200 mm.
The details of the deposition process are given in [14]. The chosen
bond coat is also based on Co to enhance the adhesion to the Co-
base substrate and reduce the amount of interdiffusion due to its
similar fraction of Co. The chemical composition of the bond coat
is listed in Table 1.
Diffusion annealing and age hardening were carried out in two
steps at 1050 1C for 4 h and 900 1C for 16 h in Argon atmosphere to
ensure chemical bonding to the substrate and achieve a technically
relevant γ
0 particle size. This state will be called “diffusion
annealed (DA) ”. Subsequently, the samples were aged at 1000 1C
for 200 h in Argon atmosphere and will be referred to as “long
term heat treatment (HT) ”. Although oxidation was reduced with
this measure, its effects on the substrate would have been rather
negligible even in an oxidizing atmosphere due to the large
coating thickness.
The cross-sections of the samples were ground and polished
after each heat treatment step for further characterization. Micro-
structural analysis was conducted with a Zeiss Crossbeam 1540
EsB FIB/SEM. EDX measurements were performed with an INCA
PentaFET-x3 from Oxford Instruments. Area scans of 50 mmb y
10 mm on cross-sections of the samples were carried out in the
interdiffusion zone (IDZ). In addition, point scans with a high
spatial resolution were conducted. A Nordlys2 Detector from
Oxford Instruments was used for EBSD scans. The maps consist
of 120 /C2 250 pixels with a step size of 0.3 mm. Local misorientation
maps have also been determined in order to investigate qualita-
tively the amount of deformation, e.g. the dislocation density
introduced by sandblasting. The map was created by comparing
the orientation of each pixel with its surrounding pixels in a
11/C2 1 1 matrix. A threshold subgrain angle was set to be 5 1.
Furthermore, phase characterization of the bond coat was
carried out using XRD with a Siemens D500 on samples which
have undergone the same heat treatment as the coated Co-base
superalloy.
In order to determine the hardness and Young's modulus of the
different phases nanoindentation was performed using a Berkovich
tip. This requires deformation free surfaces which were achieved by
24 h vibrational polishing with an acidic 0.04 mm grain size SiO 2
suspension. A MTS Nanoindenter XP was used in the continuous
stiffness mode (CSM), where the indenter oscillates slightly at a
frequency of 45 Hz and therefore the stiffness can be measured at
every point of the experiment. A maximum indentation depth of
500 nm was used. The tip calibration and the hardness calculation
were conducted in accordance with the Oliver –Pharr method [15].
The distance between indents was set to be at least 20 times the
indentation depth to eliminate the in ﬂuence of neighboring
indents, which corresponds to 10 mm. Cross-sections of the samples
were investigated. Detailed measurements were made in the IDZ
with a ﬁner array of indents at an indentation depth of 200 nm.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructure
The bond coat with an overall thickness of 200 mm consists of
two phases that were identi ﬁed with XRD as β-(Co,Ni)Al with a B2
crystal structure and fcc γ-(Co,Ni) solid solution. A back scattered
electron image of the microstructure after the diffusion annealing
is shown in Fig. 1 (a) and after HT in Fig. 1 (b). Binarised SEM
pictures (see Fig. 1(b)) were used to determine the area fraction of
γ:β, which equals 2:1 in both conditions. The black areas beneath
the BC were identi ﬁed with EDX as alumina, which are remaining
particles from sand blasting prior to the coating process.
An interdiffusion zone has formed beneath the bond coat
which has a constant appearance all along the interface. A detailed
view of the IDZ between the BC and the Co-base substrate is
shown in Fig. 2(a). It can be divided into two parts: a homogenous
single phase region right underneath the β depleted region of the
BC and a cellular two phase region. The single phase region was
identiﬁed with EBSD and EDX as recrystallized γ-(Co,Ni)
ss and will
be referred to as recrystallized γ (RX-γ). Similar cellular micro-
structures are known in literature as secondary reaction zones
(SRZ), which are found in high refractory Ni-base superalloys
and underneath diffusion coatings [16,17]. These are recrystallized
areas consisting of γ/γ0 discontinuously grown in a cellular
arrangement. The substrate shows a typical cuboidal two phase
microstructure with fcc γ-(Co,Ni) and L1 2 γ0-(Co,Ni)3(Al,W,Ti,Ta).
No topologically closed packed (TCP) precipitates were found in
the substrate.
After thermal loading, the RX- γ region increases in size. The
grains grow and occasionally twinning is observed (see Fig. 2(b)). In
the SRZ area the γ0 phase retains a ﬁne precipitate size adjacent to
RX-γ whereas it coarsens strongly close to the substrate. It is also
noticeable that the grain size in the bond coat coarsens after thermal
loading. The ratio of the volume content of γ and β phase, however,
stays constant as can be seen by comparing the binarized images of

4. Discussion
Between the Co-base superalloy substrate and the MCrAlY
bond coat the development of a broad interdiffusion zone with a
recrystallized area is found. The region beneath the bond coat is
identiﬁed as fcc γ phase (Co, Ni) and the area close to the substrate
as discontinuously coarsened γ/γ0. Fig. 9 shows a series of sche-
matic ﬁgures to illustrate the development of the RX-IDZ. Fig. 9(a)
represents the as sprayed condition of the bond coat with a γ/γ0
substrate below. The gray shading indicates a high dislocation
density from sandblasting prior to the coating process. Walston
et al. investigated the effect of surface preparation on the forma-
tion of secondary reaction zones (SRZ) [18]. They found that shot
peening is introducing the highest amount of strain energy and
thus the nucleation of a SRZ is enhanced and the complete
interface between bond coat and substrate is covered with SRZ.
Sandblasting however introduces a lower amount of strain energy
but it still leads to the formation of SRZ which is also observed
here. The highly strained area stretches to a certain extend into the
substrate and gives rise to the formation of the RX-IDZ during the
diffusion annealing step ( Fig. 9 (b)). The recrystallization starts at
the original bond coat/substrate interface at the beginning of the
heat treatment where the highest deformations are located. The
newly generated high angle grain boundary moves further into the
substrate with time and the elements diffuse as it is shown in the
EDX plot (see Fig. 8 ). Depending on the chemical composition,
these recrystallized grains consist of single γ or discontinuously
coarsened γ/γ
0. When the concentration of γ0 forming elements
sinks below the maximum solubility of these elements in fcc-Co,
the γ0-precipitates dissolve and a single phase region of γ is
formed. With longer annealing times ( Fig. 9 (c)), the single phase
region grows because the threshold concentration to form γ0 phase
moves further towards the substrate due to ongoing diffusion.
Therefore, a sharp interface between the two phase γ/γ0 and the
single phase γ region is observed in the large grains close to the
substrate. The IPF map in Fig. 4(b) conﬁrms that the orientation of
these grains is even constant across this interface which is possible
due to the similar crystal structure and high coherency between γ
and γ0 phases.
Basically, the development and movement of the RX-IDZ can be
linked to the area of high deformation in the substrate. This will
continue until the stored strain energy is not suf ﬁcient any more
for further transformation of ﬁne γ/γ0 to the cellular discontinu-
ously coarsened γ/γ0.
The nanoindentation maps generally show that the γ phase has
a lower hardness than γ0 which is consistent with literature for Ni-
and Co-base superalloys [19,20]. Even in the SRZ, the low hardness
data can be correlated to indentations in coarsened γ. The
substrate and the SRZ have nearly the same chemical composition
and accordingly, the same hardness. In both heat treatment states,
a gradient is found in hardness and chemical composition,
respectively.
The fact that the recrystallized, discontinuously grown areas
consist only of the γ and γ
0 phases shows the higher stability of this
alloy compared to ternary Co –Al–W alloys [21]. If the alloy was
unstable, other or further thermodynamically stable phases would
form similarly to the TCP phases that appear in the SRZ of
unstable, refractory element containing Ni-base superalloys
[16,18,22].
5. Conclusions
A multinary γ/γ0 Co-base superalloy ERBOCo-0 was coated with
a commercial CoNiCrAlY bond coat and subjected to a heat
treatment to show the in ﬂuence of the coating on the substrate.
EDX, EBSD, analyses and nanoindentation measurements were
carried out to characterize the changes in the interdiffusion zone.
The conclusions are as follows:
/C15 An interdiffusion zone developed with recrystallized grains of
single γ phase beneath the bond coat and a cellular, discon-
tinuously coarsened γ/γ0 microstructure close to the substrate.
This is attributed to the high degree of deformation that was
introduced through sandblasting prior to the coating process.
/C15 The EDX analyses show an increase of the γ0 forming elements
towards the substrate. At a certain threshold concentration,
the γ0 phase dissolves and a single phase region of γ develops.
With further annealing time, the RX-IDZ broadens until the
necessary deformation energy from the substrate has been
consumed.
/C15 Local misorientation maps indicate that after long term heat
treatment, areas in the substrate with a high local misorienta-
tion become smaller while the size of the RX-IDZ increases.
/C15 The microstructure and the chemical composition are in good
agreement with the hardness maps. The lowest hardness was
measured close to the bond coat and in the γ phase of the SRZ.
Finely dispersed γ/γ
0 in the SRZ and the Co-base superalloy
shows a higher hardness due to increased solid solution
strengthening and because of a generally higher hardness of
γ0 compared to γ.
/C15 No TCP phases were found in the Co-base alloy during the
whole heat treatment process. Therefore, the bond coat serves
its purpose to protect the underlying Co-base substrate from
oxidation without altering much of its microstructure and
hence also the mechanical properties. The fact that no further
intermetallic phases are formed in the cellular, discontinuously
coarsened areas show the high phase stability of the investi-
gated Co-base superalloy.
