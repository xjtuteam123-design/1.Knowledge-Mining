abstract
The effects of refractory ( R) elements ( R ¼ Re, Ru, W, or Ta) on a base Ni-Al-Cr alloy are studied, for an
aging temperature of 1073 K (800 /C14 C) and an aging time of 256 h, employing atom-probe tomography
(APT) and ﬁrst-principles calculations. We ﬁnd that there are strong attractive chemical binding energies
between R-elements and solute ( S) atoms ( S ¼ Al, Cr) in Ni-Al-Cr based alloys utilizing experimental
partial radial distribution function (RDF) results, and ﬁrst-principles calculations performed at 0 K. We
demonstrate that correlated R-S binding energies play a key role in the observed Ni retention-excesses at
g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-structure) interfaces at aging times as long as 256 h. The total reduction of the g(f.c.c.)/
g0(L12) interfacial energy, as a result of Ni interfacial-excesses in both g(f.c.c.)-matrix and g0(L12)-pre-
cipitates, lies between /C0 0.16 ± 0.06 mJ m /C0 2 and /C0 0.05 ± 0 . 0 2m Jm/C0 2. The R-S binding energies cause
changes in the compositional diffusion ﬂux-vectors in and out of g0(L12)-precipitates, which result in
larger solvent Ni retention-excesses and wider interfacial compositional widths at 256 h, when
compared with the base Ni-Al-Cr alloy. Refractory elements are slow diffusers in nickel and the attractive
R-Cr binding energies decelerate the solute diffusional ﬂuxes, which results in a decrease of the Ni
diffusivity, which in turn hinders the ﬂux of Ni atoms away from the
g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interfaces.

1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are the current materials utilized for tur-
bine blades of aerospace jet engines and land-based natural-gas
ﬁred power generators, due to their superior mechanical properties
and corrosion, oxidation, and creep resistance at elevated temper-
atures [1e3]. These alloys owe their extraordinary high-
temperature properties to their unique two-phase single-crystal
microstructure, comprised of a high volume-fraction, ca. 80%, of
coherent Ni
3Al-type g0(L12)-precipitates, which are dispersed in a
g(f.c.c.)-matrix. The crystal structures of the g(f.c.c.)-matrix and
g0(L12)-precipitates are both cubic and the small lattice-parameter
mismatch between the two-phases leads to the formation of
coherent g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-structure) heterophase interfaces. The
nature of the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-structure) interfaces is of utmost
importance for the physical properties of Ni-based superalloys. For
instance, interfacial segregation (Gibbsian excess) of different ele-
ments at g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-structure) interfaces reduce their interfa-
cial free energy, which is a key result of the classical Gibbs
adsorption isotherm [4e6]. This is important because the
morphological evolution of the g0-precipitates can be understood
as a competition between the elastic self-energy of g0-precipitates
and the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-structure) interfacial free energy [7]. More-
over, the coarsening resistance of g0-precipitates in Ni-based su-
peralloys is dependent on the interfacial Gibbs free energy of the g-
matrix/g0-precipitate interface [8e16], and critically on the diffu-
sivities and interdiffusivities of Ni, Al and the other alloying ele-
ments in concentrated multicomponent alloys.
Ternary Ni-Al-Cr systems are important as they represent the
basis of commercial superalloys, which can contain as many as 12
or 13 alloying elements[1,2]. Atom-probe tomography (APT) [1 7e19]
has been utilized to investigate phase separation (precipitation) in

Pareige et al. [22,23] combined APT and vacancy-mediated lattice

ternary alloy. Similarly, we have previously studied a series of
ternary Ni-Al-Cr alloys utilizing both APT and LKMC simulations
including Ni-7.5Al-8.5Cr, Ni-5.2Al-14.2Cr, and Ni-6.5Al-9.5Cr at.%,
all aged at 873 K (600 /C14 C) [24e28], and a Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr at.% alloy
aged at 1073 K (800 /C14 C) [29,30]. In our previous studies on ternary
Ni-Al-Cr alloys [25,26], localized Ni retention-excesses are
observed during the early nucleation and growth stages of g0(L12)-
precipitates, which exhibit different diffusional ﬂux couplings
across the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interface that strongly affect kinetic ex-

different diffusional ﬂux couplings two adjacent g0-precipitates can
interact, via the overlap of their diffusion ﬁelds, across their in-
terfaces over distances that are greater than the radii of the g0(L12)
precipitates, which leads to the formation of interconnecting necks
between the g0-precipitates [26] and this in turn leads to the
coagulation-coalescence mechanism of coarsening. The temporally
evolving concentration proﬁles across g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interfaces are
not in local equilibrium and therefore the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interfacial
free energy is greater than for fully equilibrated g0-precipitates at
very long aging times.
Additionally, we have implemented different techniques to
study systematically the roles of small additions (2.0 at.%) of re-
fractory elements on the g/g0 decompositions when a model
ternary Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr at.% superalloy is aged at 1073 K (800 /C14 C)
for seven aging times varying from 0 to 256 h [5,29e35]. More
speciﬁcally, we reported previously on four quaternary alloys Ni-
10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Re [5,31], Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0W [29,30], Ni-
10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Ta [32,33] and Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Ru at.% [34],
one quinary alloy Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-1.0Re-1.0Ru at.% [35] and one
alloy with six elements Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-1.0W-0.5Re-0.5Ru at.%[35],
which focus mainly on the temporal evolution of the nano-
structures and chemistry during phase separation. The temporal
evolution of precipitates' characteristics, e.g., morphology, volume
fraction, mean radii and number density are investigated utilizing
TEM and/or SEM micrographs and APT, along with predictions of
coarsening models and PrecipiCalc simulations. For instance, a 2.0
at.% addition of W [29,30] yields a larger microhardness, earlier
onset of spheroidal-to-cuboidal precipitate morphological transi-
tion, larger volume fraction, reduction in coarsening kinetics by
one-third and a larger number density of smaller mean radii pre-
cipitates. Additionally, the chemistry evolution of the precipitates
and the matrix, including the partitioning behavior of all alloying
elements between the two phases and the segregation behavior at
g/g0 interfaces has also been studied in great detail mainly based on
APT results. Overall, Al, Ta and W partition to the precipitates, while
Ni, Cr, Re and Ru prefer the matrix. As precipitates grow, the former
three elements diffuse to the g0-precipitates, while the latter
four diffuse away from the g0-precipitates. The addition of W, Ta or
Re leads to stronger partitioning of Al to the g0-precipitates, and
of Cr to the g-matrix, than was measured for the base Ni-Al-Cr
alloy, while the effectiveness of Ru for such enhancements is
insigniﬁcant.
Unlike Ni retention-excesses in ternary Ni-Al-Cr alloys [25,26],
which emerge during the early stages of phase separation and
disappear at later stages, an accumulation of Ni atoms is observed
to develop on the g-matrix side of the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interface
during aging times 0 e256 h in Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Ta at.% [32,33].
But in just one Ni-Al-Cr-Ta alloy has a Ni interfacial excess been
reported, which is lacking an in-depth explanation. In this research,
we discover that localized Ni retention-excesses can exist at aging
times as long as 256 h in Ni-Al-Cr based alloys with refractory
elemental additions, as a result of thermodynamic and kinetic
effects associated with strong attractive binding energies among
the refractory elements and solute elements, which causes changes
in the diffusional ﬂuxes across g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interfaces. Therefore,
it is signi ﬁcant to implement a deep and systematic study to
explore the details and mechanisms of Ni retention-excesses after
256 h aging, when alloys are in a quasi-stationary (or stationary)
condition.
Given the above we study in detail, in this article, the effects of
four R (R ¼ Re, Ru, W or Ta) elements on Ni retention-excesses in six
model Ni-Al-Cr based superalloys aged at 1073 K (800 /C14 C) for 256 h,
which is based on the diffusion theory presented in our prior
research on ternary Ni-Al-Cr alloys [25,26]. We calculate Ni-
excesses and Ni-depletions, GNi, the total reduction of interfacial
free energy, DsNi, caused by the Ni retention-excesses, changes of
Ni, Al and Cr diffusion ﬂux vectors D~C due to R-additions, and
interfacial compositional widths, dNi, across g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12-struc-
ture) interfaces, from our experimental APT data. We also present
the spatial correlations and the binding conditions among R and S
(S ¼ Al, Cr) elements, combining the experimental APT partial radial
distribution function (RDF) results and ﬁrst-principles calculations
of the binding energy between R and S atoms.
2. Methodologies
2.1. Atom-probe tomography (APT)
Atom-probe tomography (APT) specimens were cut from the
aged samples into 0.2 /C2 0.2 /C2 10 mm 3 rods using electrical
discharge machining (EDM) and then subjected to standard
electro-polishing techniques [36] at room temperature; coarse-
polishing in a 10% perchloric acid in glacial acetic acid solution at
15e20 Vdc, followed by ﬁne-polishing in a 2% perchloric acid in 2-
butoxyethanol solution at 5 e10 Vdc to form needle-like nanotips
with a radius of curvature of <40 nm. The specimens were analyzed
using a pulsed ultraviolet (UV) picosecond laser-assisted local-
electrode atom-probe (LEAP) tomograph [1 7,18], a LEAP 4000X Si
(Cameca, formerly Imago Scienti ﬁc Instruments, Madison, WI), at a
specimen base temperature of 40.0 ± 0.3 K and an ambient gauge
pressure of <6.7 /C2 10/C0 8 Pa. APT analyses were performed using the
following parameters [37]: (i) an UV wavelength of 355 nm; (ii) a
laser pulse energy of 20 pJ pulse /C0 1; (iii) a pulse repetition rate of
200 kHz; and (iv) a detection rate (ions per pulse) of 5%. The times-
of-ﬂight of all detected ions were converted to mass-to-charge-
state (m/n) spectra, enabling the elemental identi ﬁcations of all
collected ions. Subsequently, the x-, y- and z-position data were
employed to construct 3D tomographic reconstructions of the
analyzed volumes [19] using IVAS 3.6.4 (Cameca, Madison, WI)
software.
2.2. Computational methods
Our ﬁrst-principles calculations employ the plane-wave
pseudo-potential total-energy methodology, with the
generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) for exchange-
correlation, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation
package (VASP) [38e42]. We utilized the projector augmented
wave (PAW) potentials [43], and we relaxed fully all the structures
according to its unit-cell volume and individual atomic coordinates,
and tested the convergence of energy cutoff and k-points. The
optimized energy cutoff is 300 eV, which represents the Kohn-
Sham wave functions. A 12 /C2 12 /C2 12 Monkhorst ePack k-point
mesh was used for summing over the Brillouin zone for the bulk
structures for all calculations. A 3 /C2 3 /C2 3 f.c.c. Ni superlattice was
utilized for all binding energy calculations at 0 K.

2.3. Preparation of alloys and their processing
Six model Ni-based superalloys were investigated with a base-
line composition of Ni-10Al-8.5Cr at.%, with the addition of
2.0 at.% total refractory elements ( R) from the group W, Re, Ru, and
Ta. The nominal compositions of the alloys are as follows (all con-
centrations are in at.% unless otherwise noted): (E) Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-
2.0Ru; (F) Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Ta; (G) Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0Re; (H) Ni-
10.0Al-8.5Cr-2.0W; (I) Ni-10.0Al-8.5Cr-1.0Re-1.0Ru; and (J) Ni-
10.0Al-8.5Cr-1.0W-1.0Re at.%. In this article, we use the lettering
scheme of Ref. [34] for identifying the alloys, starting with the letter
E, to make it easy to interconnect with our other articles on Ni-Al-Cr
based alloys.
To produce master alloy ingots, high-purity constituent ele-
ments were vacuum induction melted under a partial Ar atmo-
sphere to minimize oxidation and then chill cast in a 19 mm
diameter copper mold. The cast ingots were processed using the
following three steps: (i) homogenization; (ii) vacancy annealing;
and (iii) aging annealing. All heat treatments were performed in
vacuum or a ﬂowing Ar atmosphere to reduce external oxidation.
For homogenization, the cast ingots were placed in a vacuum
furnace at 1573 K (1300 /C14 C) for 20 h followed by a power-off
furnace cool to room temperature. The ingots were then sectioned
into 10 mm lengths and the pieces wrapped in Ta foil and placed in
a tube furnace under ﬂowing argon and vacancy annealed at a
temperature above the solvus curve of the g0-phase (in the single
phase g-matrix) and held for a ﬁxed time, followed by water
quenching to room temperature. Speci ﬁcally, the temperatures
and holding times for alloys E to J for the vacancy annealing step
are as follows: 1380 K (1107 /C14 C) and 0.5 h for alloy F (with Ta);
1363 K (1090 /C14 C) and 0.5 h for alloy H (with W); and 1503 K
(1230 /C14 C) and 3 h for alloys G (with Re), E (with Ru), I (with Re and
Ru) and J (with W and Re). The vacancy annealing treatment is
performed to re-solution the alloy while decreasing the vacancy
concentration to minimize the nucleation and growth of g0-pre-
cipitates during the subsequent water quench. Finally, ingot sec-
tions 10 mm in length were aged at 1073 K (800 /C14 C) for 256 h in a
preheated furnace, followed by a water quench to room temper-
ature, to freeze in the nanostructure.

3.3. The effects of refractory elements on the compositional
diffusion ﬂuxes
The interfacial excesses of the solvent Ni are related to different
diffusional ﬂux couplings across the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interface. The
kinetic compositional paths of Ni, Al and Cr can be described by a
unit vector DC proposed in Ref. [26], whose components
[x(Ni) þ y(Al) þ z(Cr)] are proportional to the differences of the
equilibrium g0-precipitate and initial supersaturation concentra-
tions. DC is a compositional deviation from the thermally stable
ﬁnal composition in the matrix. This deviation is caused by the
current diffusional ﬂux, which is different from the ﬁnal thermally
stable diffusional ﬂux. Such deviation can be represented using the
composition differences for each element. In this study, the pro-
cesses of precipitate growth and coarsening imply that Ni- and Cr-
ﬂuxes diffuse out of the g0-precipitates toward the g-matrix, while
the Al ﬂux is toward the g0-precipitates. Thus, a positive term
means the ﬂux is toward the g0-precipitates, while a negative term
implies the ﬂux is toward the g-matrix.
We calculated the components of the ﬂux vector (unit vector
DC), for the base Ni-Al-Cr alloy from the APT data in Ref. [29] and
the Ni-Al-Cr based alloys E to J from APT results in this research. The
detailed results are presented in Table 3 . The components of the
unit vector for the base Ni-Al-Cr alloy are: /C0 0.537(Ni), 0.801(Al),
and /C0 0.264(Cr). We ﬁnd that for all the alloys studied, there are
similar diffusional ﬂux vector trends in DC: negative Ni compo-
nents ( /C0 0.403 in alloy E to /C0 0.318 in alloy H); positive Al compo-
nents (0.717 in alloy F to 0.853 in alloy E); and negative Cr
components (/C0 0.578 in alloy F to /C0 0.330 in alloy E), implying that
Ni and Cr diffuse away from the g0-precipitates and Al diffuses to-
ward the g0-precipitates.

3.7. The effects of refractory elements on solute diffusional behavior
In prior studies [25,26], Ni excesses emerge only during the
early stages of the temporal evolution of g0-precipitates and
disappear at later stages in ternary Ni-Al-Cr alloys. The kinetic
pathways involved in the formation of g0-precipitates during aging
of concentrated Ni-Al-Cr alloys at 873 K (600 /C14 C), for three distinct
alloy compositions Ni-5.35Al-9.4Cr, Ni-3.4Al-15.23Cr and Ni-
5.42Al-10.3Cr at.%, were studied experimentally by APT and
computationally with vacancy-mediated lattice kinetic Monte
(LKMC) simulations [25]. It was found that during the early stages
the fastest diffusion eigenmode contributes to the formation of g0-
precipitates; the fast diffusion eigenmode is dominated by Al,
whose ﬂux is accompanied by Ni and Cr ﬂuxes, and the ﬂux of Al
atoms to the g0-precipitates opposes the outward ﬂux of Cr and Ni
atoms. As a consequence, the concentration pro ﬁles exhibit Ni and
Cr excesses (accumulations) and an Al depletion in the vicinity of
the g0-precipitates [25]. In this research it is noteworthy that a
chemical binding af ﬁnity exists between refractory elements R (Re,
Ru, W, or Ta) and Cr when their solute ﬂuxes cross the g(f.c.c.)/
g0(L12) interfaces; the latter is based on both experimental partial
RDF results and ﬁrst-principles calculations. The R-Cr binding cor-
relations affect the delayed Ni retention-excesses at aging times as
long as 256 h.
The calculated diffusivities of Al and Cr in the g-matrix of the
base Ni-Al-Cr alloy are 13.9 /C2 10/C0 18 and 5.13 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1,
respectively [30]. Aluminum is the fastest diffusing solute in alloys
E through J and its calculated diffusivity values in the g-matrix
range from 12.6 /C2 10/C0 18 in alloy F [32,33] to 14.7 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1 in
alloy E [34]. The calculated Cr diffusivity in the g-matrix is smaller
than that of Al in all alloys; varying from 4.80 /C2 10/C0 18 in alloy F
[32,33] to 5.42 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1 in alloy E [34]. Thus, compared to
the base Ni-Al-Cr alloy, the diffusivities of Al and Cr in alloys E to J
do not increase or decrease by more than 1 1%, implying a small
inﬂuence of the refractory elemental additions on the diffusivities
of Al and Cr. The refractory elements W, Re, Ru, Ta are slow diffusers
in the g-matrix. The diffusivity values of W, Re, Ru and Ta are
4.93 /C2 10/C0 19, 1.05/C2 10/C0 19, 5.58 /C2 10/C0 21,5 . 1 4/C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1 in their
respective quaternary alloys [5,30,32e34]. The R-Cr attractive
binding energies, either at the 1 st or 2 nd NN distance, tend to
decelerate the Cr diffusion ﬂux, resulting in a decrease of the Ni
diffusivity. As Ni partitions to the g-matrix during phase separation,
the decreased diffusivity of Ni is not suf ﬁciently large to transport
all of the Ni atoms away from the g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interface and an
accumulation of Ni atoms arises in the g-matrix phase (or a
depletion of Ni atoms in the g0-precipitate phase) is observed. The
diffusivity of Ni in the g-matrix decreases from 6.06 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1
in the base Ni-Al-Cr alloy [30] to different values resulting from the
addition of different refractory elements. The diffusivity of Ni in
alloy F (with Ta) is 2.02 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1[32,33], which is the
smallest among all the analyzed alloys, and the smallest among all
atomic species in alloy F. Hence, the inhibition effect on the
transport of Ni atoms is signi ﬁcant in alloy F, because Ni is the least
mobile element in the
g-matrix. Alternatively, the calculated Ni
diffusivity in alloy E (with Ru) is 5.89 /C2 10/C0 18 m/C0 2s/C0 1[34], which is
the least affected by the addition of a refractory element, leading to
the smallest Ni excess (accumulation) in the g-matrix and even a
zero Ni-depletion in the g0-precipitates.
4. Summary and conclusions
The effects of adding 2.0 at.% of the refractory elements, R,
(R ¼ Re, Ru, W, or Ta), to a model Ni-based superalloy Ni-10.0Al-
8.5Cr at.% aged at 1073 K (800 /C14 C) for 256 h, on the interfacial
retention-excesses of the solvent Ni, were studied experimentally
using 3D atom-probe tomography (APT) plus ﬁrst-principles cal-
culations. Our ﬁndings and conclusions are summarized as follows:
/C15 There is a strong chemical binding af ﬁnity between refractory
elements and solute elements, S,( S ¼ Al, Cr), as demonstrated by
experimental partial radial distribution functions (RDFs), and
ﬁrst-principles calculations.
/C15 We ﬁnd that correlated R-S binding energies play a key role in
the measured Ni excesses at g(f.c.c.)/g0(L12) interfaces at an
aging time of 256 h. Based on R-Cr partial RDF pro ﬁles (Fig. 3a),
Ta has a negative correlation with Cr, while Re and Ru reveal
positive correlations with Cr, suggesting a repulsive interaction
exists between Ta and Cr atoms and a chemical af ﬁnity exists
between Re and Cr atoms, and between Ru and Cr atoms,
consistent with strong Re-Cr and Ru-Cr attractive binding en-
ergies. Additionally, we ﬁnd that the W-Cr partial RDF is weakly
correlated and that W does not have a strong preference for Cr.
In the R-Al partial RDF pro ﬁle ( Fig. 3 b), Ta and W have strong
positive correlations whereas Re and Ru have negative correla-
tions with Al. These ﬁndings indicate that Ta and W have strong
effects on the Al diffusional- ﬂux into
g0-precipitates and
concomitantly partitioning of Al atoms to them.
/C15 The R-S binding energies at 0 K ( Table 5 and Fig. 4 ) were also
calculated utilizing ﬁrst-principles calculations. Rhenium and
Ru have attractive binding energies with Cr at 1 st and 2 nd NN
distances. Tungsten and Ta exhibit repulsive binding energies
with Cr in the 1 st NN shell, but they have attractive binding
energies with Cr at the 2 nd NN distance. These results are in
excellent agreement with our experimental APT partitioning
and partial RDF results. The R-Al binding energies for Ta, W, Re
