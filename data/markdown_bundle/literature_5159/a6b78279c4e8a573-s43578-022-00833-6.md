The Mo25Nb25Ta25W25 refractory high‑entropy alloy, which was prepared by mechanical alloying utilizing 
pure elemental powder and spark plasma sintering (SPS), was explored in this work to determine the 
effects of sintering temperature on phases, microstructure, and mechanical characteristics. The alloyed 
powder with single BCC structure was fabricated after 30 h of ball‑milling process and then was sintered 
under the SPS conditions of 1700 °C, 1800 °C, 1900 °C, along with the pressure of 50 MPa as well as the 
dwell time of 20 min. The findings demonstrated that the alloys consisted of BCC matrix phase and 
Ta and Nb‑enriched FCC carbide precipitation phase. And the alloy sintered at 1800 °C possessed a 
compressive strength of 2571 MPa and fracture strain of 14.51%, which were mostly caused by grain 
refinement strengthening and particle strengthening. Additionally, the fracture mechanism of alloy was 
intergranular brittle fracture.
Introduction
Advanced materials with higher performance compared to the 
maturely developed structural materials are urgently required 
to be applied to engineering, particularly in the aerospace and 
weapon-made industry. In 2004, high-entropy alloy (HEA) 
composing of multi-principle elements with equal or near 
molar ratios (at %) ranging from 5 to 35% was proposed by 
Y eh et al. [1] and Cantor et al. [2 ]. In contrast to conventional 
“solute–solvent” alloys, it is discovered that four effects includ-
ing high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect, sluggish diffu-
sion effect and “cocktail” effect form in HEAs because the lat -
tice cell contains multi-principal elements with different atomic 
radii and electronic structure, which leads to some promising 
properties [3–8]. To further improvement of high temperature 
performance, Senkov et al. [ 9, 10] prepared refractory high-
entropy alloys, W25Mo25Ta25Nb25 and W20Mo20Ta20Nb20V20 by 
means of vacuum arc melting in 2010. Interestingly, these two 
alloys only form single BCC disordered solid solution phase 
with great structural stability at 1400 °C. It is worth noting that 
these two refractory high-entropy alloys possessed an undesir -
able mechanical properties (peak strength of ~ 1200 MPa and 
fracture strain of < 3%) at room temperature but an excellent 
mechanical properties at elevated temperature (> 400 MPa of 
yield strength at 1600 °C). Because of this, the development of 
cutting-edge materials has benefited greatly from the enormous 
application potential of refractory high-entropy alloys at the 
increased temperature.
Due of the extremely high melting temperatures of the com-
ponent elements, vacuum arc melting is frequently utilized to 
make ingots. The big difference between melting temperature of 
elements results in prominent elemental segregation and chemi-
cal composition heterogeneity. Additionally, coarse dendritic 
microstructure, shrinkage porosity and metastable eutectic 
phase enriched at grain boundaries are harmful to mechanical 
properties and workability [11]. Nguyen et al. [12] researched 
 TixZrNbTa (x = 25, 30, 35, 40, 45) prepared by vacuum arc melt-
ing. The typical dendrite microstructure with coarse grains in 
size of ~ 250 μm was observed in the alloy. Ti and Zr segregated 
in the inter-dendrite region because of lower melting tempera-
ture. The Ti45 alloy exhibited more than 20% of ductility while 
Ti25 alloy exhibits poor tensile strain (< 2.5%). However, the 
peak strengths of all alloys did not overtake 1200 MPa.

Recent years, swells of high-entropy alloys fabricated by 
MA and SPS have been reported. Fu et al. [13] reported that 
 CoNiFeCrAl0.6Ti0.4 high-entropy alloy powder was first prepared 
by MA and then spark plasma sintered at 1000 °C to obtain alloy 
bulks. The high-entropy alloy powder consisted of FCC matrix 
phase (~ 9 nm of crystalline size), twin FCC phase and BCC 
phase (~ 10 nm of crystalline size) after 49 h of mechanical alloy-
ing. The yield strength, compressive strength and facture strain 
of alloy sintered at 1000 °C were 2.08 GPa, 2.52 GPa and 11.5%, 
respectively. The author attributed the excellent mechanical 
properties to twin strengthening, grain refinement strengthen-
ing, and Al/Ti solid solution strengthening. Moskovskikh et al. 
[14] prepared high-entropy (HfTaTiNbZr)C and (HfTaTiNbMo)
C carbide ceramics through high-energy ball-milling and SPS. 
The single FCC phase formed in the alloyed powder prepared 
through high-energy ball-milling (HEBM) using the mixture 
of elemental powder and graphite with high purity and still 
remained after spark plasma sintered at 1750 °C and 2000 °C 
in these two high-entropy ceramics. Besides, ZrHfOx and HfO2 
precipitated in two carbide ceramic owing to the oxygen dis-
solved in the HEBM powder. As-sintered (HfTaTiNbZr)C and 
(HfTaTiNbMo)C high-entropy ceramic bulks with ultra-fine 
grain and uniformly dispersive oxide phase achieved the consid-
erably high hardness of 25.7 ± 3.5GPa and 23.8 ± 2.7GPa, respec-
tively. Based on the researches abovementioned, the refractory 
high-entropy alloy obtained by mechanical alloying (MA) and 
spark plasma sintering (SPS) has ultra-fine grain size, uniform 
microstructure and the excellent mechanical properties showing 
great development and application potential.
In this work, equimolar Mo25Nb25Ta25W25 refractory high-
entropy alloy is prepared by MA and SPS. A set of reliable 
parameters of powder preparation and sintering for MoNbTaW 
refractory high-entropy alloy are given. The alloying sequence of 
elements and micromorphology of alloyed powder are investi -
gated. Based on the analysis of the microstructure, phase trans-
formation and mechanical properties of the alloy, the effects of 
sintering temperature on alloy properties are studied. Further -
more, the strengthening mechanism is deeply discussed.
Results
The characteristics of mechanically alloyed powders
Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the alloy powder at incre-
mental ball-milling time. The diffraction peaks of elements W , 
Mo, Ta and Nb can obviously be seen in original powder. As 
the milling time steps up to 2 h, the disappearance of Mo peaks 
is an indication that alloying occurs. Each diffraction peak has 
weakened and broadened. It is apparent that the elemental dif-
fraction peaks merge as well as only diffraction peaks of W can 
be still detected after 5 h ball-milling. These components have 
a significant potential to form single solid solution in a substi-
tutional fashion because of their similar physical parameters. It 
is more difficult for the element with higher melting tempera-
ture to dissolve into lattice, as shown by the phenomenon that 
elements with high melting temperature gradually dissolves in 
the wake of the ones with low melting temperature because the 
elements with high melting temperature have stronger bond 
energy and low diffusivity [15, 16]. Moreover, the elemental 
powders (Ta, Nb) with better ductility can translate bulk or 
planar shape into flat shape after continual breaking to provide 
the broader area for alloying. During the alloying process, the 
dislocation density in metallic powder particles is increasing and 
work hardening is introduced resulting from repeated broken 
and cold welding. Subsequently, the diffraction peaks are fur -
ther broadened and BCC solid solution basically forms when 
the ball-milling time reaches 20 h. This is due to severe lattice 
distortion aroused by the stronger extent of solid solution and 
high lattice strain. The existence of high lattice strain caused by 
plastic deformation becomes the driving force of atomic diffu -
sion [17, 18]. The long-time wear gives rise to the introduction 
of WC impurity captured in diffraction pattern. The measured 
carbon content in 30 h-milled alloyed powder is about 1.12 wt%. 
According to the Scherrer’s formula, the lattice constant and 
the crystallite size of the alloyed powder after 30 h-milled are 
estimated to be 3.234 Å and ~ 13.9 nm, respectively.
Figure  1(b) and (c) show the morphology of 30 h-milled 
powder at low and high magnifications, respectively. The size 
of the powder particles is extremely small, falling in the range 
of between 200 and 500 nm due to repeat crushing and mutual 
colliding. A part of particles aggregate into big particles stretch-
ing from 2 to 5 μm owing to tremendous surface energy of 
nanoparticles.
Phase transformation and microstructure
As shown in Fig. S2, BCC matrix phase (Im-3m, Space group 
[229]) and FCC carbide precipitation phase (Fm-3m, Space 
group [225]) are identified from the XRD patterns of the MoN-
bTaW alloys irrespective of the change of sintering temperature. 
The lattice constants of these two phases at different temperature 
are estimated to be 3.199 ± 0.002 Å and 4.418 ± 0.006 Å, respec-
tively. It is substantially extrapolated that it is the FCC carbide 
that precipitates from BCC matrix followed by decomposition 
of WC.
Based on the X-ray diffraction theory, the angle of the dif-
fraction peak depends on the lattice constant, and the peak 
width depends on the grain size. As MA process proceeds, the 
mixed powder undergoes the MA process and transforms into 
a supersaturated solid solution with a large quantity of defects 
owing to the continual plastic deformation and fracture. Sub-
sequently, alleviation of lattice stress and growth of grain size

Figure 7 illustrates the compressive fracture morphologies 
of the sintered alloys at room temperature. All samples display 
similar fracture morphologies. The rock candy-shape fracture 
surface with steps is clearly viewed, which is predominantly 
characterized by intergranular brittle fracture. The phenomenon 
of the growing grain size can be viewed in fracture morphologies 
as well, especially from 1800 to 1900 °C. The cracks appear and 
expand at sintering defects, such as pores during compressive 
process. Additionally, the inferior compatibility between BCC 
matrix phase and FCC carbide phase finally results in failure, 
namely intergranular brittle fracture.

Conclusion
Generally, a MoNbTaW refractory high-entropy alloy was 
successfully fabricated by mechanical alloying and spark 
plasma sintering. Through characterization and analysis of 
the phase, microstructure and mechanical properties of MoN-
bTaW refractory high-entropy alloy, the main conclusions are 
as follows:
(1) The single BCC supersaturated solid solution alloyed 
powder with nanoparticle of 200–500 nm was formed 
after 30 h ball-milling process.
(2) The alloy is comprised by high-entropy BCC matrix 
phase and FCC Ta and Nb-rich carbide precipitation 
phase after the SPS process. No phase transformation 
takes place as sintering temperature increases.
(3) The average grain size of BCC phase and FCC phase 
increases slightly and still remains fine, along with 
which the volume fraction of FCC phase rises (19.3–
21.7%) when sintering temperature increases.
(4) The highest compressive peak strength (2571 MPa) and 
fracture strain (14.51%) is reached in the alloy sintered 
at 1800 °C. The Vicker’s hardness of this alloy arrives at 
8.144 GPa. The substitution solid solution strengthen-
ing from lattice misfit and the interstitial solid solution 
strengthening from dispersion of C atoms effectively 
impede the dislocation motion and crack expansion. 
The fine grain size and homogeneous microstructure 
make the alloys achieve not only high peak strength 
but also relatively excellent plasticity compared with 
casting alloys. The dispersion strengthening from FCC

Methodology
W , Mo, Ta and Nb powder (Beijing Xingrongyuan Co., Ltd., 
China) with a purity of 99.9% were mixed in equimolar ratio 
in this work. Particle size of elemental powder was less than 
75 μm. The SEM images of elemental powder are showed 
in Fig. S1 and some characteristic parameters of W , Mo, 
Ta and Nb elements are given in Table S1. WC vials and 
balls (diameters in φ 6 mm, φ12 mm and φ 20 mm) were 
selected as the ball-milling media. The mechanical alloying 
process was conducted in a planetary ball-milling machine 
(QM-3SP4, Nanjing Chi Shun Technology Development 
Co., Ltd). The ball-to-powder weight ratio was 20:1 and 
the milling speed was 400 rpm. High pure argon was filled 
to prevent powder from being oxidized. Stearic acid was 
added in powders as a process controlling agent (PCA) to 
avoid cold welding and oxidation during MA process. A 
small quantity of powder mixed in an equimolar manner 
was taken out for phase analysis at 0 h, 2 h, 5 h, 10 h, 20 h 
and 30 h. Subsequently, alloyed powder after ball-milling 
for 30 h was spark plasma sintered in SPS-211H machine 
(Fuji Kikai Kogyo Co., Ltd., Japan) at 1700 °C, 1800 °C and 
1900 °C in argon, with a dwell time of 20 min. A pressure 
of 50 MPa was applied on alloyed powder during the entire 
sintering process. Finally, Cylindrical samples in the shape 
of φ10 mm × 6 mm were acquired after electrical discharge 
machining and grinding.
The carbon content in alloyed powder was quantified 
by CS-901 high frequency infrared ray carbon&sulfur ana-
lyser. X-ray diffraction patterns of powder and alloy bulks 
were obtained from a SmartLab diffractometer with Cu-Kα 
radiation. The scanning speed is 2°/min. The micromorphol-
ogy of powders and alloy bulks were observed by Scanning 
Electron Microscopy (SEM, JSM-7900F) equipped with an 
Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS) and electron back-
scatter diffraction (EBSD) detectors. The phase constituent 
and microstructure of alloy bulks were further analyzed by 
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F30 
S-TWIN). Archimedes method was used to measure rela-
tive density of alloys.
Cylindrical specimens with a size of φ3 mm × 6 mm were 
electrical discharge machined from the sintered cylindrical 
samples for compressive test at room temperature. Compres -
sive test at a strain rate of 0.001  s−1 was carried out using 
computer-controlled WDW-100 mechanical testing machine. 
The micro-hardness of phases was measured by STeP500_
NHT3_MCT3 Nano-indentation tester from Anton Paar 
equipped with a Berkovich diamond indenter. The applied
