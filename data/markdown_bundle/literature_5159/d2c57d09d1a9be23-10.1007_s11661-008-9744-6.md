Effect of Ruthenium on Creep Strength
of Ni-Base Single-Crystal Superalloys
at 750 /C176 C and 750 MPa
N. TSUNO, K. KAKEHI, C.M.F. RAE, and
R. HASHIZUME
The eﬀect of Ru on the creep behavior of two kinds of
single-crystal Ni-base superalloys was studied at 750 /C176 C
and 750 MPa to discern the beneﬁt of Ru as a
strengthening element. The addition of Ru decreased the
primary creep strain and improved the creep strength.
Further, the addition of Ru enhanced the dissociation of
dislocations in the matrix channels. This inhibited the
nucleation of the stacking fault ribbons and, as a result,
impeded the operation of {111} h112i slip.

The demand to develop an increasingly high-temper-
ature capability for Ni-base single-crystal superalloys
has led to increases in the amount of refractory alloying
additions. These additions improve the high-tempera-
ture creep resistance. However, they also lead to the
formation of topological close packed (tcp) phases.
[1]
These precipitates degrade the mechanical properties of
superalloys in service due to the loss of these solid
solution strengthening elements from the matrix. The
Ru additions to single-crystal Ni-base superalloys have
been found to suppress the formation of tcp phases and,
therefore, to improve high-temperature creep proper-
ties.
[2] At lower temperatures, where creep properties are
not determined by microstructural instabilities, Ru
additions also improve creep properties. [3] However,
the mechanism by which Ru additions improve the low-
temperature creep strength of Ni-base single-crystal
superalloys is not well understood. In this study, the
eﬀect of Ru addition on creep behavior at a low
temperature under high stress is examined.
The investigation was carried out on two Ni-base
single-crystal superalloys: NKH-304 and NKH-510.
NKH-304 is the base alloy, and NKH-510 is a 3 pct-
Ru-addition alloy, where 3 pct Ru is substituted for Ni.
Their chemical compositions are listed in Table I. The
single-crystal alloys used here were provided by Hitachi,
Ltd., Chiyoda, Tokyo in the heat-treated conditions, as
shown in Table II. The alloys were fully solutionized,
and any c¢-c eutectic was not observed. After analysis of
the crystallographic orientation by the back-reﬂection
Laue method, the specimens were cut from single-crystal
rods by electrodischarge machining. The tensile axes of
the specimens examined were within 4 deg of the [001]
pole.
The rectangular-cross-sectional specimens had a gage
section of 19.6 9 2.8 9 3.0 mm. A tensile test was per-
formed at 750 /C176 C under strain rates of 4.25 9 10
-4 s-1.
A creep test was performed in a h001i orientation at
750 /C176 C and under a nominal stress of 750 MPa. The
microstructure of creep specimens was observed with a
transmission electron microscope (TEM) to investigate
the deformation mechanism. Thin foils for TEM anal-
ysis were obtained by cutting discs from the interrupted
specimens. These were electropolished using a twin jet
with a solution of 15 pct perchloric acid in ethanol at
30 V and 0 /C176 C. The foils were observed in a JEM-3010
microscope (JEOL LTD., Akishima, Tokyo) at 300 kV.
The Burgers vectors and the natures of the stacking
faults were determined using the contrast visibility of the
dislocations and stacking fault fringes.
[4]
The microstructures of the c¢ precipitates after an
aging heat treatment are shown in Figure 1. No obvious
diﬀerences in the morphology of the c¢ precipitates
between two alloys were observed. The mean c¢ precipi-
tate lengths were 345 and 310 nm in the NKH-304 and
NKH-510 alloys, respectively. The c¢ precipitate size of
the Ru-containing alloy NKH-510 was a little smaller
than that of the base alloy NKH-304. Creep curves and
the relationship between creep strain and creep rate are
presented in Figure 2. NKH-304 shows a large primary
creep strain. A strain-softening phenomenon, which
means that the creep rate increased with accumulation
of the creep strain, was observed (Figure 2(b)). The
large value for the primary creep strain and strain
softening are likely to be due to the operation of
{111}h112i slip systems. The large primary creep strain
will increase the eﬀective stress at the onset of secondary
creep; therefore, it will decrease the creep rupture life.
[5]
As a result of the Ru addition, the primary creep strain
in [001] was reduced from 2.9 to 0.1 pct, and the
minimum creep rate was also reduced. Strain softening,
which is associated with {111} h112i slip, was not
observed. It is clear that primary creep strain was
decreased and creep strength was improved by the
addition of Ru. The 0.2 pct proof stresses at 750 /C176 C for
NKH-304 and NKH-510 were 1070 and 1040 MPa,
respectively. No solid solution strengthening eﬀect of
Ru on the proof stress was observed. In order to
investigate the creep deformation mechanism, the
microstructures of the crept specimens were observed
by TEM. Figure 3 shows the TEM microstructure of
crept specimens cut perpendicularly to the tensile axis.
In the base alloy NKH-304, many stacking faults in c¢
phase and dislocations in the c phase were observed.

indicate that {111} h112i slip systems operate freely in

must be given to the eﬀect of Ru on these processes.
When the stacking fault energy of the c phase is low, the
dislocations would shear both c¢ and c phases succes-
sively keeping the dissociated-dislocation structure with-
in the c¢ precipitate.[9] Therefore, the low stacking fault
energy of the matrix channel will promote extensive
cooperative shearing of the c-c¢ structure by the
{111}h112i slip. Both Re and Co promote primary
creep caused by the {111} h112i slip, because they
decrease the stacking fault energy of the matrix. [10]
Since the element Ru also has an hcp structure, its
addition would be expected to decrease the stacking
fault energy of the matrix, too.
[11] However, in this
study, the Ru addition decreased primary creep strain.
Therefore, Ru additions inhibit primary creep, not
through a propagation process, but by interfering with
the nucleation process. The a/2h110i dislocation motion
in the matrix channel leaves interfacial dislocations
around cuboidal precipitates during the incubation
period and forms homogeneous c-c¢ interfacial disloca-
tion networks.
[12,13] The interaction between two matrix
a/2h110i dislocations creates a stacking fault ribbon. [8]
Since the proof strength, which is associated with
a/2h110i dislocations shearing both c and c¢ phases,
showed very little diﬀerence between the two alloys, the
glide motion of the a/2[110] dislocation in the matrix did
not appear to be aﬀected by Ru-solid solution strength-
ening at a high strain rate. Frequent dissociations and
cross-slips in the matrix dislocations were observed in
NKH-510 (Figure 5). It is likely that the dissociations
and cross-slips occur by the following mechanisms,
which are schematically illustrated in Figure 6.
(1) The a/2h110i dislocation moves in a c horizontal
channel leaving a dislocation dipole along the c/c¢
interface (Figure 6(a)).
(2) When the dislocation arrives at the c vertical chan-
nel, it splits into two Shockley partial dislocations
due to a difference in the internal stress state
between the horizontal and vertical channels
[11]
and assisted by a lower c-stacking fault energy,
and it forms stacking faults in the vertical channels
of the c phase (Figure 6(b)). Only a leading partial
emerges from the channel and the propagation of
partials in the horizontal channels does not appear
to be energetically favorable. Hence, propagation
of the a/2h110i dislocations between the horizontal
layers appears to be inhibited.
[11]

directions; i.e., {111} h112i ribbon requires a reaction

matrix dislocation dissociation in the vertical channels
by the addition of Ru greatly reduces the frequency of
the nucleation of stacking fault ribbons.
In summary, the addition of Ru inhibited dislocation
motion caused by dissociation in the matrix channels,
which reduces the frequency of nucleation of the stacking
fault ribbon. As a result, the operation of the {111} h112i
slip was impeded, and the creep strength was improved at
intermediate temperatures and at high stresses.
