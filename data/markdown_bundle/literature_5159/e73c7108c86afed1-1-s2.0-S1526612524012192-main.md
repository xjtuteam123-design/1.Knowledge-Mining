ABSTRACT
Ni-based superalloys, with their high mechanical integrity at high temperature, have become critical engineering 
materials in modern advanced mechanical systems. However, this characteristic also makes them difficult to 
process. The laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technique, with its high processing temperature, high design 
flexibility, and no tooling requirements, presents a promising way for manufacturing high-performance Ni-based 
superalloy components. However, the LPBF process also presents additional challenges, such as controlling 
precipitates, suppressing micro-defects, and achieving the desired microstructure. To address these challenges, it 
is crucial to enhance the intelligence of LPBF technology through advanced engineering software and computer 
techniques. Therefore, in this review, we introduce the concept of the “ ready-to-use ” mode of LPBF technology, 
that is, real-time control. We survey recently reported studies related to data-driven LPBF of Ni-based superal -
loys, focusing on the relevant achievements in the processing stage. Simulation and machine learning are two 
main techniques that have received increasing attention. The review begins with a description of the complex 
microstructural features of Ni-based superalloys, providing a basis for discussing the data-driven optimization of 
alloy microstructure in the following sections. The process parameters involved in LPBF and their complex effects 
on microstructure are subsequently reviewed. The main section reviews the specific applications of simulation 
and machine learning in the LPBF of Ni-based superalloys, such as microstructure-related, manufacturing 
process-related, correlation analysis-related, and process monitoring-related. This review provides insights for 
researchers studying the LPBF of Ni-based superalloys or other difficult-to-machine materials to achieve intel -
ligent manufacturing through data-driven approaches.

a superior microstructure that differs from the quasi-equilibrium state of 
wrought and casting processes. These characteristics make LPBF 
increasingly attractive for Ni-based superalloys, promising the devel -

macroscopic defects and microstructure evolution mechanisms, anisot -

predictive models linking process parameters, microstructure, residual 
stress, and mechanical properties.

Current research on LPBF Ni-based superalloys is gradually shifting 
from process optimization to exploring deeper principles like precipi -
tation and cracking mechanisms. Adegoke et al., in their 2020 review of 
γ ′ -strengthened Ni-based superalloys fabricated by LPBF, concluded that 
these superalloys are highly sensitive to thermal gradients and residual 
stresses, making them prone to various types of cracking such as solid -
ification, liquation, stress-aging, and ductility-dip cracks, similar to is -
sues found in laser welding [ 10 ]. In a recent review, Teixeira et al. 
focused on optimizing residual stresses in LPBF Ni-based superalloys, 
suggesting that residual stresses can be managed through processing 
parameters via precipitation and cracking mechanisms [ 11 ]. Clearly, 
research on LPBF Ni-based superalloys mainly employs a trial-and-error 
approach to investigate the relationships between process parameters, 
microstructure, and macroscopic properties. Given that the performance 
of Ni-based superalloys arises from the complex interplay of alloying 
elements and varied precipitates and their distributions (detailed in 
Section 2 ), these strengthening mechanisms are intricately influenced by 
the LPBF process (detailed in Section 3 ). This makes exploring Ni-based 
superalloys more challenging compared to other engineering materials 
with fewer alloying elements. Currently, research on LPBF Ni-based 
superalloys focuses on more easily fabricated types, such as Hastelloy, 
In718, In625, and In738LC (see Fig. 2 ) [ 12 ].
Recently, we have observed a growing trend in computer-aided 
research on LPBF Ni-based superalloys, as Fig. 3 shows. Analysis of 
original data from the Web of Science database using keywords such as 
selective laser melting, laser powder bed fusion, nickel alloy, data- 
driven, data-assisted, and prediction shows that the main techniques 
currently used are simulation models based on physical principles and 
data-driven methods based on machine learning (ML) algorithms. In this 
paper, we collectively refer to these techniques as data-driven methods 
because they are founded on digital research. Simulation models use 
analytical or numerical methods to simulate physical behavior at 
various scales (atomic, micro, or macro). These models, established 
either on thermomechanical principles or through empirical results from 
experiments, can explain process mechanics and provide clear insights 
into process phenomena, offering guidance [ 13 , 14 ]. On the other hand, 
Machine learning algorithms leverage high-dimensional and hetero -
geneous data, continuously updating themselves by learning from data 
and experience. Once properly trained, they are computationally effi -
cient, making them well-suited for analyzing dynamic thermodynamic 
phenomena in metal additive manufacturing processes [ 15 , 16 ]. These 
algorithms address various auxiliary problems in additive 
manufacturing that are challenging to solve using traditional methods, 
such as cost estimation, manufacturability assessment, and closed-loop 
quality control [ 17 – 19 ]. The advantages of using scientific and 
mathematical approaches to drive the development and optimization of 
new Ni-based superalloys are evident [ 20 ]. However, there is currently 
no comprehensive review summarizing the specific applications of these 
data-driven methods in LPBF Ni-based superalloys.
For the above description, we propose that the ultimate goal of LPBF 
technology should be to achieve a “ ready-to-use ” model. This requires 
making additive manufacturing equipment “ intelligent. ” To this end, we 
have created a blueprint, as shown in Fig. 4 . This blueprint illustrates 
that data generated during the manufacturing process can be captured 
by sensors and used as input for a trained machine-learning model to 
predict the desired performance. While machine learning models are 
highly effective at identifying patterns and making predictions based on 
data, they can sometimes lack the physical intuition necessary for model 
construction. This can lead to impractical prediction results and make 
the models susceptible to issues such as data pollution from noise, 
missing data, or incorrect data labels [ 21 – 23 ]. Therefore, simulation 
models become essential. They provide a detailed, physics-based un -
derstanding of the manufacturing process, incorporating thermo -
mechanical principles and empirical results from experiments. By 
feeding the results from the machine learning model into an optimized 
simulation model, we can ensure that process adjustments are grounded 
in a robust understanding of the underlying physics. The results are then 
fed into an optimized simulation model to adjust process parameters. 
Additionally, these adjusted process parameters are fed back into the 
machine-learning model for further optimization, forming a closed 
feedback loop. That is, this approach aims to develop a “ ready-to-use ” 
model for LPBF, applicable not only to Ni-based superalloys but also to 
other critical materials. Simulation models with real-time capabilities 
and machine-learning models with high-speed computational power are 
integrated into a database rich in metallurgical knowledge of Ni-based 
superalloys. This database acts as a “ brain, ” processing data from real- 
time monitoring to fine-tune the microstructure and achieve the 
desired performance. Therefore, the purpose of this work is also to 
analyze how close we are to realizing this blueprint.
Therefore, this work reviews the current state of development of 
relevant technologies that may contribute to the realization of the above 
blueprint, with a focus on simulation and machine learning technolo -
gies. Firstly, we briefly describe the microalloying elements and typical 
precipitates of Ni-based superalloys, along with their roles, as these are 
key factors causing difficulty in establishing the process-performance

ters involved in LPBF that affect the material's microstructure, posing 
another challenge for intelligent manufacturing processes. However, 
combining simulation and machine learning can bridge these complex 
features, promoting intelligent manufacturing by optimizing both 
microstructure and macroscopic performance, and supporting the con -
struction of the process-performance relationship. Subsequently, we

strength and ductility [ 50 ]. Their typical morphologies are shown in 
Fig. 6 . The σ and Laves phases are more commonly studied in LPBFed Ni-

and spherical morphologies [ 50 , 51 ]. The plate-like or needle-like σ 
phase, as displayed in Fig. 6 b – c, can hinder the movement of disloca -
tions and serve as channels for crack propagation [ 52 , 53 ]. When the 
brittle σ phase precipitates at grain boundaries, it can lead to interfacial 
decohesion and cracking, reducing the fatigue life and tensile ductility of 
Ni-based superalloys [ 50 , 54 , 55 ]. The Laves phase has an irregular 
shape, generally classified as either long (or needle-like) or granular, as 
shown in Fig. 6 d – e [ 56 ]. This phase is enriched with Si, Mo, and Nb 
compared to the γ matrix, and its formation consumes a significant 
amount of the main hardening elements, especially Nb [ 57 , 58 ]. 
Although the Laves phase is brittle and prone to cracking ( Fig. 6 f), it can 
have a hardening effect on the alloy when precipitated in a finely 
dispersed manner. Therefore, regulating the growth and distribution of 
the Laves phase may help improve the performance of Ni-based super -
alloys [ 59 , 60 ].

3. Digitized complexity of LPBF processing and microstructure 
of Ni-based superalloys
To optimize the LPBF manufacturing process for Ni-based superal -
loys, simulation and machine learning can leverage the substantial data 
space provided by the design space of microstructure and the LPBF 
process parameter window. These tools can help modulate and refine 
the process. The core structural components and process parameters of 
an LPBF printer — such as the laser generator, scanner, powder chamber, 
and building chamber — are critical factors that influence the LPBF 
manufacturing process. By understanding and controlling these com -
ponents and parameters, precise manufacturing of Ni-based superalloys 
can be achieved. Therefore, a deep understanding of the LPBF printer 
components and process parameters is essential for optimizing the LPBF 
manufacturing process.
Fig. 8 presents a simplified schematic diagram of the structure of an 
LPBF printer, which comprises several core components, including a 
powder chamber, building chamber, recoater, and laser generator 
[ 64 , 65 ]. The laser generator produces the laser beam that fuses layers of 
metal powder, as illustrated in Fig. 8 a. After each layer is formed, the 
substrate in the building chamber lowers by one layer, and the recoater 
then spreads a new layer of powder for successive laser scans. This 
process is repeated until the entire digital model is fully manufactured, 
as depicted in Fig. 8 b. The process parameters can be broadly catego -
rized as laser-related, scan-related, powder-related, and temperature- 
related, forming an extensive manufacturing window, as shown in 
Fig. 8 c [ 66 ].
To use metallurgical knowledge as a data driver, it is necessary to 
briefly introduce the complex effects of process parameters on micro -
structure, as outlined below.
3.1. LPBFed precipitates
In the data-driven optimization of the LPBF manufacturing process 
for Ni-based superalloys, supersaturation, thermal cycling, and high 
cooling rates are key factors that distinguish LPBF from traditional 
manufacturing processes and are critical for controlling precipitation 
behavior. For example, supersaturation can cause Nb and Mo to be 
distributed not only around sub-grains (as shown in Fig. 9 a) but also 
inside grains. Consequently, in addition to the formation of the γ’ phase, 
the Laves phase also forms between dendritic arms, with its dispersion 
controlled by dendrite-arm spacing (as shown in Fig. 9 b) [ 67 , 68 ]. 
Furthermore, the Laves phase can be dissolved during heating cycles. 
Wang et al. used LPBF to fabricate a staircase In718 block to study the 
effects of thermal cycling on the Laves phase. They found that increased 
thermal cycling led to a lower proportion of the Laves phase (as shown in 
Fig. 9 d) and higher dispersion (as shown in Figs. 9c1-c3) [ 69 ]. However, 
increased thermal cycling can also coarsen columnar grains, enhance 
grain texture, and increase the density of high-angle dislocations at grain 
boundaries, which may deteriorate the mechanical properties of Ni- 
based superalloys [ 70 – 72 ]. In addition to the γ’ and Laves phases, 
several studies have investigated the formation of carbides in LPBFed Ni- 
based superalloys. Marchese et al. reported an M

cooling rates are important factors that affect precipitation behavior in 
LPBFed Ni-based superalloys, but other factors may also play a role in 
determining the microstructure and mechanical properties.

3.3. LPBFed microdefects
Laser power ( P ), scanning speed ( v ), hatch spacing ( h ), layer thick -
ness ( t ), and scanning strategy are among the most common process 
parameters in LPBF, and their effect on microscopic defects is a current 
focus of research. Insufficient energy input can result in a molten pool 
that is too narrow to overlap with adjacent pools, leading to defects such 
as unfused pores ( Fig. 11 a) [ 82 , 83 ]. These defects can be eliminated by 
increasing the energy input to generate sufficient overlap ( Figs. 11 b-d), 
for example, by decreasing the scanning speed and increasing the laser 
power. However, excessive energy input can disturb the flow of the 
molten pool, causing enhanced convection and spattering, leading to the 
formation of micropores, such as keyholes ( Figs. 11 b-e) [ 84 – 90 ]. 
Therefore, there is a threshold value for the energy density required to 
fabricate a defect-free part [ 91 ]. For example, Nataliya et al. successfully 
produced an In738LC alloy with a porosity smaller than 0.5 % and no 
microcracks by using medium energy density [ 92 ]. Moreover, different 
combinations of process parameters may yield the same energy density, 
as determined by the general formula for energy density, E = P/vht . In 
such cases, optimizing energy density alone may not achieve the desired 
result of manufacturing a defect-free part. Tachibana et al. used high 
laser power and scanning speed to address the time-consuming issue of 
manufacturing In718 alloy by LPBF but found that the relative density 
was lower than that of samples fabricated using lower laser power and 
scanning speed [ 93 ]. In conclusion, it is complex to study the effect of 
process parameters on microscopic defects and regulate them solely 
through manufacturing experiments. Therefore, understanding the for -
mation mechanism of micropore defects is an important issue in the 
data-driven optimization of LPBF for Ni-based superalloys.

microstructure of LPBFed In625 alloy. This model considers the time-

demonstrated higher accuracy than the traditional MC model ( Fig. 19 a).

Optimizing grain growth to achieve the desired microstructure is 
crucial for Ni-based superalloys and can be achieved by adjusting pro -

thermal gradient, and diffusion rate. However, due to the ultra-high 
cooling rates in LPBF processing, simulations are necessary to predict

4.1.3. Manufacturing process related
In fact, quality control of manufacturing processes is the most critical 
scientific issue in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), and it is heavily data- 
driven [ 125 – 127 ]. As discussed in Section 2.3 , the microstructure design 
space of Ni-based superalloys is complex, leading to a significant volume 
of data involved in the manufacturing processes. Since LPBF is a layer- 
by-layer fabrication strategy, each layer's quality directly impacts the 
subsequent layers. Therefore, controlling the formation of the melt pool 
is crucial for ensuring uniform thermal gradients, consistent cooling 
rates, and optimal solidification conditions, which are essential for 
achieving a high-quality microstructure. Additionally, suppressing the 
formation of defects such as porosity, cracks, and incomplete fusion is 
vital to maintain the integrity and mechanical properties of the final part 
[ 128 – 131 ]. Recent studies have focused on simulating the formation of 
molten pools, exploring three main aspects: 1) the variation of the 
temperature field and the size of the molten pool, 2) the physical effects

Commercial simulation software, such as Fluent and Comsol, has 
been widely used to model the dynamics of the molten pool. These 
simulations have shown that increasing laser power and decreasing 
scanning speed result in a larger molten pool [ 132 , 133 ]. However, it 
should be noted that the peak temperature in the molten pool is not at 
the laser spot's center but lags behind it by about 4 μ m [ 133 , 134 ]. This 
phenomenon can be explained by the time required for the powder to 
melt. Simulations have linked the peak temperature to heat accumula -
tion and changes in thermal conductivity [ 134 , 135 ]. It has been 
observed that powder at the leading edge of the molten pool may not 
fully fuse due to insufficient heat transfer at low energy input. This leads 
to increased viscosity and incomplete diffusion of the molten pool, ul -
timately compromising the integrity of the final solidified track. 
Conversely, excessive energy input can also result in a poor solidified 
track, such as a rough surface, due to an unstable molten pool caused by 
a sharp decrease in viscosity. Therefore, multiple simulations are rec -
ommended to determine the optimal energy input, considering the 
experimental cost.
Despite their usefulness, such non-open-source conventional com -
mercial software assumes a linear relationship between liquid-phase 
fraction and solidification range during the solidification process for 
Ni-based superalloys ( Fig. 20 b1), which is widely considered nonlinear. 
This assumption can lead to discrepancies in the temperature profile, 
resulting in inaccurate predictions of the melt pool size. To address this 
issue, Le et al. [ 136 ] developed a CFD model based on nonlinear 
solidification-behavior equations for the LPBF of the In718 alloy to 
simulate the formation of a single track ( Fig. 20 b2). The results 
confirmed that this model has higher accuracy in predicting the length 
of the molten pool ( Fig. 20 b3). In addition to laser power and scanning 
speed, various factors such as hatch spacing, laser spot size, heat-source 
model, and laser absorption rate can also affect the formation behavior 
of the molten pool. For instance, Luis E. et al. [ 137 ] found that the latent 
heat of phase transformation has a negligible effect on the peak tem -
perature, while reflectivity and laser power have the most significant 
impact. Variations in spot size have little effect on the molten pool ge -
ometry, whereas reflectivity, laser power, and scanning speed signifi -
cantly influence it.
As discussed in Section 3 , the digitized LPBF of Ni-based superalloys 
is complex due to the various physical effects involved in the 
manufacturing process. These effects include viscous forces, surface 
tension, thermal capillary forces, heat conduction, Marangoni convec -
tion, thermal evaporation, and recoil pressure, which all interact to 
impact the morphology and temperature field of the molten pool. 
Consequently, researchers have made significant progress in modeling 
these factors using different simulation methods. Weirather et al. [ 138 ] 
developed a smooth particle hydrodynamic (SPH) model, while Zheng 
et al. [ 139 ] used the lattice Boltzmann method to investigate the surface 
morphology of the molten pool of In718 alloy during LPBF. The SPH 
simulations by Weirather et al. showed that the width of the molten pool 
is initially wider due to direct interaction between the material and the 
laser radiation. However, over time, heat conduction and convection 
occur, causing the width to decrease more quickly at higher scanning 
speeds and lower laser power [ 140 ]. On the other hand, the lattice 
Boltzmann simulations by Zheng et al. ( Fig. 21 a) demonstrated that high 
laser power can stabilize the melt flow due to recoil pressure, leading to 
a high surface quality of the formed molten pool (Fig. 21b1). Conversely, 
low laser power can cause difficulties in maintaining the stability of the

4.1.4. Thermal management related
The high-temperature gradient around the laser irradiation area 
typically results in higher thermal stress during the manufacturing 
process, leading to residual stress, part deformation, and crack genera -

parameters on thermal stress, thereby controlling residual stress, part 
deformation, and crack generation. For instance, Zhao et al. [ 162 ] used 
simulations to identify the highest temperature at the corner of the end 
of the laser scanning path. They found that the powder around this 
position would be reheated, resulting in higher thermal stress due to the 
shrinking of the solidified molten pool in different directions [ 163 ].

separate from the substrate, as shown in Fig. 25 a. As a result, controlling 
thermal stress is a scientific issue of great interest in data-driven LPBF

with deposition height ( Z -axis), resulting in the highest tensile residual 
stress being concentrated at the two ends of the interface between the 
thin-walled part and the substrate. In contrast, the smallest residual 
stresses were observed in the direction perpendicular (Y-axis) to the 
scanning direction (X-axis).

Up to now, thermal management has been based on user experience, 
using simple beam paths and constant laser power/speed to control the 
thermal field, followed by post-analysis through simulation or charac -
terization techniques. However, a new concept called the inverse ther -
mal placement problem has recently emerged ( Fig. 28 a). This method 
starts from the desired thermal field in the powder bed and solves the 
inverse thermal placement problem to derive time-varying beam pa -
rameters (path, scan speed, energy delivered). For instance, Billingham 
et al. demonstrated this strategy in LPBF In625 alloy [ 177 ]. They 
established a cost function and a set of constraints that define the beam 
path, power delivery profile, and evolving 3D temperature field, 
including its gradients in both space and time, required in the built part. 
The solution provides a near-optimal path (position and velocity) and 
power of the energy beam as a function of time. The beam path is not 
pre-defined based on user experience but is determined as the output of 
a partial differential equation-constrained optimization problem. Addi -
tional constraints governed by machine limitations, such as maximum 
and minimum beam power and maximum and minimum velocity and 
acceleration of the beam scanning system for a particular PBF system, 
can also be enforced during optimization. This rational approach 
potentially gives users control of the entire heating and cooling cycle 
and opens the possibility of using complex beam paths that may revisit 
some segments of the part more than once to control the cooling rate. In 
contrast, the current method involves selecting a set of parameters a 
priori and estimating the thermal field, requiring multiple iterations. 
The inverse thermal placement problem, however, controls the spatial 
and temporal distribution of heat to drive the sample's solidification, 
recrystallization, and in-situ heat treatment processes. For example, 
Yang et al. applied the inverse thermal placement problem to achieve a 
more uniform thermal field in LPBF In718 alloy [ 178 ]. As shown in the 
Fig. 28 b, the laser scanning paths obtained through this method are not 
regular straight lines but result in a more uniform thermal distribution.
All in all, data-driven optimization methods are crucial for thermal 
management issues during the LPBF process of Ni-based superalloys. By 
acquiring data through simulations and experiments, it is possible to 
optimize laser parameters, scanning strategies, and preheating to 
manage residual stress, prevent part deformation, and reduce crack 
generation. Future research should further explore data-driven optimi -
zation methods to enhance the development and application of LPBF 
technology. Additionally, the inverse thermal placement problem might 
be key to achieving intelligent development in LPBF in the future, as it 
uses mathematical foundations to solve thermal field equations 
inversely.

4.2.2. Process monitoring related
Although it is feasible to directly establish the relationship between 
process parameters and target performance through machine learning 
models, the amount of data required from experiments for training 
models for predicting with high accuracy is huge. As mentioned earlier, 
Ni-based superalloys have complex design spaces, which can generate 
abundant data during the manufacturing process. These data can be 
captured by sensors to drive the LPBF process. In recent years, features 
during the manufacturing process, such as spattering, light intensity, 
and temperature field, have been considered the data to solve this dif -
ficulty. This is because these in-process features can be obtained via 
image sensors, light signal sensors, temperature sensors, etc. Therefore, 
process monitoring is developing rapidly to be utilized in LPBF. Of 
course, some work [ 189 – 200 ] has been done using this method in the 
LPBF of Ni-based superalloys ( Fig. 32 ).
Regarding the process monitoring with image signals ( Fig. 32 a), 
Scime et al. [ 192 ] captured the dynamic morphology of the molten pool 
during its formation with a high-speed camera. The resulting image 
signals were analyzed using computer vision techniques to extract key 
features describing the molten pool. These features were classified using 
an unsupervised algorithm to correlate with specific microdefects, such 
as pores. This method, which links in-situ and ex-situ information, can 
help identify the types of microdefects in a new molten pool when new 
image data is acquired. However, manual labeling is often needed to 
compensate for the information loss during image signal parsing. In their 
study, Scime et al. used Scale Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) to 
convert molten pool information into gradient field grayscale images 
based on the gradient directions around image pixels. The gradient field 
data was then dimensionally reduced through clustering to obtain key 
features. This process involved manually selecting the image contrast, 
which may lead to information loss. Fathizadan et al. [ 193 ] recognized 
that manual feature engineering was insufficient to fully characterize 
molten pools due to potential human error. To address this, they 
employed a neural network model, specifically conventional autoen -
coding (CAE), to automatically extract features from molten pool images 
and identify anomalies during the LPBF process. Their method enabled 
the model to automatically detect and label “ anomalous ” molten pools, 
achieving an accuracy of 95 % and an F1 score of 82 % in distinguishing 
between normal and anomalous molten pools in the LPBF process of the 
In625 alloy.

plume and sputtering during LPBF can be analyzed by measuring this

4.2.3. Coupling of machine learning and simulation
As described above, machine learning can be used to mine the po -
tential relationship between input and output, while simulation based 
on physical models can reveal the forming mechanism in visualization. 
Hence, researchers have utilized machine learning and simulation 
models to optimize Ni-based superalloys in LPBF manufacturing 
[ 158 , 159 , 201 – 208 ]. For instance, the data generated by simulations and 
experiments is high-dimensional and difficult to understand and exploit. 
By using data science, factors that have a closer influence on inputs and 
outcomes can be effectively integrated and mined. A self-organizing 
map (SOM), an unsupervised machine learning algorithm based on 
neural networks, is capable of mapping high-dimensional data to a two- 
dimensional (2D) plane while maintaining topology [ 209 , 210 ]. Gan 
et al. [ 201 ] designed a series of experiments with simulations ( Fig. 33 ) 
and obtained data on the laser power, mass flow rate, energy density, 
dilution, cooling rate, dendrite arm spacing, and microhardness in a 
single track of In718 alloy under laser irradiation. They then used this 
data as input and applied the self-organizing map (SOM) algorithm to 
calculate the correlations between them, obtaining the SOM map after 
multiple iterations. Through this map, they found that the mass flow rate 
had the greatest impact on the cooling rate and dendrite arm spacing, 
while dilution and microhardness depended on the mass flow rate and 
laser power. These studies demonstrate that the combination of machine 
learning and simulation models can effectively optimize the perfor -
mance of Ni-based superalloys in LPBF manufacturing and offer more 
possibilities for their applications.
Studying thermal stress is a worthwhile research direction, as 
described in Section 4.1.4 . However, the simulation approach typically 
used has a disadvantage in terms of long-term costs and computational 
effort at the current state of the art. Consequently, several studies have 
introduced machine learning to assist in adjusting and testing the con -
structed simulation model to reduce computational effort. Anandan 
Kumar et al. [ 211 ] first simulated the thermal field of the In625 alloy 
during LPBF. They then used the obtained temperature data as input for 
the Gaussian regression model, with the data from experiments as 
output, instead of going through the adjustment and test steps of the 
simulation model, as indicated in Fig. 34 . The conventional optimizing 
process is generally considered to be time-consuming. The results show 
that this method effectively shortens the calibration process, saving time 
and maintaining high prediction accuracy. Similarly, Tran et al. [ 205 ] 
also used an alternative model to simplify the calibration process in the 
multiscale model they developed. They used material information, 
namely the particle size and reflectance of the In718 alloy, to calculate 
the laser energy absorption rate first. They then used the laser power and 
scanning speed as input for the thermal simulation to finally obtain the 
size of the solidified track.
Bayesian optimization (BO) is a sequential strategy for optimizing 
black-box objective functions that has been considered suitable for 
simulations with huge computational effort in additive manufacturing 
[ 203 , 212 ]. Kitano et al. [ 158 , 159 ] used a BO model based on the 
simulated results of solidification cracking to find the best model pa -
rameters with a minimized deviation between the simulated and real -
istic results, thereby obtaining the best process parameters for 
manufacturing the Hastelloy X alloy without microcracks. Therefore, 
machine learning models can be integrated into the simulation process.
In summary, machine learning can be used to analyze simulation 
results and identify connections between different process factors. It can 
also describe the correlations between process factors by examining the

5. Challenges and perspectives
In summary, the current understanding of Ni-based superalloys has 
advanced significantly, particularly in comprehending their micro -
structure's influence on macroscopic properties. Traditional 
manufacturing methods, such as rolling and forging, primarily rely on 
deformation processes to achieve specific microstructures, but they lack 
the flexibility to produce complex structures. LPBF technology has led to 
a shift in human demand for high-performance nickel-based superalloy 
components. One now seeks to satisfy both mechanical performance and 
complex structural requirements. Consequently, the primary concern is 
in successfully fabricating well-designed microstructures using LPBF 
technology while preserving the integrity of the pre-designed complex 
geometrical structures. Based on this premise, the two challenges faced 
by Ni-based superalloys manufactured through LPBF can be succinctly 
summarized as follows: 
a) Non-equilibrium solidification is characteristic of the LPBF and dif -
fers from the conventional metallurgical process. The establishment 
of guidelines for the control of solid solution, elemental segregation, 
and thermal distribution is an urgent requirement for the fabrication 
of Ni-based superalloys with the desired properties.
b) There is a trade-off between the process parameters, microdefects, 
and precipitate phases. For instance, the accompanying microdefects 
should be considered simultaneously when tuning the precipitate to 
obtain the desired properties.
These two problems are, in fact, only the tip of the iceberg for

establishing the process-structure-performance relationship, but study -

significantly difficult and expensive. For this, one technique currently 
attracting the LPBF manufacturing field is integrated computational 
materials engineering. Simulation and machine learning as representa -
tives of data-driven LPBF, focusing on Ni-based superalloys, are 
reviewed in this work. The challenges and perspectives are summarized 
below: 
c) The variety of precipitates involved in LPBF Ni-based superalloys 
studied by simulation is still relatively small, which requires more 
attention. In addition, simulations can help to study grain growth 
behavior based on solidification parameters and to obtain methods to 
customize grain growth. For this purpose, it is suggested to explore 
the construction of coupled multi-scale models to improve the pre -
diction accuracy of solidified structures. Meanwhile, the simulation 
technique is adept at studying the LPBF processing via visualization 
to explore the mechanisms involved, including the formation of 
microdefects, laser-material interaction, and molten pool flow 
behavior. However, the study of simulation models on the cracking 
of LPBF Ni-based superalloys is still lacking. Since one of the causes 
of cracking is attributed to thermal stress, the research direction on 
thermal stress optimization via simulation is also addressed in LPBF 
Ni-based superalloys. However, thermal stress management in the 
manufacturing process is still in its infancy, so it is suggested to keep 
up the effort. It is important to emphasize the significance of inverse 
thermal management, which conducts reverse thermal management 
by taking the expected thermal field as the input and laser parame -
ters as the output. This approach deserves future attention.
d) Applying machine learning to LPBF Ni alloys generally uses the 
process parameters as input and the target performance as output. 
However, the generalization ability of machine learning models 
usually depends on the volume of data. Obtaining samples with a 
sufficient amount of data for LPBF Ni-based superalloys is very time- 
consuming and expensive. Moreover, different machine learning 
models are applicable to different situations, especially for Ni-based 
superalloys, where many influencing factors are involved. Hence, it 
is recommended to build a decision scheme to select an appropriate 
machine learning model for different prediction objectives. There -
fore, establishing a direct process-property relationship is still at a 
challenging stage. On the other hand, the employing of sensors to 
acquire data generated during the manufacturing process is a 
mainstream research direction, i.e., the acquired images, optical 
signals, thermal signals, etc. are used as inputs and the corresponding 
properties are used as outputs to build models. This approach can 
greatly save time and reduce the cost of acquiring data. In addition, 
machine learning can be combined with numerical simulation to 
reduce computation time and improve efficiency by replacing the 
calibration process.
Accordingly, around these challenges, it is necessary to continue 
exploring the metallurgical mechanism and clarifying the metallurgical 
processes of different elements for LPBF of Ni-based superalloys, to 
continue the macroscopic properties studies, and to develop special 
alloy systems used for LPBF. In order to achieve a prospective forecast of 
the material-process-microstructure-property relationship, the author 
summarizes the existing work and proposes several topics of possible 
interest:
Simulation efficiency-LPBF toward smart manufacturing must 
develop simulation models with more accurate predictions and higher 
computational efficiency. More reliable fidelity for precipitation phases, 
microstructure control, thermal management, and micro-defects are 
urgent in Ni-based superalloys during LPBF.
Database building-The reliability of machine learning largely de -
pends on the amount of data. Researchers could obtain large amounts of 
data by developing in-situ sensor and non-in-situ sensor characterization 
techniques. Therefore, it is suggested that these data be shared online to 
build a database that greatly leverages the power of artificial 
intelligence.
Signal collection equipment-The scale of the LPBF process is 
extremely small, and the melting and solidification processes are tran -
sient, making it difficult to capture and analyze the generated signals. 
Therefore, developing advanced signal collection equipment is crucial. 
At the same time, establishing sustainable connections between signals 
and corresponding microstructure features is a prerequisite for 
achieving feedback.
Develop inverse thermal management technology-Thermal man -
agement is a crucial means to enhance the performance of components 
manufactured by LPBF, especially precipitation-strengthened Ni-based 
superalloys. The high cooling rates in the LPBF process can easily lead to 
matrix supersaturation, making it difficult to control the content and 
morphology of precipitates. Current methods use a trial-and-error 
approach to control the thermal field, which is time-consuming and 
imprecise. Therefore, developing mathematically-based inverse thermal 
placement technology is essential for achieving “ready-to-use” additive 
manufacturing.
