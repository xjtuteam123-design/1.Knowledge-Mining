ABSTRACT 
The development of sound cost-effective alloys is always the pursuit of engineering application materials. A non- 
equiatomic FeMnCrNiAl high-entropy alloy (HEA) has been processed by cold rolling and annealing to prepare 
samples with a fully-recrystallized structure, and composite structures of recovered and recrystallized structures. 
The composite structure with a dual heterogeneous structure of both the matrix and precipitates can be designed 
to provide a synergistic combination of strength and ductility at ambient and cryogenic temperatures. For the 
heterogeneous structure, the rule of the mixture has been applied to calculate the flow stress, revealing an extra 
strengthening component from dislocation hardening, precipitation hardening, and back-stress hardening. A 
minor multi-component incoherent B2 phase is still innocuous to low-temperature toughness. 
1. Introduction 
No matter if the environment, the strength, and toughness of metals 
or alloys are important indicators for load-bearing applications, the 
strength-ductility synergy is a long-standing issue. Usually, conventional 
grain refinement, severe plastic deformation, and precipitation 
strengthening will generally increase strength at the expense of ductility 
at ambient temperature [ 1 , 2 ]. Recently, multi-component high-entropy 
alloys (HEAs) have attracted intensive interests in the metallic materials 
field [ 3 – 6 ]. The wider composition selection of HEAs could bring more 
possibilities for alloy properties. At present, there have been many 
routes to prepare novel HEAs with a combination of high strength and 
toughness. Among them, HEAs with heterogeneous structures often 
exhibit an excellent strength – ductility synergy [ 7 – 10 ]. A striking 
example is the non-equiatomic VCoCrFeNiMn HEA, which displays a 
remarkable tensile ductility (~46.3%) and tensile strength (~1314 
MPa) at 77 K [ 11 ]. A heterogeneous structure not only refers to the 
difference in grain sizes but also the heterogeneity of phase structures 
[ 12 , 13 ]. This utilizable strategy greatly enhances the improvement po -
tential of alloy properties. On the other hand, low costs are one of the 
pursuits of alloys for engineering applications. The exploration for 
higher strength and toughness of alloys together with low costs is badly 
needed. 
Therefore, based on the classic CoCrFeMnNi alloy, the rare and 
environmentally-unfriendly Co element is removed. The Cr content 
should be 12.5 – 15 atomic percent (at. %), which can not only form a 
dense corrosion-resistant passivation film but also avoid the formation 
of brittle σ phase [ 14 ]. Here, the Cr content is taken to be 15 at. %. The 
less expensive Al element is added to bring greater solid-solution 
strengthening and second-phase-precipitation strengthening. Keep 
enough Fe and Mn elements that are cheap as well. At the same time, 
referring to the calculation of the JMatPro phase diagram [ 15 ], an

3.3. Microstructural evolution 
The alloy exhibits different deformation behaviors under various 
microstructures and different temperatures. The fundamental cause may 
be the difference in the dislocation-storage capacity and dislocation 
multiplication. After the tensile tests at room temperature, all samples 
displayed high-density dislocations, most of which were in the 
morphology of dislocation cells. The dislocation density in partially- 
recrystallized samples is significantly higher than that of fully- 
recrystallized samples. It is due to the high-density storage of disloca -
tions within coarsened grains and back-stress hardening. 
For the cryogenic tests, as shown in Fig. 5 (c), a small number of 
deformation twins appeared within the fully-recrystallized alloy. The 
presence of precipitated phases may increase the critical twin stress of 
the alloys, leading to the postponed availability of mechanical twins.

successfully developed in the present study. Microstructure evolutions 
of the present alloys subjected to two annealing conditions (inducing 
partial recrystallization and full recrystallization) have been investi -
gated and correlated with the resultant tensile properties at room and 
cryogenic temperatures. 
1. The fully-recrystallized single-phase HEAs exhibit low strength and 
large ductility, similar to most single-phase FCC alloys. The yield 
strength is 240 MPa, tensile strength is 540 MPa, and elongation is 
47%. Due to the appearance of deformation twins, high strength and 
high toughness can be achieved at cryogenic temperatures, and the 
mechanical properties are significantly improved. The ultimate yield 
strength is 400 MPa, the tensile strength is 820 MPa, and the elon -
gation has increased to 64% at cryogenic temperatures. 
2. The single-phase HEAs can be transformed into heterogeneous dual- 
phase HEAs by proper thermomechanical treatments to achieve a 
better combination of strength and ductility. The yield strength is 
780 MPa, the tensile strength is 1,080 MPa, and the elongation is 
17% at room temperature. B2-phase precipitates and heterogeneous 
structures bring dislocation hardening, precipitation hardening, and 
back-stress hardening three hardening mechanisms interaction. At 
cryogenic temperatures, the yield strength, tensile strength, and 
elongation are 900 MPa, 1280 MPa, and 19%, respectively. The dual 
heterogeneous structure can also improve the strength-ductility at 
low temperature. 
3. An optimization has been sought through the design of microstruc -
tures containing hard and soft regions contributing to strength and 
ductility, respectively. This heterogeneous dual-phase strategy is 
feasible and can guide a wider range of alloy design.
