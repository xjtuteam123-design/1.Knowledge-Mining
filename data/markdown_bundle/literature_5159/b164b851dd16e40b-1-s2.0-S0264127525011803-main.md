ABSTRACT
To facilitate the evaluation and prediction of hot-end component performance, scanning electron microscopy and 
quasi-static compression tests were carried out on Ni-based single crystal superalloys, and the influence of γ ′ 
phase on microstructural evolution and compressive properties was systematically investigated. Results show 
that γ ′ phases exhibit spherical, cubic, or lath-like morphologies, and their average size increases from ~ 180 nm 
to ~ 450 nm after thermal exposure; and superalloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase gradually pre -
cipitate topologically close-packed (TCP) phase. The compressive properties display pronounced anisotropy, 
governed by both microstructure and loading direction. For superalloys with lower volume fraction of γ ′ phase, 
yield strength decreases from 670 MPa to 505 MPa and ultimate compressive strength from 4690 MPa to 4240 
MPa as the γ ′ phase coarsens. In contrast, for superalloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase, ultimate 
compressive strength initially decreases and then increases, accompanied by rise in failure strain from 22 % to 
46 % after thermal exposure. With increasing loading angle, ultimate compressive strength initially decreases 
and then rises, whereas yield strength, failure strain and hardening modulus exhibit more complex trends. These 
variations are closely related to γ ′ and TCP phase, and microstructure and loading direction collectively affect 
mechanical behavior.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal (SC) superalloys, known for their exceptional 
strength, hardness, fracture toughness, as well as outstanding oxidation 
resistance, are indispensable for the advancement of high-temperature 
technologies and are extensively employed in critical hot-end compo -
nents of aerospace engines, advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power 
plants, and heavy-duty gas turbines [ 1 – 4 ]. During the service life, these 
components are exposed to harsh environments such as high tempera -
tures, high pressures, and complex mechanical loads, which can pro -
mote microstructural evolution, especially the coarsening of γ ′ phase 
caused by Ostwald ripening [ 5 , 6 ]. This microstructural evolution could 
affect the mechanical properties of superalloys [ 7 – 9 ], posing substantial 
threats to equipment stability. In practice, Ni-based superalloy compo -
nents often encounter substantial compressive stresses in addition to 
tensile loads [ 10 – 12 ], and with the growing complexity of service con -
ditions, the risk of compression-related failure in hot-end components 
has increased. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the relationship 
between compressive behavior and microstructural features in SC Ni- 
based superalloys is essential for accurate performance evaluation and 
service life prediction. Such knowledge not only deepens the mecha -
nistic understanding of their outstanding properties but also provides 
scientific guidance for the safe design and optimization of high- 
temperature components.
SC Ni-based alloys are two-phase alloys, and the superior perfor -
mance from a precisely engineered γ / γ ′ microstructure, in which the γ ′ 
phase plays a dominant strengthening role [ 1 , 13 ]. Previous studies 
[ 14 , 15 ] have shown that the γ ′ phase is distributed within the γ phase as 
spherical or cubic particles, and Meyers and Chawla [ 16 ] and Duan et al. 
[ 17 ] reported that precipitation strengthening significantly enhances 
the high-temperature strength and thermal stability of superalloys. In 
addition, Hao et al. [ 18 ] investigated the influence of γ ′ phase on the 
mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys subjected to different 
solution treatment temperatures. Their results indicated that high

correlations between these mechanical properties and both micro -

provide both theoretical insights and experimental support for opti -
mizing the design and performance prediction of Ni-based superalloys 
under complex service conditions, holding significant value for 
advancing their application in key high-temperature components such 
as aerospace engines and turbine blades.
2. Materials and experimental procedure
2.1. Experimental material
As two-phase materials, the mechanical properties of SC Ni-based 
superalloys, such as strength, fracture toughness and hardness, are 
closely linked to microstructural characteristics and loading direction. 
In this study, two typical volume fractions of γ ′ phase ( φ = 30 % and φ =
60 %) are selected, representing the typical lower and higher ends 
observed in SC Ni-based superalloys [ 1 , 40 ]. Through a higher volume 
fraction of γ ′ phase is more widely used in the practical engineering, the 
corresponding γ phase is too narrow and the γ ′ phase is usually no longer 
exhibits a particle configuration (see Ref. [ 13 ]), to provide a clear 
comparative investigation on the influences of γ ′ phase characteristics 
(morphology, size, volume fraction) on the microstructural evolution, 
mechanical properties, especially the anisotropy of mechanical proper -
ties and its correlation with phase coarsening, superalloys with these 
two levels (30 % and 60 %) of volume fraction of γ ′ phase are adopted in 
the present investigation, and four thermal exposure time ( t = 0h, 100 h, 
500 h and 1000 h) are applied for comparative research. For conve -
nience of discussion, superalloys with φ = 30 % are sequentially referred 
to as LV-T0, LV-T100, LV-T500, and LV-T1000 alloy according to 
increasing thermal exposure time; similarly, superalloys with φ = 60 % 
are named as HV-T0, HV-T100, HV-T500 and HV-T1000 alloy.

2.2. Microstructure characterization
All samples are obtained by slicing along the central axis of the test 
rod, and observed using SEM and XRD equipped. Initially, the sample is

2.3. Quasi-static compression test
Quasi-static compression tests are carried out using the universal

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure evolution and structural analysis
3.1.1. XRD phase analysis
Fig. 3 presents the XRD patterns of precipitates in SC Ni-based su -
peralloys with different volume fractions of γ ′ phase. The results indicate 
that for superalloys with lower volume fraction of γ ′ phase ( φ = 30 %), 
only the (111) and (200) diffraction peaks are observed, i.e., the su -
peralloys are primarily composed of γ and γ ′ phases. Although no new 
phases are observed, the (111) peak area gradually decreases with the 
increase of thermal exposure time, while the (200) peak area shows the 
opposite trend. This can be attributed to the progressive coarsening of γ ′ 
precipitates, which increases the lattice mismatch between the γ and γ ′ 
phases [ 48 , 49 ]. The accumulated local strain and coherency loss at the 
γ / γ ′ interface may reduce the long-range order and diffraction coherence 
in the γ matrix, particularly along the densely packed (1 1 1) planes. In 
addition, long-term thermal exposure may promote defect formation or 
substructural evolution (e.g., low-angle boundaries), further weakening 
the (1 1 1) reflection.
In contrast, for superalloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase ( φ 
= 60 %), the XRD pattern of the unexposed superalloys (i.e., HV-T0 
alloy) likewise exhibits only the (111) and (200) diffraction peaks. 
However, an additional (110) diffraction peak gradually appears after 
thermal exposure, alongside the original peaks, indicating the 
precipitation of TCP phases. The TCP phase is typically characterized by 
densely packed layers of smaller atoms with larger atoms interstitially 
embedded. Meanwhile, as the thermal exposure time increases, the 
(200) peak area decreases while (111) peak area increases gradually, 
and this can be attributed to the combined effects of extensive γ ′ 
coarsening and the precipitation of TCP phases [ 1 , 50 – 52 ]. The forma -
tion of these phases introduces crystallographic complexity and lattice 
distortions, particularly affecting the (200) plane reflections.
3.1.2. Microstructural evolution
In the following, the microstructural and structural evolution of SC 
Ni-based superalloys during thermal exposure is examined in detail. 
Fig. 4 shows the microstructural evolution and size distribution of γ ′ 
phases. In the unexposed superalloys, the γ ′ phase is small and uniformly 
distributed. As thermal exposure time increases, the γ ′ phase gradually 
coarsens, with an increasing proportion of larger particles and a sparse 
overall distribution. Moreover, the morphology of γ ′ phase is closely 
related to volume fraction of γ ′ phase. Specifically, in superalloys with φ 
= 30 %, γ ′ phase appears predominantly as uniformly dispersed spher -
ical particles within the matrix phase; whereas in superalloys with φ =
60 %, the γ ′ phase mainly exhibits blocky or lath-like morphology with a 
denser distribution. These observations are consistent with the XRD 
analysis and further confirm the coarsening behavior of the γ ′ phase 
during thermal exposure. The measurement methods for determining γ ′ 
precipitate size follow those described by Cao et al. [ 53 ] and Horst et al. 
[ 54 ].

3.1.3. TCP phase precipitation
Under high-temperature service conditions, the refractory elements 
enriched in SC Ni-based superalloys promote precipitation of TCP phase, 
as confirmed by combined XRD and SEM/EDS characterization. During 
the coarsening of γ ′ phase, the internal elements of superalloys are 
redistributed, with solid-solution elements in the γ phase diffusing into 
the TCP phase. This phenomenon becomes significantly amplified in 
alloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase. As demonstrated in Fig. 6 , 
the as-cast microstructure exhibits uniformly distributed cubic γ ′ phase, 
whereas thermal exposure induces the precipitation of TCP phases with 
needle-like or blocky morphologies. There phases are identified ac -
cording to EDS results, XRD patterns as well as SEM morphology 
[ 20 , 46 ]. As the thermal exposure time increases, the amount of TCP 
phase progressively increases, and after 1000 h of thermal exposure, a 
large number of TCP phase are distributed throughout the matrix.

Fig. 9 (b-c) further shows the engineering stress – strain curves from

that the stress increases rapidly in the early elastic stage, reflecting the 
linear elastic behavior. Beyond the yield point, the slope of curve de -
creases, and the strain hardening can be observed. Subsequently, as 
plastic deformation progresses, the stress rises at an accelerated rate, 
and it is closely related to the significant bulging observed in later 
loading stages (see Fig. 7 ). In addition, some specimens display a linear 
hardening behavior during plastic deformation. When damage occurs 
and cracks form, rapid crack propagation leads to a sharp drop in the 
stress – strain curve, ultimately resulting in complete fracture with the 
stress falling to zero.

γ ′ phase, a sharp drop in the strain is observed after thermal exposure, 
most notably in the case of the HV-100 alloy (see Fig. 9(c) ). This 
behavior may be attributed to the combined effects of γ ′ phase coars -

phase reduces the precipitation strengthening capability, while the TCP 
phases, being intrinsically brittle and incoherent with the γ matrix

3.3. Correlation analysis of microstructural and compressive properties
3.3.1. Influence of γ / γ ′ microstructural and dislocation dynamics
The microstructure of SC Ni-based superalloys primarily consists of γ 
phase and γ ′ phase, where the γ ′ phase, coherently embedded within the 
γ matrix phase, serves as the primary strengthening phase due to its 
higher resistance to plastic deformation [ 58 , 71 , 73 ]. The volume frac -

the compressive properties of superalloys. During compressive defor -
mation, dislocations in the γ matrix phase are strongly hindered by the γ ′ 
phase. Two principal mechanisms dominate this interaction: (I) cutting, 
which occurs when fine γ ′ phase is coherently sheared by dislocations, 
and (II) bypassing via the Orowan mechanism, which becomes dominant 
when γ ′ phase grow beyond a critical size. As expressed by classical

increasing the average spacing between precipitation particles. This 
reduces the hindrance to dislocation movement, leading to a gradual 
decrease in yield strength, ultimate compressive strength, and hard -

induce substantial stress concentrations. In regions where dislocations 
pile up at large or clustered γ ′ phases, this leads to the formation of 
micro-voids or interfacial decohesion, ultimately promoting crack 
nucleation. For example, Zhang and Yue [ 83 ] shows that the γ / γ ′

precipitate interface, and form dislocation network near the γ ′ phase, 
leading to a concentration of interfacial stress. When the stress con -

interface, as shown in Fig. 16(c) . Besides, in He et al. [ 29 ] ’ s compression 
test for PM Ni-based superalloys, micro-voids and micro-cracks are 
noticeable at the grain boundaries of the specimen, and the corre -
sponding image is shown in Fig. 16(d) . This damage initiation mecha -
nism becomes more pronounced in superalloys with high volume 
fraction of γ ′ phases. As the volume fraction of γ ′ phase increases, the 
density of these stress concentrators rises, strengthening the alloy by 
further impeding dislocation movement. However, the elevated inter -

and increasing the likelihood of failure.

nucleation along the interfaces, significantly reducing failure strain.

become more widely spaced. This reduces both the obstacle strength and 
interface stress levels, which delays new micro-void nucleation and al -

3.3.2. Influence of TCP phase
The TCP phase is considered to be one of the important reasons for 
the degradation of Ni-based superalloy properties in most studies 
[ 46 , 85 , 86 ]. These phases act as incoherent and brittle inclusions 
embedded within the γ / γ ′ phase. Their mismatch in both lattice structure 
and elastic modulus leads to severe local stress concentrations during 
deformation, particularly under complex loading conditions [ 21 , 87 ]. 
These stress concentrations not only promote dislocation pile-up at 
phase boundaries, but also facilitate interfacial decohesion and crack 
propagation. From a crystallographic perspective, the TCP phases, due 
to their limited number of active slip systems, are less capable of plastic 
accommodation under stress, and thus function as effective crack initi -
ation sites when surrounded by more ductile γ and γ ′ phases [ 66 , 83 , 88 ].
Furthermore, the formation of TCP phases depletes solid solution 
strengthening elements (such as Re, W, Mo), thereby reducing the 
matrix ’ s intrinsic strength and impairing its ability to sustain deforma -
tion. This combined effect of localized embrittlement and matrix 
weakening significantly accelerates failure, especially in alloys with 
prolonged thermal exposure and higher γ ′ content [ 89 , 90 ]. As a result, 
for superalloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase, the precipitation 
of TCP phases after thermal exposure reduces plasticity, and it results in 
significant brittle fracture during compression, as well as decreasing the 
ultimate compressive strength and failure strain. Similar features are 
also observed in the published studies, Zhang and Yue [ 83 ] conducted 
tests for SC Ni-based superalloys and found that the crack is easily 
initiated and propagated along the TCP phase under the combination of 
misfit stress and the dislocation pile-up stress, as shown in Fig. 17 .
However, there is a relatively small influence of TCP phase on the 
compressive properties because of its lower content, hence the coars -
ening mechanism of γ ′ phase still plays a dominant role in the late stage 
of thermal exposure [ 91 , 92 ]. Therefore, the ultimate compressive 
strength still increases with increasing γ ′ phase average size. This 
observation is also consistent with previous studies, e.g., Simonetti et al. 
[ 91 ] did works on the effect of μ phase and suggested that the small 
volume fraction of μ phase ( < 1 vol%) did not affect the fracture mech -
anism during creep, tensile, impact and fatigue properties until a large 
number of TCP phases exist.

volume fraction of γ ′ phase, this coarsening is further accompa -
nied by the precipitation of TCP phases, highlighting the key role 
of interparticle interactions and interface effects in driving the 
evolution process.
(2) The compressive properties are closely related to both micro -
structural characteristics and loading direction, exhibiting com -
plex anisotropic features. Generally, superalloys with lower 
volume fraction of γ ′ phase demonstrate higher plastic deforma -
tion ability, with a gradual decrease in strength as the phase 
coarsening progresses. Conversely, superalloys with higher vol -
ume fraction of γ ′ phase tend to exhibit brittle failure, and their 
yield strength increases initially and then decreases along with 
thermal exposure, while the ultimate compressive strength and 
failure strain display an opposite trend. Under different loading 
directions, yield strength, ultimate compressive strength, and 
failure strain vary either in a “ V ” shape or monotonic pattern, 
indicating that anisotropic mechanical behavior is strongly 
influenced by particle density and distribution.
(3) Strain hardening in superalloys is governed by multiple factors. 
For most specimens, the Hollomon model accurately describes 
the strain hardening during plastic deformation, whereas for 
materials exhibiting brittle fracture characteristics, a linear 
hardening model provides a better fit. For superalloys with lower 
volume fraction of γ ′ phase, a high hardening exponent is 
observed at early exposure times; however, as the γ ′ phase 
coarsens and loading direction varies, the hardening behavior 
gradually weakens and exhibits clear directional dependence. For 
superalloys with higher volume fraction of γ ′ phase, TCP phase 
precipitation significantly enhances local hardening, with sig -
nificant differences in hardening parameters observed for various 
loading directions.
(4) The morphology, size, orientation, and distribution of γ ′ phase, 
along with the precipitation of TCP phases, collectively govern 
the macroscopic mechanical behavior of superalloys. As the 
volume fraction of γ ′ phase increases, the hindering on the 
propagation of plastic deformation bands is enhanced, thereby 
improving yield strength. However, as phase coarsening causes 
the interparticle spacing to increase, the hindrance to dislocation 
movement is reduced, leading to a gradual decrease in overall 
strength and strain hardening. For superalloys with higher vol -
ume fraction of γ ′ phase, the precipitation of TCP phases can 
partially improve ultimate compressive strength and strain 
hardening. Moreover, the loading direction significantly in -
fluences dislocation nucleation and movement; especially at the 
loading angle of 45
◦
, easier dislocation glide along aligned 
channels results in reduced compressive properties in that 
direction.
