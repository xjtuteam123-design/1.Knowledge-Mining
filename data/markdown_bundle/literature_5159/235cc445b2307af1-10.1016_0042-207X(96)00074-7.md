Superalloys made by conventional vacuum melting 
and a novel spray forming process 
R L Kennedy, R M Forbes Jones and R M Davis, Teledyne Allvac, Monroe, NC, USA 
and 
M G Benz and W T Carter, GE Corporate Research and Development, Schenectady, NY, USA 
Wrought Ni-base superalloys used in modern gas turbine engines are typically produced by vacuum induction 
melting (VIM) plus consumable remelting (ESR and/or VAR). For the more advanced alloys, these processes 
have certain limitations; namely, the ability to produce sound ingots of a reasonable size, free of harmful 
segregation related defects, which can readily be converted into wrought product. Powder metallurgy 
processes have been developed to overcome these problems but substitute another problem: high cost. To 
address these concerns, a clean, ceramicless spray forming process has been developed which uniquely 
combines electroslag remelting (ESR), bottom pouring through a cold induction guide (GIG) and spray forming 
(Osprey Metals Ltd). This paper discusses some of the issues encountered in current superalloy manufacturing 
processes and describes the clean metal spray forming (CMSF) plant which has been constructed to address 
them. Copyright 0 1996 Elsevier Science Ltd. 
Key words: Clean metal spray forming, consumable remelting, superalloys, vacuum melting, rapid 
solidification. 
Introduction 
Vacuum induction melting (VIM), coupled with consumable 
remelting, either vacuum arc remelting (VAR) or electroslag 
remelting (ESR), has served the aerospace industry well for many 
years and continues to do so today as the principal manufacturing 
process for critical rotating component hardware of Ni-base 
superalloys. Over the years, however, the increasingly stringent 
demands of higher operating temperatures and stresses have 
resulted in tnodified processes (e.g. triple melt) and entirely new 
processes (powder metallurgy) designed to solve some of the 
problems of the conventional processes. Both of these new pro- 
cesses are now well established in the superalloy industry, having 
found their niche of alloys and products to which they are well 
suited. However. none of these processes is without its problems. 
and, in recent years, intense international competition has added 
severe cost pressures. 
Recently a new processing route has been introduced’ to 
achieve further improvements over existing methods. Clean metal 
spray forming (CMSF) combines an ESR melting operation to 
produce clean, molten metal with an Osprey type spray forming 
process (Osprey Metals Ltd) to go directly from the melt to a 
dense, fine grained preform in a single step. A ceramic free system 
assures that no re-contamination occurs subsequent to ESR. The 
high rate. single step process from molten metal to solid preform 
is projected to offer the advantage of rapid solidification with a 
substantial cost advantage over PM processing. 
Current manufacturing practices (conventional cast-wrought pro- 
cessing) 
Vacuum induction melting of superalloys was first used as a 
production process in the 1950s and vacuum arc remelting fol- 
lowed shortly thereafter. Based on this long history, the capa- 
bilities as well as the limitations are well known. Table I is a list 
of chemical compositions of several wrought superalloys typically 
produced by VIM followed by VAR or ESR. Several of these 
alloys can be remelted by either method, depending upon cus- 
tomer requirements, product size, or manufacturing con- 
siderations (cost, yield and capacity). The base element in most 
cases is less than 60% of the composition, making these alloys 
very susceptible to segregation. While VAR and ESR processes 
were developed to minimize segregation and produce improved 
macrostructures compared to static casting, it is still possible to 
observe a number of forms of segregation in remelted superalloy 
ingots if proper controls are not exercised.

indicative of poor transverse properties. Grain size banding is 
most frequently observed in the precipitation hardened alloys 
such as Alloy 718, Waspaloy, etc. and results from local differ- 
ences in precipitate solution temperature due to slight differences 
in chemistry. In most cases, it is not possible to completely elim- 
inate evidence of solidification segregation in normal cast- 
wrought superalloys, but it is possible to minimize the effects. 
Control of remelting conditions to maximize local solidification 
rate can decrease the severity of banding. This generally means 
low melting rates. Homogenization, prolonged heating at tem- 
peratures near the melting point of an alloy, will promote 
diffusion of segregated elements. Because of the cost and time 
required to completely homogenize an alloy, it is not practical to 
totally eliminate chemical segregation by this method. Grain size 
and second phase particle banding can also be largely eliminated 
by employing very heavy amounts of hot work from the starting 
ingot to the finished product. 
White spots are a solute-lean segregate area resulting from the 
inherent characteristics of the VAR process. They appear as a 
light etching region on a macro plate (Figure 2). Grain size in a 
white spot is often larger than the surrounding matrix and, in 
some cases, can be associated with clusters of oxide, nitride 
and/or carbon&de particles. Jackman et d2.’ have reported

Freckles are another type of macrosegregation harmful to 
mechanical properties which can be found in conventionally pro- 
cessed superalloys. This defect is typically observed as a dark 
etching radial spot on a macroplate. Freckles are formed by the 
flow of solute-rich, interdendritic fluid in the mushy zone.4 The 
need to prevent freckling results in many practical limitations 
imposed on superalloy ingot manufacturing. In general, increas- 
ing the alloying content, particularly the level of the refractory 
elements and Al and Ti, increases the tendency for freckling. 
Likewise, alloys with large liquidus-solidus zones are more prone 
to freckling. Ingot size limitations for both VAR and ESR are 
frequently set based on the maximum diameter which can be 
produced without freckles. There are also a number of equip- 
ment-related issues which can cause freckling, including unidi- 
rectional stirring, excessive melt rate or rate fluctuations and melt 
interruptions. 
Over the years, the response to higher temperatures and 
stresses for superalloy applications has been to increase the alloy 
content, particularly Al, Ti, MO, W, Nb, etc. This quickly leads 
to a number of problems. As the alloy content is increased, the 
difficulty of making larger ingots is increased. Freckling tendency 
rises at any given ingot size with increasing alloy content, as 
previously discussed. In addition, with some alloy compositions, 
producing an ingot free of cracks after melting or which will 
survive heating for forging can be a problem. 
Hot workability also suffers dramatically as alloy content is 
increased for several reasons. Flow stress at hot working tem- 
peratures increases with alloy content, particularly at lower tem- 
peratures. These high flow stresses could exceed equipment 
capabilities. More important, however, is the reduction in the 
temperature range over which hot working can be carried out. 
This is due mainly to a significant reduction in hot ductility at 
low working temperatures for the more highly alloyed materials.

usually also decreases the solidus temperature, and thereby may 
cause the maximum allowable hot working temperature to be

Recent industry solutions 
In 1983 the triple melt (VIM + ESR + VAR) process was started 
for Alloy 718 to combine the benefits of VAR and ESR. This 
process produces the improved oxide cleanliness of ESR with the 
superior macrostructure/larger ingot capability of VAR. Over 
the ensuing years, the usage of triple melt has grown to the 
point that it is the predominant manufacturing route for critical 
rotating Alloy 7 18 components for turbine engines. 
Based on the many years of experience with the triple melt 
process, the advantages have been well documented for Alloy 
718. It has been reported that there is a 90% reduction in 
inclusion related, low cycle fatigue (LCF) specimen failures com- 
pared to VIM + VAR materiaL6 Figure 5 presents a comparison 
of average cleanliness results for VIM, VIM +ESR and 
VIM +ESR+ VAR Alloy 71X measured by the EB button test. 
These data show that the triple melt material was even cleaner 
than VIM + ESR and is consistent with reports of other authors 
who showed fewer and smaller oxide particles in triple melt Alloy 
718 than in double melt (VIM +VAR).7 Significant reductions in 
the frequency of ultrasonic defects found in Alloy 7 18 fine grain 
billet have also been documented for triple melt Alloy 7 I8 com- 
pared to double melt* material. Figure 6 shows that, over a 12- 
month period, the triple melt process resulted in an order of 
magnitude fewer ultrasonic indications than the double melt pro- 
cess for fine grain Alloy 718 billet. Because the ESR electrode is 
of a better quality than a VIM electrode, the subsequent VAR 
operation in triple melting also provides a superior product. This 
is because there are fewer instabilities during melting which, in 
turn, results in fewer of the macro defects described previously. 
Moyer et al. have also reported a lower incidence of freckles and 
certain types of white spots in triple melt Alloys 718 and 706.*

issues inherent in the more advanced alloys. Although the PM 
process has been extremely successful in meeting technical 
requirements, anticipated cost savings have not materialized.

are several PM superalloy processes in use today, but the most 
common involves atomization to produce a pre-alloyed powder 
followed by HIP or extrusion consolidation. The extremely fine 
microstructure, which is produced by rapid solidification of the 
fine powder, results in a superplastic billet product which can be 
forged isothermally to a near-net-shape part, typically a disk. 
There are several intermediate steps in this process, including 
screening the powder to the required mesh size and preparation 
and filling of a can which adds to the cost of the PM product. A 
key design property, minimum fatigue life, of superalloy disks is 
controlled by the presence of oxide inclusions which originate in 
the ingot remelt stock and the powder making process.’ The most 
common approach to limiting inclusions is to screen the powder 
to the size of the maximum allowable defect. This significantly 
reduces yields and raises costs. 
Advanced processes 
Clean metal spray forming (CMSF) is a new materials processing 
technology that is designed to overcome the inherent problem 
with ceramic based melting systems; namely, that of controlling 
the non-metallic inclusions while, at the same time, providing a 
product with equivalent mechanical properties to that from the 
PM process.’ The process is being jointly developed by General 
Electric, Teledyne Allvac, ALD Vacuum Technologies GmbH 
and Osprey Metals Ltd. It integrates electroslag remelting (ESR), 
a cold walled induction guide (CIG) and spray forming to directly 
produce a “clean” (i.e. free of large nonmetallic inclusions), solid 
preform. It is expected that this novel process will provide high 
performance powder metallurgy alloys at substantially lower cost 
relative to conventional PM processing. There is also potential 
for improvements in existing high performance, cast--wrought 
nickel-base alloys like U-720 that are difficult to manufacture 
using conventional methods.

unit which has recently begun spray forming trials. A 25001b 
vacuum induction melted (VIM) electrode is used as input to the 
CMSF process. It is melted by immersing one end of the electrode 
in a hot liquid slag comprising calcium fluoride, alumina and 
lime. As the electrode melts, droplets of molten metal pass 
through the slag. Non-metallic inclusions are removed from the 
molten metal at the slag-metal interface, thus reducing their size 
and frequency. During steady-state melting, a constant metal 
flow rate is maintained through the CIG nozzle by controlling

Transfer of molten metal from the ESR pool to the spray 
forming system is accomplished by the CIG unit. The CIG is a 
water-cooled copper nozzle with multiple coil induction heating 
capabilities to avoid freezing of the liquid metal. Because no 
ceramic lining is employed to contain the molten metal, the intro- 
duction of nonmetallic inclusions is eliminated. 
Finally, a stream of molten metal is delivered by the CIG to 
the spray forming chamber. Spray forming, pioneered by Osprey 
Metals Ltd in the U.K., is a process in which a molten metal 
stream is converted into a spray of liquid metal droplets by gas 
atomization.“’ The droplets, at various stages of solidification, 
are immediately reconsolidated onto a rotating collector and 
gradually form a solid preform. In general, spray forming results 
in a rapidly solidified material that is characterized by a fine, 
equiaxed grain structure and near-zero macrosegregation. It has 
also been demonstrated that. relative to conventional cast- 
wrought material, spray formed material exhibits significantly 
less microsegregation.” The CMSF process will produce cyl- 
indrical and ring-shaped preforms suitable for subsequent ther- 
momechanical processing. 
To be economically viable, the CMSF process must operate at 
relatively high production rates. Specifically, ESR melting must 
be performed at melt rates that are significantly higher than 
normally employed in conventional cast-wrought processing. To 
demonstrate the refining potential of high melt rate ESR, a feasi- 
bility study was performed using a 14in RD Alloy 718 VIM 
electrode, remelted into a 17 in RD ingot using a conventional 
ESR unit operating at near maximum power. Under these con- 
ditions, the steady-state melt rate approached that expected for 
the CMSF process and was approximately 3- 4 times the con- 
ventional melt rate for this alloy. Following melting, samples 
from the VIM, vim+high melt rate ESR and conventional 
VIM,‘ESR/VAR material were compared for oxide cleanliness 
using the electron beam (EB) button melting test. In this test, 
machined blanks were prepared and EB melted in a randomized 
fashion using practices developed by Teledyne Allvac for Alloy 
71X.” Following melting, the button rafts were semi-quan- 
titatively analyzed using a SEMiEDX system equipped with light 
element and backscatter detectors and image analysis software. 
Cleanliness was assessed by identifying and measuring the surface 
area of oxide inclusions greater than or equal to 0.5 mm’. For 
each individual button. the total oxide area was determined and 
divided by the button weight. This value. which is used as a 
measure of alloy cleanliness. is termed the specific oxide area 
(mm’kg ‘).

Conclusions 
The demands placed on superalloys over the years have escalated 
significantly, and it is expected that this trend will continue and 
even accelerate. In addition, there is potential competition for 
superalloy applications from other materials such as composites, 
intermetallics and ceramics. At the same time, strong inter- 
national and domestic competition will maintain pressure to keep 
costs down. A number of manufacturing processes for super- 
alloys have evolved over the years to handle the problems of 
manufacturing these difficult materials. In particular, triple melt- 
ing has been shown to improve cleanliness and reduce defect 
infrequency in Alloy 718. A new process, the clean metal spray 
forming process (CMSF) is being developed to combine the ben- 
efits of cleanliness produced by the ESR process with rapid sol- 
idification of the Osprey process. This rapid, integrated process 
has the potential to produce PM-type alloys at a substantial 
reduction in cost.
