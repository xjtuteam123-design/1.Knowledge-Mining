1. Introduction
Al-Cu alloys are widely used in aerospace, national defense, military 
industry, and automotive engines due to their high strength and heat 
resistance [1,2] . However, advancing technology has increased the de -
mand for better heat resistance in these alloys. Heat treatment can 
enhance the mechanical properties of Al-Cu alloys by forming numerous 
strengthening phases in the eutectic structure. Yet, at temperatures 
above 250 
◦
C, the main strengthening phase, Al
2
Cu, coarsens and 
transforms into a stable phase incoherent with α -Al, reducing the alloy ’ s 
high-temperature strength [3,4] . Therefore, the thermal stability of the 
precipitated phase is crucial for maintaining high-temperature 
resistance.
To improve high-temperature performance, producing dispersed, 
high-resistant phases through alloying is a common approach. Key 
alloying elements include Ag, Mg, Cu, and Mn [5 – 7] , while 
micro-alloying elements include Ti, Ge, Fe, Zr, RE, and Sc [8 – 13] . These 
elements participate in eutectic reactions within the alloy system, 
strengthening atomic bonds, slowing atomic diffusion and solid solution 
decomposition, and thus improving thermal stability. In addition, 
varying interface energy causes these elements to segregate at grain 
boundaries, forming high-resistant phases that pin the grain boundaries 
as temperature increases, hindering dislocation movement and 
enhancing high-temperature performance.
The literature shows that Ni and Cu can effectively improve the high- 
temperature performance of aluminum alloys [14] . Ni forms complex 
Al-Ni and Al-Cu-Ni intermetallic compounds in Al-Cu alloys. Zhang et al. 
[15] studied the effect of Ni on the high-temperature properties of

Under high-temperature conditions, changes in the engineering 
stress-strain curve are mainly influenced by precipitation strengthening 
and matrix softening mechanisms [15] . Initially, during tensile loading, 
the α -Al phase of the alloy undergoes rapid softening. At this stage, the 
precipitation strengthening mechanism predominates over matrix soft -
ening, resulting in a rapid increase in the engineering stress-strain curve. 
As stress exceeds the yield strength of the alloy, significant deformation 
occurs. The Ni-rich phase at the grain boundaries plays a beneficial role 
in hindering grain boundary migration under high-temperature condi -
tions, thereby improving the high-temperature strength of the alloy. 
Notably, the high-temperature strength of the 2# alloy surpasses that of 
the 1# alloy. According to Fig. 3 (c), the Ni-rich phase in the 2# alloy is 
finely dispersed along grain boundaries. In addition, the Ni-rich phase 
exists at the interface between phases and the matrix, exhibiting a 
smooth spherical morphology that relaxes stress concentrations during 
high-temperature deformation. Consequently, this configuration en -
hances the high-temperature strength of the alloy.

in 1# alloy to 41.97 nm. Adding 0.2 % Ni and 0.1 % Ni reduces PFZs in 
2# alloy to 17.65 nm, 72.3 % lower than the base alloy. Unwin P N T 
[35,36] reported that the formation of PFZs is mainly controlled by 
solute-poor and vacancy-poor effects. Due to the different atomic radii 
between Ni and Al, adding Ni increases system distortion energy, pro -
moting Ni diffusion to grain boundaries with uneven atomic arrange -
ments and low distortion energy to balance system distortions. This 
forms the Al-Cu-Ni phase at grain boundaries, reducing Cu concentra -
tions and causing solute depletion effects, thus narrowing PFZs in 1# 
alloy. Sc addition in 2# alloy further slows Cu and Ni diffusion rates in 
the matrix, reducing segregation to grain boundaries. In addition, since 
the Zr-vacancy binding energy (0.24 eV) is greater than the Sc-vacancy 
binding energy [37] . This promotes the formation of high-temperature 
resistant Al
3
(Zr, Sc) phases that further inhibit PFZ formation and 
growth. PFZs, less stable than intragranular structures, accumulate grain 
boundary dislocations and stress concentrations during 
high-temperature slip, leading to early yielding and plastic deformation. 
Effective PFZ inhibition alleviates precipitated phase nucleation at grain 
boundaries and prevents intergranular fractures caused by microcrack 
expansion along PFZs, thereby enhancing alloy high-temperature 
strength.

3.5. Fracture characterization
Fig. 12 shows the tensile fracture morphology of the base alloy, 1#

dimples, and numerous tearing edges. The base alloy predominantly 
shows dense and shallow dimples, indicating a ductile fracture. The 1# 
alloy shows many cleavage platforms and a few large, shallow dimples, 
indicating a brittle fracture. After Ni and Sc modification, the 2# alloy 
exhibits typical intergranular/quasi-cleavage surfaces and high-density 
small, deep dimples, indicating a brittle-ductile mixed fracture.

◦

base alloy. The 1# alloy primarily exhibits transgranular fracture at this

4. Conclusion
The effects of Ni and Sc compound modification on the microstruc -
ture and high-temperature mechanical properties of Al-Cu-Mn alloy 
were studied. The following conclusions were drawn:
(1) The grain size of the base alloy is about 49.6 μ m. After alloying 
with 0.3 % Ni, the grain size of 1# alloy decreases to 25.5 μ m. After 
alloying with 0.2 % Ni and 0.1 % Sc, the grain size of the 2# alloy de -
creases to 12.8 μ m. Ni alloying promotes numerous Ni-containing 
intermetallic compounds with a continuous network structure at grain 
boundaries. However, Sc addition disperses and refines these interme -
tallic compounds, enhancing their spherical morphology.
(2) At room temperature, adding 0.3 % Ni adversely affects the alloy 
strength, while adding 0.2 % Ni and 0.1 % Sc shows only a 18.7 MPa 
increase over the base alloy. However, under high-temperature condi -
tions, the Ni and Sc-modified alloy exhibits superior strength. At 300
◦
C 
and 350
◦
C, the 2# alloy achieves strengths of 164.7 MPa and 
116.2 MPa, respectively, making increases of 28.4 % and 34.2 % 
compared to the base alloy and 10.9 % and 21.3 % compared to the 1# 
alloy. This highlights the beneficial effect of Ni and Sc modification on 
alloy high-temperature performance.
(3) Both 1# and 2# alloys contain Ni in the form of Al
7
Cu
4
Ni and 
Al
3
CuNi phases. At 300
◦
C, Ni in the 2# alloy also segregates at the 
Al

20

neously, the segregation of Mn element on the surface of Ni-rich phase 
also hinders the diffusion of Cu and Ni atoms in Ni-rich phase at high 
temperature. This improves the high-temperature resistance of the Ni- 
rich phase and strengthens grain boundaries by effectively pinning 
them and preventing migration. Furthermore, Ni and Sc modification 
effectively inhibits the growth of PFZs under high temperatures.
(4) The Zr-rich shell effectively hinders Sc diffusion and improves the 
heat resistance of the Al
3
(Zr, Sc) phase under high temperatures. The 
coherent relationship between the Al
3
(Zr, Sc) phase and the matrix 
stabilizes the matrix under high-temperature conditions.
(5) The high-temperature strengthening mechanism of the alloy 
primarily involves precipitation strengthening and solid solution 
strengthening. Precipitation strengthening contributes more signifi -
cantly to the alloy strength than solid solution strengthening. In addi -
tion, Ni and Sc modification significantly enhances yield strength 
through stress transfer and eutectic strengthening mechanisms, albeit 
less than 0.3 % Ni alloying alone.
(6) Fracture surface analysis: at room temperature, the basic alloy 
exhibits predominantly ductile fracture, while the 1# alloy shows brittle 
fracture. With Ni and Sc modification, the alloy transitions to a ductile- 
brittle mixed fracture mode. From 300
◦

transgranular fracture. The 2# alloy displays dense and fine dimples at 
these temperatures, indicative of a ductile-brittle mixed fracture mode, 
albeit with a predominance of brittle fracture.
