ABSTRACT 
Molybdenum, processed by laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF), is susceptible to hot cracking because segregated 
oxygen impurities significantly weaken grain boundaries through the formation of MoO 2. The present study 
reports on the LPBF processing of the most important molybdenum alloy TZM, whose alloying element -
s—titanium, zirconium, and carbon—lead to particle and solid solution strengthening. Results of investigations 
into the resulting microstructure and mechanical properties when processing TZM by LPBF are presented. The 
alloying elements suppress the segregation of oxygen to the grain boundaries so that crack-free samples with a 
density of 99.7 ± 0.3% may be produced. The microstructure shows grains that are columnar due to epitaxial 
grain growth and a weak 〈111〉 fiber texture parallel to the building direction. Mo
2C and ZrO2 particles with a 
size of < 50 nm are precipitated in the grain interior. Oxygen is not only bound by ZrO 2. Research has shown 
that the ternary molybdenum‑titanium carbide, which can be found in LPBF – TZM – as is the case with pressed/ 
sintered TZM—can dissolve oxygen. While the bending strength is 591 ± 26 MPa for samples in which only 
pores with a diameter of < 50 μm could be detected on the fracture surface, the bending strength drops to 
267 ± 50 MPa for samples with defects of 400 μm. In both cases, the fracture mode is transgranular brittle. 
1. State of the art 
The successful use of materials in high-temperature applications – 
for example, in the aerospace and medical industries or for metal 
working tools – requires materials that have excellent high-temperature 
properties. Molybdenum has a high melting point, a low thermal ex -
pansion coefficient, a high thermal conductivity, and an excellent high 
temperature strength and creep resistance [
1]. The molybdenum alloy 
TZM has demonstrated its potential for use in applications where robust 
high-temperature resilience is required. TZM contains 0.5 wt% tita -
nium, 0.08 wt% zirconium, and typically 0.01 to 0.04 wt% carbon and 
is mainly produced by powder metallurgy (PM). For PM-TZM, most of 
the titanium is dissolved in the molybdenum matrix, thus increasing the 
alloy's strength through solid solution hardening [
2]. The remaining 
titanium forms ternary molybdenum‑titanium carbides [2–4] and tita -
nium carbide precipitates [
3–5]. Additionally, molybdenum and carbon 
form Mo2C precipitates [2]. These precipitates are located at grain and 
sub-grain boundaries [2]. In the PM process, the presence of approxi -
mately 200 μg/g oxygen is inevitable [
6,7]. Zirconium is the strongest 
oxide former in this alloy and therefore reacts with the oxygen forming 
ZrO2 particles, which are located at grain boundaries [2,3,8,9]. In a few 
publications, ZrC precipitated at grain boundaries is mentioned 
[4,5,10]. At elevated temperatures, the carbide and oxide particles re
-
tard grain and sub-grain migration, thereby significantly increasing the 
recrystallization temperature and thus the creep resistance at elevated 
temperatures compared to pure molybdenum [
2,4,10–12]. 
However, PM molybdenum and TZM are associated with several 
limitations regarding the production of near-net-shape parts, as the 
manufacturing process does not allow for geometric flexibility. PM 
products are usually semi-finished and must be machined to achieve 
their final form. By contrast, laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF) is a 
manufacturing method ideally suited to the production of highly com -
plex near-net-shape parts in small or even individualized batches from 
powder feedstock. Industry analyst SmarTech Publishing issued a 
market forecast [
13] that posited LPBF as a potentially disruptive 
processing method for molybdenum and molybdenum alloy products. 
In contrast to the typical PM production of molybdenum and TZM by 
solid state sintering, LPBF includes melting of the powder material,

despite the fact that molybdenum and TZM are prone to defects when 
processed in liquid state [14,15]. Several attempts to produce mo -
lybdenum with adequate mechanical properties via LPBF have been 
made [
16–20] but have hitherto been unsuccessful. The reason for this 
is twofold. First, molybdenum forms an anisotropic, coarse-grained 
microstructure [18–20] due to epitaxial grain growth along the tem -
perature gradient in the build direction. Second, defects occur due to 
oxygen impurities [
20]. Several hundred μg/g oxygen are typically 
introduced into the LPBF part via the powder and the process atmo -
sphere [
20]. Oxygen is soluble in the molybdenum melt but has almost 
no solubility in the solid. During solidification, the residual melt is 
enriched in oxygen with an increasing proportion of α-Mo phase, which 
leads to a liquid film between the α-Mo grains with an eutectic con
-
centration, which solidifies through eutectic transformation in α-Mo 
and MoO2. Process-inherent thermal stresses lead to the formation of 
hot cracks along the grain boundaries weakened by MoO 2 [20]. Ad-
ditionally, oxygen exacerbates the wetting action [
21] and gives rise to 
pore formation. The processing of TZM by LPBF has not yet been de -
scribed in any publication. However, several conclusions regarding the 
behavior of TZM in the LPBF process may be drawn from the literature 
on TZM welding, which reveals an improved melt metallurgical pro -
cessability compared to that of pure molybdenum [
11]. The presence of 
zirconium leads to the formation of ZrO 2 precipitates within grains 
[9,22]. Crack formation can therefore be inhibited in laser-welded TZM 
[23]. For TZM, the main drawback to laser welding is the formation of 
pores in the welding zone, which reduces the strength of the welded 
component by 23 to 50% [23]. 
This study aims to investigate for the first time the microstructure 
and mechanical properties of TZM processed via LPBF. 
2. Experimental procedure 
Spherical, pre-alloyed TZM powder with metallic purity > 99.9% 
produced by gas atomization was used in this study. The powder was 
fractioned by sieving with a mesh size of 63 μm. The particle size was 
measured using a MALVERN MASTERSIZER 3000 laser diffraction 
particle-size analyzer; the carbon content was measured by applying 
the combustion method using an LECO CS-230; the oxygen, hydrogen, 
and nitrogen contents were measured by carrier gas hot extraction 
using an LECO TC-500; and the titanium and zirconium contents were 
measured by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy 
(ICP-OES) using a THERMO SCIENTIFIC ICAP 6000 DUO. The powder's 
tap / bulk density was determined according to ASTM B527. 
The LPBF experiments were performed using a customized, la -
boratory-scale LPBF machine equipped with a 400 W, 1064 nm Nd:YAG 
fiber laser and substrate plate heating. The oxygen level in the building 
chamber was kept below 20 ppmv for all experiments. Argon was used 
as shielding gas. Samples with the dimensions 
15 mm × 15 mm × 10 mm for density measurements, 
35 mm × 6 mm × 6 mm for mechanical testing, and 
2.5 mm × 2.5 mm × 10 mm for transmission electron microscope 
(TEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) were fabricated on 
pure molybdenum substrate plates. Optimized process parameters for 
specimens with the highest density were determined in a pre-study. The 
linear energy density (LED) was calculated using LED = P/v, with laser 
power P and scanning speed v. The substrate plate was kept at a 
temperature of 800 °C during the building process. The scanning 
strategy details are presented in Table 1
. The building process was 
followed by a controlled cooldown at a rate of 100 K per hour. The 
overall build time, including cooldown, was about 14 h. All samples 
were separated from the substrate plate using wire-cut electrical dis -
charge machining (EDM). 
The density was measured using Archimedes' method. Specimens 
were cut in half along (referred to as “side view”) and perpendicular to 
(referred to as “top view”) the building direction. TEM images were 
captured using a FEI Tecnai F20 microscope, fitted with a Schottky field 
emission gun and operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. The 
EDX spectra were acquired with an EDAX Si(Li) detector and the ele
-
mental maps were detected with a GATAN imaging filter and an ultra- 
scan CCD camera. EBSD measurements were carried out using a ZEISS 
ULTRA 55 PLUS with a forward scatter detector (FSD). For this purpose, 
metallographic sections of the specimens in side view and top view 
were prepared by OPS polishing and etching with Murakami reagent. 
EBSD was carried out using an acceleration voltage of 20 kV, magni -
fication of 50, aperture of 120 μm, stepsize of 1 μm and a camera 
binning of 8 × 8. Texture information was gathered by creating and 
evaluating inverse pole figure (IPF) maps. The calculation method 
harmonic series expansion was used. The fracture surfaces and the 
powder morphology were investigated using the JEOL JSM–7610F 
scanning electron microscope (SEM). The ambient temperature three- 
point bending test was conducted according to DIN EN ISO 3327 with 
N = 7 samples. Prior to the test, the samples' surfaces were polished to 
avoid crack initiation as a result of surface roughness. The load was 
applied in the building direction. All samples' hardness was character
-
ized according to Vickers with a load of 98.0665 N using the EMCO 
TEST M1C 010. For bending strength and hardness, mean values and 
standard deviations were calculated.

Figs. 2 a) and b) show only a few spherical pores with a size of less than 
50 μm. A relative density value of 99.7% or higher for LPBF mo

lybdenum and by [19] for molybdenum alloyed with 0.45 wt% C. Pure 
molybdenum, however, shows a microstructure with cracks, while the 
microstructure observed here, similar to Mo-0.45 wt% C from the same 
research group, is completely crack-free. 
Comparing the chemical compositions of the powder in Table 2 and 
the LPBF sample in Table 3, the zirconium content remained un -
changed, while the titanium, carbon, and oxygen contents were re -
duced by 8%, 37%, and 57%, respectively. This may be due to the 
outgassing of carbon oxide, titanium, and titanium oxides. As proposed 
by [9,19], the carbothermic reduction MoO2 + 2 C → Mo + 2 CO and 
the subsequent outgassing of CO may account for the reduced carbon 
and oxygen contents when comparing the LPBF sample with the 
powder. Furthermore, the loss of titanium indicates the outgassing of 
titanium and titanium oxides, which—at 3287 °C (Ti), 3227 °C (TiO), 
and 2970 °C (TiO
2)—have lower boiling points than molybdenum 
(4639 °C). The boiling temperatures of titanium and its oxidic com -
pounds are significantly exceeded locally in the melt pool during LPBF 
molybdenum production [25]. Oxides and other volatile species that do 
not outgas during the short duration of the liquid phase may also be the 
reason for the spherical pores observed in Fig. 2 a) and b), just as in 
laser welding [23]. 
The microstructure in the top view clearly reflects the scanning 
strategy (see Figs. 2 b), d) and f)). The EBSD map in the side view (Fig. 2 
c)) shows elongated grains toward the build direction. The distribution 
function of aspect ratios for the same area in Fig. 3 a) confirms this 
impression. The mean aspect ratio is 0.4 ± 0.17. Grains elongated 
toward the build direction in LPBF are common for molybdenum [18] 
as well as alloys with narrow freezing ranges that do not develop suf
-
ficient constitutional supercooling [26]. 
A comparison of the area fraction grain size distribution in the side 
and top views in Fig. 3 b) reveals that grains in the side view are larger 
than those in the top view. The area-weighted average grain size is 
70 ± 45 μm for the side view and 51 ± 31 μm for the top view, while 
the number-weighted average grain size is 11 ± 14 μm and 
13 ± 17 μm, respectively. The area-weighted average grain size in 
electron-beam-welded TZM was reported to be within the same size 
range between 45 μm and 60 μm [9,22], although the melt pool in 
electron beam welding is significantly larger than that in LPBF. How -
ever, the number-weighted average grain size is significantly lower in 
LPBF due to the high number of small grains < 10 μm that are not 
observed in electron-beam-welded samples. 
In Fig. 2 e) and f) image quality maps with the grain boundary angle 
depiction as overlay in side view and top view are shown. Statistical 
evaluation of the area in Fig. 2 f) shows that 61% of the grain bound
-
aries are high angle grain boundaries, 18% low angle grain boundaries 
between 5° and 15° and 21 % between 2° and 5°. Compared to [20] 
where molybdenum was processed via LPBF without substrate plate 
heating, the proportion of low angle grain boundaries in LPBF TZM 
measured here is smaller due to lower thermal stresses.

3.2. Analysis of precipitates 
Precipitates were investigated using TEM (see Figs. 5 and 6). Pre -
cipitates within the grains typically have a size of < 50 nm and are 
spherical in shape. Using jump-ratio maps, elemental maps, and energy- 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spot measures, two types of 
spherical precipitates were found. Particles that are rich in zirconium 
and oxygen and that lack the carbon signal may be detected (see 
Fig. 5a)). Then particles can be found, which are rich in carbon and 
molybdenum (see Fig. 5 b)). The majority of spherical precipitates as 
well as elongated precipitates rich in molybdenum, carbon, titanium, 
and oxygen were found inside the grains (see Fig. 6 a)). No precipitates 
were found on grain boundaries. 
Precipitates rich in zirconium and oxygen are regularly observed in 
the grain interiors of melt metallurgically processed TZM (e.g., in 
welding) [8,9,22]. Having a lower ΔG than molybdenum‑oxygen and 
titanium‑oxygen compounds [8], zirconium, and oxygen react pre
-
ferentially, forming ZrO2. ZrO2 has a higher melting point than mo -
lybdenum, causing it to solidify first and to be distributed in the grain 
interior [8,9,22]. In welded TZM, MoO2 and TiO2 are typically found on 
grain boundaries [8,9]. No MoO2 or TiO2 at all can be found in LPBF- 
TZM. The atomic ratio of zirconium to oxygen content in the sample is 
1:1.08. This means that zirconium is present super-stoichiometrically in 
relation to oxygen. A zirconium content of 885 ± 10 μg/g in the 
sample would be sufficient to bind 168 ± 16 μg/g of oxygen as ZrO2. 
No unbound oxygen would remain, and the formation of MoO 2 and 
TiO2, which both have lower melting points than that of molybdenum 
and which would therefore segregate to high-angle grain boundaries 
[8], would be prevented. Consequently, grain boundaries would be free 
of precipitates. 
Spherical precipitates rich in molybdenum and carbon were also 
found in the grain interior (see Fig. 5 b)). Similar precipitates, identified 
as Mo2C, were found in PM- [2] and electron-beam-welded TZM [9,22] 
as well as in molybdenum manufactured by LPBF [20] and mo -
lybdenum alloyed with 0.45 wt% C manufactured by LPBF [19]. Mo2C 
is more likely than titanium or zirconium carbides to be formed due to 
the greater molybdenum presence [9]. Besides Mo 2C and ZrO 2 pre-
cipitates, a ternary non-spherical oxygen enriched carbide was found 
(see Fig. 6 a)). Ternary molybdenum‑titanium carbides (Mo,Ti)
xCy [2,3] 
and with unspecified stoichiometry [4] were previously reported in PM- 
TZM, but this is the first time that evidence for oxygen solubility in a 
ternary molybdenum‑titanium carbide has been found. The ability to 
dissolve oxygen in ternary molybdenum‑titanium carbide is a me
-
chanism that assists in suppressing the segregation of oxygen and thus 
probably purifies the grain boundaries not only in LPBF-TZM but also in 
conventional PM-TZM. 
No titanium-containing precipitates were found except in complex 
carbides. Similar to PM-TZM [2], titanium in LPBF-TZM is almost ex
-
clusively dissolved in the molybdenum solid, its strength increasing 
through solid solution hardening. The microstructure of LPBF-TZM 
shows low angle grain boundaries and numerous dislocations (see Fig. 6 
b)), comparable to LPBF‑molybdenum [20].

3.3. Mechanical properties and fracture surface 
The bending strengths are detailed in Fig. 7. All samples showed 
brittle fracture without any ductility. Samples 1 and 4 showed a sig -
nificantly lower bending strength than the other samples. Without 
samples 1 and 4, a mean bending strength of 591 ± 26 MPa was 
calculated. A hardness of 264 ± 19 HV10 was measured. Fracture 
surfaces of samples 4 and 5 were investigated using SEM. The trans -
granular brittle fracture with river patterns is illustrated in Fig. 7. The 
fracture surface of sample 5—representative of samples with a bending 
strength of approx. 550 to 630 MPa—shows spherical pores that 
were < 50 μm in diameter. Sample 4—representative of samples with 
a bending strength of < 250 MPa—shows powder-filled lack-of-fusion 
porosity with a size of approx. 400 μm. 
The river patterns shown in Fig. 8 indicate a transgranular brittle 
cleavage fracture. In contrast to electron beam-welded TZM [9,22] and 
LPBF-Mo [19], the fracture surfaces do not show any intergranular 
portions. This indicates that the samples do not show weakened grain 
boundaries as they have been effectively purified from segregated 
oxygen. The brittle fracture without detectable ductility and pores 
visible in the fracture surface indicate that the strength of the samples 
might be limited due to residual porosity. For samples 1 and 4, lack-of- 
fusion porosity with a size of approx. 400 μm was detected on the 
fracture surface (Figs. 8 c) and d)). Consequently, samples 1 and 4, with 
approx. 250 MPa, have a bending strength similar to pore- and crack- 
containing pure molybdenum LPBF samples (267 ± 50 MPa) pro
-
cessed under comparable conditions [19]. For the samples with a 
bending strength of approx. 550 to 630 MPa, spherical pores with a size 
of < 50 μm can be detected in the fracture surface. Compared to the 
bending strength of PM-TZM sheets in the recrystallized state 
(1150 MPa, value from Plansee internal database), these LPBF-TZM 
samples have a ~ 50% lower bending strength. Three factors are re
-
sponsible for this difference: residual porosity, a high dislocation den -
sity, and high internal stresses. An estimate of the fracture mechanical 
properties shows that the LPBF samples have defects that are in the 
same size range as the critical defect size. The critical defect size ac for

4. Conclusions 
The present study constitutes the first detailed analysis of processing 
TZM via LPBF. Dense and crack-free samples were produced. The fol -
lowing conclusions are drawn: 
•TZM was produced via LPBF with a density of 99.7% of the theo -
retical density and a crack-free microstructure. 
• Epitaxial grain growth leads to elongated grains toward the BD with 
an aspect ratio of 0.4 ± 0.17. A weak < 111 > fiber texture par-
allel to the BD is formed. 
• Mo2C precipitates are found in the grain interior. 
•Oxygen impurities are bound as ZrO 2 and in ternary molybde -
num‑titanium carbide precipitates, which are both located in the 
grain interior. Segregations of oxygen at grain boundaries could not 
be detected and grain boundaries are effectively purified. 
• The fracture mode is transgranular. 
• The mechanical properties are determined by the residual porosity 
since their size corresponds to the critical defect size of TZM.
