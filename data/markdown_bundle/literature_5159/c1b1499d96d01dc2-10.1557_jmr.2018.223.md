Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) have recently attracted much attention, primarily due to
their mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. However, the equilibrium phase-stability of
these alloy systems is not well established. The present investigation focuses on the phase
stability of Al
0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr RHEA at temperatures ranging from 600 to 1200 °C. The
detailed phase characterization involves coupling of scanning electron microscopy, transmission
electron microscopy, and atom probe tomography. The stable phases present at these temperatures
are (i) 1200 °C — body-centered cubic (BCC) matrix with nano-B2 precipitates; (ii) 1000 °C and
800 °C — a BCC matrix phase with Al –Zr rich hexagonal closed packed intermetallic precipitates
and, (iii) 600 °C — a BCC 1 B2 microstructure, comprising a continuous BCC matrix with
discrete B2 precipitates. These results highlight the substantial changes in phase stability as
a function of temperature in RHEAs, and high-entropy alloys in general, and also the importance
of accounting for these changes especially while designing alloys for high temperature
applications.

II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
A. Sample preparation
The Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr alloy was prepared by arc
melting under vacuum back ﬁlled with argon, using high
purity (99.9%) elements. Details of the alloy preparation
are explained elsewhere.
15 The nominal alloy composi-
tion from SEM-EDS is Al-11.3 1.0.7, Nb-22.3 6 0.4,
Ta-13.1 6 1.2, Ti-27.9 6 0.5, V-4.5 6 0.8, and
Zr-20.9 6 0.9 (all in atomic percent). After solidi ﬁcation
in a water-cooled copper hearth of the arc melter, the
alloy was wrapped in a Ta foil and hot isostatically
pressed (HIP ’d) at 1200 °C for 2 h at a pressure of
210 MPa under high-purity argon. The alloy was then
homogenized by annealing at 1200 °C for 24 h in a tube
furnace with continuously ﬂowing argon, and then slowly
cooled at a controlled rate of 10 °C/min. This condition
will be referred as the “as-processed” alloy. The AP alloy
was sealed in a quartz tube ﬁlled with high-purity argon
and annealed at 1200 °C for 2 h followed by water
quenching. This condition will be referred to as the AP 1
solution annealed or APSA. The APSA alloy was cut in
several pieces, which were sealed in argon- ﬁlled quartz
tubes and annealed at 600 °C for 120 h, 800 °C for 20 h,
or 1000 °C for 20 h. All solutionizing/annealing treat-
ments that were performed on the AP alloy were followed
by water quenching to retain the phases formed at the
annealing temperatures.

A. AP condition
The microstructure of the alloy in the AP condition has
been previously reported to have a mixture of two BCC
phases with similar lattice parameters forming a very ﬁne
interwoven basket-weave like nanophase.
15 SEM backscat-
ter imaging reveals a coarse-grained microstructure
[Fig. 1(a)] appearing to be a single-phase. TEM examina-
tion identiﬁes a two-phase nanostructure inside the grains.
Selected area electron diffraction patterns (SADP) from the
[001]
BCC and [011] BCC zone axes [Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)
respectively] clearly show the presence of superlattice
reﬂections at {001} positions, i ndicating the presence of
an ordered BCC-based phase in this alloy. This ordered
BCC-based phase is the B2 phase (Strukturbericht nota-
tion). A dark-ﬁeld TEM (DFTEM) image [Fig. 1(d)] created
using a {001} type superlattice re ﬂection in the [001]
BCC

matrix phase. These phases exhibit a cube-on-cube orien-

[100]BCC//[100]B2. The BCC precipitate thickness along
the h100i direction is ;20 nm and that of the B2 channels

Fig. 1(e), clearly depicts the arrangement of the B2/BCC

parameters of the BCC and B2 phases as calculated using
X-ray diffraction are 3.30 Å and 3.32 Å, respectively.

E. Temperature-dependent phase stability and
proposed transformation pathways
The current alloy in the AP condition exhibited a B2/
BCC microstructure with B2 being the continuous matrix
phase and BCC formed the cuboidal discrete phase. As
the alloy was annealed at various temperatures and water
quenched, the phase stability at different temperatures
was investigated. In the APSA condition, the alloy
exhibited a SS BCC matrix and ordered B2 nanopreci-
pitates randomly distributed inside the matrix. During
annealing at 1000 and 800 °C, an additional Al –Zr-rich
intermetallic phase having a hexagonal crystal structure
nucleates and grows heterogeneously at grain boundaries
as well as at certain intragranular locations. The exact
nature of the intragranular nucleation sites for these
intermetallic precipitates has not yet been determined.
Since the number density of intragranular intermetallic
precipitates is substantially lower than the extremely high
number density observed for the B2 nanoprecipitates in
the APSA condition, it appears that the B2 nanoprecipi-
tates dissolved on annealing at 800 and 1000 °C, and the
new phase stability regime at these elevated temperatures
leads to the precipitation of the hexagonal Al –Zr-rich
intermetallic phase. However, during isothermal anneal-
ing at 600 °C, the hexagonal intermetallic phase does not
form, but the B2 1 BCC microstructure is stable and
develops further from the initial APSA condition.
The present investigation of the phase stability at
different temperatures suggests that the decomposition
of the high temperature BCC phase into BCC 1 B2
phases, observed in the initial AP condition, occurred
below 800 °C during slow cooling at 10 °C/min from
1200 °C. The “superalloy-like” microstructure consisting
of cuboidal pockets of BCC, within a continuous matrix
of the ordered B2 phase, could potentially be the result of
phase separation via spinodal decomposition accompa-
nied by concomitant ordering, as described previously.
35
However, it should be noted that the details of the
transformation pathway require more detailed investiga-
tion that are presently underway. The results presented in

IV. SUMMARY
The microstructural evolution and phase stability in an
Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr RHEA has been investigated in
detail as a function of temperature, viz. 600, 800, 1000,
and 1200 °C.
With progressively decreasing temperature, the se-
quence of phase evolution and stability in this alloy can
be described as folllows:
(1) In the AP condition [AP, annealed at 1200 °C for
24 h and furnace cooled (10 °C/min)] — a BCC 1 B2
microstructure, comprising a continuous B2 matrix phase
with discrete cuboidal BCC precipitates. Additionally,
a small fraction of omega phase is also observed in this
microstructure
(2) At 1200 °C — a BCC SS phase matrix with B2
nanoprecipitates;
(3) At 1000 °C — a BCC matrix phase with sparsely
distributed second phase precipitates, at grain boundaries
and also within grains, of a faceted Al –Zr rich HCP
intermetallic phase of size scale of ;2 lm;
(4) At 800 °C — a BCC matrix with regions exhibiting
discontinuous precipitation to form the Al –Zr rich HCP
phase and a Al and Zr depleted, BCC 9 phase, and ﬁnally;
(5) At 600 °C — a BCC 1 B2 microstructure, com-
prising a continuous BCC matrix phase with discrete B2
precipitates. An ordered omega phase is detected within
the B2 phase, which is enriched in Ti and Zr and lean in
all the other elements.
The microstructure at 600 °C possibly emerged via
a spinodal decomposition within the BCC phase, coupled
with concurrent B2 ordering of one of the resultant BCC
phases. However, in the early stages, the B2 ordering
appears to occur within the lower phase fraction contin-
uous BCC phase. Subsequently, longer term isothermal
annealing at 600 °C, results in an inversion of the
microstructure, presumably driven by the increase in
the B2 phase fraction, ﬁnally resulting in discrete B2
precipitates within a continuous BCC matrix. These
results highlight the substantial changes in phase stability
as a function of temperature in RHEAs, and HEAs in
general, and the importance of accounting for these
changes especially while designing alloys for high
temperature applications.
