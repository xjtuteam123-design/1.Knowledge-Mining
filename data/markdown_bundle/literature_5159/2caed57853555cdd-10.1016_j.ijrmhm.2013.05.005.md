Recrystallized, commercially pure Mo, binary Mo –27 at.% Re, and as-extruded and variously annealed
Mo–0.3 at.% Si alloys were tested in uniaxial tension as a function of temperature in vacuum. The Mo –Re
solid solution is signi ﬁcantly stronger than Mo and retains the strength to at least 800 °C. The alloy exhibits
serrated ﬂow in the 500 °C –800 °C range and this is accompanied by a continuous decrease in ductility with
increasing temperature in this temperature range. The recrystallized Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy is softer than pure
Mo at room temperature and has high ductility (~ 60% tensile elongation). In the worked condition, it is sig-
niﬁcantly stronger ( σ
y > 600 MPa) but still is ductile at room temperature (~ 10% tensile elongation). The
observation of ductility at room temperature in this Mo –0.3 at.% Si solid solution alloy has important impli-
cations for multiphase ternary Mo –Si–B alloys of technological interest.

1. Introduction
Molybdenum and Mo-based alloys including Mo –Si–B multiphase
alloys are of signiﬁcant recent research interest as possible candidates
for high temperature applications beyond the capabilities of Ni-based
superalloys[1–5]. In these multiphase alloys, the Mo-rich solid solution
phase is expected to provide the much-needed low-temperature dam-
age tolerance. An important recognition however has been that Si is a
potent solid solution strengthener of Mo and is capable of providing
enhanced strength at low and high temperatures and even enhanced
creep resistance at elevated temperatures [6–8].H o w e v e r ,w h e nt h e
Mo matrix in the multiphase ternary Mo –Si–B alloy contains a high
level of Si due to supersaturation (the solubility of Si in Mo below
1200 °C is thought to be b 1 at.%), as is often the case, due to sluggish
diffusion [9], the solid solution is brittle at room temperature [10].O n
the other hand, commercially pure recrystallized and unrecrystallized
Mo has been shown to be ductile at room temperature [11]. Therefore,
it is likely if the Si in solid solution is maintained at a low level, it
might be possible to obtain the bene ﬁt of some ductility at room tem-
perature and enhanced strength at elevated temperatures. This aspect
has been explored using a binary Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy and the results
are presented in this paper.
In studying the solid solution effects in Mo, Mo –Re alloys have
received a fair amount of attention in the past [12–14]. Re exhibits
signiﬁcant solubility in Mo and binary alloys with compositions as
high as 31 at.% Re have been evaluated. It has also been shown that
Re addition to Mo can improve the room temperature ductility of
Mo. Interestingly, there was a study [13] that claimed a ductility max-
imum at around 6 –8 at.% Re but a more recent study [14] found this
not to be the case; instead, the tensile ductility at room temperature
remained more or less constant between 35 and 45% for recrystallized
Mo–Re alloys with concentrations ranging from 5 to 27 at.% Re. In this
study, a Mo–27 at.% Re alloy that is commercially available was included
for evaluation; the eventual goal of course is to determine if the negative
effect of Si addition to Mo on the ductility could be offset by the im-
provement in ductility of Mo through Re addition.

3.2. Yield strength
The variation in yield strength in uniaxial tension with test temper-
ature for the annealed CP Mo, the recrystallized Mo –27 at.% Re and
the extruded as well as the annealed Mo–0.3 at.% Si solid solution alloys
are shown in Fig. 5a( a n dTable 2). The yield strength of the annealed
CP Mo in Fig. 5a at room temperature (~0.1 T
m) is ~340 MPa but then
drops sharply to 190 MPa at 200 °C (0.2 T m); thereafter, it decreases
gradually as temperature increases to 500 °C. This behavior is charac-
teristic of bcc metals and has in the past been attributed to the
non-planar screw dislocation cores that render them immobile at low
temperature; as temperature increases, thermally activation eases the
motion of the screw components and cross-slip activity increases as
well [15,16].
The Mo–27 at.% Re alloy is signi ﬁcantly solid solution hardened at
room temperature, registering more than double the yield strength of
CP Mo (similar grain size), but then shows a decline in strength sim-
ilar to that of pure Mo until ~ 500 °C (Fig. 5a) beyond which a plateau in
strength is noted until 800 °C. It is worth noting that this plateau
strength is similar in magnitude to the strength of pure Mo at room tem-
perature (~ 340 MPa). The yield strength for the Mo–27 at.% Re alloy at
room temperature is in good agreement with the value reported for a
similar grain size material in[14].
The as-extruded Mo–0.3 at.% Si alloy displays a high yield strength
at room temperature, the yield strength being more than a time-and-
a-half that of CP Mo. Furthermore, this high strength decreases fairly
gradually with temperature up to 900 °C. The contribution to the incre-
ment in strength is expected from both, the heavily dislocated structure
that was observed in the extruded rod (Fig. 2c) and from solid solution
hardening by Si and the interstitial impurities (the measured O level
in the Mo –Si alloy was 0.013 wt.% relative to the CP Mo where is was
b0.001 wt.%). The yield stress measured for the Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy
in this study in the as-extruded condition is around 550 MPa ( Fig. 3a,
Table 2 ) and is comparable to that reported previously for the
as-received Mo sheet that was in the unrecrystallized condition that
showed a yield strength of around 625 MPa [11]. In contrast, as noted
above, the recrystallized Mo was much softer and had a yield stress of
around 350 MPa, con ﬁrming that a high initial dislocation density can
substantially account for most of the difference in strength.

3.3. Plastic ﬂow
The stress –strain response at room temperature for CP Mo in the
as-received (unrecrystallized) and in the annealed condition was pre-
viously discussed in [11]. Relevant to the current work, annealed CP
Mo [11] hardens over the ﬁrst 10% of plastic strain, reaches the ulti-
mate strength and thereafter deforms locally by necking until failure.
Additionally, the annealed CP Mo exhibits serrated ﬂow at room and
elevated temperatures up to 500 °C [11]. Serrated ﬂow at the micro-
scopic scale is often associated with solute segregation to dislocations
and the ability of pinned dislocations to tear away from these atmo-
spheres [26–28]. There are, however, other non-diffusional theories
for serrated ﬂow reported in the literature including twinning, twin
boundary-slip band intersection, planar slip and shearing of strength-
ening particles by dislocations [29–32].
Elevated temperature serrated ﬂow behavior in arc-cast Mo has
been previously attributed to interstitials, particularly C, and to a lesser
extent, N and O [33]. In contrast, discontinuous yielding has also been
reported in arc-cast Mo from−55 °C to 400 °C and has been attributed
to interstitial impurities as well [34].M o r er e c e n t l y ,C h e n ge ta l .[35]
state that Mo exhibits multiple strain aging temperature regimes at
low strain rates in the 27 –827 °C regime and claim that the response
is associated with different mobility of different solute species. In the
CP Mo utilized in this study, the C level is in the range of 0.008 –
0.01 wt.% and O level is of the order of 0.003 wt.%, and these species
could be responsible for the observed serrated ﬂow response as twin-
ning was not observed and neither were second-phase particles.
The monotonic tensile stress –strain curves for the Mo –27 at.% Re
at room temperature, 300 °C and 500 °C are shown in Fig. 6 a and at
600 °C and 800 °C in Fig. 6 b. Minor serrations are observed at all
temperatures up to and including 600 °C but at 800 °C (and 700 °C
as well—curve not shown), the serration amplitudes are larger; in ad-
dition, a pronounced plateau stress is observed at room temperature
immediately beyond yielding and before the onset of work hardening;
this is less pronounced at 300 °C and 500 °C. The strength plateau is
even less prominent at 600 °C and 800 °C. Serrated ﬂow at 700 °C and
800 °C is accompanied by a measurable loss in tensile elongation at
these temperatures and an increase in work hardening in the early stages
of plastic deformation. Serratedﬂow was previously reported in a Mo–Re
alloy of this composition at 800 °C as well in [36] and it is worth noting
that even though that study was focu sed on the effect of irradiation,
the serrated ﬂow response was observed in un-irradiated annealed
material. The origin of serrated ﬂow in this alloy in this temperature
range is not known. Optical metallography of the deformed specimens

hardening rates and loss in ductility often accompany serrated ﬂow as
noted in Fig. 6b and have been previous reported in many other metallic
systems [26,40].

3.4. Tensile elongation
CP Mo in the recrystallized condition exhibited signi ﬁcant tensile
elongation at room temperature (> 50% under the conditions it was
tested) as seen in Fig. 5 b. With increase in temperature, there is
some loss in ductility but beyond that up to 500 °C, ductility remains
more or less constant at around 45%. In contrast, the solid solution alloys
show reduced ductility at room temperature, with the Mo –27 at.% Re
alloy being more ductile than the as-extruded Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy.
However, it is worth noting that the as-extruded Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy
is not brittle at room temperature but exhibits tensile elongation of
~10% even though it is in a high-strength condition. The effect of
the various annealing treatments on the tensile elongation of the
Mo–0.3 at.% Si alloy at room temperature and at 300 °C is summarized
in Table 3. Following annealing, the tensile elongation for this Mo –Si
alloy at room temperature, 300 °C and 700 °C is consistently in excess
of ﬁfty percent.
From a technological perspective, this suggests that if the Si level
in the matrix solid solution phase of ternary multiphase Mo-rich,
Mo–Si–B alloys can be kept low (say, to near-equilibrium values
thought to be around 0.5 at.%) at low temperatures, and/or perhaps
even further lowered by suitable quaternary alloying, then there is
an opportunity to obtain plasticity at low temperatures in the solid
solution phase that could improve the fracture toughness of the
multiphase Mo –Si–B alloys that are being examined as possible
candidates for high temperature applications.

cells; in particular, long straight dislocations traversed the cells and
ran into opposite cell walls and it was hypothesized that these might
be screw dislocation debris, characteristic of the deformation of bcc
metals at low temperatures. After deformation at 400 °C, cell structures
were observed as well, but in many locations, subgrain boundaries,
indicative of recovery, were noted. In the intra-cellular region, the dislo-
cation density was comparatively lower (relative to room temperature)
and consistent with recovery; furthermore the long straight dislocation
debris was replaced by shorter segments.
Representative micrographs for the Mo –27 at.% Re alloy are
shown in Fig. 8 a–e for tests conducted at room temperature, 500 °C
and 800 °C. At room temperature (after ~ 20% plastic strain at the
location where the TEM specimen was extracted), dislocations appear
uniformly distributed and the beginnings of the formation of disloca-
tion cells are evident ( Fig. 8 a). Unlike in pure Mo, interestingly, long
straight dislocation debris was less evident in this alloy deformed at
room temperature, although still present in some locations. There
was no evidence for deformation twinning in this specimen. In a spec-
imen that had seen 25% tensile elongation at 500 °C, in some
locations the microstructure is reminiscent of the room temperature
structure ( Fig. 8 b), whereas in others, the dislocations lie in well-
deﬁned highly elongated cell walls roughly a micrometer in width,
with the dislocation density within the cells being visibly lower
(Fig. 8c,d). At 800 °C, the specimen failed after about 15% tensile elon-
gation (much less than the specimens at room temperature and at
500 °C) and in this specimen, the elongated cell structure observed
at 500 °C is again observed but not as well formed; this may be related
to the lower level of plastic strain in this specimen. In addition, disloca-
tions appear to form transverse walls (Fig. 8e) giving the appearance of
two orthogonal arrays of dislocation cell walls. Such an arrangement of
dislocations arising from pinning events could account for the reduced
ductility at 800 °C and is reminiscent of dislocation structures in the
dynamic strain aging regime in many metals and alloys, although not
as acute as those observed in stainless steels by Hong and Lee [41].

Mo deformed to much higher strain levels (10% in the Mo –0.3Si
versus ~ 50% for the pure Mo) at room temperature. In essence, a dis-

Electron microscopy examination of the 1600 °C/4 h annealed
Mo–0.3 at.% Si specimen deformed at room temperature (~60% plastic
strain) shows a high dislocation density with dislocations being fairly
homogeneously distributed although cell walls are evident in the struc-
ture (Fig. 10a). After deformation at 700 °C that produced a similar level
of tensile elongation (Table 1), the microstructure consists of an orthog-
onal array of cell walls (Fig. 10b), with a visibly lower dislocation densi-
ty within the cells. This structure is quite different from that observed in
this alloy when deformed at the same temperature in the as-extruded
condition. There was some evidence for recovery in the material in
that case (Fig. 9c,d). This supports the notion that the initial high dislo-
cation density in the as-extruded material provides the stored energy
for recovery to progress whereas this is not available in the early stages
of deformation in the 1600 °C/4 h annealed specimen.
4. Conclusions
The effects of initial microstructure and test temperature on the
tensile response of a commercial Mo –27 at.% Re and an experimental
Mo–0.3 at.% Si alloy have been studied and compared to that for CP Mo.
Major ﬁndings include:
1. Annealed Mo, Mo –27Re and Mo –0.3Si in the fully recrystallized
condition are ductile at room temperature, at least in the condi-
tions they were examined in this study.
2. The addition of 27 at.% Re to Mo signi ﬁcantly solid solution
strengthens the alloy in comparison to pure Mo with a similar
grain size in the fully recrystallized condition at room temperature
and at temperatures up to 800 °C.
3. The Mo –Re alloy exhibits serrated ﬂow from 500 °C to 800 °C, the
serration amplitudes increasing with temperature within this
range; serrated ﬂow is accompanied by a steady loss in tensile
elongation. Deformation twinning was not observed in the condi-
tions examined.
4. The addition of 0.3 at.% Si to Mo only marginally affects strength and
ductility compared to CP Mo when both materials are in the fully
recrystallized condition; in fact, the Mo –0.3 at.% Si showed a lower
strength at room temperature even though it had a higher interstitial
oxygen level than did the CP Mo and comparable carbon levels.
5. At room temperature, in the unrecrystallized condition (as-extruded),
the Mo–0.3 at.% Si alloy yields at strength levels comparable to that of
unrecrystallized pure Mo[11] a n dr e c r y s t a l l i z e dM o–27 at.% Re alloy;
furthermore, the Mo–Si solid solution retains a high strength level up
to about 1200 °C in the as-extruded condition.
6. The unrecrystallized and recrystallized Mo –0.3 at.% Si alloy exhibit
serrated ﬂow that is ampli ﬁed in the vicinity of 700 °C and is ac-
companied by some loss in ductility.
7. An important ﬁnding in this work is that Si in solid solution in Mo
at a level of 0.3 at.% (which is thought to be in the proximity of
room temperature solubility of Si in Mo) does not render the
solid solution brittle in uniaxial tension (at least a nominal strain
rate of 10
−4 s−1). This has important implications for Mo –Si–B al-
loys that are of current technological interest.
