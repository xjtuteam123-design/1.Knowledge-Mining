ABSTRACT
The present review is focused on the additive manufacturing (AM) of refractory materials (metal and alloys only 
in present review), emphasizing its growing significance as a transformative approach for fabricating high- 
temperature structural components. Refractory metals and alloys, like tungsten, molybdenum, tantalum, and 
niobium, and their alloys have very high melting points, high-temperature strength, and corrosion resistance. 
However, they are intrinsically hard and brittle, which makes traditional manufacturing methods like forging, 
sintering, and machining very difficult. AM fabrication techniques such as laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), laser 
direct energy deposition (LDED), and electron beam (EB) based processes have demonstrated the potential to 
overcome the temperature and mechanical limitations associated with conventional methods. This review fo -
cuses on the importance of AM techniques in establishing process-structure-property relation in refractory ma -
terials and emerging refractory complex concentrated alloys. The importance of integrated computational 
materials engineering (ICME) techniques such as CALPHAD, molecular dynamics, and density functional theory 
has been explored, as well as their potential role in enhancing understanding of process-structure-property 
correlations and guiding process optimization. A detailed discussion on defects formation such as crack, 
porosity and residual stress during AM and strategies such as thermal management, compositional tuning, and 
tailored scanning paths have been shown to mitigate these defects. Overall, the present review underscores the 
significance of additive manufacturing as a pivotal approach for enhancing the design and performance of re -
fractory materials for next-generation high-temperature and extreme-environment technology.

Additive Manufacturing (AM) presents a promising pathway for 
expanding the use of refractory materials [27 – 31] , as it offers the po -
tential to bypass conventional processing steps, enable the fabrication of 
complex geometries in near-net shapes, and significantly reduce raw 
material consumption. Initial investigations into laser powder fusion 
have shown that, with the aid of computer-aided design (CAD) and 
precise process control systems, it is possible to fabricate metals into 
highly complex geometries. These early successes have demonstrated 
the potential of the technology for processing refractory materials 
traditionally considered difficult to shape due to their high melting 
points and poor machinability [32] . As a result of these initial efforts, 
subsequent technological advancements have increasingly focused on 
the development of high-purity, spherical powders optimized for addi -
tive manufacturing. This shift reflects a critical recognition of the in -
fluence of powder morphology, purity, and flowability on process 
stability and final part quality [33] . Additive manufacturing primarily 
depends on the physical and thermophysical properties of the feedstock 
material. In addition, power and the scanning strategy of the focused 
heat source plays a critical role in determining how effectively the 
material is melted and processed. Furthermore, during additive 
manufacturing (AM)-based fabrication, continuous and localized melt 
pools are created by the heat source. Thus, the solidification kinetics 
associated with AM techniques strongly influenced by the thermo -
physical properties of the refractory material systems. These thermo -
physical parameters are melting temperature, surface tension, viscosity, 
thermal conductivity, specific heat, thermal diffusivity, density, and the 
physical characteristics of the feedstock materials [34 – 37] . Table 1
presents the thermophysical properties of the key refractory material

1.2. Current and potential applications
The fabrication-related aspects remain a major consideration along 
with the structural and mechanical properties of refractory materials for 
any given application. Of all the refractories, Nb and Ta are the easiest to 
process, as are their alloys. They have high solubilities of interstitial 
atoms such as oxygen, carbon, and nitrogen which are embrittling en -
tities and even though they do not pose any adverse effects during 
fabrication at elevated temperatures, they contribute to a significant 
reduction in the ductility at room temperature thereby limiting their 
applications at ambient temperature [60] . The refractory materials are 
readily degraded in oxidizing environments even at moderately low 
temperatures, a property that restricts their applicability either in 
low-temperature or nonoxidizing high-temperature environments [61] . 
The refractory materials are extensively used in electrical connections, 
heating elements, lamp filaments, and electron tube grids. Furthermore, 
although, refractory materials are also widely used in the chemical 
process, electronics, nuclear, and aerospace sectors [62] , their applica -
tions continue to span practically every industry, largely due to the 
experience acquired in aerospace programs.
Commercially pure Nb is ductile and easy to shape at room tem -
perature using standard forming techniques. Nb alloys are widely 
employed in aircraft applications due to their lightness and good 
elevated-temperature strength [62] . Fasteners for aircraft structures are

2. Process/fabrication of refractory materials
Most of the conventional methodologies used to fabricate refractory 
materials are confined to fusion - based (arc melting, induction melting, 
electron beam melting), powder-based (powder metallurgy, mechanical 
alloying) and some form of modification of these processes in integrated 
form (electro discharge compaction, plasma activated sintering, powder 
rolling, sinter forging) ( Fig. 1 ). Most of these methodologies are confined 
to produce bulk volumes of less intricate geometries (sphere, cube, 
cylinder, etc.) due to high melting temperatures combined with higher 
thermal conductivity and higher brittle to ductile transition tempera -
tures of refractory materials. On the contrary, in the advent of devel -
opment of non-conventional methodologies involving highly confined 
high energy source such as laser beam and electron beam, there exists a 
tremendous potential to fabricate complex geometries in one step 
outside of the thermokinetic regimes of these materials associated with 
the conventional methodologies to avoid generation of defects (porosity, 
cracking, compositional segregation). In light of these following sections 
provide general and brief description of both conventional and non- 
conventional methodologies used to fabricate refractory materials. 
Whereas rest of the manuscript focuses on the detailed description 
related various process/fabrication and material aspects during non- 
conventional methodologies.

Processing and fabrication of refractory materials and their de -
rivatives have traditionally relied on several conventional techniques, 
each offering unique capabilities and facing distinct limitations. Arc 
melting is most commonly used technique for synthesizing refractory

Arc melting provides superior alloy homogeneity through repeated 
melting cycles; however, it frequently encounters segregation, coarser 
grain, and evaporative losses of low melting elements. Moreover, it 
possesses limited scalability and is unsuitable for handling elements that 
are extremely reactive or volatile at elevated temperatures [71 – 75] .
Induction melting, another conventional technique, which involves 
heating metal charges in crucibles using electromagnetic fields, offering 
more control over melting temperatures and ambient conditions. 
Though it offers a faster heating rates and clean melts, they can also 
cause contamination which is a serious issue for refractory materials 
where high purity is crucial. Besides induction melting is generally 
constrained by the crucible material limitation and may not be suitable 
for ultra-high temperature metals due to potential degradation of the 
contaminant vessel [76] .
Electron beam (EB) melting, which involves directing a focused 
electron beam of high-energy electrons over the material under vacuum, 
provides exceptionally high energy densities ideal for refractory mate -
rials. EB melting facilitates precise control of temperature, fine grains, 
and exceptional purity due to the high vacuum environment, which 
prevents oxidation and contamination. However, due to complex 
infrastructure requirements and high operating expenses, this technol -
ogy is capital and energy-intensive and often limited to laboratory or 
pilot-scale [77,78] .
Powder metallurgy (PM) is a solid-state synthesis technique that 
provides an alternative way to circumvent the high melting tempera -
tures of refractory materials involving compacting and sintering or both 
simultaneously of metal powders, particularly under vacuum or inert 
gas atmospheres. PM entails near-net-shape fabrication, microstructural 
control, fine grain ’ s structure and compositional flexibility. Despite of 
these mentioned advantages, there are still some challenges in achieving 
full densification, particularly for high-density elements like W and Mo. 
In addition, residual porosity, grain coarsening during sintering, and 
impurity entrapment are essential challenges [79 – 81] .
Mechanical alloying (MA), a high-energy ball milling technique, is 
also often employed for synthesizing refractory materials, notably for 
metastable or nanostructured phases. MA facilitate alloying of immis -
cible elements, uniform dispersion of reinforcements, and synthesis of 
non-equilibrium phases. Nonetheless, there are a few serious issues 
including lengthy processing duration, contamination from milling 
media, and subsequently requirement of further consolidation and sin -
tering such as hot pressing and spark plasma sintering [82 – 84] .
Conventional synthesis methods for refractory materials, including 
casting, forging, and powder metallurgy, encounter substantial chal -
lenges due to their exceptionally high melting points, poor workability, 
and strong affinity for oxygen and nitrogen at elevated temperatures. 
These constraints result in high processing costs and limited component 
geometries. Furthermore, the need for significant post-processing re -
stricts the design flexibility and scalability of these materials. In light of 
these challenges, AM offers a promising pathway to overcome such 
barriers by enabling precise control over process, compositional tunning 
and complex geometries, as discussed in the following section. More -
over, additive manufacturing techniques enable near-net-shape fabri -
cation, reduce material waste, and facilitate rapid prototyping of 
complex designs. The layer-wise processing method used in additive 
manufacturing enables site-specific tailoring of properties, which is 
challenging being achieved by conventional techniques. Fig. 1 repre -
sents a flow chart outlining the different process/fabrication used to 
synthesize refractory materials.
2.2. Additive manufacturing
Additive manufacturing (AM) [82,85 – 87] , frequently referred to 3D 
printing, holds tremendous potential as a transformative technique for 
synthesis and manufacturing of refractory materials. AM technique such 
as Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) [88] , Electron Beam Powder Bed 
Fusion (EB-PBF) [89] , Laser Directed Energy Deposition (Laser-DED) 
[90] , and Electron Beam Directed Energy Deposition (EB-DED) [91]
enable layer-by-layer fabrication directly from raw materials using 
digital models, resulting in novel design flexibility, near-net- shape 
manufacturing, and eliminating post fabrication technique such as cut -
ting, milling, grinding thus enhances the material and process efficiency. 
These abilities are particularly useful for refractory materials which are 
highly challenging due to their high melting temperature, poor 
machinability, and susceptibility to cracking and oxidation. Further -
more, AM enables unique alloy design strategies by leveraging in-situ 
alloying using elemental blend and gradient compositions and micro -
structures, enables the development of new alloy system and function -
ally graded materials. These advantages are difficult to achieve using 
conventional methods, which frequently lack of in-situ compositional 
control. Additionally, AM decrease the material waste, which is signif -
icant consideration due to the high price and rarity of refractory mate -
rials, by eliminating the extensive machining, which is difficult due to 
the inherent hardness and brittleness associated with the refractory 
materials. Despite such potential, there are very few works referenced in 
the open literature on AM based processing/manufacturing of materials. 
As most of AM techniques involve high energy density heat sources, the 
thermokinectics and thermomechanics inherent to these techniques are 
often far from equilibrium and additionally they become more complex 
due to thermal cycling associated with in-layer multi-track and 
multi-layer processing. In light of this, the current paper attempts to 
provide an in-depth review of current state of the art and future op -
portunities for AM based processing and manufacturing of refractory 
materials. Table 1 compares the performance of additively manufac -
tured refractory materials with their conventionally processed coun -
terparts, highlighting mechanical properties. Table 3 compares laser- 
and electron-beam-based AM parameters.
3. Integrated computational materials engineering (ICME) 
approach in additive synthesis and fabrication of refractory 
materials
The AM process involves vast range of processing window parameter 
such as power, beam spot size powder feeding rate, scanning strategy 
and speed, hatch spacing, layer height. The interaction among these 
parameters affects the quality and properties of the final part. Conse -
quently, the intricate interaction of process parameters continues to 
provide significant challenges due to the variation in part properties. 
Substantial efforts have been undertaken to create predictive compu -
tational models and simulations by comprehending the fundamental 
physics of the process, encompassing heat generation and phase tran -
sitions. However, the most transformative advancements are expected to 
emerge through the implementation of Integrated Computational Ma -
terials Engineering (ICME) — a comprehensive framework that in -
terconnects multi-scale models and simulations through the 
process – structure – property – performance (PSPP) paradigm, enabling 
predictive design and optimization of material behavior and component 
performance.
During additive manufacturing-based fabrication employing pre- 
alloyed or elemental powders, the composition frequently deviates 
from the specified or desired composition, resulting in unsatisfactory 
AM component attributes. Part shape/design, scanning technique, build 
chamber environment, and feedstock qualities are all aspects that have a 
direct impact on the reliability of AM components [129] . There is an 
inevitable variance in quality due to the inherent uncertainty in these 
factors [130] . These issues can be potentially resolved/mitigated by 
using ICME method, which uses the process – structure – property re -
lationships to determine the permissible deviation from the spec -
ified/desired composition [131] . While some progress has been made in 
computational high throughput screening for material design, ongoing 
efforts are being made to integrate novel machine learning and artificial

3.1.3. Density functional theory (DFT)
DFT uses quantum mechanical computations to model the collective 
behavior of atoms in order to decipher the properties of materials. Such 
calculations are critical in comprehending molecular characteristics, 
which influence the meso- and macroscale material properties. Atom -
istic modeling is based on precise and efficient techniques for numeri -
cally solving the Schr ¨odinger equation for complicated systems with 
many atoms [158,159] . While Density Functional Theory (DFT) cannot 
be directly incorporated into the additive manufacturing (AM) process, 
it functions as a potent auxiliary tool for predicting such as thermody -
namic stability, phase formation, and physical properties that ultimately 
govern the printability and performance of materials.
DFT can calculate physical properties such as thermal and electrical 
conductivity, surface energies, and defect concentration with greater 
accuracy than other computational tools. In powder bed fusion (PBF) 
AM, for example, the surface energies of distinct material phases influ -
ence the melt penetration phenomenon and have a significant impact on 
the quality of the outcome component [160] . Surface energies are 
difficult to quantify experimentally, however, DFT simulations allow for 
the calculation of these energies in various types of metallic surfaces 
[161,162] . Curry et al [163] . shows that how physical properties such as 
optical and dielectric properties of material affect the non-equilibrium 
melting and solidification during AM process in association with DFT 
calculation during laser-matter interaction. DFT-calculated optical 
properties of the alloy indicate variations that directly influence the 
radiative heating and cooling rates during the AM process. Thus, DFT

3.2. Process/fabrication design approach
3.2.1. Parametric approach/analytical model
Analytical modeling is typically used to develop plausible solutions 
in closed form for various physical variables of a system. When 
considering material chemistry/sciences, these models are referred to as 
constitutive models, which are defined as a set of equations character -
izing a physical entity that meets all its features, such as geometry, 
thermodynamics, and mechanical aspects. The main advantage of any 
analytical model lies in its simplistic approach based on theory that 
doesn ’ t need or require the least computational cost and time or any 
iteration-based simulation. Several parameters associated with the AM 
process, the analytical model is a key approach to understanding the 
process optimization (scanning speed and strategy, beam diameter and 
energy, powder feed rate, etc.) and resulting properties (grain 
morphology, residual stress, pores, and defect density) of the AM com -
ponents in a very rapid and efficient manner.

ometry intersecting ratio was used to account for the powder attenua -
tion impact for the laser beam. To calculate the substrate temperature 
field, the energy from the heated powder and the laser beam were 
included as the heat source. Their model's relative simplicity allows it to 
yield quick findings about scanning speed, powder feed rate, and 
catchment efficiency. A mathematical model of LPF-AM was created by 
Fathi et al [168] . to predict the temperature field, dilution, and melt 
pool depth with given clad height and clad width data. In order to

3.2.2. FEM approach
The macro models using the FE approach and computational fluid 
dynamics [171] are largely concerned with the evolution of the built 
part's thermo-mechanical field, which is responsible for temperature, 
residual stress, and distortion [172 – 174] of AM parts. Materials in FEM 
simulation are viewed as a continuum with flexibility in the computa -
tional domain dimension. These computational domains, known as FE, 
have been discretized (known as meshing) into a finite number of sub -
domains of random geometry. In order to produce a more accurate 
prediction, FE models typically utilize a tiny mesh size less than the heat 
source radius [175] especially, to capture the peak temperature distri -
bution. Nonetheless, as build size grows, the mesh number increases 
considerably, resulting in high calculation costs, particularly in PBF 
where thousands of scanning tracks in a layer and multiple layers are 
involved. To overcome this issue researchers adopted high efficiency 
simulation [176 – 182] based on modified inherent strain method, 
adaptive mesh refinement, and coarsening and finite cell methods. Liang 
et al [178] . used LBPF to forecast the accumulative deformations in 
complicated canonical components and a double cantilever beam by 
extracting the mean inherent strain vector from a tiny representative 
volume and applying it to a part-scale layer-by-layer model. Addition -
ally, they conducted experiments to validate the accuracy and efficiency 
of the model. A finite element (FE) based thermo-kinetic and 
thermo-mechanical computational model was developed by Sharma et 
al [183] . to simulate the process from the melt pool scale down to the 
component level to print the W by LPBF process. They compared the 
melt pool geometry obtained by FEM with the experimentally deter -
mined melt pool which is in close agreement with each other [ Fig. 7 (a), 
(b), and (c)]. FE models showed significant heat accumulation with 
shorter scanning lengths. Increased heat accumulation led to larger melt 
pools (width and depth) [ Fig. 7 (d), (e) and (f)]and more favorable 
rheological properties, leading to denser printed tungsten parts. Chiu -
menti et al [179] . calculated the temperature field of LPBF blocks using

3.2.3. Artificial intelligence (AI)
AI and AM are both revolutionary new technologies. Although AI has 
permeated many facets of human lives, its potential in AM remains 
unrealized. The enormous volume of data and digital nature of AM 
technology present amazing possibilities for machine learning (ML) and, 
by extension, AI. AI can be divided into two categories: symbolic intel -
ligence, also known as conventional intelligence, which solves issues 
using knowledge and reasoning, and computational intelligence, which 
solves problems and makes decisions using sample data. According to 
the IEEE Computational Intelligence Society, artificial neural networks, 
fuzzy systems, and evolutionary programming are all included in 
computational AI [186] . AI has progressed in numerous fields, such as 
computer graphics, computer hacking, robotics, speech recognition, 
driverless and autonomous cars, and spam combat. One industry that 
will be greatly impacted by the advancements in AI is manufacturing. 
The industry with the highest investment in AI is manufacturing, fol -
lowed by banking, financial, and insurance services.
AM is a digital technology that stands to gain a great deal from the 
latest developments in ML, AI, and data science [186,187] The AM 
method facilitates data collection and process organization because 
every stage is completed digitally. On the other hand, AM is a highly 
automated process during the design, process preparation, and printing 
stage. This produces numerous system data that are difficult to visualize 
and interpret for human. In addition, the AM process proceeds rapidly, 
making monitoring difficult. To better comprehend this process, AI and 
ML may be used for system modelling, picture recognition, and data 
visualization. Similar to other applications, AI for AM processes can only 
be used if ML programs have been created, and the data is accessible for 
analysis. Process learning and in-situ monitoring in AM are relatively 
new concepts. In terms of research, ML has been used to several aspects 
of process modification, optimization, and tailoring. Defect density, 
local flaws, internal stresses created during the process, dimensional 
accuracy and design, microstructural variabilities, and other factors are 
of interest. Because there are so many factors that might affect any one 
of these metrics, controlling any one of them is difficult. The output is 
affected not only by the adjustable process parameters but also by 
additional variables, including geometry, material type, design type, 
part shape, and ambient conditions. Only a limited number of variables 
are under our control; the rest are usually noise or extra factors whose 
effects become apparent only with time. Due to the intricacy of AM 
processes, over 50 parameters have been identified to affect the results, 
particularly in the powder bed fusing process [187] . One of the major 
factors in AM parts is defects. To find defects in a continuous process, 
image processing methods have been applied [187] . Gobert et al. [188]
used layer-wise photos captured during the process coupled with linear 
support vector analysis employing binary classifiers to allocate specific 
properties to pixels and compared the results to a computed tomography 
(CT) scan of the final build image. However, given the part's expansion 
and any residual stresses that might decrease after the part is removed 
from the process, this could be erroneous. In contrast, Caggiano et al. 
employed a bi-stream deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) in 
place of a binary classifier to extract the embedded patterns most 
pertinent to the process circumstances. Photographing the layer after 
SLM and after the powder has been distributed is part of this process. 
They suggest being able to take into account the influence of surface 
abnormalities in the powder spread of the following phase by using 
bi-stream DCNN. Their approach has a 99.4 % accuracy rate in identi -
fying faulty conditions. Khanzadehan et al. use infrared imaging tech -
nology in tandem with ML algorithms such as decision tree (DT), 
K-nearest neighbour (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), linear 
discriminant analysis (LDA), and quadratic discriminant analysis [189]
to assess melt pool dimensions and, as a result, porosity in powder bed 
technology parts. In their investigation, they compared numerous al -
gorithms and discovered that the KNN technique has the highest accu -
racy in identifying melt pools as defect or non-defect. There is no 
denying the benefit of direct defect quantification, which lowers pro -
cessing and interpretation mistakes. Algorithms vary in the benefits they 
provide. While some offer faster and use less memory, others offer more 
precision. Factors include the amount of training data, how interpret -
able the models are (flexible vs. restrictive), how long it takes to train 
and predict, how linear the data is, how big the features are, how much 
memory is needed, and more. Choosing the appropriate algorithm and 
methodology is crucial for creating a successful ML algorithm [190] . For 
instance, certain neural network types, SVM, linear regression, logistic 
regression, and other algorithms are capable of producing predictions 
quickly. Nevertheless, the prediction process of ensemble models and 
KNN algorithms frequently takes longer.

4.4. Miscellaneous additive manufacturing routes for synthesis of

Apart from laser-based additive manufacturing people also attempt 
arc-based additive manufacturing processes. Marinelli et al. [249]
showed that the Wire + Arc Additive Manufacturing method can effi -

structures on a huge scale. Extensive research has been conducted to 
analyze and enhance the wire feeding direction to achieve flawless 
tungsten deposits. The wire feeding orientation significantly influences 
the occurrence of spatter and structural defects, such as porosity and 
lack of fusion. Particularly, a defect-free tungsten structure was pro -
duced by employing the front wire feeding ( Fig. 19 g-19 l) method while 
side wire feeding leads to splatter causing the formation of voids and 
lack of fusion ( Fig. 19 a 19 f). In the side wire feeding the high velocity 
of lateral streams of plasma gas causes the ejection of liquid tungsten at 
the end of the wire. While in front feeding the wire was quickly lowered 
into the liquid pool without disrupting the lateral streams of 
high-velocity plasma gases. The wire feeding direction additionally 
impacted the deposit's microstructure. The structure deposited using 
front wire feeding exhibits a symmetrical and consistent grain distri -
bution, contrasting to the structure created using side wire feeding.

Cold gas-dynamic spray, commonly known as cold spray, is other 
non-melt metal AM technology for processing refractory materials. It 
uses a supersonic stream of compressed gas to accelerate powdered

generated. The appearance of UFGs due to dynamic recrystallization 
during the particle/particle impact phenomenon, rather than static 
recrystallization, which would have followed from steady heating and 
cooling during processing and the subsequent formation of strain-free 
grains. The cold spray method was used to consolidate Ta powder for 
use as explosively coated liners in 25 mm cannon barrels. Ta tubes were 
produced using cold spray, with a final density of 99.6 % TD and a 
hardness of 21 HRC [257] . There is great potential for using the cold 
spray approach to materials that are sensitive to oxygen. There's a good 
risk of breaking the oxide layer on the powder when the powder feed -
stock hits the substrate at a very high velocity, which lowers the amount 
of oxygen in the finished coating. This is helpful for refractory material 
systems because oxygen causes brittle oxide phases, which cause prob -

Another cutting-edge method for creating high-temperature carbide 
composites is selective laser curing (SLC). Using this method, powders 
are simultaneously exposed to radiation, which causes a curing reaction 
to occur in the pre-ceramic polymer binder phase. Since the curing 
process and powder irradiation occur simultaneously in SLC, the pre- 
ceramic binder phase does not need to be removed. Consequently, the 
simultaneous operations lessen the shrinkage brought on by pyrolysis. 
The fully dense component is subsequently formed by infiltrating the 
porous structure with liquid metal [260] . For effective sintering in SLC, 
lower temperatures than in SLS are needed. Because SLS lacks a binder 
phase, greater processing temperatures are needed to cause the ceramic 
particles to sinter. In contrast, SLC requires a lower temperature, which 
leads to reduced damage caused by thermal gradients and higher 
dimensional precision in the parts [261,262] .

6. Physical defects and potential approaches for mitigation of 
defects during AM fabrication of refractory materials
Inherent nature of the brittleness and high melting point of re -

based fabrication routes make them prone to generation of defects such 
as cracking, pores, lack of fusion, and residual stress which in turn make 
them challenging to fabricate. It is worthwhile to discuss about defects 
formation and their possible remedies. The formation of cracking in 
refractory materials not only associated with their synthesis techniques 
but most importantly in their solidification kinetics.

and around the melt pool and its solidification at extremely rapid rates.

6.2. Possible approaches for crack and pores suppression
A ratio of laser power to scanning speed is known as the linear energy 
density (LED). By increasing LED, Vrancken et al. [285] were able to 
reduce the distance per crack (a ratio of the overall track length to the 
total number of cracks) in LPBF fabricated pure W. The potential for 
decreasing number of cracks by attaining higher melting temperatures 
and extended solidification times through use of higher laser power was 
established. It was also indicated that the microcracking can be further 
reduced through the strengthening mechanisms such as solid solution 
strengthening and dispersion strengthening [297] .

temperature difference above 800 K were likely to induce cracks in pure

8. Major findings and perspective based on the present 
literature
1. Despite the inherent challenges associated with refractory materials, 
such as their exceptionally high melting points, high thermal con -
ductivity, low ductility, and pronounced brittleness — significant 
progress has been made in advancing their additive manufacturing 
(AM) over the past decade. These materials, traditionally considered 
difficult to process using conventional methods, pose even greater 
complexity in AM due to issues like residual stress accumulation, 
oxidation sensitivity at elevated temperatures, and limited process 
parameter windows. Nevertheless, through the development of 
specialized feedstock materials, optimized laser processing strate -
gies, and advanced thermal management techniques, researchers 
have begun to overcome these obstacles. These efforts have not only 
demonstrated the feasibility of AM for this class of materials but have 
also opened up new opportunities for fabricating components with 
intricate geometries and tailored microstructures, which were pre -
viously unachievable through traditional manufacturing routes.

2. Cracking remains one of the most persistent and challenging issues in 
the additive manufacturing (AM) of refractory materials, particularly 
tungsten and its alloys. These materials are inherently prone to 
cracking due to their low ductility, high residual stresses induced by 
rapid thermal cycles, and the presence of brittle intergranular pha -
ses. Although substantial efforts have been directed at mitigating this 
issue, it continues to be a major barrier to widespread adoption. 
However, cracking is not the only microstructural defect of concern. 
Another critical yet comparatively underexplored challenge lies in 
the generation and control of porosity, particularly under keyhole 
mode processing conditions where localized vaporization and 
instability of the melt pool can lead to pore entrapment. These pores, 
much like cracks, can significantly degrade the mechanical integrity 
of the final component by acting as stress concentrators and initia -

been made in addressing cracking, a holistic approach that simul -

additive manufacturing. Anisotropy in build direction and perpen -

exhibit varying mechanical properties, complicating quality control 
and reliability of the AM component.
3. Much research have been carried out using finite element (FE)-based 
thermal modeling to clarify the links between process, structure, and 
properties in additively produced (AM) components. These models 
play a crucial role in process optimization. and defect mitigation by 
forecasting thermal histories, residual stresses, and microstructural 
evolution. Reliable, temperature-dependent thermophysical charac -
teristics, including as density, viscosity, specific heat, elastic 
modulus, and thermal conductivity, are crucial for the precision and 
predictive power of FE-based models. Though commonly used, 
CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) approaches have 
inherent limitations and are frequently employed to estimate these 
values. Particularly, CALPHAD's application to new materials like 
refractory materials is limited since it depends on thermodynamic 
databases that might not have enough information for complicated 
or innovative alloy systems, such as multicomponent or high-entropy 
alloys (HEAs). 
This gap underscores a critical deficiency in the current modeling 
landscape: the lack of integrated multiscale modeling frameworks 
Combining continuum-scale models with atomistic simulations, 
including density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics 
(MD), provides a potent pathway to overcome this limitation. For 
both new and current alloy systems, including those that have not yet 
been experimentally defined, atomistic methods can yield precise, 
temperature-dependent thermophysical data. Such information 
could greatly improve the fidelity and robustness of FE-based 
modeling to develop a robust process parameter optimization for 
the development of AM refractory component.

9. Potential industrial application of AM of refractory materials
Additively manufactured components are generally employed for 
non-safety-critical parts and are more common in military rather than 
commercial aircraft due to variability inherent in the manufacturing 
process. For example, new Eurofighter Typhoon models incorporate AM 
components in their air-conditioning systems, and the Airbus A380 uses 
them for passenger cabin brackets [320] . In the construction sector, 
additive manufacturing is likely to be adopted primarily for elements 
that do not impact overall structural stability, unless the structural 
reliability of the part can be accurately predicted. In aerospace, GE 
Aviation has produced a metal AM fuel nozzle which consolidates 18 
individual parts into a single component, reduces mass by 25 %, and 
increases the part ’ s service life fivefold [321] . Additive manufacturing 
has been applied to fabricate titanium alloy hydraulic components, 
which were developed and evaluated following Red Bull Racing ’ s in -
ternal standards. The components met all performance criteria, and the 
use of AM enabled the creation of lighter parts with more streamlined 
designs, improving fluid flow efficiency [322] . However, the validation 
process for additively manufactured components is generally more 
rigorous than that for traditionally manufactured parts, to ensure their 
reliability and structural integrity.
Despite being a novel process, the application of additive 
manufacturing (AM) for the synthesis of refractory materials remains 
nascent and is not extensively documented in publicly available litera -

10. Summary
Despite the high melting temperatures, high thermal conductivities, 
and brittle nature of refractory materials, which make them infamously 
challenging to print, significant advancements in recent years have been 
made in the additive manufacturing of this special class of materials. The 
present manuscript reviewed the AM publication of refractory materials 
discussing various methodologies for 3-D printing in terms of material, 
process parameters, microstructure, and properties. The articles 
addressed here illustrate a few key points, which are stated below: 
1. Additive manufacturing (AM) has considerable advantages over 
traditional techniques for fabricating refractory materials, allowing 
near-net-shape components without major post-processing. Howev -
er, cracking, particularly along grain boundaries, remains a signifi -
cant concern, which is frequently worsened by interstitial oxygen 
and nitrogen in feedstock. Furthermore, anisotropic grain 
morphology along and across the build direction results in structural 
and mechanical anisotropy.
2. Additive manufacturing (AM) allows for a wide range of processing 
window parameters, including energy input, scanning speed and 
strategy, power, and deposition rate for printability. However, these 
vast processing parameters usually fail for novel materials or com -
plex geometries, making parameter tuning extremely difficult. In 
such instances, computational tools are very helpful for navigating 
the vast and interdependent process parameter. Integrated Compu -
tational Materials Engineering (ICME), which include FEM, MD and 
DFT offers a systematic framework for narrowing processing condi -
tions by combining thermophysical features with laser-matter 
interaction thermokinetic. This technique helps to understand 
defect evolution caused by AM's fast cooling rate and steep thermal 
gradients.
3. Additive manufacturing (AM) of refractory complex concentrated 
alloys (RCCAs) attained significant attention as next-generation 
materials with exceptional mechanical, thermal, and chemical 
properties. The inherent benefits of AM, such as geometric flexibility, 
near-net-shape fabrication, and localized microstructural control, 
complement well with the vast compositional space of RCCAs, 
enabling rapid alloy design for extreme environments. However, the 
availability of high-quality feedstock remains a major challenge 
owing to high cost, long production times, and dependence on so -
phisticated techniques such as atomization. In this context, in-situ 
alloying from elemental powders via AM presents a promising 
route to explore RCCAs while reducing feedstock costs and, when 
coupled with an ICME framework, enables efficient narrowing of the 
process parameter window.
4. The present review is an integrative approach, analyzing several AM 
processes across a wide range of refractory materials and comparing 
processing approaches to microstructural development and me -
chanical performance. This broader perspective improves compre -
hension of process-structure-property relations while revealing 
crucial gaps and opportunities for material optimization. Further -
more, this paper emphasizes the importance of the Integrated 
Computational Materials Engineering (ICME) framework in accel -
erating refractory of AM via multiscale modeling, predictive capa -
bilities, and efficient material and process design, thus, allows for 
effective narrowing of the process parameter window.
