ABSTRACT
To elucidate the mechanistic link between dislocation reactions and texture evolution in refractory BCC alloys, 
this study investigates a Mo-Re alloy under axial compression at 650 ℃ . Multi-scale characterization reveals that 
kink band formation acts as a critical accommodation mechanism governing texture evolution. Crucially, the 
observation of kinking signifies strong plastic anisotropy, driven by the enhanced activation of single {112} slip 
systems (promoted by Re addition and temperature). While the coordinated activity of {110} and {112} families 
generates sessile networks that contribute to hardening, preferential pile-ups of edge dislocations on {112} 
planes create the lattice instability necessary for kinking. This instability is accommodated by cooperative block 
rotations (kinking). In this process, active dislocations are consumed to generate kink boundaries. Consequently, 
kinking drives the rapid transformation of single-slip-dominant < 101 > //AD grains toward the stable 
< 111 > //AD texture, whereas multi-slip < 001 > //AD grains reorient only partially. Concurrently, continuous 
deformation bands bridge crystallographic gaps between rigid blocks and the matrix to maintain compatibility. 
The synergistic interplay between abrupt kink bands and continuous deformation bands effectively accommo -
dates strain during moderate deformation. However, at high strains, the prevalence of the hard-oriented 
< 111 > //AD texture imposes geometric hardening, effectively suppressing further kinking and slip, which 
leads to the loss of strain accommodation. These findings clarify how kink-mediated plasticity governs texture 
evolution, thereby enhancing the ductility and damage tolerance of refractory alloys.
1. Introduction
Refractory body ‑ centered cubic (BCC) alloys, such as molybdenum 
(Mo) and its alloys, are critical for high ‑ temperature structural appli -
cations, including aerospace propulsion and nuclear reactors, owing to 
their exceptional thermal and mechanical properties [1,2] . However, 
the intrinsic brittleness of pure Mo at lower temperatures and its limited 
ductility at elevated temperatures present significant challenges for 
forming [3 – 7] . The addition of rhenium (Re) — known as the “ rhenium 
effect ”— substantially improves ductility, lowers the ductile ‑ to ‑ brittle 
transition temperature, and raises the recrystallization temperature, 
thereby enhancing both formability and thermal stability [8 – 10] .
While the deformation of Mo-Re alloys at very high temperatures ( >
900 ℃ ) is well documented [11,12] , behavior under intermediate con -
ditions (~650 ℃ ) remains inadequately characterized. This temperature 
range is industrially critical for forming precision components, where an 
optimal strength-ductility balance is required. Although texture transi -
tions in Mo plates have been studied during rolling [11] , the specific 
microstructural mechanisms driving such evolution in Mo-Re alloys at 
650 ℃ remain unclear.
To understand these transitions, one must consider basic BCC slip 
behavior. Although plasticity involves 1/2 < 111 > dislocations gliding 
on {110}, {112}, and {123} planes [13,14] , this study focuses on the 
coordinated activity of the {110} and {112} families. This focus is 
warranted by the temperature sensitivity of BCC slip: as temperature 
increases relative to room temperature, the critical resolved shear stress 
(CRSS) for {112} slip decreases markedly [15 – 17] . Consequently, at the 
experimental temperature of 650 ℃ , the {112} system is expected to 
operate competitively alongside the primary {110} slip. Furthermore, 
Re addition is known to promote a transition from {110} to {112} slip

link between elementary dislocation reactions (specifically between

Beyond conventional dislocation glide, grains in polycrystalline 
metals rarely deform homogeneously. Instead, they accommodate strain 
incompatibilities by subdividing into distinct misoriented domains [19] . 
A primary manifestation of this inhomogeneity is the deformation band 
[20] , which typically accommodate strain through continuous lattice 
curvature. However, under conditions where slip systems are severely 
constrained, deformation localizes into a more abrupt form known as 
kink band, originally identified by Orowan [21] . Kink bands in BCC 
alloys are distinct from general deformation bands; they are nucleated 
by concentrated {112} edge dislocation walls and bounded by sharp 
opposing “ tilt walls. ” Fundamentally, this structure arises from effective 
plastic anisotropy, where coordinated dislocation slip on a dominant 
system drives rigid lattice rotation, thereby creating the sharp kink 
boundaries. This localized reorientation accommodates strain in -
compatibility and alters the local Schmid factor distribution, often 
triggering secondary slip systems (crossed kinking) and accelerating 
texture evolution [22 – 25] . While recent studies in HCP and other BCC 
systems [26 – 31] have highlighted kink bands as a ubiquitous defor -
mation mechanism, reports in refractory alloys (e.g., W, Mo) are scarce. 
In particular, the nucleation criteria, crystallography, and interaction 
with dislocation networks in Mo-Re systems remain unexplored.

{112} edge dislocation pile-ups serve as preferential nucleation sites for

2. Material and methods
The experimental material was a Mo-Re (Re, 6 – 9 wt%) alloy rod 
processed by rotary swaging and subsequent recrystallization annealing 
at 1350 ℃ for 60 min in vacuum, yielding a fully recrystallized 
microstructure. Cylindrical specimens ( Φ 10 mm × 12 mm) were 
machined perpendicular to the rod axis. Uniaxial compression tests were 
performed using a Gleeble 3500 simulator: specimens were heated to 
650 ℃ at 10 ℃ /s, held for 60 s to ensure thermal equilibrium, com -
pressed to prescribed engineering strains (10 %, 20 %, 40 %, 60 %) at a 
constant strain rate of 0.01 s
 1
, and immediately water-quenched to 
preserve the deformed microstructure.
Longitudinal sections were cut parallel to the compression axis via 
wire electrical discharge machining. The characterization plane is 
defined by the Axial Direction (AD) and Radial Direction (RD). After 
standard metallographic grinding and mechanical polishing, the surface 
deformation layer was removed via electrolytic polishing (electrolyte: 
30 % perchloric acid and 70 % methanol at 30 ℃ ) prior to EBSD 
characterization.

3. Results
3.1. Initial microstructure
The microstructure of the as-recrystallized Mo-Re alloy rod was 
characterized via EBSD on both the transverse (perpendicular to AD) 
and longitudinal (AD – RD) sections. While the transverse section exhibits 
a predominantly equiaxed morphology ( Fig. 1 a), the longitudinal sec -
tion ( Fig. 1 b) reveals pronounced morphological anisotropy with grains 
elongated along AD, reflecting the prior rotary swaging. Quantitative 
analysis using area-weighted grain size distributions ( Fig. 1 c) deter -
mined an average equivalent circle diameter of 95 µ m (range: 
12 – 213 µ m ) for the transverse section, compared to 117 µ m (range: 
18 – 250 µ m ) for the longitudinal section. This broad size span implies 
that subsequent deformation may be mechanically heterogeneous, 
governed by the distinct responses of coarse versus fine grains.
3.2. Compressive flow behavior and phase stability
Fig. 2 a illustrates the true stress-true strain response for the Mo-Re 
alloy compressed at 650 ℃ (0.01 s

tonic work hardening without significant dynamic softening. Macro -
scopic inspection of the deformed specimen (inset) reveals a smooth 
surface devoid of visible cracks or defects.
Fig. 2 b displays XRD patterns for the fully recrystallized and 
deformed states (20 %, 60 %). Peak positions align with pure BCC Mo, 
indicating that Re remains in solid solution without σ or χ phases pre -
cipitation. Although peak positions remain constant, the (110) peak 
intensity diminishes markedly with strain, reflecting texture evolution 
rather than phase transformation. This aligns with the thermodynamic 
equilibrium for Mo-(6 – 9 wt%)Re, which lies within the stable single- 
phase BCC region [6] . Furthermore, the vacuum environment and 
rapid thermal cycle effectively suppressed diffusion-controlled precipi -
tation and oxidation. Consequently, the absence of second-phase parti -
cles confirms that the observed deformation mechanisms are intrinsic to 
the BCC solid solution and precipitation-free.

onset of intergranular cracking in localized regions. Corresponding 
EBSD IPF maps ( Fig. 11 b) confirm that the < 101 > //AD texture has 
undergone marked depletion, while the < 111 > //AD texture has 
intensified. As the texture transition approaches saturation, the density 
of active band structures is significantly reduced compared to the 40 % 
state.
Detailed analysis of region ‘c ’ ( Fig. 11 c) illustrates the final stages of 
texture evolution. In Grain I, kinking mediates the reorientation from 
< 101 > //AD toward < 111 > //AD ( Fig. 11 h), whereas in Grain II, it 
drives the rotation from < 001 > //AD to < 111 > //AD rotation 
( Fig. 11 i). In both cases, the rotation axes for LABs and HABs cluster 
predominantly near the < 101 > axis. The high proportion of LABs, 
combined with smooth orientation gradients, indicates that during late- 
stage deformation, abrupt kink band rotations are increasingly com -
plemented by progressive deformation bands. These bands bridge the 
crystallographic gap between the matrix and the kinked domains, 
thereby smoothing the global texture transition and reducing local strain 
energy.
In region ‘d ’ ( Fig. 11 d), intergranular crack propagation is observed. 
High-magnification analysis of the sub-region “ g ” ( Fig. 11 e, g) reveals 
pronounced strain localization (high KAM) in the upper-left area. In 
contrast, the adjacent crossed kink band zone exhibits lower KAM 
values; this is attributed to the “ Z-shaped ” kink boundary formed by 
intersecting bands ( Fig. 11 f), which effectively disperses stress concen -
trations. Finally, pole-figure analysis confirms that under 60 % 
compression, while canonical {112} planes dominate, a minority of 
{101} planes also participate in kink rotation, collectively driving the 
alloy ’ s macroscopic texture reorientation.

network reorganization, and boundary interconnection constitute the 
core mechanisms of subgrain boundary formation. This process not only 
dissipates intragranular strain energy but also fragments the original

plastic deformation.

4.3. Grain boundary cracking at 60 % compression
Fig. 15 a provides TEM confirmation of intergranular crack propa -
gation, corroborating the SEM observations in Fig. 10 d. High- 
magnification imaging ( Fig. 15 b) shows dislocation walls terminating 
abruptly at the grain boundary. This confirms that the boundary func -
tions as a robust barrier to slip transmission, resulting in severe localized 
stress concentrations. Moreover, a comparison of adjacent grains 
( Fig. 15 c, d) reveals a stark contrast in intragranular dislocation density. 
This heterogeneity arises from plastic strain incompatibility between 
grains of distinct crystallographic orientations (and consequently 
differing Schmid factors). The resulting mismatch in slip activity gen -
erates critical stress gradients across the interface that ultimately exceed 
the grain boundary cohesive strength, triggering intergranular fracture.
During low-to-moderate strains, the subdivision of grains by kink 
bands and deformation bands drives continuous lattice reorientation, 
effectively mitigating stress concentrations. By 60 % compression, the 
microstructure evolves into a texture co-dominated by < 001 > //AD 
and < 111 > //AD texture ( Figs. 3, 11 ). Notably, the divergent stability 
of the < 001 > //AD and < 101 > //AD texture — despite sharing an 
identical maximum geometric Schmid factor ( ≈ 0.4714, Table 1 ) — is 
dictated by slip system multiplicity.
The < 001 > //AD orientation activates four symmetrically equiv -
alent {112} < 111 > systems, promoting homogeneous multiple slip 
that stabilizes the orientation. This mechanism explains the “ delayed ” 
response observed in Fig. 9 , where < 001 > grains sustain their orien -
tation until 40 % compression before finally transforming toward 
< 111 > via kinking. Conversely, the < 101 > //AD orientation acti -
vates only two equivalent systems; this lower symmetry induces un -
balanced plastic flow, driving rapid rotation toward the 
< 111 > texture.
Upon locking into the final < 111 > //AD orientation, the maximum 
Schmid factor drops sharply to ≈ 0.3143. This “ geometric hardening ” 
restricts further dislocation glide, leading to severe stress accumulation 
at grain boundaries and ultimately triggering intergranular cracking 
where slip activity is insufficient to blunt nascent cracks. This failure 
mode stands in sharp contrast to the deformation behavior at lower 
strains, where active kinking served as a potent shielding mechanism. 
Prior to texture saturation, kink bands effectively subdivided grains into 
micron-scale subdomains, increasing internal boundary density and 
restricting the dislocation mean free path. This substructural refinement 
promoted distributed pile-ups rather than singular stress concentrations, 
thereby raising the critical threshold for crack nucleation. Furthermore,

5. Conclusions
This study elucidates the microstructural mechanisms governing the 
plastic deformation and texture evolution of a Mo-Re alloy under axial 
compression at 650 ℃ (strain range 10 – 60 %). The results demonstrate 
that deformation kinking, triggered by specific dislocation reactions, 
serves as the primary driver for lattice reorientation. The interplay be -
tween abrupt kinking and continuous lattice curvature dictates the 
texture stability. The principal conclusions are:
1. Dislocation reactions: Coordinated slip on {110} and {112} 
planes leads to the formation of sessile hexagonal dislocation networks. 
These networks act as effective barriers to glide, contributing to early- 
stage work hardening and providing the structural precursors for 
dislocation wall formation.
2. Kink band nucleation and texture evolution: Driven by the 
plastic anisotropy arising from the dominant activation of single 
{112} < 111 > slip systems (promoted by Re and temperature), kink 
bands facilitate the rapid rotation of single slip dominant < 101 > //AD 
grains toward < 111 > //AD. In contrast, < 001 > //AD grains reorient 
more gradually due to higher slip system multiplicity (isotropic-like 
behavior), resulting in a final texture co-dominated by < 001 > //AD 
and < 111 > //AD texture.
3. Deformation bands as bridges: Unlike the rigid block rotation of 
kink bands, deformation bands accommodate strain via continuous 
lattice curvature. They serve as “ orientation bridges, ” smoothing the 
misorientation gaps between kinked domains and the matrix to maintain 
strain compatibility.
4. Texture saturation and failure mechanism: The dominance of 
the “ hard-oriented ” < 111 > //AD at high strains leads to geometric 
hardening and the cessation of kinking activity. With these accommo -
dation mechanisms exhausted, strain incompatibility intensifies at grain 
boundaries (particularly between distinct texture components), result -
ing in crack initiation.
