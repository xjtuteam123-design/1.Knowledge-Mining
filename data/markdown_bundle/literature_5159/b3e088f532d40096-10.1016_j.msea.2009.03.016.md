The role of Hf and TiC additions in the mechanical properties and

abstract
The microstructures and mechanical properties of Nb–12Al–20V (at.%) containing Hf or TiC have been
investigated. The microstructures were characterised using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The NbAlV alloy is single B2 phase, the 5% Hf added completely
dissolving into the matrix. The addition of 5% Hf and 5% TiC resulted in the formation of MC-type Hf-
rich carbide. Compression testing has been carried out at room temperature, 1273 and 1473 K. The alloys
show good compression ductility at room temperature. Hf addition gave a slight increase in strength at
both room temperature and high temperature. The addition of both Hf and TiC signiﬁcantly improves
the strength. The room temperature strength of these alloys could be well described by solid solution
strengthening and a load transfer mechanism. The alloys deform primarily via dislocation slip, with some
mechanical twinning.

1. Introduction
The efﬁciency of gas turbine engines is limited by the
temperature-dependent properties of materials, e.g., creep
strength, thermal mechanical fatigue, oxidation and corrosion
resistance, as well as the weight of engine components. Currently,
Ni-base alloys are the materials of choice for high temperature
turbine blade applications as they satisfy a unique combina-
tion of desired properties. A great deal of research is underway
to design new materials capable of operating at temperatures
higher than current capability allows. Niobium-based alloys are
one of the most promising refractory alloys for high temper-
ature structural applications in place of Ni-based superalloys,
which have a maximum operating temperature of about 1450 K.
Notwithstanding the sensitivity of niobium alloys to oxygen,
niobium has a very high melting point of 2741 K, a relatively
low density (8.57 g/cm
3) compared to the other refractory
metals and good ductility at room temperature. However, the
strength of niobium decreases substantially at temperatures above
1273 K.
Various approaches have hence been used to improve the
strength of Nb at elevated temperature, including solid solution
strengthening with Mo, W, Ta, Hf and V [1–3], and dispersion or
composite strengthening with intermetallic compounds such as
Nb
3Al and Nb5Si [4,5], as well as with a carbide[6–8]. Another major

2. Experimental procedure
The nominal compositions of the alloys investigated in this study
are Nb12Al20V, Nb12Al20V5Hf and Nb12Al20V5Hf5TiC (composi-
tions in at.%) . The alloys are referred to as NbAlV, NbAlVHf and
NbAlVHfTiC. High purity Nb, Al, V, Hf and TiC powder were used
as the starting materials. The mean size of the TiC powder particles
was 5 /H9262m according to the supplier speciﬁcation. The alloys were
prepared in a Retech transferred-arc plasma melter on a water-
cooled hearth under a positive pressure of Ar. The buttons were
remelted ﬁve times to promote homogeneity.
Conventional constant strain rate compression with a strain
rate of about 10
−3 s−1 was performed on all three alloys in the
as-cast condition. Room temperature compression tests were per-
formed in an ESH frame with a 200 kN capacity. Specimens with
dimensions 4.5 mm × 4.5 mm× 9.0 mm were cut using an electro-
discharge machine (EDM).
High temperature compression was carried out in a 1-kN Instron
8501 frame under vacuum. The limited loading capacity of this

3.2. Mechanical tests
The values of Vickers hardness for these alloys are given in
Table 2, which shows that the addition of Hf and/or TiC increases
the hardness. Fig. 5 shows the compression stress–strain curves of
the Nb–Al–V alloys with Hf or TiC at room temperature. The addi-
tion of Hf to the Nb–Al–V alloy improved the yield strength of the
alloy. TiC also has a signiﬁcant effect on the strength. The 0.2% offset
strength of NbAlVHfTiC is about 1200 MPa at room temperature.
The 0.2% offset strengths for the three alloys at 1273 and 1473 K
were obtained by constant strain rate compression under vacuum.
The results are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 6. Similarly to the results
at room temperature, the yield stress increases with the addition
of Hf or TiC. With increasing test temperature, the 0.2% ﬂow stress
decreases, although at 1273 K, the alloys still show a high yield stress
(530 and 660 MPa for the NbAlV and NbAlVHfTiC alloys, respec-
tively).
3.3. Deformation microstructure
The alloys exhibited good room temperature compression
ductility. After 20% compression at room temperature, the
microstructure was examined using TEM. A high density of dis-
locations can be seen ( Fig. 7 ), which clearly endured large scale
deformation. Diffraction contrast experiments revealed that the
Burgers vectors of the dislocations, b, were parallel to ⟨111 ⟩,
although it was not possible to identify the magnitude of these
unambiguously. In addition to these dislocations, some linear
defects were observed, as shown in Fig. 8 . TEM analysis shows
that whilst they had a crystallographic relationship with the matrix
which is similar to that of a b.c.c. twin, they are actually pseu-
dotwins: regions which have transformed to an orthorhombic
crystal structure. Similar features have been identiﬁed previously
in Nb–Ti–Al alloys [12] and can be described as a stress-induced
martensitic transformation. In samples deformed at elevated tem-
perature, the dislocations are curved and distributed much more
homogeneously than those observed after room temperature defor-
mation ( Fig. 9 ). Some dislocation networks (i.e., Fig. 10 ) were
observed, with frequent interactions between two set of disloca-
tions with b//⟨111 ⟩to form segments with b//⟨100 ⟩. This indicates
that climb of dislocations occurred at high temperature. Since it is
not possible that the Burgers vector has a magnitude larger than
⟨100 ⟩, we deduce that the Burgers vectors are 1/2⟨111 ⟩and ⟨100 ⟩.
This means that the dislocations with b//⟨111 ⟩ are superpartial
dislocations in the B2 matrix although we were not able to iden-
tify a corresponding superpartial paired with any of these defects.
This is similar to dislocations observed in Nb–15Al–20/40V [1 1]
and Nb–Al–Ti [13] alloys. Rong et al. [1 1]suggested that uncoupled
superpartial dislocations may occur due to the small domain sizes
and/or APB energy of these alloys. Another important microstruc-
tural evolution of the alloys deformed at high temperature is
the formation of subgrains via dynamic recovery (polygonisa-

4. Discussion
The results obtained from the above study clearly illustrate that
adding 5 at.% Hf does not modify the microstructure: the alloy is
still a single phase. This indicates that Hf is completely dissolved
into the B2 matrix. King’s study showed that the maximum solid
solution of Hf in Nb at room temperature is 50 at.% [15]. However,
5 at.% TiC addition clearly inﬂuenced the microstructure. Two types
of carbide (primary and secondary) are formed in the alloy. This is
because the solubility limit of carbon in Nb is about 0.55 at.% at
1873–2453 K with almost no solubility at room temperature [16].
Therefore, needle-like secondary carbide is formed in the matrix
during cooling after solidiﬁcation.
The results showed that the compressive yield strength of this
Nb–Al–V alloy improved signiﬁcantly with Hf and/or TiC addi-
tion at room and elevated temperatures. The inﬂuence of Hf and
TiC addition on the strength could be described via solid solution
strengthening and composite strengthening, respectively. In the
NbAlVHf alloy, Hf is completely dissolved into the matrix and there-
fore contributes solid solution strengthening. In the NbAlVHfTiC

Therefore, only conventional solid solution theories are considered

The NbAlVHfTiC alloy has a much higher strength than the
NbAlVHf, indicating that the addition of carbide confers signif-

strengthening of Nb-based alloys by carbide. Several potential
strengthening mechanisms deriving from the addition of carbide
include: Orowan strengthening, load transfer mechanism, ther-
mal expansion mismatch and geometrically necessary dislocation
(GND) strengthening. In the present case, however, the strengthen-
ing effects from Orowan, thermal expansion mismatch and GND are
small. More details are given in Ref. [20]. The major contribution to
the improvement of strength in the NbAlVHfTiC alloy is from direct
strengthening – the load transfer mechanism.

Although compression testing showed that the alloys have good
compressive ductility, the real proof of the ductility of materi-
als would be to tensile test. Here, it is necessary to comment
on the effect of carbide on the tensile ductility and fracture
toughness of the Nb-based alloys. To our knowledge, there are
few reports on tensile testing in Nb-based B2 alloys. Jiao et al.
[27] reported there is almost no tensile ductility in an as-cast
Nb–15Al–20V alloy. The study of Soboyejo et al. [28] showed that
forged Nb–12Al–44Ti–1.5Mo alloy (partially ordered B2) has good
room temperature ductility (1 1–14 pct). Our investigation [20] on
forged Nb–Ti–Al alloys with A2 structure showed that adding 1 at.%
C does not improve the strength, slightly impairs the ductility, but
gives a slight increase in the toughness; a 10 at.% C addition dramat-
ically decreases the ductility and the toughness, but signiﬁcantly
improves the strength. Kim et al. [29] observed that the addition
of suitable amounts of carbide (<5%) improved the toughness of
Nb–5Mo–15W. They found that the presence of carbide particles
hinders straight crack propagation and eventually leads to crack
deﬂection and branching, and thus improves the fracture tough-
ness.
5. Conclusions
We have studied the effects of Hf and carbide on the microstruc-
ture and mechanical properties of as-cast NbAlV alloy. The
conclusions of this work are as follows:
(1) NbAlV is a single B2 phase. 5 at.% Hf addition is completely dis-
solved into the matrix. 5 at.% Hf and TiC addition led to the
formation of MC-type carbides: irregularly shaped primary and
needle shaped secondary.
(2) The addition of Hf slightly increases the 0.2% offset strength.
Adding Hf and TiC signiﬁcantly improves the yield stress.
(3) At room temperature, deformation microstructures were domi-
nated by dislocations, with occasional mechanical pseudotwins.
At high temperature, the dislocations were distributed more
homogeneously and climb was observed. The carbide could
accommodate some deformation at elevated temperature.
