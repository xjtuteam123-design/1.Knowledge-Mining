ABSTRACT
High-performance lightweight materials are in demand for a wide range of critical applications in the auto -
motive, aviation, and aerospace industries. However, it remains difficult to enhance the strength of these alloys 
without significantly reducing their tensile ductility. Here, using different heat treatment routes, we overcome 
this inherent strength and ductility trade-off dilemma by introducing low lattice misfit precipitates in a light -
weight high-entropy alloy (LHEA). In particular, the A700 alloy exhibits a high yield strength of 609 MPa and a 
tensile ductility of ~24 % at room temperature, benefitting from the highly dispersed, low lattice misfit L1
2 
and 
nano-lamellar body-centered cubic (BCC) phases.
1. Introduction
The development of new lightweight materials is crucial for dimin -
ishing fuel consumption and emissions in various industries [1,2] , such 
as automotive [3] , aviation [4] , and aerospace [5,6] . An innovative 
alloying approach has been exploited to design new types of metallic 
alloys, termed as high-entropy alloys (HEAs), and has demonstrated 
great potential for seeking new materials [7 – 10] . HEAs offer unique 
combinations of properties that are not gainable in conventional alloys, 
including outstanding mechanical properties [11 – 13] , attractive resis -
tance to high-temperature softening [14,15] , excellent oxidation resis -
tance [16] , superior tribological properties [17 – 19] , and high thermal 
stability [20,21] . In particular, lightweight HEAs (LHEAs) composed of 
multiple light elements, like Al, Ti, Zr, Nb, and V, have attracted 
considerable attention owing to their low density [22,23] . Various 
permutations and combinations have already been attempted and 
studied to design a LHEA with a density of less than or nearly 7 ⋅ g cm
3
. 
Most of these compositions consist of solid solutions and brittle in -
termetallics, with only a limited number forming single-phase solid so -
lutions. Currently, advanced LHEAs are primarily developed by 
modifying the alloy composition, thereby influencing the constitutive 
phases within the microstructure to achieve the desired physical and 
mechanical properties while maintaining a low density. The pursuit of 
high-strength and ductile lightweight materials has always been a key 
objective for material scientists [23,24] . Unfortunately, many 
conventional strategies often acquire high material strength at the 
expense of useful tensile ductility. Finding lightweight materials that 
improve both strength and ductility remains a significant challenge.
Recently, LHEAs strengthened by low lattice misfit nano -
precipitation have shown promise in overcoming the strength-ductility 
trade-off [25 – 27] . In recent years, low lattice misfit strengthened 
LHEAs, particularly FCC-structured LHEAs with L1
2 
nanoprecipitates, 
have garnered considerable attention. The presence of a high-volume 
fraction of L1
2 
nanoprecipitates with coherent FCC/L1
2 
interfaces con -
tributes to a notable enhancement in strength without substantial loss of 
ductility, thereby achieving excellent combinations of strength and 
ductility [28] . Notably, the L1
2 
nanoprecipitates in previously reported 
LHEAs with volume fractions of less than 30 % can result in strength 
increments ranging from 200 to 800 MPa [28] . Consequently, the key to 
overcoming this trade-off is the characteristics of nanoprecipitates. For 
example, the intrinsic brittleness of nanoprecipitates, such as the B2 and 
Laves phases, seriously damages the tensile ductility of alloys [29 – 32] . 
More specifically, nanoprecipitates need to possess a low lattice 
mismatch with the matrix, a higher shear modulus, a highly stable phase 
structure, and an antiphase-domain-boundary energy [33 – 35] . In 
addition, Hence, precise control over the type, shape, size, volume 
fraction, and distribution of nanoprecipitates is crucial for the 
advancement of LHEAs with high strength while maintaining adequate 
ductility [36,37] .

2.2. Characterization
The crystal structures were identified by an Empyrean X-ray

characterized by a Zeiss SUPRA 55 scanning electron microscope (SEM) 
with a Channel 5.0 system for electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) 
analysis and a JEOL JEM 2100 F transmission electron microscope 
(TEM). Before the microstructural analysis using SEM, all samples were 
ground with SiC abrasive paper to 4000#, polished, and then etched

(Gatan 697) method were used to prepare TEM thin foils.
Flat, dog-bone-shaped tensile samples with gauge dimensions of 
16 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm (length × width × thickness) were cut by

4. Conclusions
In summary, this study highlights the influence of different heat 
treatment routes on the microstructure and mechanical properties of 
LHEAs. The realization of such a wide range of strength-ductility com -
binations is facilitated by different heat treatment routes. For instance, 
the strength of our alloy can be controlled within a very wide range, at 
least, of ~362 – 706 MPa, and the largest elongation is > 50 %. Among 
them, the A700 alloy exhibits a high yield strength of 609 MPa and a 
tensile ductility of ~24 % at room temperature, benefitting from the 
highly dispersed, low lattice misfit L1
2 
and nano-lamellar BCC phases. 
The low lattice misfit nanoprecipitates can be generally applied to other 
HEA systems to improve their mechanical properties. The precipitation 
mechanism of low lattice misfit L1
