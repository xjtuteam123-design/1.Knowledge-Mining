ABSTRACT 
Superalloys play a critical role in aerospace applications due to their exceptional mechanical properties at high 
temperatures. The application of magnetic fields to regulate superalloys presents novel avenues for optimizing 
microstructures, controlling solidification defects, and enhancing their high-temperature properties. This paper 
provides a comprehensive review of research findings on the influence of magnetic fields on the microstructure 
and properties of superalloys. It systematically elucidates the effects of different types and intensities of magnetic 
fields on alloy solidification. Moreover, this paper extensively explores the effects of various types of magnetic 
fields on the phase composition, microstructure, and properties of superalloys, including Ni-based and Co-based 
alloys, as well as high-entropy alloys. The underlying mechanisms behind the impact of magnetic fields on these 
alloy types are thoroughly analyzed. Finally, the last section offers a brief summary of the current development 
status and prospects of magnetic field-regulated superalloys.

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) represent a novel class of multi-principal 
alloys comprising five or more elements, either metallic or non-metallic, 
in an equiatomic or near-equiatomic ratio [ 29 ]. Since their introduction 
by Yeh et al. [ 30 ] and Cantor et al. [ 31 ] in 2004, these alloys have 
garnered considerable attention due to their exceptional properties, 
particularly at high temperatures. Previous investigations have identi -
fied four key effects exhibited by high-entropy alloys: (i) the 
high-entropy effect [ 32 , 33 ], (ii) lattice distortion effect [ 34 , 35 ], (iii) 
slow diffusion effect [ 36 , 37 ], and (iv) cocktail effect [ 38 ]. These effects 
synergistically contribute to the enhancement of phase stability and 
mechanical properties in HEAs, surpassing those of traditional alloys. 
Notably, HEAs demonstrate a distinctive combination of “ high 
strength-high ductility ” [ 39 , 40 ], excellent radiation resistance [ 41 , 42 ], 
high corrosion resistance [ 43 ], and superior creep resistance [ 44 ]. The 
exceptional high-temperature properties of HEAs primarily stem from 
the slow diffusion and high-entropy effects. The slow diffusion effect 
implies that diffusion in HEAs occurs at a slower rate compared to 
conventional alloys, thereby explaining their exceptional 
high-temperature strength [ 45 ] and structural stability [ 46 , 47 ]. 
Furthermore, the high-temperature properties of HEAs are also attrib -
uted to the high-entropy effect, which substantially reduces the Gibbs 
free energy G , as determined by the thermodynamic equation: G = H - TS . 
In particular, a significantly high entropy S results in a substantial 
reduction in G (assuming a negligible difference in H ) when incorpo -
rating the TS term in the Gibbs free energy equation. This phenomenon 
becomes even more pronounced at high temperatures. A low value of 
Gibbs free energy indicates enhanced structural stability in the phases of 
HEAs, leading to exceptional mechanical properties at high tempera -
tures, such as outstanding high-temperature strength [ 48 , 49 ] and 
oxidation resistance [ 50 ]. HEAs are considered as potential substitutes 
for Ni-based superalloys, positioning them as the next generation of 
high-temperature materials [ 51 ]. Studies have reported that two re -
fractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), namely NbMoTaW and VNbMo -
TaW, maintain a yield strength above 400 MPa at 1600 
◦
C [ 48 ], while 
the yield strength of the IN718 Ni-based superalloy drops below 200 
MPa at 1200 
◦
C [ 52 ]. 
The investigation of the impact of external physical fields on metal 
materials assumes a pivotal role in the exploration of novel phenomena, 
effects, and the advancement of innovative functional materials. For 
example, the addition of an ultrasonic field during alloy melting can 
bring acoustic flow and ultrasonic cavitation effects to the molten pool, 
promoting grain nucleation and refinement during the solidification 
process [ 53 – 55 ]. An external electric field can drive materials into and 
expand the molten pool, reducing the cooling rate [ 56 ]. Some studies 
have also reported that electric fields can reduce the residual tensile 
stress of low-melting point alloys [ 57 ]. Different from other external 
physical fields such as ultrasound and electric fields, magnetic fields 
play a unique role in controlling melt flow during alloy solidification 
[ 58 , 59 ], affecting dendrite morphology and element distribution [ 60 , 
61 ], as well as improving cracks and microporosity [ 62 , 63 ]. In recent 
years, considerable advancements in superconducting and cryogenic 
technologies have facilitated notable progress in the generation of 
magnetic fields, rendering the attainment of high magnetic fields 
(HMFs) more accessible [ 64 ]. As a non-contact physical field, the 
magnetic field exerts influence on the microstructure and properties of 
metal materials through the application of force and energy. Conse -
quently, this ability to regulate the manufacturing process of metal 
materials becomes feasible [ 65 ]. The interaction between the magnetic 
field and metal materials primarily manifests through various mecha -
nisms, including magnetizing force, Lorentz force, magnetic moment, 
interaction force between magnetic poles, and magnetization energy 
[ 66 – 69 ]. These forces and energies enable the magnetic field to exert 
influence on the flow state of the melt during metal solidification [ 70 ], 
modulate the migration and distribution of solute during solidification 
[ 71 ], and govern the morphology of the alloy ’ s microstructure [ 72 ]. 
Furthermore, such manipulation results in modified metal materials 
possessing enhanced properties, such as heightened strength, wear 
resistance, and magnetic characteristics. Within the solidification pro -
cess of metal materials, the thermal electromagnetic convection effect 
and the electromagnetic braking effect induced by the magnetic field 
can either promote or inhibit the flow of the alloy melt. For instance, 
Zhao et al. [ 73 ] employed a dynamic magnetic field (DMF) on laser 
cladding (LC) process to fabricate an NbMoTaW RHEA coating. The 
introduction of a magnetic field led to alterations in the dendrite dis -
tribution and orientation of the alloy coating. In another experiment, 
Zhao et al. [ 74 ] utilized a transverse magnetic field for DS, resulting in a 
substantial reduction in the volume fraction of the low melting point γ / γ ′ 
eutectic. Due to the inherent characteristics of the magnetic field, such 
as its non-contact nature, high energy density, and capacity to influence 
the microstructure and properties of metal materials, it is increasingly 
employed in the fabrication of metal materials. This avenue offers a 
novel approach to enhance the properties of emerging metal materials, 
including Ni-based superalloys and high-entropy alloys. 
The existing studies on the preparation of superalloys regulated by 
magnetic fields are limited, with most studies focusing on the effects of 
magnetic fields on the microstructure of Ni-based superalloys prepared 
through DS [ 75 , 76 ]. Furthermore, these reviews tend to cover only a 
narrow range of alloy types, and the described preparation methods are 
relatively singular. Consequently, the summarization of related mech -
anisms in these reviews is incomplete. This paper aims to provide a 
comprehensive review of the advancements made in preparing super -
alloys under the influence of magnetic fields. The research theme of this 
paper is to introduce the effect of magnetic field on the microstructure of 
superalloy, and then discuss the effect of magnetic field on the properties 
of superalloy. This paper systematically describes the effects of different 
types and intensities of magnetic fields on the solidification of alloys. 
Additionally, it offers a detailed analysis of how magnetic fields influ -
ence the microstructure and properties of not only Ni-based superalloys 
but also Co-based superalloys and high-entropy alloys. The underlying 
mechanisms behind the impact of magnetic fields on these alloy types 
are thoroughly analyzed. In the concluding section, the current devel -
opment status of superalloys prepared under magnetic fields is briefly 
summarized, and future research directions are suggested.

2. Effect of magnetic field on solidification of alloys 
During the solidification process of an alloy, the presence of a 
magnetic field plays a crucial role in various aspects, encompassing 
crystal orientation, solute migration and distribution, dislocation 
movement, and grain size. These factors, in turn, significantly influence 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloy. Magnetic 
fields can be categorized into two types based on whether the direction 
of the magnetic field changes: static magnetic fields (SMFs) and DMFs. 
In the case of an SMF, the primary effects that influence alloy solidifi -
cation are the thermal electromagnetic convection effect (TEMC) and 
the electromagnetic braking effect (EMB). Conversely, under a DMF, the 
principal impact on alloy solidification is exerted through electromag -
netic stirring effect (EMS).

3. Microstructure and properties of Ni-based superalloys 
regulated by magnetic fields 
Early studies have shown that SMFs can have a significant effect on 
the dendrites, element segregation and primary dendrite spacing (PDAS) 
of Ni-based superalloys. Ren et al. [ 79 ] conducted experiments utilizing 
the DZ417G superalloy, subjecting it to DS at various drawing speeds 
under an HMF. The findings revealed that the number of dendrites per 
unit area approximately doubled compared to samples without mag -
netic field exposure when an 8 T magnetic field was applied. The pri -
mary factor contributing to this phenomenon was identified as the 
TEMC induced by the SMF. Furthermore, Zhang et al. [ 85 ] investigated 
the influence of an HMF on the PDAS and element segregation in the 
DZ417G Ni-based superalloy prepared through DS. The segregation 
degree of elements can be quantified by segregation ratio ( SR ). It is 
defined as SR = C

respectively. The proximity of SR to 1 indicates a more homogeneous 
distribution of elements. A magnetic field of 6T has a significant effect in 
reducing the PDAS and the SR of Ti and Mo. However, as depicted in 
Fig. 7 , when the magnetic field strength exceeds 6 T, the PDAS increases, 
accompanied by an increase in the elements ’ SR . This phenomenon can 
be attributed to the competitive relationship between the TEMC and 
EMB. 
Regarding the effect of DMF on Ni-based superalloy, the early studies 
focused on the dendritic fragmentation and grain refinement by DMF. 
Jin et al. [ 89 ] examined the impact of a rotating magnetic field on the 
grain size of the IN100 superalloy. The findings revealed that the EMS 
induced by the rotating magnetic field generates forced convection 
within the alloy melt, resulting in dendritic fragmentation and grain 
refinement. In a separate study, Ma et al. [ 90 ] applied a pulsed magnetic 
field (PMF) during the solidification process of the IN718 alloy. Fig. 8 
illustrates the as-cast structure of the IN718 alloy with and without the 
application of a PMF. The introduction of a PMF leads to the refinement 
of equiaxed grains. 
Early studies on the influence of magnetic fields on Ni-based super -
alloys were incomplete. It should be noted that the microstructure of 
superalloys is the fundamental reason that determines their properties, 
and magnetic fields affect the properties of superalloys by affecting 
several key microstructures. In order to make the expression clearer and 
more reasonable, this review first introduces the key microstructure of 
Ni-based superalloys affected by magnetic field, and then discusses the 
effects of magnetic field on their properties. Based on this logic, the 
following sections classify Ni-based superalloys according to the key 
microstructures such as microsegregation, γ ′ phase, γ / γ ′ eutectic, PDAS, 
equiaxed grains and stray grains, and the influences of static and DMFs 
on the microstructure are introduced in each section. Finally, the in -

3.1. Microstructure of Ni-based superalloy prepared by traditional 
preparation technology under magnetic field 
For Ni-based superalloys prepared by traditional processes such as 
DS, casting and heat treatment (HT), the exceptional high-temperature 
properties of Ni-based superalloys are intrinsically linked to their 
microstructure. Research has demonstrated that the microstructural 
features of Ni-based superalloys, including the γ ′ phase, γ / γ ′ eutectic, 
PDAS, equiaxed grain, and stray grains, significantly influence their 
high-temperature strength, creep, and fatigue properties. The intro -

morphology of microstructural elements, such as microsegregation, γ ′ 
phase, γ / γ ′ eutectic, equiaxed grains, carbides, and PDAS, thereby 
enhancing the overall performance of the alloy.

3.1.4. Equiaxed grain 
During Ni-based single-crystal superalloy preparation under mag -
netic field, columnar grains transform into equiaxed grains (CET). Since 
the refined equiaxed grains contain higher volume fraction of grain 
boundaries and are more likely to creep at high temperatures. Compared 
to larger columnar grains, there are certain insufficient high- 
temperature mechanical properties. Ni-based superalloys working in 
high-temperature environments usually need to use columnar grains to 
reduce grain boundaries ’ volume fraction to improve the alloy ’ s high- 
temperature mechanical properties. Therefore, CET occurrence during 
single-crystal superalloy preparation is considered detrimental and 
should be avoided as much as possible [ 118 ]. Previous studies have 
shown that magnetic fields can affect the occurrence of CET during DS, 
where drawing speed and temperature gradient are key factors. A high 
SMF can cause dendrite core breakage, leading to CET [ 115 , 116 ]. As 
illustrated in Fig. 23 , CET increases with the intensity of the magnetic 
field [ 28 ]. Additionally, a lower drawing speed has been observed to

3.1.5. Stray grain 
Stray grains represent a common defect that arises during the 
preparation of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys. For example, in the 
production of single-crystal superalloy blades, stray grains often emerge 
due to changes in the cross-sectional profile of the alloy’s microstruc -
ture. Two primary mechanisms have been identified for the formation of 
stray grains. The first mechanism involves the local alteration of solid -
ification conditions, leading to heterogeneous nucleation in advance of 
the dendrite tip and subsequent generation of stray grains. The second 
mechanism entails the breaking of primary or secondary dendrites in the 
mushy zone due to thermal dissolution convection or solid force in the 
liquid phase, resulting in the formation of stray grains at the dendrite tip. 
During the preparation of superalloys, stray grains nucleate heteroge -
neously in random directions, introducing high-angle grain boundaries 
with different crystal orientations from the original dendrites. When 
blades are in operation, these high-angle grain boundaries may become 
favorable sites for crack initiation, thereby significantly reducing the 
blade’s service life [124,125]. Consequently, it is imperative to improve 
process parameters or introduce new physical fields to suppress the 
generation of stray grains. Research has shown that the application of a 
specific intensity of magnetic field during the preparation of superalloys 
can effectively inhibit the formation of stray grains. 
Transverse magnetic field shows beneficial effect on improving stray 
grains in Ni-based superalloys. Xuan et al. [126] conducted a study on 
the preparation of PWA1483 Ni-based superalloy using DS under a 0.7 T 
transverse SMF to investigate the impact of this field on the formation of 
stray grains. As illustrated in Fig. 25, the researchers found that under a 
temperature gradient of 50 K/cm and a drawing speed of 100 μ m/s, the 
transverse magnetic field effectively suppressed the formation of stray 
grains at the edge of the alloy. The magnetic field drives solute move -
ment along the TEMC direction, decreasing the content of solute ele -
ments and the degree of supercooling on the diverging side. This process 
inhibits heterogeneous nucleation and promotes the growth of 
higher-order dendrites at the edges, ultimately suppressing the forma -
tion of stray grains. 
Xuan et al. [119,127,128] conducted a series of experiments to 
investigate the impact of HMFs on the formation of stray grains during 
the preparation of Ni-based superalloys. Specifically, they examined the 
influence of a 12 T HMF on stray grains in the cross-section change 
platform during the DS of DZ417G Ni-based superalloy [28,127]. The 
results revealed that under a drawing speed of 150 μ m/s and a tem -
perature gradient of 70 K/cm, the application of a 12 T HMF effectively 
suppressed the formation of stray grains, as depicted in Fig. 26. This 
suppression was attributed to the increase in solid-liquid interface en -
ergy induced by the HMF, which raised the critical supercooling degree 
for nucleation and hindered heterogeneous nucleation. However, it 
should be noted that the inhibitory effect of the HMF on stray grains in 
the cross-section change platform diminished as the drawing speed 
increased [28]. Subsequently, Xuan et al. [119] employed an 8 T HMF 
during the remelting process of PWA1483 Ni-based superalloy seed 
crystals. They observed that the HMF effectively suppressed the occur -
rence of stray grains and promoted the development of an orderly 
dendrite structure. Nevertheless, subsequent research indicated that 
HMFs could also contribute to the formation of stray grains. In their 
investigation on the formation mechanism of stray grains in Ni-based 
single-crystal superalloys during DS under HMFs, Xuan et al. [128] 
discovered that stray grains were produced at the edge of the PWA1483 
sample solidified directionally at a drawing speed of 50 μ m/s under a 5 T 
HMF. This occurrence was attributed to the EMB generated by the HMF, 
resulting in increased solute concentration at the alloy sample’s edge, 
elevated supercooling degree, and heterogeneous nucleation of stray 
grains.

3.1.6. Other microstructures 
In addition to the microstructures discussed earlier, recent studies 
have also investigated the impact of magnetic fields on other structural 
characteristics of Ni-based superalloys. For instance, the content of 
carbides in Ni-based superalloys can be reduced by applying an external 
magnetic field during preparation. The carbide content is closely related 
to microsegregation, and a more uniform element distribution results in 
lower carbide content [ 130 , 131 ]. Ren et al. [ 59 , 61 ] found that at a 
drawing speed of 25 μ m/s, a 1.5 T magnetic field caused a 16.4% 
reduction in the carbide content of Ni-based superalloys. Moreover, as 
shown in Fig. 27 , under a drawing speed of 40 μ m/s and a temperature 
gradient of 70 K/cm, an 8 T HMF reduced the carbide content of 
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys by 82.4% [ 61 ]. However, there are 
still few reports on the influence of magnetic fields on carbides, and the 
underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. Further research is 
therefore necessary to elucidate these mechanisms and improve our 
understanding of the impact of magnetic fields on carbide content in 
Ni-based superalloys.

Freckles represent a type of solidification defect that can occur 
during the preparation of superalloys. The formation of freckles is 
influenced by various factors, such as the length of the mushy zone, melt 
convection, and magnetic field effects. Specifically, the application of a 
magnetic field during superalloy preparation can lead to the generation 
of freckles. At low magnetic field intensities, the solute in the melt mi -
grates towards the edge driven by the TEMC, resulting in the formation 
of freckles. At HMF intensities, although the TEMC is weakened, the 
mushy zone becomes longer, and the CET occurs. The equiaxed grains 
rotate under the influence of the TEMF, leading to the formation of 
freckles. For example, Dong et al. [ 114 ] observed freckles on the left side 
of the magnetic field direction during the DS of DZ417G alloy under a 
transverse magnetic field with a drawing speed of 5 μ m/s and a tem -
perature gradient of 150 K/cm. Xuan et al. [ 115 ] reported that a weak 
axial magnetic field of 0.6 T could induce freckles in the mushy zone 
during the DS of DZ417G alloy, which was attributed to the dominance 
of the TEMC. Conversely, under an HMF of 6 T, the TEMF was consid -
ered the main cause of freckle formation. Some studies have suggested 
that applying a traveling magnetic field with an appropriate intensity 
during the DS of CMSX-4 alloy can reduce the occurrence of freckles 
[ 117 ]. Moreover, the occurrence of freckles is also influenced by the 
drawing speed, with higher speeds suppressing freckle formation by 
decreasing the melt convection velocity and the convection velocity 
induced by the magnetic field [ 28 ].

3.2. Microstructure and mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloy 
prepared by additive manufacturing under magnetic field 
Additive manufacturing (AM), especially that involving metals and 
alloys, has received academic attention in recent years due to its ability 
to rapidly manufacture parts with complex geometries. So far, AM has 
been widely used in part manufacturing processes in industries such as 
aerospace, energy, defense, medical, and material forming [ 144 ]. Its 
materials include superalloys [ 145 – 147 ], Titanium alloys, Aluminum 
alloys, and so on. AM of Ni-based superalloys is the most widely studied 
superalloy in the field of AM and has become the material of choice for 
high-temperature industrial application components [ 144 ]. 
However, during the AM process of Ni-based superalloys, rapid 
heating and cooling will produce large temperature gradients and un -
even cooling rates, which will cause strong residual stress inside the 
parts. When the tensile strength is exceeded, residual stress can even 
cause failure of Ni-based superalloy parts [ 148 ]. In addition, AM parts 
are prone to defects such as cracks and micropores. These factors greatly 
limit their application. Studies show that magnetic fields have a signif -
icant effect on microsegregation, grain size, PDAS and CET of Ni-based 
superalloys prepared by AM. When an external magnetic field is applied 
during the AM process, the TEMF generated by the magnetic field can 
minimize the flow field within the molten pool, suppress heat transfer 
and minimize the cooling rate [ 149 ], which helps improve defects such 
as residual stress, cracks and microporosity in AM superalloys. 
3.2.1. Microstructure 
The microstructure of Ni-based superalloys prepared by AM plays a 
decisive role in their mechanical properties and service life. The appli -
cation of an SMF during the AM process can effectively limit Marangoni 
convection and affect the microsegregation, grain size and dendrite 
morphology of Ni-based superalloys. For example, the 0.2 T SMF 
significantly reduced the insoluble Nb-rich phase of the IN718 alloy 
prepared by direct energy deposition (DED), as shown in Fig. 30 (a). This 
is attributed to magnetic field induced TEMC [ 150 ]. Similarly, under a 
transverse SMF of 20 mT, Wang et al. [ 151 ] used wire arc additive 
manufacturing (WAAM) to prepare IN625 alloy. The TEMC effect 
generated by the magnetic field significantly improves the micro -
segregation of Nb and Mo and refines the dendrite size. Nie et al. [ 149 ] 
found that the average dendrite spacing of IN718 alloy was reduced by 
about 32% when laser remelted under a transverse SMF. However, SMFs 
have also been reported to coarsen the grain size. Du et al. [ 152 ] pointed 
out that under a transverse magnetic field of 0 – 0.1 T, the PDAS of IN718 
alloy prepared by laser direct energy deposition (LDED) increases with 
the increase of magnetic field intensity, as shown in Fig. 30 (b). This 
phenomenon is mainly attributed to the suppression of melt flow by the 
EMB effect of the SMF. In addition, some studies have found that as 
TEMC of the SMF suppresses the microsegregation of Nb and Ti, the size 
and content of the Laves phase of the IN718 alloy prepared by LC 
significantly decreases. The morphology also changes from long 
chain-like to discrete worm-like, as shown in Fig. 30 (c) [ 153 ].

Ni-based superalloy parts prepared by AM usually produce strong 
residual stress due to excessive solidification rates. Residual stress is an 
important cause of cracks and structural deformation of parts. The 
complex heat and mass transfer can lead to reduced quality of AM parts 
[ 160 , 161 ]. Magnetic fields have a potential positive effect on residual 
stress. The magnetic field ’ s suppression of unstable convection in the 
molten pool and its destructive effect on dendrites during the solidifi -
cation process can reduce residual stress.

IN718 alloy. The residual stress dropped from 392.50 MPa without 
magnetic field to 315.45 MPa. The authors pointed out that the elec -
tromagnetic force of the transverse magnetic field on the molten pool 
inhibited the unsteady convection and heat transfer of the melt, result -
ing in a reduction in the cooling rate. This directly causes the residual 
stress to decrease. The TEMF of the order of 10

3.2.4. Porosity and densification 
Porosity is a common defect in superalloy parts during AM. Previous 
studies have shown that there are three main causes of pores in AM: (i) 
lack of fusion due to insufficient energy input, which usually results in 
irregular-shaped pores [ 164 , 165 ]; (ii) due to excessive energy input, the 
energy will lead to keyhole fluctuations and unstable vapor settlement, 
thereby causing keyhole porosity. They are usually concentrated at the 
bottom of the molten pool and are roughly spherical in shape [ 166 , 167 ]; 
(iii) pores caused by gas pores in alloy powder or protective gas entering 
the molten pool [ 168 ]. 
The application of SMFs during AM has shown the feasibility of 
reducing the porosity of superalloys [ 162 ]. For example, under the ac -
tion of a 0.2 T axial magnetic field, the microporosity of IN718 alloy 
prepared by LDED dropped from 0.3% to 0.2% [ 150 ]. The suppression of 
harmful Marangoni convection by the SMF is the main reason for the 
decrease in microporosity. In order to further understand the relation -
ship between densification and SMF, Du et al. [ 169 ] studied the effect of 
SMF on IN718 alloy prepared by laser powder bed melting (LPBF). In 
this study, the densification of IN718 alloy increased with increase of 
SMF, as shown in Fig. 32 . The author proposed that the 
thermo-electromagnetic effect due to the SMF acting on the molten pool 
increases the energy absorption of the laser, resulting in reducing the (i) 
type micropores. In addition, numerical simulations found that a 0.3 T 
axial SMF can significantly suppress unstable convection in the molten 
pool when preparing IN718 alloy by LPBF, thereby improving pore de -
fects [ 170 ]. Furthermore, the addition of TiC nanoparticles and the 
application of a 70 mT SMF during the preparation of IN738 alloy by 
SLM significantly reduced microporosity [ 157 ]. When a transverse 
magnetic field was applied during the preparation of superalloy coatings 
by LC, the microporosity volume fraction decreased significantly as the 
magnetic field intensity increased from 0 to 75 mT due to the Lorentz 
force [ 154 ]. 
The current study shows that magnetic fields can reduce the micro -
porosity and increase the densification of superalloys by AM, but further 
work is needed to reveal more details of the magnetic field regulation 
mechanism. Therefore, in order to achieve the purpose of reducing the 
microporosity of superalloys, AM and external magnetic fields should be 
combined and adjusted to appropriate process parameters. 
3.2.5. Mechanical properties 
Table 3 summarizes the mechanical properties of superalloys pre -
pared by AM under magnetic field, including yield strength (YS), ulti -
mate tensile strength (UTS), and elongation at break ( δ ). Since magnetic 
fields can improve microsegregation, grain size, microstructures such as 
PDAS and CET, as well as defects such as cracks and pores, the me -
chanical properties of superalloys prepared by AM under magnetic field 
are generally higher than those of superalloys without magnetic fields. 
The transverse SMF simultaneously improves the strength and 
elongation of IN625 alloy prepared by WAAM [ 151 ], as shown in Fig. 33 
(a). This is attributed to the improvement of dendrite structure, dendrite 
orientation and microsegregation by the transverse magnetic field. The 
IN718 alloy prepared by LDED assisted by a transverse SMF showed a 
UTS of 1063 MPa, which was improved compared with that without a 
magnetic field, while δ did not increase significantly [ 150 ]. The appli -
cation of a axial magnetic field of 200 mT increased the UTS and δ of the 
IN718 alloy prepared by LDED from 821 MPa to 23% to 831 MPa and 
27% respectively [ 150 ]. 
For DMFs, the results reported by Huan et al. [ 158 ] showed that the 
IN718 alloy prepared by AMF-assisted WAAM showed higher UTS, but 
the δ decreased. The use of AMFs enhanced the strength and ductility of 
the IN718 alloy prepared by PAAM. The UTS and δ of the sample 
improved slightly under the action of the magnetic field, while the YS 
increased from 472 MPa without a magnetic field to 507 MPa with a 
magnetic field [ 159 ]. In addition, some studies have also reported that 
the microhardness of AM superalloys has been improved under static or 
DMF, as shown in Fig. 33 (b) and (d) [ 151 , 159 ]. 
As mentioned before, the preparation of superalloys by external 
magnetic field-regulated AM can significantly improve defects such as 
residual stress, microporosity, and cracks, and has a significant impact 
on microsegregation, grain size, and dendrite morphology. These factors 
have a positive effect on improving the mechanical properties of Ni- 
based superalloys. The review results in this section show that the 
application of different types of magnetic fields in AM can have potential 
beneficial effects on superalloys prepared by AM. However, it should be 
noted that the mechanism of different types of magnetic fields on melt 
flow has not yet been fully revealed, and further work is needed to 
improve it.

5. Microstructure and properties of HEAs regulated by magnetic 
fields 
HEAs are renowned for their exceptional combination of strength, 
ductility, corrosion resistance, fracture resistance at low temperatures, 
and magnetic properties [ 178 – 181 ]. These remarkable properties are 
closely linked to the phase composition and microstructure of HEAs. 
Research findings indicate that the application of a magnetic field can 
have a profound impact on the volume fraction and lattice parameters of 
constituent phases within HEAs, as well as on various aspects of their 
microstructure, including dendrite morphology, grain size, and pre -
cipitates. Furthermore, the presence of an external magnetic field can 
noticeably enhance the quality of solidification by mitigating solidifi -
cation defects such as cracks, microporosities, and residual stresses. 
Consequently, the utilization of a magnetic field holds significant po -
tential for effectively improving the properties of HEAs. 
5.1. Phase composition

including body-centered cubic (BCC), face-centered cubic (FCC), and 
hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phases [ 182 ]. In some cases, HEAs may 
also contain intermetallic compound phases. The phase composition of 
HEAs is a critical factor that profoundly influences its properties and 
range of applications. According to studies, applying a magnetic field 
does not typically alter the type of HEA ’ s phase composition. However, 
it can significantly influence the lattice parameters and volume fraction 
of the constituent phase.

6. Summary and prospects 
This paper reviews the different types of magnetic fields used to 
regulate the preparation process of superalloys, including SMFs and 
DMFs, and involves superalloy preparation technologies such as DS, AM, 
arc melting and HT and so on. 
The SMF has a significant impact on the convection and dendrite 
structure of the superalloy molten pool through the TEMC effect and 
EMB effect. TEMC enhances the convection of the Ni-based superalloy 
melt, which is beneficial to the diffusion of alloy elements, thereby 
improving the microsegregation of each component element and 
reducing the content of γ / γ ′ eutectic. The influence of SMF on Ni-based 
superalloy PDAS depends on Ha . When Ha is close to 10, the TEMC effect 
is most significant and PDAS reaches its minimum. TEMF acting on 
dendrites by an SMF can destroy columnar grains and induce the for -
mation of equiaxed grains. CET caused by an SMF has a beneficial effect 
on reducing the residual stress of Ni-based superalloys, but equiaxed 
grains usually bring more grain boundaries, which will adversely affect 
the high-temperature properties of Ni-based superalloys. AN SMF of a 
certain intensity can raise the critical supercooling degree for nucleation 
and hinder heterogeneous nucleation, thereby effectively inhibiting the 
formation of stray grains in Ni-based superalloys. For Ni-based super -
alloys prepared by AM, the addition of an SMF can effectively limit 
Marangoni convection and affect the microsegregation, grain size and 
dendrite morphology of Ni-based superalloys. In addition, the SMF has a 
significant improvement effect on defects such as residual stress, cracks 
and porosity in Ni-based superalloys prepared by AM. 
The EMS effect of DMFs causes convection within the molten pool of 
superalloys and exerts forces on the solid phase. This promotes the 
diffusion of elements within the molten pool and is beneficial to 
improving microsegregation. DMFs can also lead to the formation of 
equiaxed grains. This is because the DMF forms forced convection at the 
solidification front of Ni-based superalloy, causing the growth of 
columnar grains to be destroyed. In addition, during the AM process, the 
EMS effect of the DMF can promote mass transfer in the molten pool, 
thereby reducing the microsegregation of Ni-based superalloys prepared 
by AM and improving the harmful Laves phase. 
In general, both static and DMFs can have a positive effect on the

For the preparation of HEA under magnetic fields, this paper aims to 
investigate the impact of magnetic fields on various aspects of HEA’s 
preparation. Specifically, the effects of magnetic fields on phase 
composition, microstructure, mechanical properties, and magnetic 
properties of HEA are explored. The experimental results highlight that 
the appropriate intensity of a magnetic field can induce changes in the 
volume fraction and lattice parameters of constituent phases in HEA. 
Furthermore, magnetic fields exert significant influence on the element 
distribution, grain structure, texture, and dislocation behavior of HEA 
prepared through methods such as arc melting, induction melting, DS, 
and LC. Regarding the mechanical properties of HEA, the application of 
a magnetic field during preparation shows promise in achieving a 
desirable combination of high strength and high ductility, thereby 
enhancing the overall alloy properties. Notably, the hardness and wear 
resistance of HEA also exhibit improvements to some extent when 
subjected to the influence of a magnetic field. Additionally, the magnetic 
properties of HEA can be significantly enhanced through the application 
of a magnetic field, potentially leading to the development of HEA with 
excellent soft magnetic properties. However, it is worth noting that the 
preparation of HEA under magnetic fields is still at an early stage, and 
the underlying mechanisms governing its effects on microstructure and 
properties remain unclear. 
Given the current limitations in research, this paper presents the 
following prospects for further investigation and development. 
(i) Current research on the preparation of Ni-based superalloys 
under magnetic fields primarily concentrates on investigating the 
influence of various types of magnetic fields on the microstruc -
ture of DS Ni-based superalloys. However, there is limited 
research exploring the control of mechanical properties in Ni- 
based superalloys through magnetic fields. The existing studies 
predominantly examine the impact of magnetic fields on prop -
erties such as fatigue resistance and creep resistance of the Ni- 
based superalloys. Prior studies have corroborated the signifi -
cant enhancement of microstructural parameters such as the γ ′ 
phase, texture, grain boundary angle, microporosity, as well as 
mechanical properties like wear resistance, tensile strength, and 
creep resistance in laser-produced superalloys [154,155,157, 
199]. Nonetheless, superalloys fabricated via AM often encounter 
substantial residual stresses, leading to crack formation and 
structural deformations that curtail their utility. Magnetic fields 
can be a potential method to eliminate the residual stress in laser 
additive manufacturing components. The magnetic field can 
suppress unsteady convection and heat transfer in the molten 
pool and refine the grains, which has a positive effect on 
improving the residual stress of superalloys prepared by AM. Nie 
et al. [149] observed that the application of an SMF with an in -
tensity of 0.55 T induced TEMF measuring 10
6 
N/m
3 
and lower 
cooling rates. Consequently, this significantly mitigated residual 
stresses in laser-remelted Ni-based superalloys. These findings 
highlight the pivotal role of magnetic fields in reducing residual 
stresses within superalloys, thereby potentially serving as a viable 
approach to alleviate such stresses in AM processes. Despite these 
advancements, current investigations into the reduction of re -
sidual stresses in superalloys through magnetic fields confront 
limitations concerning the diversity of magnetic field types 
examined and the constrained range of superalloy types explored. 
As a result, the underlying mechanisms behind these effects 
remain inadequately elucidated. Further studies are warranted to 
explore the implications of different magnetic field configura -
tions in the AM of superalloys. By systematically investigating 
these effects, we can gain insights into the mechanisms by which 
magnetic fields ameliorate residual stresses and subsequently 
enhance the mechanical properties of superalloys. 
(ii) There are limited reports on the preparation of HEA under 
magnetic fields, and most research has focused on the effects of 
magnetic fields on their phase composition, microstructure, and 
mechanical and magnetic properties at room temperature. 
However, investigating the mechanical properties of HEA at 
elevated temperatures is crucial as it determines their perfor -
mance in high-temperature environments. Furthermore, the in -
dustrial application of HEA primarily relies on their high- 
temperature properties, which directly impact their range of 
use. Unfortunately, there is insufficient research on the effects of 
magnetic fields on the high-temperature properties of HEA, thus 
leading to an unclear understanding of the mechanisms under -
lying their influence. HEA has excellent high-temperature prop -
erties, especially RHEA, which is expected to replace Ni-based 
superalloys with broad application prospects as the next gener -
ation of superalloys. Recent studies have shown that the micro -
structure and defects of RHEA significantly affect their high- 
temperature properties [200,201]. For instance, reciprocating 
DMFs can increase the fluidity of the molten pool of NbMoTaW 
RHEA coatings, leading to improved microstructural uniformity, 
and reduced residual stress, microporosity, and cracks [73]. 
Therefore, magnetic fields could serve as a potential approach to 
regulate the high-temperature properties of RHEA. Additionally, 
slow diffusion effects and solid solution strengthening are also 
critical factors for RHEA to achieve superior high-temperature 
properties [202]. Nonetheless, few studies have investigated 
how magnetic fields regulate these two factors to enhance the 
high-temperature performance of RHEA, and the mechanism of 
action remains unresolved. Future research could explore the 
impact of magnetic fields on the slow diffusion effect and solid 
solution strengthening of RHEA to control microstructure and 
solidification defects and ultimately improve their 
high-temperature properties. 
(iii) Studies have demonstrated that HMFs exceeding 10 T can 
significantly alter the convection diffusion, solute migration, 
crystal orientation, and phase transformation during alloy solid -

preferred orientation of the alloy, and inhibit solute diffusion. 
HMFs have emerged as an important approach to modify the 
microstructure of alloys and enhance their mechanical, 
high-temperature, and magnetic properties. Nonetheless, there 
are few studies on the effects of HMFs on the microstructure and 
properties of Ni-based and Co-based superalloys and HEAs. Some 
existing reports have found that HMFs can decrease the diffu -
sivity of Ni-based superalloy elements, induce dendrite breakage 
leading to columnar-to-equiaxed transition, inhibit stray grain 
formation, and increase the PDAS [105,106,127]. For HEAs, 
HMFs reduce microsegregation and increase tensile strength, 
ductility, and soft magnetic properties [184,186,205]. Since the 
high-temperature properties of superalloys and HEAs are closely 
related to microstructural changes, rational utilization of HMFs 
can regulate their high-temperature properties and unveil the 
mechanisms underlying HMFs’ effects on such properties. How -
ever, research on the high-temperature strength and creep

(iv) As the service environment for materials continues to grow in

magnetic fields present a potential approach for enhancing the 
saturation magnetization of HEAs while reducing magnetic 
coercivity and residual magnetization. Conducting relevant 
research in this area holds great significance for developing HEAs 
with improved soft magnetic properties and expanding their 
range of applications.
