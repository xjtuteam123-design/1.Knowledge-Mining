ABSTRACT
Refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs) have attracted much attention due to their excellent high 
temperature mechanical properties. However, extremely low room temperature tensile ductility hinders further 
engineering applications. In this work, we designed novel HfNbTaTiAl RMPEAs and studied the structure- 
properties relation and deformation mechanism at room temperature. Results show that the yield strength of 
the as-cast alloy is up to 1029 MPa and the tensile ductility at room temperature is between 17.8 % and 22.8 %.

1. Introduction
In the past two decades, the rapid development of modern industry 
and aerospace industry has put forward more strict requirements for 
high-temperature structural materials. The development of new mate -
rials with excellent high-temperature mechanical properties is one of the 
key issues in the field of structural metal materials [ 1 – 3 ]. In recent years, 
multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) have emerged as a new type of 
metal materials, which have broken the design concept of single prin -
cipal element of traditional alloys [ 4 ]. RMPEAs, composed of high 
melting point elements such as Ti, Zr, Ta, W, Mo, V, Nb, Cr and Hf, have 
been widely concerned as potential choices for high temperature ap -
plications, due to their excellent high temperature strength, oxidation 
resistance, radiation resistance and phase stability [ 5 – 8 ]. For example, 
in 2010, Senkov et al. [ 9 ] prepared two RMPEAs, NbMoTaW and 
NbMoTaWV alloy, via arc melting method, and their strengths at high 
temperature were significantly higher than those of two traditional 
Inconel 718 and Haynes 230 superalloys. However, although RMPEAs 
retain excellent strength, most RMPEAs have extremely low ductility at 
room temperature, thus limiting their wide application as structural 
materials in complex engineering environments. Therefore, most 
RMPEAs can only detect compression properties, not tensile properties. 
For example, the compressive fracture strain of NbMoTaW [ 9 ] alloy is 
less than 2 % at room temperature and the tensile ductility is almost 
zero. The compressive yield strength of Al
x
CrTaTi [ 10 ] RMPEAs at room 
temperature is up to 1780 MPa, but the compressive fracture strain is 
only 10 %, which makes it impossible to evaluate the tensile properties. 
In order to solve the problem of poor room temperature ductility for 
RMPEAs, several strategies have been proposed. Studies have shown 
that group IV and group V elements (Hf, Nb, Ta, Ti, V, Zr) in the periodic 
table have intrinsic excellent ductility, thus RMPEAs with both high 
strength and ductility can be obtained by adjusting element composition 
[ 11 – 13 ]. For example, adding Zr element into brittle NbMoTaW can 
significantly increase its compressive yield strength from 1070 MPa to 
1618 MPa, and compressive fracture strain from 1.5 % to 20 % [ 14 ]. 
Sheikh et al. [ 15 ] proposed a criterion based on valence electron con -
centration (VEC) to predict the mechanical properties of BCC alloys, 
thereby obtaining RMPEAs with excellent ductility. In addition, some 
literatures indicate that the insertion of TRIP effect [ 16 ] or addition of 
oxygen atoms [ 17 ] can also enhance the ductility of RMPEAs.
Recently, our group [ 18 ] reported an Al

3.2. Microstructure characterization
Fig. 2 (a) shows the XRD patterns of the three as-cast alloys N0, N10 
and N15, all of which exhibit typical BCC structures, consistent with the 
predicted results. Fig. 2 (b) shows the magnified (110) peaks in the XRD 
patterns. Compared with N0 alloy, the XRD peak of N10 alloy is slightly 
shifted to the left. With the continuous increase of Nb content, the main 
peak of N15 alloy is shifted to the right compared with N10 alloy. There 
is little difference in atomic radius between Nb (0.146 nm) element and 
Ta (0.143 nm) element, resulting in small deviations for the diffraction 
angles of the peaks. Fig. 2 (c) shows the lattice constants of the three 
alloys, which are 3.3685 Å, 3.3702 Å and 3.3557 Å, respectively.
To further observe the microstructure evolution of the alloys, Fig. 3
(a) – (c) present the EBSD-IPF maps and grain size of the alloys. It can be 
seen that the three alloys are composed of coarse grains with random 
orientation. In general, the average grain size of the alloy increases from 
257 μ m for N0 to 427 μ m for N15 with the increase of Nb content.
The microstructure and chemical composition of the three alloys 
were studied by SEM-EDS. Fig. 4 (a) – (c) show the microstructure of N0, 
N10 and N15 as-cast alloys, respectively. The results show that all alloys 
exhibit dendrite structures due to segregation of elements, without the 
formation of secondary phase.
Fig. 4 (d) – (f) are magnified images of the local regions marked by red 
rectangles in Fig. 4 (a) – (c). Combined with the EDS results shown in 
Table 3 , it can be seen that Ta element with high melting point is mainly 
enriched in the dendrite region, while Al and Ti elements with low 
melting points are enriched in the interdendritic region. The content of 
Hf in the dendrite region is little different from that of the nominal 
components, and more enriched in the dendrite region. In the Nb-added 
alloys, Nb is mainly enriched in the dendrite region, and the content of 
other elements shows the same trend as the sample without Nb.

3.4. Fracture morphology
Fig. 6 shows the fracture morphology of N0, N10 and N15 alloys after 
tensile testing. As shown in Fig. 6(a1)-(a3) , more slip traces were found 
in the three alloys after tensile fracture, indicating excellent plastic 
deformation capacity. Fig. 6(b1)-(b3) shows the necking region of the 
alloys, which is more pronounced for N15 alloy than for the other two 
alloys, indicating that a large amount of plastic deformation occurred 
before fracture. It is consistent with the results of engineering stress- 
strain curves. Fig. 6(c1)-(c3) shows the fracture morphology observed 
in the red dotted line area in Fig. 6(b1)-(b3) . Fig. 6(d1)-(d3) shows a 
magnified view in Fig. 6(c1)-(c3) . It can be seen that dense dimples exist 
in the fracture surfaces, indicating typical ductile fractures. The fracture 
surface of N0 sample exhibits numerous dense dimples. Compared with 
N0 sample, the number of dimples for N10 sample significantly reduces, 
but the dimple depth slightly increases. The N15 sample possesses much 
deeper dimples compared with N0 and N10 samples, and thus exhibits 
much higher ductility.
4. Discussion
4.1. Deformation mechanism
In order to explain the differences of deformation behaviors among 
the as-cast RMPEAs at room temperature, two typical alloys N0 and N15 
were selected to observe the microstructure of the alloys at different 
strain levels using EBSD. Fig. 7 shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) maps, 
kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps and EBSD phase maps of N0 
alloy at tensile strains of 0 %, 7.4 % and 18.6 %. It can be seen from 
Fig. 7 (a) – (c) that the orientation of some grains changes during tensile 
deformation, indicating obvious grain rotation. Fig. 7 (d) – (f) shows the 
corresponding KAM maps of the N0 alloy. With the increase of global 
strain, stress concentration appears near the grain boundary, indicating 
that the dislocation density in this region is high, which leads to serious 
dislocation tangle and hinders the dislocation movement. When the 
strain is further increased to 18.6 %, the tensile specimen is fractured. 
From the KAM maps, it can be found that the KAM value inside the grain 
increases significantly, indicating that the dislocation density inside the 
grain increases, and the strain concentration transfers from the grain 
boundary to the grain interior. The phase maps of Fig. 7 (g) – (f) shows 
that the N0 alloy has a single-phase BCC structure during deformation 
until fracture, indicating that it has excellent phase stability during 
deformation.
The IPF maps, KAM maps and phase maps of N15 alloy at tensile 
strains of 0 %, 7.4 % and 22.8 % are shown in Fig. 8 . It can be seen from 
Fig. 8 (a) – (c) that the orientation of the grains in N15 alloy changes 
gradually during the tensile process, and most of the grains appear red, 
indicating that the grains preferentially have a < 0 0 1 > orientation 
parallel to the tensile direction during the tensile process. Similarly, 
with the increase of global strain, the deformation concentration first 
appears at the grain boundary, and with the further increase of global 
strain, the deformation is concentrated from the grain boundary to the 
grain interior. Similar to the N0 alloy, the N15 alloy still maintains 
excellent phase stability until fracture during the tensile deformation. 
Interestingly, lamellar deformation bands with crystallographic orien -
tation different from the matrix were observed in the grain of the frac -
tured N15 specimen. During the deformation of RMPEAs, these 
deformation bands may be identified as micro-shear bands [ 36 ], twin -
ning bands [ 37 ], or kink bands (KBs) [ 38 ]. However, different defor -
mation bands have different effects on the mechanical properties of the 
alloys, so it is necessary to distinguish the deformation bands observed 
in N15 alloy.
Fig. 9 (a) shows the deformation bands observed in the N15 alloy. 
Fig. 9 (b) shows the cumulative misorientation of the red line in Fig. 9 (a).

and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > , with constant misorientation of 60
◦

◦

bility of shear bands. Therefore, the deformation bands in the N15 alloy 
are identified as the kink bands (KBs).

significantly improve the ductility of N15 alloy.
