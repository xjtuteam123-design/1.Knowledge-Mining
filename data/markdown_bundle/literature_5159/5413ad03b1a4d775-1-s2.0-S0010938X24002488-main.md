ABSTRACT 
The influence of Ni-rich intermetallic particles (IMPs) on the microstructure, high-temperature mechanical 
properties, and corrosion behaviour of Al – Si – Mg – Cu – Ni alloys was investigated. The presence of coarse block- 
like γ -Al
7
Cu
4
Ni and thin needle-like δ -Al
3
NiCu IMPs contributed to low-temperature alloy strengthening dur -
ing tensile tests. However, void nucleation and coalescence within the IMPs facilitated ductile fracture and alloy 
rupture, resulting in a lower tensile strength at high temperatures. Although both IMP phases had a cathodic 
electrochemical nature, resulting in strong galvanic corrosion, the γ -Al
7
Cu
4
Ni phase acted as the primary 
cathode, amplifying localised pitting while reducing the overall alloy corrosion resistance. 
1. Introduction 
In the automotive industry, the deterioration of structural materials, 
often in corrosive environments, leads to the early failure of components 
in service and immensely increased fabrication costs [1 – 3] . Classical 
theories predict that corrosion is a uniform process; however, localised 
corrosion often accelerates at material defect sites and in specific envi -
ronments [4 – 8] . Therefore, localised corrosion offers a greater risk to 
component integrity [9 – 11] . Identifying localised corrosion mechanisms 
in materials is extremely challenging because they occur at small scales 
(i.e., the micro-, and nanoscale), making their detection by conventional 
non-destructive testing challenging. Electron microscopy is an efficient 
approach for studying the early stages of localised corrosion mecha -
nisms. In recent decades, a great understanding of localised corrosion 
has been developed for automotive Fe-based alloys as a result of com -
bined industrial and research efforts [12 – 17] . However, the need for 
carbon neutrality as a result of climate change has led to the replacement 
of Fe-based alloys with lightweight Al alloys, especially in the automo -
bile industry [18 – 29] . However, there is a lack of understanding of 
localised corrosion mechanisms in these less-studied Al alloys, which 
adds to uncertainty about automotive component failure. 
Al – Si alloys have been extensively used in car components such as 
engines and transmission components owing to their excellent cast -
ability, good strength, and excellent corrosion resistance [30,31] . The 
typical microstructure of Al – Si alloys consists of a percolating α -Al 
matrix and rigid Si particles that are segregated during eutectic solidi -
fication. Cu and Mg alloying increases the room- and high-temperature 
strength of these alloys by dispersing secondary phases in the eutectic

Recently, the effect of Ni addition on the corrosion resistance of

Previous studies have unanimously ascribed the lower corrosion 
resistance of Cu- and Ni-containing Al – Si – Mg alloys to the higher elec -
trochemical potential of Ni than that of Al [40,45,46] . For instance, 
microgalvanic coupling occurs between the IMPs and the surrounding Al 
matrix during a corrosion attack; in particular, the IMPs act as cathodes, 
causing the preferential dissolution of the anodic matrix [47 – 49] . 
Therefore, localised corrosion sites are mainly observed in the eutectic 
region during the early stage of corrosion, particularly adjacent to the 
cathodic IMPs [50 – 52] . In previous studies, the microgalvanic coupling 
between the matrix and IMPs has been determined by the electro -
chemical testing of alloy samples [40,45 – 47] . However, the electro -
chemical nature of IMPs strongly depends on their composition. In a 
previous study, we established that, compared to Mn- and Fe-rich IMPs, 
Cu-rich IMPs act as the primary cathode in Al – Si – Mg alloys, thereby 
promoting pitting corrosion within the alloy [53] . Moreover, stress 
localisation tends to occur around coarse IMPs during corrosion, exac -
erbating localised pitting corrosion. Consequently, traditional ap -
proaches for corrosion prevention, such as tailoring the chemical 
composition and heat treatments [54] , may not be effective. Previous 
studies have predominantly focused on the role of Ni-rich IMPs in 
strengthening at high temperatures, but the precise corrosion mecha -
nism remains elusive [40,45 – 47] . To ensure structural integrity and 
prolonged service life in lightweight engineering applications, a 
comprehensive examination of the high-temperature strength and 
corrosion behaviour of Al – Si – Mg – Ni alloys is required. 
Herein, we present a comprehensive analysis of the microstructure, 
high-temperature mechanical properties, and corrosion behaviour of 
Al – Si – Mg – Cu – Ni alloys. The mechanical properties of these alloys were 
assessed using tensile tests conducted at room and elevated tempera -
tures. In addition, the corrosion resistance of the alloys was investigated 
using salt spray tests. Kelvin probe atomic force microscopy (KPAFM) 
was employed to gain insights into the electrochemical nature of the 
IMPs. Furthermore, we conducted detailed analyses of the deformation 
behaviour and localised corrosion of the IMPs by examining their mi -
crostructures using SEM and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) 
measurements.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Effect of IMPs on high-temperature strengthening 
The tensile properties of Al
–
Si
–
Mg
–
Cu
–
Ni alloys are strongly 
influenced by their constituent phases. During tension, the load is 
transferred from the eutectic α -Al matrix to rigid phases such as eutectic 
Si particles and IMPs [62,63] . The interconnectivity and contiguity of 
the eutectic Si particles, along with the size and type of IMPs, play a 
crucial role in dictating the strengthening mechanism of the alloy. Both 
eutectic Si and IMPs strongly hinder dislocation motion during defor -
mation, leading to stress localisation at their respective interphase 
boundaries with the α -Al matrix [64] . Consequently, the alloy tends to 
fracture without significant elongation, exhibiting brittle fracture 
behaviour. Thermodynamic calculations (provided in the Supplemen -
tary Material ) revealed that the volume fractions of Si particles in both 
alloys are similar. The particle circularity analysis shown in Fig. 2 
demonstrates that the interconnectivity of the eutectic Si particles in the 
alloys is also similar. Therefore, the lower stress and elongation 
observed in the Al
–
Si
–
Mg
–
Cu
–
Ni alloy at lower testing temperatures
