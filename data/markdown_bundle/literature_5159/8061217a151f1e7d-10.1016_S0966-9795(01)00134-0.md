Abstract
With good corrosion resistance, reasonable room-temperature ductility, and excellent strength up to temperatures of 700/C14C,
Ni3Si-based alloys show considerable potential for structural applications. The Ni–Si alloys used for acid-corrosion resistance suﬀer
from a dynamic environmental embrittlement when tested at intermediate temperatures around 600/C14C. To assess these Ni–Si alloys
for elevated-temperature structural application, the mechanical properties of these alloys strengthened by Ni3Si precipitates were
systematically evaluated at diﬀerent temperatures in various test environments. Oxygen was identiﬁed as the embrittling species
responsible for the low ductility and premature fracture of the Ni–Si alloys. A strong dependence of elongation and fracture mode
on strain rate was observed. Based on the understanding of the embrittlement mechanism, some unique approaches for improving
the intermediate-temperature ductility, strength and fabricability of Ni–Ni
3Si alloys were identiﬁed: reactive element doping (such
as Zr and Y) to change the grain boundary chemistry; preoxidation to form adherent oxide layers; and thermomechanical proces-
sing to tailor the grain structure/shape. Some other properties such as creep resistance and weldability of these alloys were also
brieﬂy evaluated and are discussed in this paper.# 2002 Published by Elsevier Science Ltd.

1. Introduction
The need for structural materials to be used in corro-
sive environments has renewed the interest in Ni 3Si-
based alloys [1–6]. These alloys oﬀer superior acid
resistance and have the potential to be used as structural
members in industrial chemical systems. Furthermore,
Ni
3Si alloys oﬀer many other attractive physical and
mechanical properties that are common to intermetallic
alloys. These include relatively low density, elevated-
temperature strength, oxidation resistance, and others.
The room temperature ductility of Ni
3Si can be
improved by microalloying with boron via reducing the
susceptibility of the compound to environmental
hydrogen embrittlement [5,7]. One of the major hurdles
for the commercial application of these alloys is the
ductility minimum at intermediate temperatures around
600
/C14C [1,4,8–11]. Oxygen in air has been identiﬁed as
the harmful species responsible for the intermediate
temperature embrittlement. The intermediate tempera-
ture oxygen embrittlement, however, is diﬃcult to elim-
inate in single-phase Ni3Si alloys. Such intermediate
temperature ductility minimum also contributes to the
poor hot fabricability of Ni
3Si-based alloys. While
attempts have been made to reduce/eliminate such
embrittlement, so far no signiﬁcant progress has been
made in this area. Most of the previous studies focus on
either single-phase Ni
3Si alloys [1,8] or two-phase
Ni3Si–Ni alloy with interconnected Ni3Si grains [4,9].
Since the Ni3Si phase is extremely susceptible to the
dynamic oxygen embrittlement, these alloys show very
low ductility at intermediate temperatures. Obviously,
one possible approach to reduce the brittleness is to
introduce a ductile phase matrix, i.e. Ni-base solid
solution with disordered FCC structure into the alloy,
forming a two-phase structure (Ni-Ni
3Si) with Ni3Si
particles distributed throughout the disordered Ni. This
can be accomplished by reducing the Si content in the
Ni–Si alloys, according to binary Ni–Si phase diagram
[12].

2. Experimental procedures
The base alloy used for this investigation has a com-
position of Ni–12.9% Si–4% Cr–1% Mo–0.5% Nb (the
alloy compositions are all in at.% in this paper, unless
noted speciﬁcally), doped with 50 wt ppm B. The Si
content in this alloy is lower than that in the alloys used
in previous studies, and is very close to the maximum
solubility of Si in Ni [12]. Macroalloying with Cr, Mo
and Nb increases the hot workability, corrosion resis-
tance, or the elevated temperature strength of the Ni
3Si-
based alloys [4]. B addition improves the room tem-
perature ductility and eliminates moisture-induced
hydrogen embrittlement at ambient temperatures [5,7].
Other alloys with additional alloying elements such as Y
and Zr, or with increased amount of Nb, were also pre-
pared. All the alloys were arc melted ﬁve times in an
inert argon atmosphere, then drop cast into a water-
cooled copper mold with a thickness of 0.5’’. Sectioned
ingots were then double clad in stainless steel and hot
forged at 1050
/C14C with a reduction of 30%. All the
alloys were then successfully hot rolled to a 0.8-mm
thick sheet without any signs of cracking, indicating the
excellent hot workability of these alloys. The base alloy
was also successfully cold rolled to about 50% reduc-
tion after quenching from 1050
/C14C, indicating the
excellent cold fabricability of this alloy. The Ni–Si
alloys were generally given a ﬁnal heat treatment of
950
/C14C for 3 days to precipitate out Ni3Si particles.
Tensile specimens with a gauge section of 0.8/C23/C213
mm were electro-discharge machined out of the hot
rolled sheets and carefully hand polished to remove
surface imperfections. Selected samples were subjected
to a pre-oxidation treatment as will be described in the
next section. Tensile tests were conducted at tempera-
tures ranging from room-temperature to 1000
/C14Co na
screw-driven Instron testing machine equipped with a
vacuum chamber and an induction heating unit. For the
controlled-environment tests the chamber was ﬁrst
evacuated to a pressure of/C242/C210

with the appropriate gas(es). The test environments
include vacuum (/C242/C210

tests for the base alloy at diﬀerent strain rates were
undertaken to study the eﬀect of strain rate on the
mechanical properties of this alloy in diﬀerent environ-

Ductility in terms of percent elongation to fracture
was calculated by measuring the specimen length before
and after fracture, assuming that all the strain occurred
in the gage section. Yield strengths (YS) were measured
using the 0.2% oﬀset method. Fracture modes were
determined by examining the fracture surfaces in an H-
800 ﬁeld-emission-gun scanning electron microscope
(SEM).

4. Discussion
4.1. Embrittlementmechanism
Two types of environmental embrittlement have been
identiﬁed recently in a number of intermetallic alloys.
The ﬁrst one occurs near room temperature and is
usually associated with hydrogen released from moist-
ure in air, which subsequently penetrates into the inter-
ior of the material and leads to hydrogen embrittlement
of the material accompanied by reduced ductility and
fracture toughness [14–19]. The second type of environ-
mental embrittlement occurs at intermediate tempera-
ture and is associated with oxygen in air, which diﬀuses
into the interior of the material and leads to the so-
called dynamic oxygen embrittlement of the material.
Such intermediate temperature embrittlement not only
occurs in intermetallic alloys such as Ni
3Al [20–23] and
Ni3Si [1,4,8,13], but also in two-phase alloys with a Ni
matrix reinforced by an intermetallic phase [24–26].
The results obtained in this study clearly show that
with the reduction of Si content in the Ni–Ni3Si system,
the two-phase alloys still show severe embrittlement
tested in air as compared to vacuum, even though the
matrix is mainly Ni-based solid solution. The study of
the ductility behavior in various environments indicates

operating embrittlement mechanism. Obviously, this is
not the case for the intermediate temperature embrittle-

Many intermetallic alloys exhibiting oxygen embrit-
tlement show a strong dependence of ductility on strain
rate, with ductility decreasing as the strain rate decrea-
ses when tested in oxygen-containing environments [27].
The sensitivity of the ductility of the Ni–Ni
3Si alloy to
strain rate at 600/C14C further conﬁrms extrinsic nature of
the alloy’s intermediate temperature brittleness. The
fact that the ductility in air increases while the amount
of intergranular fracture decreases as the strain rate
increases suggests that such embrittlement is essentially
controlled by the diﬀusion of oxygen through the grain
boundaries into the alloy interior. Based on such
understanding, a schematic of the embrittlement
mechanism is shown in Fig. 7. The steps involved in the
embrittlement include at least the following:
(1). Surface reaction of H
2Oo rO2 to generate atomic
oxygen;
(2). Oxygen diﬀusion along grain boundaries into the
alloy interior; and
(3). Reduction of grain-boundary strength due to the
presence of oxygen.
Interestingly, such embrittlement only occurs at
intermediate temperatures and low strain rates. If the
temperature is too low, the diﬀusion of oxygen will be
too slow without noticeable oxygen penetration into the
interior, whilst if the temperature is high enough, there
will be quick formation of protective surface oxides
which block the penetration of oxygen into the interior.
In both cases, the oxygen embrittlement will not be
prominent. Also, as the embrittlement process is diﬀu-
sion-controlled and the diﬀusion of oxygen is relatively
slow, such embrittlement only takes place at relatively
low strain rates. If the strain rate increases to certain
critical value (3.3/C210
/C02 s/C01), the embrittlement is com-
pletely eliminated and the ductility in air is essentially
the same as that in vacuum. The fracture surface obser-
vation is consistent with such an embrittlement
mechanism, that is, the depth of intergranular fracture
increases as the strain rate decreases. The decrease of
ductility with increasing strain rate above the critical
value is presumably due to the increase of yield strength
at high strain rates. The yield strength of the base alloy
was not measured at strain rates higher than 3.3/C210
/C02
s/C01, because of the limitation of the data acquisition
system used in this study.
4.2. Mitigationoftheoxygenembrittlement
Silicon content in the Ni–Si alloys is a very important
factor controlling the intermediate temperature ductility
of these alloys. For single-phase Ni
3Si alloys with high
Si content, the ductility in air is essentially zero at
intermediate temperature [1]. For a number of two-
phase Ni–Si alloys with similar level of alloying addi-
tions such as Cr and Mo, the ductility increases as the Si
content decreases. For example, when tested in air at
600
/C14C, an alloy with 18.9 at.% Si has a ductility of 3%
in air [9], while an alloy with 15.9 at.% Si possesses a
ductility of 4% in air [28]. The ductility of the current
base alloy with 12.9 at.% Si almost doubles that of 18.9
at.% Si alloy, presumably due to the elimination/
reduction of interconnected Ni
3Si grains and the corre-
sponding reduction of oxygen embrittlement.

Mechanistic understanding of the intermediate-tem-
perature embrittlement is essential for the exploration of
avenues to eliminate such embrittlement. Since the oxy-
gen diﬀusion along the grain boundary is the rate-con-
trolling step in the embrittlement process, any means to
slow down the oxygen diﬀusion along the grain bound-
ary will increase the ductility of the alloy at intermediate
temperatures and thus reduce the severity of ductility
minimum. The eﬀects of the Zr and Y additions on
increasing the intermediate temperature ductility may
be related to their possible segregation to the grain
boundary, formation of precipitates along the bound-
ary, and/or tie-up with the diﬀusing oxygen, thereby
reducing oxygen diﬀusion along the boundary [29,30]. It
is possible that Zr and Y, which have a strong aﬃnity
for oxygen, trap and pull oxygen away form the grain
boundary regions. Preoxidation will lead to the forma-
tion of protective surface oxide layers which block the
oxygen penetration and thus reduce the embrittlement.
Such pre-oxidation has been shown to be eﬀective in
reducing intermediate-temperature embrittlement in
alloys based on Ni
3Al [13,23,31,32] and Ni3Si [9]. Here,
the key is to form ductile adherent oxide layers during
preoxidation. If the oxide layers are brittle or porous,
oxygen could easily penetrate through them during
testing. The fact that pre-oxidation at 700
/C14C is more
eﬀective than at 850/C14C in improving the tensile ductility
might imply that the oxide formed at 700/C14C is more
dense and ductile than that formed at 850/C14C. Note that
the oxide layer formed at 700 /C14C is thinner than that
formed at 850/C14C and also that a thinner layer is usually
more deformable. Thermomechanical processing (TMP)
is eﬀective in improving the ductility, because of the
formation of elongated grain morphologies and also
possible change in the grain boundary character dis-
tribution. Such grain morphologies reduce the pro-
pensity of grain boundaries perpendicular to the loading
direction, thus eﬀectively reducing oxygen penetration
along the direction perpendicular to the loading axis
[33]. Further eﬀorts on alloy design and processing
modiﬁcation are required to completely eliminate this
environmental embrittlement.

5. Conclusions
The following conclusions could be drawn from this
study.
(1). The Ni–Si alloy with reduced Si content has a
two-phase microstructure with ﬁne Ni 3Si particles
embedded in a Ni matrix. This alloy shows overall
attractive mechanical properties; however, a ductility
minimum at around 600
/C14C was observed when tested in
air.
(2). The ductility minimum at intermediate tempera-
tures is related to dynamic oxygen embrittlement. Oxy-
gen could be released from oxygen-containing species,
such as O
2 and H2O in air. Furthermore, oxygen diﬀu-
sion along grain boundaries is the rate controlling step
in the embrittlement process. Increasing the strain rate
will improve the intermediate temperature ductility of
the alloy by reducing oxygen penetration along the
grain boundaries.
(3). Doping with reactive elements such as Zr and Y
improves the intermediate temperature ductility of the
Ni–Si alloy, possibly through their interaction with dif-
fusing oxygen atoms.
(4). The intermediate temperature ductility of the Ni–
Si alloys can also be signiﬁcantly improved through pre-
oxidation to form protective oxide layer and thermo-
mechanical processing to produce elongated grain
morphologies.
(5). The creep resistance of the Ni–Si alloys can be
eﬀectively improved by alloying addition of Zr and Nb.
Furthermore, the Ni–Ni
3Si alloys possess excellent fab-
ricability and weldability.
