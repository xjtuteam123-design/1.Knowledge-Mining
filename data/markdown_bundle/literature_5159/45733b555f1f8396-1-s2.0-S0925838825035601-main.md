ABSTRACT
Wire-arc directed energy deposition (WA-DED) has provided a promising method for the production of mag -
nesium rare earth (Mg-RE) alloy components, which have vast potential for lightweight structural applications in 
the aerospace industry. Subsequent heat treatment plays an essential role in improving the mechanical properties 
of additively manufactured Mg-RE alloys. In this work, the WA-DED manufactured Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr thin-wall 
components achieved an enhanced strength-ductility synergy by implementing a customized subsequent T6 heat 
treatment. At room temperature (20
◦
C), the T6 specimens exhibited excellent strength-ductility synergy with 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 304.07 MPa, yield strength (YS) of 196.10 MPa, and elongation (EL) of 
15.20 %. The improvement in mechanical properties mainly relied on the synergistic effect of the precipitation 
strengthening mechanism and the grain boundary strengthening mechanism. At high temperature (250
◦
C), the 
T6 specimens exhibit excellent high temperature mechanical properties with an elongation of 27.72 %. 
Numerous deformed grains were observed at the fracture of the high temperature tensile specimens, and the 
synergistic interaction between grain boundary slip and dislocation climb at high temperature contributes to 
performance. The results will provide theoretical guidance for the strength-ductility synergy enhancement of the 
Mg-RE alloy thin-wall parts fabricated by WA-DED.
1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) and its alloys are widely employed in aerospace, 
automotive, and other critical industries due to the remarkable char -
acterizations of lightweight properties, exceptional damping perfor -
mance, and excellent specific strength [1,2] . The magnesium rare earth 
(Mg-RE) alloys exhibit excellent comprehensive performance in terms of 
mechanical properties, heat resistance, and lightweight characteristics 
[3] . However, limited by the poor formability of the magnesium alloy 
[4] , the demands of industrial applications for large-sized, complex- -
structured magnesium alloy components are hardly satisfied by the 
traditional manufacturing processes such as casting, forging, and rolling. 
Therefore, there is an emergent requirement for a novel fabrication 
technique to manufacture Mg-RE alloy structural components.
Additive manufacturing (AM) has the advantages of high material 
utilization, high mechanical automation, and no molds required, which 
has received a considerable amount of attention from the manufacturing 
industry [5 – 7] . Because of the lower sensitivity to equipment, working 
environment, and materials, wire-arc directed energy deposition 
(WA-DED) demonstrates significant potential for the production of 
magnesium alloy structural components. Wang et al. [8] investigated 
the WA-DED process in Mg-Y-RE-Zr alloys, revealing that the micro -
structural homogeneity along the building direction was affected by the 
variations in cooling rates and the repeated thermal cycles. Ma et al. [9]
reported that the Mg-Y-Nd-Zr alloy produced via cold metal transfer 
(CMT) based WA-DED exhibited a microstructure composed entirely of 
equiaxed grains, and the UTS reached 233 MPa at room temperature. Ji 
et al. [10] fabricated Mg-Gd-Al-Zr-Zn alloys by CMT-based WA-DED 
with Al assistance, and the excellent strength-ductility combination was 
achieved. Generally, WA-DED fabricated Mg-RE alloys had satisfactory 
mechanical properties, but the strength and plasticity are capable of 
further enhancement.

improvement are achieved by the addition of heterogeneous nucleating 
elements/particles, the application of plastic deformation and external 
fields during the WA-DED process. Wang et al. [13] found that the 
incorporation of an optimal amount of Al into the WA-DED fabricated 
Mg-4Y-3Nd alloy resulted in the absence of coarse columnar crystals in 
the microstructure, accompanied by enhanced mechanical properties. 
Fang et al. [14] utilized an interlayer hammering technique within the 
WA-DED process to obtain a high-performance AZ31 magnesium alloy, 
where the improvements in strength and plasticity were primarily 
attributed to grain refinement and a notable increase in dislocation 
density. Stirring the molten pool during the WAAM process also acts to 
refine the grains, reduce defects, and optimize properties, such as 
applying pulse currents [15] . Suitable heat treatment is able to eliminate 
the eutectic phases and provides considerable precipitation strength -
ening, resulting in strength enhancement. Guo et al. [16] explored how 
heat treatment processes influence the grains, phases, and mechanical 
characteristics of AZ80M magnesium alloys produced via WA-DED and 
found that the T6 heat treatment significantly improved the alloy ’ s 
mechanical performance. Deng et al. [17] successfully produced a 
Mg-Gd alloy using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technique followed 
by post-processing heat treatment, achieving a high-strength. In sum -
mary, the grain refinement and heat treatment are effective methods to 
obtain enhanced strength-ductility synergy.
For magnesium alloys, studies of the deformation and fracture 
mechanisms are necessary. Shi et al. [18] analyzed the fracture of the 
WAAM fabricated ZM6 alloy, and found that the stresses were more 
likely concentrated at the interface between the phases and the matrix, 
contributing to crack initiation. Under the continuous tensile loading, 
cracks propagated and grains elongated. Wang et al. [19] found that the 
AZ31 magnesium alloy prepared by WAAM process exhibits excellent 
tensile ductility under the synergistic effect of basal < a > slip and 
{10 12} tensile twinning. The tensile twinning in magnesium alloys 
could effectively improve the tensile ductility through the 
twinning-induced slip activity and changes in grain size. Chen et al. [20]
characterized the high-temperature tensile properties of WAAM pre -
pared WE43 alloys, and found that as the tensile temperature increases, 
high-density pyramidal < c + a > dislocations emerged in the grains with 
short length, indicating that the non-basal slip is intensified during the 
high temperature tensile process.
In this work, thin-walled Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr components were produced 
using the WA-DED technique. The strength-ductility synergy was 
enhanced by customized subsequent heat treatment. The T6 specimens 
(with customized subsequent T6 heat treatment) were examined to 
systematically investigate microstructure evolution and the underlying 
mechanisms responsible for enhanced mechanical properties at both 
room and high temperatures. Additionally, the influences of pulse cur -
rent and T6 heat treatment on the enhanced strength-ductility synergy 
were thoroughly explored.
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Wire-arc directed energy deposition and heat treatment
The thin-walled Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloy components were fabricated by 
the WA-DED technique, as shown in Fig. 1 (a). A custom-designed wire 
feeder, a TIG welding unit (YC-350WX5, Panasonic, Japan), and a three- 
axis computerized numerical control workbench were integrated in the 
WA-DED system. The Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys were chosen for both the 
feeding wire (diameter of 3 mm and length of 80 cm) and substrate 
(dimension is 200 mm × 80 mm × 30 mm), and the nominal chemical 
compositions were provided in Table 1 . Before depositing, the oxide 
layer and impurities on the feeding wire and substrate surfaces were 
removed to ensure optimal conditions for the process. The main process 
parameters of WA-DED were consistent with our previous study [21] , 
the pulse current with a frequency of 10 Hz was applied in this work, as 
shown in Fig. 1 (b). The experimental parameters included pulse currents 
of 150/90 A, corresponding voltages of 12/10 V. Additionally, the wire 
feed speed was maintained at 20 mm/s, while the travel speed was set to 
180 mm/min.
In this work, the WA-DED fabricated thin-walled Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloy 
components were subjected to a customized subsequent T6 heat treat -
ment, which were referred to as T6 specimens. The customized param -
eters for the T6 heat treatment were described in the next section. The 
temperature range of solution treatment was determined by the differ -
ential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve, and the heating and cooling 
rates of DSC were 10 ℃ /min. A heat absorption peak was observed at

2.2. Material characterization and mechanical property testing
The metallographic and tensile specimens were taken for material 
characterization and property testing, as shown in Fig. 1 (a). The 
metallographic samples were meticulously polished to achieve a mirror 
surface, followed by etching with a 4 vol% nitric acid alcohol solution 
for a duration of 10 – 25 s. The microstructural and crystallographic 
properties of the T6 specimens were investigated using a combination of 
microscopy techniques. Optical microscopy (OM, Axio Scope A1, ZEISS, 
Germany) was employed for preliminary microstructural assessment, 
while scanning electron microscopy (SEM, VEGA3, TESCAN, Czech 
Republic) provided detailed morphology and element analysis. To 
further elucidate crystallographic information, electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD, JSM-7900F, JEOL, Japan) was utilized. Phase iden -
tification was performed via X-ray diffraction (XRD, Smartlab-9KW, 
Rigaku, Japan) with a scanning range of 20
◦
to 90
◦
. Additionally, 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan) was 
applied to examine the nanoscale features and precipitated phases 
within the specimens. Additionally, energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS) was employed to analyze the elemental distribution within the 
specimens. The nanoscale structural and crystallographic features were 
analyzed using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy 
(HRTEM) alongside selected area electron diffraction (SAED).
The microhardness measurements were performed using a DHV- 
1000Z microhardness tester (Shangcai, China) under a 200 g load and 
a dwell time of 15 s. At room temperature, the tensile testing of the 
specimens was conducted in accordance with the GB/T 228.1 – 2021 
standard, employing a quasistatic loading rate of 2 mm/min. Given the 
extreme operating temperature range of Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys [24,25] , 
the high temperature mechanical properties were measured at 250 ℃ 
with the same test standard. During the high-temperature tensile test, 
the specimens were subjected to a controlled heating process, increasing 
the temperature from 20 ℃ to 250 ℃ with a consistent heating speed of 
10 ℃ /min, and the specimens were maintained at 250 ℃ for a duration 
of 10 min to ensure thermal equilibrium. The tensile specimens were 
extracted in both the traveling direction (TD) and building direction 
(BD) to account for potential anisotropy. To ensure the scientific rigor of 
the experimental data, the average values were calculated for each 
group of tensile specimens. Detailed dimensional specifications of the 
tensile samples are provided in Fig. 1 (d).

3.2. Mechanical properties
The mechanical properties of the T6 specimens under both room 
temperature (20 ℃ ) and high temperatures (250 ℃ ) are illustrated in 
Fig. 4 . At room temperature, the mechanical properties of the T6 spec -
imens exhibited isotropic behavior regardless of the building direction 
(BD) or traveling direction (TD). Specifically, the UTS, YS, and EL for the 
building direction were measured at 308.92 MPa, 204.80 MPa, and 
13.56 %, respectively. Similarly, in the traveling direction, the corre -
sponding values were 304.07 MPa, 196.10 MPa, and 15.20 %, respec -
tively. The WA-DED fabricated Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys decreased in UTS 
and enhanced in ductility, when tested in high-temperature 
environments. At high-temperature (250 ℃ ), the UTS, YS, and EL in the 
building direction were 198.36 MPa, 155.45 MPa, and 27.72 %, 
respectively, and those in the traveling direction were 188.31 MPa, 
149.27 MPa, and 27.27 %, respectively. At room and high temperatures, 
the typical stress-strain curves and the tensile property data for the WA- 
DED fabricated Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys are shown in Figs. 4 (a) and 4 (b).
At room temperature, the comparative analysis of the results of this 
work with those reported in other literatures are illustrated in Fig. 4 (c) 
[6,16,26 – 30] . Firstly, the T6 specimens in this work have better strength 
and plasticity compared to the T6 heat-treated as-cast and 
as-compressed Mg-Nd-Zn-Zn alloys. Furthermore, a superior 
strength-ductility synergy is exhibited in this work, compared to other 
reported Mg-RE alloys fabricated via similar additive manufacturing 
methods. It is commonly observed that other additively manufactured 
Mg-RE alloys typically exhibit superior strength, which is predominantly 
linked to the elevated concentrations of rare earth elements in these 
alloys. However, this excessive rare earth element concentration has 
been found to aggravate the plasticity of Mg alloys. For the customized 
Mg-Al alloys (such as AZ80M and AEX11), the favorable plasticity was 
obtained in this work. The combination of pulse current and customized 
subsequent T6 heat treatment in this work enhanced strength-ductility 
synergy, which exhibited superior performance compared to that of 
the additively manufactured Mg alloys.

properties (at 250 ℃ ) between the alloys investigated in this study and

3.3. Microstructure
Fig. 6 illustrated the typical optical microscopies of the T6 speci -
mens, and they were composed of fine equiaxed grains. As shown in 
Fig. 6 (a), the microstructure of the T6 specimens exhibited distinct 
inner-layer and inter-layer regions, which is attributed to the layer-by- 
layer manufacturing characteristic of the WA-DED process. Grain size 
in the inter-layer region was marginally larger than that of the inner- 
layer region, caused by the remelting effect of the subsequent layer 
deposition. Additionally, a limited number of void defects (highlighted 
by red circles) were identified in the T6 specimens. As shown in Fig. 6

the eutectic phases distributed on the grain boundaries were discon -

4. Discussion
4.1. Microstructure evolution
The schematic representation of microstructural evolution

4.3. Fracture mechanisms in high temperature
When exposed to a high temperature of 250 ℃ , the T6 specimens 
exhibited excellent plasticity, though strength was reduced. The com -
mon characteristic of the mechanical properties for metallic materials is 
that the strength tends to decline as the tensile temperature rises, while 
the plasticity improves due to the recovery softening effect caused by 
thermal activation [49] . During high temperature tensile testing, a va -
riety of deformation mechanisms become active, such as grain boundary 
slip, dislocation climb, etc. [50] Thanks to the combined influence of 
various deformation mechanisms, the brittleness of the alloys reduced 
and the plastic deformation ability improved [51] . It has been indicated 
that diffusion creep controlled by grain boundary is believed to be the 
main deformation mechanism under the conditions of high tempera -
tures and low strain rates [52] . The strengthening effect of the precipi -
tated phases hindering dislocation motion was weakened by grain 
boundary slip, leading to a significant decline in material strength. 
Normally, the smaller grain size generally correlates with an increased 
density of grain boundaries. Therefore, small grain size is believed to 
provide a high contribution to grain boundary slip, resulting in poorer 
strength at high temperature. The schematic illustration of the grain 
boundary slip was shown in Fig. 12 (c).
During the high-temperature tensile process, the γ
1 
and β ′ phases 
exhibited good thermal stability, and the β ′ phases blocked the motion of 
dislocations along the basal plane [53,54] . As a result, extensive 
< a > and < c + a > type dislocation climbs and cross-slips (non-basal 
slips) are activated and moved to coordinate the deformation [55] , as 
shown in Fig. 12 (c). However, the obstruction of β ′ phases to the 
non-basal dislocation slip is limited. Therefore, the enhanced activation 
of multiple slip systems at elevated temperatures and the weakening of 
dislocation-phase interactions also contribute to the softening of the 
alloys.
5. Conclusion
In this study, the Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys were manufactured using WA- 
DED, and the strength-ductility synergy was enhanced by customized 
subsequent heat treatment. A detailed analysis was conducted on the 
mechanical properties (both at room and high temperatures) and the 
microstructure characteristics of the T6 specimens. The microstructure 
evolution and strengthening mechanisms were explored. The main 
conclusions are summarized as follows: 
(1) The optimal solution parameter is holding at 500 ℃ for 10 h, 
which is determined by the eutectic phase percentages and the 
avoidance of abnormally coarse grains. And the peak hardness 
(77.69 HV
0.2
) was observed with subsequent aging at 200 ℃ for 
20 h. Therefore, the optimum T6 heat treatment parameters for 
WA-DED fabricated Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys were determined to be 
solution treatment at 500 ℃ for 10 h and aging at 200 ℃ for 20 h.
(2) The T6 specimens consisted of minimal defects and finely equi -
axed crystals (13.93 μ m) with random grain orientations. During 
the WA-DED process, the melt pools were stirred by pulse current, 
leading to grain refinement and defect reduction. During the heat 
treatment, the intergranular eutectic phases were dissolved, 
while the fine γ
1 
and β’ phases precipitated and were recognized 
as reinforcing phases. The percentage of LAGB in the T6 specimen 
was 9.61 %.
(3) The T6 specimens exhibited excellent strength-ductility synergy 
at room temperature, without significant anisotropy. The yield 
strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elongation of the T6 
specimens achieved 196.10 MPa, 304.07 MPa, and 15.20 %, 
respectively. The strengthening mechanisms of the T6 specimens 
at room temperature were investigated, and the theoretical 
contributed strength of the grain boundary strengthening and 
precipitation strengthening are 56.27 MPa and 47.03 MPa, 
respectively. Furthermore, the strength-ductility synergy of WA- 
DED fabricated Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloys at room temperature was 
also contributed by the fine grains and fewer defects.
(4) The T6 specimens exhibit excellent plasticity at high temperature 
(250
◦
C). The elongations were 27.72 % and 27.27 % in the BD 
and TD, respectively. Correspondingly, the UTS reached 
198.36 MPa and 188.31 MPa in the BD and TD directions, 
respectively. Severe grain deformations were found at the T6 
fractures in the high temperature. The excellent plasticity at high 
temperatures was related to the combined effect of grain 
boundary slip and dislocation climb.
