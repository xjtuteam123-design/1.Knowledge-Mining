Nickel-based, high-temperature alloy GH3536 and high-strength low-alloy
steel HY130 samples were prepared by the laser melting deposition method.
The high-temperature tensile properties of the as-manufactured GH3536 and
the corrosion resistance of the as-manufactured HY130 were highlighted. The
physical properties, mechanical properties, and microstructure of the two
materials under laser additive manufacturing were analyzed and compared.
The effects of different printing parameters on the mechanical and corrosion
properties were also explored. It was found that GH3536 maintained high
toughness in high-temperature tensile tests at 500 /C176C and 815 /C176C, with elon-
gation reaching 74.45% and 60.48%, respectively, and tensile strength
reaching 762 MPa at room temperature. Optical microscopy and SEM showed
that thermal cracks were generated in the GH3536 during the printing pro-
cess, reducing the strength. Combining XRD and EDS analysis demonstrated
that GH3536 samples have a face-centered cubic structure, while HY130 is
mainly a-Fe phase. HY130 has the best corrosion resistance and strength at
low laser power (1000 W). These ﬁndings provide a valuable reference for
further optimization of the properties of both materials in the laser additive
manufacturing process.
INTRODUCTION
With the rapid development of industrial tech-
nology, high-performance metallic materials are
highly demanded in engineering areas such as
aerospace, energy, and automotive manufacturing.
Nickel-based high-temperature alloys (e.g.,
GH3536) and high-strength low-alloy steels (HSLA,
e.g., HY130) are two important metallic materials
widely used in these key ﬁelds. However, these two
materials exhibit some differences in performance
and processing, so comparative studies are needed
to understand their advantages, disadvantages, and
applications. HSLA steels are widely used in the
construction of high-speed railroads, offshore struc-
tures, naval vessels, and submarines due to their
inherent high strength, good low-temperature
impact toughness, and excellent weldability.
1–3 As
a kind of high-strength low-alloy steel, HY130 is
often used in the manufacture of offshore oil
platforms, submarine pipelines, ship structures,
and other marine facilities. As a comparison,
nickel-based high-temperature alloys present supe-
rior oxidation resistance and high-temperature
strength in critical applications. GH3536 is a solid-
solution-strengthened nickel-based high-tempera-
ture alloy with good weldability, high oxidation
resistance, corrosion resistance and excellent
mechanical properties at high temperatures, and
is widely utilized in extremely hot environments
such as aerospace gas turbine thermal compo-
nents.

Laser metal deposition (LMD) belongs to a laser
additive manufacturing method, a process in which
near-net metal parts with complex structures are
fabricated by a layer-by-layer deposition of metal
powders.
7 Different methods, such as direct laser
deposition, laser cladding, and laser solid-state
forming (LSF), have also been used in other stud-
ies.
8–10 Similar to many additive manufacturing
techniques, a variety of alloy powders have been
successfully applied to LMD, such as titanium
alloys, stainless steels, and nickel-based alloys.
11,12
However, it is challenging to apply both GH3536
and HY130 powders in laser additive manufactur-
ing. High-strength alloys such as GH3536 and
HY130 are susceptible to the inﬂuence of thermal
cracking and residual stresses, increasing the risk
of crack formation. These alloys may exhibit differ-
ent melt pool behaviors including variances in melt
pool shape, ﬂuidity, and cooling rates during the
laser melting process, necessitating precise control
of laser heating parameters and scanning paths to
ensure the stability and uniformity. Therefore, the
LMD process needs to be matched with suitable pro-
cess parameters, such as laser power, scan rate, and
powder feeding rate to overcome these challenges.
13
The purpose of this study is to optimize the process
parameters to produce defect-free net ﬁnished parts
and provide data for subsequent applications of the
GH3536 and HY130 alloys.
In recent years, relevant LMD studies have been
reported. Among them, Wang et al.
14 explored the
preparation of HSLA steel by direct LMD, and
derived the optimized parameters to achieve excel-
lent mechanical properties of the constructed sam-
ples. The results show that printed HSLA steel is
mainly composed of a-Fe (ferrite) and a small
amount of C
6Cr23 and Fe 3C phases. Xiao et al. 15
prepared dense and crack-free GH3536 alloy using
the LSF technique and studied the fatigue crack
extension (FCG) and tensile mechanical properties
at room temperature and 500/C176C. They reported that
the parts showed no signiﬁcant individual aniso-
tropy in FCG rates in different directions, but
exhibited higher tensile strength when the tensile
loading was parallel to the deposition direction.
Recently, Min et al.
16 studied the microstructure
evolution of laser power bed fusion-manufactured
GH3536 alloy after 500 h oxidation at 950 /C176C and
discussed the effect of the microstructural diversity
of the fabricated samples on oxidation behavior. Du
et al.
17 reported the use of laser-directed energy
deposition to improve the surface properties by
depositing HSLA onto cast twinning-induced plas-
ticity (TWIP) steel plates. The results demonstrated
that the prepared ultrathin composite functional
gradient material layer occupied only 3% of the total
part thickness. Therefore, the surface-modiﬁed
high-manganese steel exhibited both excellent
mechanical properties of TWIP steel and the good
surface properties of HSLA steel.
According to the above analysis, it has been found
that the additive manufacturing of GH3536 focuses
on the fatigue and high-temperature oxidation
properties. In this paper, we mainly concentrate
on the study of high-temperature tensile properties
under different process parameters. As a naval
steel, the mechanical properties at room tempera-
ture and the corrosion resistance in seawater of
HY130 are important for its applications, which
were also investigated in this study. The correlation
between the two materials regarding process
parameters–microstructure–properties is also
discussed.
EXPERIMENTAL
Materials
Both GH3536 powder and HY130 powder were
provided by Nanjing Mingchang New Material
Technology. The powders were prepared by the
gas atomization method, and the obtained particles
are spherical with a log normal size distribution.
The powder particle size affects the ﬂowability of
the powder during printing, with smaller particle
sizes typically exhibiting better ﬂowability and
reducing powder clogging, ensuring better surface
quality. Larger powder sizes may result in increased
surface roughness of the printed parts and can
impact part density, but they often contribute to
faster deposition rates. Whether the performance of
a speciﬁc powder size is superior or inferior largely
depends on the speciﬁc application requirements,
material characteristics, and process parameters.
The particle size in this study was in the range of
15–53 lm, and the Hall ﬂow rates of the powders
were 20.2 s/50 g (GH3536) and 18.5 s/50 g (HY130),
which could ensure the good printability. Optimiz-
ing the process parameters during printing can
enhance powder utilization and reduce powder
wastage. Subsequently, the recyclability of the
powder can be improved through the design of
powder recovery and ﬁltration systems. The chem-
ical compositions of the GH3536 and HY130 pow-
ders are illustrated in supplementary Tables SI and
SII (refer to online supplementary material),
respectively.
Sample Preparation by LMD Process
The deposition process of the GH3536 alloy and
HY130 steel was carried out using a LM60 mode
machine with a cross-ﬂow high-voltage DC yttrium-
doped solid-state laser. The laser had a wavelength
of 1070 nm, and a laser spot diameter of about
1.8 mm, as well as a maximum output power of 5
KW. High-purity argon gas was used as the shield-
ing gas during printing. 45# steel was employed as
the substrate for the processing. The deposition
process is shown in supplementary Fig. S1, which
reveals that two back-and-forth scans were repeated
layer by layer from the substrate to generate the

Microstructural Characterization
and Mechanical Properties
The deposited pieces were further manufactured
using a wire-electrode cutter to prepare the samples
for microstructure characterization and tensile test-
ing. To make the samples for microstructure char-
acterization, the samples were encapsulated by
epoxy resin, followed by grinding using 240-, 400-,
800-, 1200-, and 2000-grit sandpaper, and polishing
using a diamond polishing solution with a particle
size of 2.5 lm. After polishing, the GH3536 and
HY130 samples were etched using aqua regia and
4% HNO
3 solutions for 1 s and 7–8 s, respectively.
Optical microscopy (MIT500) and scanning electron
microscopy (SEM; Axia ChemisSEM HiVac) were
applied to reveal the microstructural features.
Physical phase identiﬁcation and crystal structure
analysis were performed by an X-ray diffractometer
(XRD; Bruker D8 Advance) equipped with a Cu-Ka
radiation source with a ray scanning angle range of
20/C176–100/C176and a speed of 10 /C176/min. In addition, SEM-
EDX (energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) was
used to check the chemical composition of speciﬁc
regions of the samples.
The tensile test samples were designed according
to the GB/T228.1 standard and the dimensions are
shown in supplementary Fig. S2. The longitudinal
sections (Y–Z) of the printed samples were cut and
processed by wire cutting. The tensile properties of
the cut samples were measured by a high-temper-
ature universal tensile machine at a rate of 0.3 mm/
min. Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and
elongation were measured at room temperature
(25/C176C) and at different high temperatures (500 /C176C
and 815 /C176C). After the tensile test, SEM for cross-
sectional observation was employed to analyze the
fracture mechanism to explain the different
mechanical properties.
Electrochemical Measurements
Electrochemical experiments were performed
using an electrochemical workstation (CORRT-
EST-CS350H) containing a conventional three-elec-
trode system. Samples with a surface area of
approximately 1 9 1c m
2 were exposed to 3.5 wt.%
NaCl solution after being encapsulated in epoxy
resin. For the three-electrode system, a platinum
sheet and saturated calomel were applied as the
counter electrode and reference electrode, respec-
tively, and the samples were used as the working
electrode, which was made to be parallel with the
counter and reference electrodes. The corrosion
properties of the HY130 material were evaluated
by potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemi-
cal impedance (EIS). To ensure stable potentials, an
open-circuit test was ﬁrst performed for 1800 s.
Polarization and EIS data were ﬁtted by CS Studio
software.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Microstructures
The microstructures of GH3536 and HY130 parts
after LMD printing are shown in Fig. 1. As depicted
in Fig. 1a and c, the samples have obvious layered
boundaries and the width between the deposited
layers is about 250–400 lm, while the remelting
zone between the layers ranges from 30 to around
50 lm. It can be seen that the printed GH3536 has
higher porosity, which is probably due to the uneven
melt pool temperature induced by the high laser
power. In contrast, HY130 has a better fusion state
with only minor porosity. GH3536 tends to develop
thermal cracks during rapid heating and cooling
processes, consequently affecting porosity. GH3536
has a higher melting point, requiring higher tem-
peratures for melting during LMD. The elevated
melting point can make it challenging for the
formation and diffusion of the melt pool, thereby
increasing porosity. On the other hand, HY130
powder has a lower melting point, resulting in a
relatively smaller melt pool, facilitating better pow-
der fusion and reducing porosity. From Fig. 1a and
b, it can be seen that GH3536 has mainly slate-like
and ﬁsh-scale grains, and the grain size is in the
range of 10–20 lm. In contrast, HY130 has much
ﬁner grains, with an average size of 10 lm (Fig. 1d).
Figure 1c indicates that B5 has coarse and ﬁne
equiaxed grains of different sizes, ranging from 15
to 25 lm, and 5 to 10 lm, respectively. The appear-
ance of grains of different sizes is mainly due to the
changes in temperature gradient.
18 The tempera-
ture gradient variation is primarily caused by the
latent heat of phase transition during the melting
process and rapid heating and cooling. The uneven
stacking of layers also affects the material’s laser
absorption efﬁciency, thereby inﬂuencing the tem-
perature gradient. The grains of both prints mostly
grow in the deposition direction, but the grain
orientation is multi-directional due to the feature of
single-wall printing that allows for more broad
thermal directions. An equiaxed dendritic structure
can be found in Fig. 1d, with a uniform and ﬁne
microstructure.

This comprehensive study investigated the laser

HY130 high-strength low-alloy steel, focusing on
analyzing the microstructure, physical properties,
mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance of
the two materials after the laser additive manufac-
turing process.

(1) The microstructure analysis revealed that
GH3536 exhibited slate-like and ﬁsh-scale
grains, while HY130 had ﬁner equiaxed
grains. GH3536 presented more porosity and
thermal cracks, which reduced its strength.
XRD analysis conﬁrmed that GH3536 had an
FCC structure, while HY130 had a BCC
structure. EDS analysis showed the composi-
tion of the materials, with GH3536 containing
nickel, chromium, and other elements, and
HY130 consisting of iron and other alloying
elements.
(2) The temperature-dependent tensile properties
were evaluated, and it was found that GH3536
maintained high toughness at elevated tem-
peratures. The elongation reached 74.45% and
60.48% at 500/C176C and 815/C176C, respectively, with
a tensile strength of 762 MPa at room tem-
perature. The fracture surfaces of GH3536
exhibited ductile characteristics with dimples
and tear ridges. However, thermal cracks
were observed, which could affect the mate-
rial’s strength.
(3) For HY130, the inﬂuence of laser power and
scanning speed on the tensile properties were
evaluated. The optimal parameters for achiev-
ing both good strength and ductility were
determined as a laser power of 1000 W and a
scanning speed of 300 mm/min. The fracture
surfaces of HY130 showed slight ductile fail-
ure with dimples and the presence of ﬁne
carbide particles. Corrosion performance eval-
uation in a 3.5% NaCl solution revealed that
the corrosion resistance of HY130 varied with
laser power. The sample processed with a
laser power of 1000 W showed the best corro-
sion resistance.
