ABSTRACT 
Conventional dual-phase titanium (Ti) alloy exhibits high strength and toughness, but the high elastic modulus 
makes it unsuitable for biomedical implants. In this study, two novel metastable dual-phase ( α " + β ) Ti – Nb – Sn – 
(Zr/Fe) systems have been developed using [Mo]
eq 
theory. The dual-phase α " + β structure contributed to the 
alloys ’ characteristics of high strength and low elastic modulus. The bending strengths of Ti – (16 22)Nb – 8Sn – 
(5 8)Zr and Ti – (17 19)Nb – 8Sn – (0.5 – 1.5)Fe alloys (1023 – 1190 MPa and 960 – 1308 MPa, respectively) were 
obviously higher than that of Ti – 25 Nb – 8Sn (962 MPa). Among them, both Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe with dual phase had lower elastic moduli (50.4 and 50.5 GPa, respectively) than 
Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (52 GPa), which can avoid the stress shielding effect after implantation. Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe alloys exhibited greater bending strength-to-modulus ratios (20.3 and 19.0, respectively) 
than those of c.p. Ti (7.1) and Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (18.5). Both dual-phase Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe 
with low elastic moduli and high strength-to-modulus ratios are promising candidates for bio-implant 
applications. 
1. Introduction 
Pure titanium (Ti) and Ti alloys are widely used in the field of 
biomedicine due to their good biocompatibility, excellent corrosion 
resistance, and high specific strength [1 – 6] . The most commonly used 
commercial Ti alloy is Ti – 6Al – 4V, which has outstanding mechanical 
strength and corrosion resistance [7] . However, Ti – 6Al – 4V contains 
aluminum (Al) and vanadium (V) that are harmful to the human body 
[8,9] . Furthermore, the Ti – 6Al – 4V has a far higher elastic modulus (110 
GPa) than human bone ( ≤ 30 GPa), which can cause stress shielding 
after implantation [10 – 12] . In order to avoid the phenomenon, many 
β -type Ti alloys with low elastic modulus have been developed in recent 
years [13 – 17] . Some of the low-modulus Ti alloys have been included in 
ASTM certification, such as Ti – 12Mo – 6Zr – 2Fe [18] , Ti – 13Nb – 13Zr 
[19] , and Ti – 15Mo [20] . However, the elastic moduli of the 
above-mentioned β -type Ti alloys were still much higher than that of 
human bone. Therefore, there is a need to develop new Ti alloys through 
suitable alloy design approaches [21,22] . 
In order to design new types of Ti alloy system with low elastic 
modulus, theoretical calculations of phase stability has been adopted by 
many researchers to adjust the compositions of Ti alloys to obtain 
metastable β phase, such as Ti – 24Nb – 4Zr – 7.9Sn [23] , 
Ti – 29Nb – 13Ta – 4.6Zr [24] , and Ti – 5Mo – Fe – 3Sn [25] . Molybdenum 
equivalent theory ([Mo]
eq
) is widely used in the calculations of phase 
stability in the Ti alloy systems [26 – 28] . [Mo]
eq 
is an empirical formula 
summarized through experiments, which quantifies the contribution of 
stability for each alloying element in β -type Ti alloys [29] . When the 
[Mo]
eq 
of a Ti alloy is greater than 13 wt%, the structure of the Ti alloy 
would form a single β phase. The formula of [Mo]
eq 
is shown as follows 
[29] : [Mo]
eq 
= 1.0 Mo + 23.24 Be + 3.81Co + 1.59 Cr + 0.97 Cu + 4.03 
Fe + 0.02 Hf + 2.42 Mn + 0.55 Nb + 7.58 Ni + 1.05 Os + 0.71 Pd + 0.39 
Pt + 0.91Re + 1.42 Rh + 1.39 Ru + 0.79 Si + 0.002 Sn + 0.29 Ta + 1.0 
V + 0.3 W + 0.27 Zr – 1.0 Al (wt%). 
A metastable β -type Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn alloy was developed by Hsu et al. 
[30] . Although the Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn exhibited a very low elastic modulus of 
52 GPa, its bending strength-to-elastic modulus ratio ( × 1000) was 
relatively low (18.5). Both high strength and low modulus of elasticity 
are often required for an ideal implant alloy. Also, Ti alloys with high

can be replaced by the common low cost, low melting point, and

exhibited the lowest elastic modulus. However, its elastic modulus was 
still higher than 50 GPa, which may be due to excessive Mo with high 
elastic modulus or the difference in the atomic sizes of the base element 
and the alloying elements. Therefore, it is worth investigating the use of 
other alloying elements instead of Mo to develop Ti alloys with lower 
elastic modulus. 
In this study, quaternary Ti – xNb – 8Sn – y(Zr/Fe) alloys were designed 
by modifying the compositions of Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn and adding a fourth 
alloying element Zr or Fe to replace part of Nb. Partial replacement of Nb 
with Zr or Fe elements can reduce the cost of the original ternary alloy 
with high Nb content, and the mechanical strength of the alloy can also 
be improved by solid solution strengthening. Furthermore, the [Mo]
eq 
theory was used to adjust the compositions of Ti – xNb – 8Sn – y(Zr/Fe) 
alloys in order to obtain low modulus. Thus, the alloys are expected to 
exhibit high strength-to-modulus ratios due to the contributions of solid 
solution strengthening and metastability design of alloys. The micro -
structures and mechanical properties of quaternary Ti – xNb – 8Sn – y(Zr/ 
Fe) alloys were studied and compared with Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn. 
2. Experimental procedures 
The alloys used for this study included Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn, 
Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr, Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr, Ti – 20Nb – 8Sn – 6Zr, 
Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1.5Fe, 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe, Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe. All the alloys were prepared 
from Ti (99.7% pure), Nb (99.95% pure), Sn (99.95% pure), Zr (99.9% 
pure), and Fe (99.9% pure) by using an arc-melting and casting system 
(A-028, DAWN SHINE, Taiwan). Prior to casting, the ingots were re- 
melted six times for chemical homogenization according to a previ -
ously described method [30] . The [Mo]
eq 
values of each alloy developed 
in this study are shown in Table 1 . 
Microstructures of the alloys were examined by using an optical 
microscope (OM; BH2, Olympus, Japan). Phase analyses of the alloys 
were evaluated using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD-6000, Shimadzu, 
Japan) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. The scanning range was at 30 – 80
◦
(2 θ ) with a step size of 0.02
◦
and a scan speed of 4
◦
/min. The volume 
percentages of the various phases in the alloys were determined using 
OriginPro 9.0 (OriginLab Corp., USA) from the integrated areas of 
diffraction peaks in the XRD results [35 – 37] . The actual chemical 
compositions of the as-cast alloys were measured by scanning electron 
microscopy (6330, JEOL, Japan) / energy dispersive spectroscopy 
(SEM/EDS). The nominal and actual chemical compositions of the alloys 
determined by EDS/SEM are shown in Table 2 . The mechanical prop -
erties of the alloys were measured using a three-point bending test 
(HT-2102AP, Hung-Ta Instrument, Taiwan) according to ASTM: E855, 
and the details were described in the previous work [30] . The dimension 
of bending test samples was 30 × 5 × 1 mm. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. phase and microstructure 
The XRD patterns of as-cast Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys 
before and after the three-point bending tests are shown in Fig. 1 . Before 
bending tests, Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe, 
Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1.5Fe, and Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe presented a single β phase, 
while Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr, Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr, Ti – 20Nb – 8Sn – 6Zr, and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe exhibited α " + β dual-phase structure. After bending 
tests, the four single β -phase Ti alloys still maintained their original 
phase. However, the β -phase peak intensities of the four dual-phase Ti 
alloys were decreased significantly, although these alloys still exhibited 
α " + β structure. The results indicated that lowering the [Mo]
eq 
values 
could effectively decrease the stability of the β phase in the Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr 
and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe systems. Furthermore, stress-induced phase trans -
formation, β→ α ", during bending test can be found in the Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr 
and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys when the [Mo]
eq 
values of the alloys were lower 
than 13 wt%. As the concentration of β -stabilizing elements in Ti alloys 
increases, it is difficult to form α " phases during quenching [38,39] . 
Table 3 presents the phase volume fractions of Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and 
Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys before and after the bending tests. Before the 
bending tests, the volume fraction of α " phase in Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr was 
as high as 85%. As the Nb content increased in the Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr system, 
the volume fraction of α " phase decreased. On the other hand, the vol -
ume percentage of α " phase decreased by increasing Fe content in the 
Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloy system. Since both Nb and Fe are β -stabilizing ele -
ments, increasing their contents in the alloys can result in the formation 
of a stable β phase, which reduces the amount of α " phase. After the 
bending tests, greater volume fractions of α " phase were found in all the 
dual-phase alloys, which was related to the stress-induced β→ α " 
martensitic transformation [40] . 
The OM images of Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys are pre -
sented in Fig. 2 . The two Ti alloy systems exhibited a typical as-cast 
dendritic microstructure. The equiaxed β grain boundaries were found 
in the OM images of the single β -Ti alloys (Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr, 
Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1.5Fe, and Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe).

3.2. Mechanical properties 
3.2.1. Three-point bending test 
The stress-deflection curves of Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn, Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr, and 
Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys after the three-point bending tests are presented in 
Fig. 3 . After the bending tests, all Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys 
did not fracture (the deflection was set as 8 mm). Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr alloys 
(1023 – 1190 MPa) exhibited significantly greater bending strengths than 
Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (962 MPa)( Fig. 4 ). Likewise, the Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe system also 
showed high bending strengths (960 – 1308 MPa). Notably, the bending 
strengths of the two quaternary Ti alloy systems in this study were 
highly affected by their phase structures. Among Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr system, 
single β -phase Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr had the highest bending strength 
(1190 MPa). Similarly, in the Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe system, single β -phase 
Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1.5Fe also had the highest bending strength (1308 MPa). 
The above results were in line with the previous research on Ti – Mo [41] 
and Ti – Nb – Mo [42] systems. The bending strengths of Ti – 10Mo 
(1750 MPa) and Ti – 5Nb – 15Mo (1802 MPa) with a single β phase were 
higher than those of Ti – 9Mo (1550 MPa) and Ti – 5Nb – 7.5Mo 
(1687 MPa) with α " + β phase, which is attributed to a stronger solid 
solution effect of the single β phase with greater alloying contents [41] . 
Among the dual-phase ( α " + β ) Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr alloys, 
Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr has the highest bending strength (1078 MPa), which 
was attributed to the higher Zr contents in the alloy. Zr has a larger 
atomic radius than Ti, Nb, and Sn. Adding more Zr can increase the 
lattice distortion caused by the differences in the atomic radii of the 
alloying elements, thereby increasing the bending strength of the alloy 
[43] . 
The elastic moduli of Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn, Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr, and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe 
alloys are shown in Fig. 5 . The Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys 
(50.4 – 60.9 and 50.5 – 67.7 GPa, respectively) had significantly lower 
elastic moduli than c.p. Ti (99 GPa) and close to Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (52 GPa). 
Among them, both Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe with 
α " + β dual-phase structure exhibited a lower elastic modulus (50.4 and 
50.5 GPa, respectively) than Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (52 GPa). 
As shown in Fig. 3 , the stress-deflection curves of α " + β dual-phase Ti 
alloys (Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr, Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr, Ti – 20Nb – 8Sn – 6Zr, and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe) exhibited double yielding phenomena, which was 
a characteristic of stress-induced β→ α " martensitic transformation [40, 
44] . The first yield point is also called the triggering stress, and the 
second yield point is the stress at the end of the stress-induced phase 
transformation [45] . Furthermore, the first yield point is related to the 
reorientation of the α ", and the second yield point is associated with the 
plastic deformation ability of the reoriented α " [46] . According to the 
result of XRD analysis ( Table 3 ), the volume fractions of α " phase in the 
four α " + β dual-phase Ti alloys significantly increased after the bending 
tests. The stress-induced α " phase generated during the bending test can 
further reduce the elastic modulus of the alloy [47,48] . However, the 
volume fraction of α " in the alloy is just one of the factors that may affect 
the elastic modulus. Haghighi et al. [39] reported that Ti – 9Fe – 2Ta alloy 
with a high volume fraction of α " had a higher elastic modulus than the 
other Ti – Fe – Ta alloys, which was due to the fact that the alloy contained 
less Fe and Ta. In the present study, although Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr had the 
highest volume fraction of α ", it exhibited a higher elastic modulus, 
which may be related to its lowest Nb content. Additionally, among the 
above four α " + β dual-phase Ti alloys, the double yielding phenomenon 
of Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr was not obvious, which may be due to its smaller 
β -phase volume fraction (15%) before the bending test. 
The segregation phenomena in as-cast Ti – Nb – Sn – (Zr/Fe) alloys were 
significantly observed by OM images ( Fig. 2 ). The element segregation 
may inhibit the formation of the stress-induced martensitic α " phase, 
thereby deteriorating the mechanical properties of the alloys. Tong et al. 
[49] stated that the hardness, strength, and ductility of an alloy are 
affected by the element segregation. Therefore, the mechanical perfor -
mance of an alloy can be improved by chemical and microstructural 
homogeneity, which can be achieved through solution treatment, ul -
trasonic treatment [49] or microwave heating [50] .

3.2.2. Bending strength-to-elastic modulus ratio 
The bending strength-to-elastic modulus ratios ( × 1000)(elastic ad -
missible strain) of c.p. Ti, Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn, Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr, and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe 
alloys are shown in Fig. 6 . The ratios of Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe 
alloys (17.7 – 20.7 and 19.0 – 20.1, respectively) were significantly higher 
than that of c.p. Ti (7.1). Except for Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr, all Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr 
and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys exhibited higher strength-to-modulus ratios 
than Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (18.5). Furthermore, though Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr had 
the highest strength-to-modulus ratio (20.7), its elastic modulus 
(57.6 GPa) was slightly higher than that of Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn (52 GPa). 
Therefore, Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe with low elastic 
moduli (50.4 and 50.5 GPa, respectively) and high strength-to-modulus 
ratios (20.3 and 19.0, respectively) are the suitable candidates for bio- 
implant applications. 
4. Conclusions 
Based on the Ti – 25Nb – 8Sn alloy developed by previous research, 
this work prepared Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloy systems by 
adding Zr or Fe elements and using the [Mo]
eq 
theory. 
Ti – 22Nb – 8Sn – 5Zr, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe, Ti – 17Nb – 8Sn – 1.5Fe, and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 1Fe presented a single β phase, because they contained 
sufficient β -stabilizing elements ([Mo]
eq 
> 13 wt%). Additionally, 
Ti – 16Nb – 8Sn – 8Zr, Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr, Ti – 20Nb – 8Sn – 6Zr, and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe ([Mo]
eq 
< 13 wt%) demonstrated a dual-phase 
( α " + β ) structure. The equiaxed β grain boundaries were observed in 
the β -phase Ti alloys, and needle-like α " grains appeared in the 
( α " + β )-phase Ti alloys. The bending strength-to-elastic modulus ratios of 
Ti – Nb – Sn – Zr and Ti – Nb – Sn – Fe alloys (17.7 – 20.7 and 19.0 – 20.1, 
respectively) were significantly higher than that of c.p. Ti (7.1). Among 
the quaternary Ti alloys, dual-phase Ti – 18Nb – 8Sn – 7Zr and 
Ti – 19Nb – 8Sn – 0.5Fe with low elastic moduli (50.4 and 50.5 GPa,
