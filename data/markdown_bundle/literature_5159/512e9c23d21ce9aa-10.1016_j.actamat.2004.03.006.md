Abstract
The composition dependence of the microstructure and the mechanical properties of nano/ultraﬁne-structured Ti–Cu–Ni–Sn–Nb
alloys were investigated. At 16.8 at.% Cu + 14.4 at.% Ni and 8 at.% Nb + 4.8 at.% Sn, the alloy contains about 20 vol% dendritic bcc-
b-Ti solid solution and a nanostructured matrix with an average grain size of about 50 nm. The nanostructured matrix consists of a
hcp-a-Ti solid solution and Ti 2Cu and Ti 2Ni intermetallics. The alloy exhibits 1313 MPa yield strength and 8.9% plastic strain. The
intermetallics in the matrix lead to intrinsic brittleness and result in a brittle fracture surface. With decreasing Cu + Ni content and
increasing Nb + Sn content, the volume fraction of the dendrites signiﬁcantly increases and the grain size of the matrix becomes
coarser. As a result, the yield strength decreases and the plastic strain increases. For 14 at.% Cu + 12 at.% Ni and 10 at.% Nb + 4
at.% Sn, the matrix forms an eutectic structure with very few intermetallics; the alloy exhibits 1052 MPa yield strength and 21.1%
plastic strain. The alloys with few or without intermetallics exhibit a typical shear fracture mode with obvious viscous ﬂow on the
fracture surface.

1. Introduction
Ti alloys have been widely used in many ﬁelds due to
their very high speciﬁc strength, excellent corrosion
resistance and good workability [1]. As Ti Alloys are
important structural materials, it is the goal of investi-
gators to further improve their mechanical properties in
order to facilitate new applications. For example, the
realization of a high durability (superior fatigue
strength) and a low modulus leads to surgical applica-
tions of Ti alloys as implant material [2,3]. In order to
achieve improved or novel properties, some advanced or
new alloy design methods or fabrication facilities should
be applied. In recent years, bulk metallic glassy alloys
have been developed [4–6]. The progress in the design of
bulk metallic glasses has also led to progress in the de-
velopment of new in situ formed nanostructured alloys,
which may exhibit even more advanced and superior
properties than bulk metallic glasses and/or traditional
Ti alloys [7–9]. Considering industrial and biomedical
applications, we have focused on the development of
new nanostructured Ti alloys and succeeded in ﬁnding
compositions with superior mechanical properties
[10,11]. Among these compositions, the combination of
Ti–TM and Ti–RM elements (TM ¼ transition metals
and RM ¼ refractory metals) has been validated to be
essential for the in situ formation of the nanostructure
and the strengthening dendritic phase. In order to gain a
better understanding of how the constituents in the al-

2. Experimental
According to the binary phase diagrams [12], Ti–RM
alloys can form b-Ti solid solutions, which have a high
liquidus temperature and can primarily solidify into
dendritic morphologies during solidiﬁcation of the li-
quid. On the other hand, the combination of Ti–Cu–Ni–
Sn leads to the in situ formation of a nanostructure in
the alloys [7]. The quinary Ti–Cu–Ni–Sn–Nb alloys in-
vestigated were thus designed based on the above un-
derstanding. The alloys A–C listed in Table 1 were
chosen to reveal the eﬀect of Cu + Ni and Nb contents
on the formation of the nanostructured matrix phase(s)
and the dendritic b-phase. The composition formulation
is (Ti
40Cu28Ni24Sn8)1/C0 x(Ti80Nb20)x (in at.%). Ti 40Cu28-
Ni24Sn8 represents the nanostructured matrix in the al-

A–C in Table 1 correspond to x ¼ 0:4, 0.5 and 0.6, re-

microstructure and the mechanical properties of the al-
loy with much higher content of the b-phase formers Nb
and Sn.
The alloys were prepared by arc melting the pure
metals (99.99%) on a water-cooled copper hearth under
an argon atmosphere. For obtaining homogeneous
samples (in both composition and microstructure), the
intermediate alloys Ti
40Cu28Ni24Sn8 and Ti 80Nb20 were
prepared by arc melting separately. Then, the ﬁnal
samples were prepared by arc melting the mixture of the
two intermediate alloys. For further improving the ho-
mogeneity of the samples, the arc melted ingots were
reshaped into about 60 mm /C2 15 mm /C2 12 mm bars by
arc melting the ingots in a copper mold with a bar-shape
cavity. The ﬁnal bar-shape samples are expected to have
a homogeneous microstructure and composition along
the axial direction of the bars. The samples for the
mechanical test and the specimens for the microstruc-
ture observations and the X-ray diﬀraction (XRD)
analysis were cut from the vicinal places in the bars.
A Siemens D 5000 XRD facility with Co K a radiation
(k ¼ 0:179 nm) was used to analyze the phases of the
alloys. The phases and the microstructures were also
characterized by using a JEOL-JSM6400 scanning
electron microscope (SEM) with an electron microprobe
analysis unit and a JEOL-2000FX transmission electron
microscope (TEM) coupled with energy-dispersive
X-ray analysis (EDX). The volume fraction of the den-
dritic phases in the alloys was evaluated directly from
the SEM images. The grain size of the matrix phases was
determined from the TEM images.
The samples for the mechanical tests were machined
into 3 mm /C2 3m m /C2 6 mm pieces. The deformation
behavior and the mechanical properties were investi-
gated by using an Instron 8562 testing machine at a
compression strain rate of 1 /C2 10
/C0 4/s at room tempera-
ture. In order to avoid test-system errors, the tested
stress–strain curves were calibrated by subtracting the
strain of the test-system, which was evaluated by going
through the compressive test process with the same
strain rate without a sample. The values of strength and
plastic strain were determined directly from the cali-
brated stress–strain curves. The yield point was deter-
mined at 0.2% plastic strain. The strain at the yield point
corresponds to the elastic strain and the strain at the
break point corresponds to the total strain. The plastic
strain is determined by subtracting the elastic strain
from the total strain. The deformation and fracture
mechanisms were investigated by SEM.

3.2. Mechanical behavior
The four alloys exhibit typical initial elastic defor-
mation, yielding followed by plastic deformation with
signiﬁcant work-hardening and ﬁnally fracture under
compressive loading, as shown in Fig. 6. From alloy D
to A, the yield strength and the work-hardening become
stronger, while the plastic strain becomes smaller. Alloy
A exhibits the highest yield strength of 1313 MPa but
the smallest plastic strain of about 8.9%. The alloys B
and C exhibit yield strengths of 1052 and 970 MPa and
plastic strains of 21% and 24%, respectively. Alloy D has
a yield strength of 1024 MPa and a plastic strain of
about 30%. Apparently, the transition from the elastic
to the plastic deformation regime is signiﬁcantly diﬀer-
ent for these alloys. Alloy D exhibits a rather sharp
transition in the stress–strain curve (Fig. 6), but when
going from alloy D to A, the transition becomes
smeared out until almost no distinct elastic–plastic
transition point can be located for alloy A.

4. Discussion
4.1. Eﬀect of composition on the microstructure and the
mechanical behavior
The dendrites of the investigated alloys reveal three
diﬀerent morphologies, which show a signiﬁcant de-
pendence on the actual composition of the alloys. The
EDX analysis (Table 2) conﬁrms that the content of Nb
in the dendrites of the four alloys is very similar. But, the
content of Sn in the dendrites of alloy A is about 2 times
that of alloy D, 2.5 times that of alloy B and 3 times that
of alloy C. In addition, the contents of Cu and Ni in the
dendrites sequentially increase in turn for the alloys A,
D, C and B. These ﬁndings indicate that the signiﬁcant
diﬀerences in the morphology of the dendrites are due to
the combined eﬀect of Sn and Cu + Ni. It is well known
that Nb, which has a very similar metallic radius as Ti
(r ¼ 0:147 nm), is a very good b-phase former in Ti al-
loys [16]. Sn with a simple cubic structure can also im-
prove the formation of the b-phase [16]. Its metallic
radius is 0.162 nm, which is about 10% larger than the
metallic radii of Ti and Nb. Cu and Ni with fcc structure
are a-phase formers in Ti alloys [16] and have metallic
radii of 0.128 and 0.125 nm, which are about 13% and
15% smaller than the radii of Ti and Nb, respectively.
Therefore, Cu and Ni act conversely compared to Sn in
the dendrites. This can somewhat counteract the eﬀect
of Sn. For example, the Sn content in the dendrites of
alloy A is far higher than the Cu + Ni content, i.e., the
eﬀect of Sn is dominant. This leads to a lattice expansion
of the b-phase and induces an epitaxial dendritic

are b-phase formers. The higher the Nb + Sn content,
the larger is the volume fraction of the dendrites. Con-
versely, the higher the Cu + Ni content, the smaller is the
volume fraction of the dendrites. The composition de-
pendence of the volume fraction of the dendrites is
plotted in Fig. 12(a). Besides the dendritic phase, the
matrix is also signiﬁcantly aﬀected by the changes in the
overall alloy composition. When the content of Cu + Ni
is changed from high to low values (alloys A–D), then
the matrix in turn forms a + b + intermetallics, eutectic
a + b + few intermetallics, and a + b phases, as shown in
Fig. 2. Apparently, higher Cu + Ni contents induce the
formation of intermetallic compounds. The eutectic
microstructure depends on the appropriate composition
balance between Cu, Ni and Ti. Alloy B is located just at
the eutectic composition. In addition, the grain size of
the matrix also depends on the composition. The rela-
tionship between the average grain size and the com-
position is plotted in Fig. 12(a). Due to the complex
microstructure of the matrix, it is impossible to deter-
mine the volume fraction and the size of each phase
accurately. Only the average grain size can be estimated
from the TEM images. The experiments reveal that the
multicomponent composition and the complex phases
are necessary for the formation of the nano/ultraﬁne-
structured matrix. As mentioned in the above para-
graph, there is a diﬀerence of more than 10% between
the metallic radii of Cu, Ni, Ti, Nb, and Sn. In multi-
component systems such as the present one, the liquid
has a highly dense-random packed structure [17], which
is not feasible for long-range atomic diﬀusion of the
constituents. This leads to the formation of the observed
nano- and ultraﬁne-structure in the solidiﬁed matrix.
Usually, multicomponent systems tend to develop mul-
tiple phases that are favorable for the formation of a
nano/ultraﬁne-structure upon solidiﬁcation given that

4.2. Fracture mechanisms
According to the observations of the fracture sur-
faces, the fracture mechanism of the alloys B–D is very
similar to other Ti-base dendrite-nanostructured com-
posites [14]. The nanostructured matrix undergoes a
shear banding-local softening course, while the dendrites
experience strain-hardening and act as obstacles to the
local shear deformation, thus inhibiting the shear frac-
ture of the alloy. As a result, the material exhibits high
strength and large plastic strain. Such a failure mecha-
nism often goes along with obvious viscous shear ﬂow
traces on the fracture surface (Figs. 8(d), 9(c) and (d),
and 10(b)). Alloy A, however, fails through a diﬀerent
mechanism. The matrix, which contains a large volume
fraction of intermetallics, exhibits essentially brittle
fracture features. Such brittleness induced by intermet-
allics diﬀers completely from the brittle features of other
nanostructured metals/alloys [18–20] and the bulk me-
tallic glasses [15,21–23]. The latter two kinds of mate-
rials exhibit brittleness under loading because of the lack
of dislocations and work-hardening in the nanostructure
or the glassy structure, which leads to local shear
banding [15,19–23]. The material itself is ductile. For
alloy A, the matrix with nano/ultraﬁne-structure not
only bears local shear banding, but also suﬀers from the
intrinsic brittleness induced by the intermetallics. This
brittleness should be the reason for the appearance of
the multiple cracks distributed throughout the whole
deformed samples (Fig. 7(f)). The value of 8.9% plastic
strain for alloy A reveals the eﬃcient ductility en-
hancement by the dendrites. The signiﬁcance of this
brittle matrix–ductile dendrite composite is a demon-
stration of the pronounced eﬀect of the micrometer-
sized ductile phase for improving the intrinsically brittle
nano/ultraﬁne-structure.

content, the yield strength decreases and the work-

vanishing of the intermetallics, the elastic–plastic tran-
