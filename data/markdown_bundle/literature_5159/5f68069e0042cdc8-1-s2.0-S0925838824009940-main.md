ABSTRACT 
The conventional Ni-based IN738LC superalloy series are typically hardened by a fine distribution of closely 
spaced fine and coarse Ni
3
(Al, Ti)-type L1
2 
ordered γ’ precipitates, which is formed by solid-state nucleation and 
growth in the disordered γ matrix. However, outstanding high-temperature mechanical performance requires a 
high amount (~9 wt%) of Co, which is the alloy ’ s most expensive adding element. Here, we report a compo -
sitionally modified IN738LC alloy with a 50% reduction in Co content and a 20% increase in comparatively 
cheaper Mo content. This compositional modification caused more γ’ precipitates with uniform distribution than 
those in the conventional counterpart subjected to the same heat treatments. The modified superalloy showed a 
twofold increase in high-temperature (750 
◦
C) tensile elongation while maintaining the conventional one ’ s 
strength (~1.1 GPa) at the temperature. This remarkable ductility gain, while no loss in strength, was pre -
dominantly attributed to a transition in the high-temperature deformation mechanism, i.e., from multiple 
intersecting slip bands-indued stress localization for the conventional IN738LC alloy to unidirectional microtwins 
for the modified IN738LC material. More Mo and less Co contents could drive more coherent γ’ precipitates in 
the annealed state and the high density of microtwins in the deformed state, resulting in stress delocalization and 
enhanced ductility at comparable strength. The findings of this study have significant implications for the 
development of cost-effective, strong, and ductile superalloys for high-temperature applications. 
1. Introduction 
There is an ever-growing demand for affordable and high-performing 
Ni- or Co-based superalloys, which are utilized for various mechanical 
components of power generation turbines and nuclear reactors that 
operate in harsh environments [1 – 5] . The superalloys offer distinct 
advantages when exposed to temperatures exceeding 600 
◦
C due to their 
exceptional creep strength. This attribute is crucial in guaranteeing the 
effectiveness and safety of material utilization [6] . Thus, many re -
searchers have investigated the effects of alloying elements on the 
microstructure, creep-fatigue, and oxidation of superalloys [7 – 13] . 
Ni-based superalloys usually have a microstructure consisting of a 
disordered face-centered cubic ( fcc ) γ matrix and Ni
3
Al precipitates ( γ ΄ ) 
with an L1
2
-ordered structure [14,15] . The mechanical properties of the 
superalloys depend on the chemical composition, size, and shape of the 
γ ΄ precipitates [16,17] . For example, the IN738LC superalloy demon -
strates enhanced durability and reliability under operating conditions 
where oxidation resistance is paramount. The IN738LC superalloy has 
been modified by incorporating alloying elements to improve its 
strength and resistance to high-temperature oxidation [18] . Transition 
metals, such as Co, Cr, Mo, W, and Ta, enhance the strength of super -
alloys through solid solution strengthening [19] , and the addition of Al 
and Ti forms γ’ Ni
3
(Al, Ti) precipitates, which act as a crucial 
strengthening component [20,21] . Both B and Ta also enhance super -
alloys ’ creep resistance and rupture strength by facilitating the forma -
tion of stable and finely dispersed precipitates [22,23] . 
However, the challenge in alloy design lies in achieving a delicate 
balance between desired properties and cost minimization, which

Therefore, in the present study, we modified the IN738LC alloy by 
reducing the amount of expensive Co by ~50% and increasing the

after high-temperature tensile deformation using various imaging 
techniques, and the predominant deformation mechanism was exam -
ined in the Mo-IN738LC alloy. 
2. Experimental procedure 
Two 5 kg ingots of IN738LC and Mo-IN738LC alloys were fabricated 
by vacuum induction melting. The nominal chemical compositions of 
the two ingots are listed in Table 1 . The ingots were subsequently re- 
melted to make cylindrical specimens measuring 13 mm in diameter

Uniaxial tensile tests were conducted on cylindrical dog-bone- 
shaped samples with a gauge length of 25 mm and a width of 6 mm, 
which were extracted from the center of the cylindrical specimens. The 
tensile tests were conducted at 450 º C and 750 º C at a strain rate of

Korea) following ASTM-E8 standard. Subsequently, the specimen was 
held at a given temperature for 30 min to ensure a uniform temperature 
distribution within the sample. After reaching the point of tensile fail -
ure, the sample was removed from the furnace and air-cooled to room 
temperature. 
A field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (JSM- 
7900 F, JEOL, Japan) with an electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) 
detector (C-Nano, Oxford Instruments, UK) was used for microstructural 
analysis of aged and tensile-fractured samples. The EBSD data were 
analyzed using TSL OIM analysis 7 software (EDAX, USA), and a mini -
mum confidence index of 0.1 was utilized. Electron channeling contrast 
imaging (ECCI) with a backscattered electron mode was conducted to 
examine the deformation mechanism. The operating voltage was 15 kV, 
and the working distance was 7 – 10 mm. Approximately 80 nm thick 
electron-transparent samples for transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM) were prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB, AMBER G, Tescan, 
Czech Republic). The specific area of interest was selected, and electron 
transparent lamella was lifted out to observe the nature of slip bands. 
Low-magnification bright-field TEM images, high-resolution TEM (HR- 
TEM) images, and scanning transmission electron microscopic (STEM) 
images were captured using a JEOL (TEM, JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan) 
microscope equipped with an EDXS detector. The accelerating voltage of 
the TEM was 200 kV. 
3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 1 shows ECCI images (a-d) and TEM-EDXS maps (e and f) of 
IN738LC and Mo-IN738LC alloys. The Mo-IN738LC alloy exhibited 
larger cuboidal γ’ precipitates (0.367 μ m) than the IN738LC alloy (0.287 
μ m), representing an approximate 27% increase in size. The increase in 
precipitate size in the Mo-IN738LC alloy indicates that the addition of 
Mo and the reduction of Co influenced the precipitation kinetics and 
growth mechanisms of γ’ precipitates by altering diffusion rates or the 
thermodynamic stability of the γ’ phase [30,31] . Further investigation is 
warranted to elucidate the mechanisms responsible for the observed size 
difference. Despite the difference in precipitate size, both alloys had 
similar volume fractions of γ’ precipitates: 31.1% for the Mo-IN738LC 
alloy and 31.9% for the IN738LC. Secondary γ’ precipitates were also 
observed alongside primary γ’ precipitates within the matrix ( Fig. 1 
(a-d)). TEM-EDXS maps ( Fig. 1 e and f) exhibited that the γ’ precipitates 
were enriched with Ni, Al, and Ti, which shows good agreement with the 
chemical composition of γ’ precipitates in Ni-based superalloys [24] .

Elevated temperatures increase atomic mobility, enhancing dislo -
cation motion and reducing resistance to plastic deformation. As a 
result, the alloys experience a decrease in yield strength at higher tem -
peratures [14] . Furthermore, microstructural changes induced by 
elevated temperatures can affect yield strength. Coarsening or dissolving 
precipitates, grain growth, or recovery processes can occur [14] . These 
changes alter the interactions between dislocations and reduce the 
overall yield strength of the alloys. Further research and analysis are 
needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms and their rela -
tive contributions to the observed behavior. 
Notably, despite a 50% reduction in the Co content, one of the major 
solid solution-strengthening elements, the strength of the Mo-modified 
IN738LC alloy only changed slightly. While the addition of Mo is 
believed to contribute to the strengthening of the alloy and prevent 
further strength reduction, it does so through mechanisms such as solid 
solution or grain boundary strengthening, as well as by influencing the 
deformation mechanism [31,32] . 
Additionally, it is important to carefully control the Mo content in 
the alloy to avoid the formation of detrimental topologically close- 
packed (TCP) phases. Previous research [34] suggests that an optimal 
Mo content of approximately 3 wt% improves creep properties. How -
ever, exceeding this value leads to the formation of the TCP phase, 
adversely affecting mechanical properties. Further elaboration on the 
potential consequences of the TCP phase, such as reduced strength or 
increased microstructural instability, would enrich the discussion [34] .

might be there but not will be dominating. Carbides materialize mainly 
by combining carbon with reactive elements like Ti, Ta, Hf, and Nb.

Increasing precipitate size and twin formation causes the synergetic 
effect of high-temperature elongation while maintaining strength at 
higher temperatures. The exceptional high-temperature performance of 
IN738LC can be attributed to its distinct microstructures and many 
strengthening mechanisms, encompassing solid solution strengthening, 
back stress strengthening, and precipitation strengthening [38,39] . To 
begin with, IN738LC has a dual-phase structure comprising a disordered 
FCC matrix and ordered L1
2 
γ’ phases. This structural composition likely 
induces the formation of a matrix-precipitate interface, creating an 
environment where dislocation motion is impeded, consequently 
contributing to enhanced temperature-related strengthening. As dislo -
cations transition from the relatively softer FCC matrix to the harder L12 
γ’ precipitate, they accumulate at the interface, giving rise to substantial 
back stress. This back stress, in turn, counteracts applied shear stresses, 
resulting in a significantly elevated apparent strength for FCC matrix. 
This mechanism enhances the overall yield strength through back stress 
strengthening. Furthermore, a substantial density of coherent L1
2 
γ’ 
precipitates is uniformly dispersed within the FCC matrix. These L1
2 
γ’ 
precipitates exhibit thermal stability at elevated temperatures, thereby 
augmenting the strength of IN738LC via precipitation strengthening. 
Experimental findings indicate the activation of multiple deformation 
mechanisms at 750 
◦
C, encompassing dislocation gliding, slip, stacking 
faults (SFs), and micro and nanotwins. These dislocation motions are 
confined around SFs, interfaces, and twins, effectively restricting the 
overall dislocation motion. 
Additionally, nanotwins may similarly impede dislocation motion, 
with a heightened density of dislocations entangled around both nano 
and micro twins [40,41] . The strengthening effect of 
nanotwins/microtwins stems from grain refinement, introducing extra 
interfaces, particularly twin boundaries, which effectively obstruct 
dislocation motion. Reducing the size of twins to the nanoscale accen -
tuates the interaction between twin boundaries and dislocations, 
resulting in heightened strain hardening and plastic deformation 
[42 – 44] . While an increased concentration of Mo may contribute to 
strengthening through elevated carbide numbers, our primary investi -
gation focused on exploring elevated temperature elongation and 
modifications in deformation mechanisms. The role of carbides remains 
a subject for future examinations. 
The schematic overview in Fig. 10 illustrates the deformation 
behavior in both IN738LC and Mo-IN738LC superalloys. In the case of 
the IN738LC alloy, the deformation behavior is characterized by 
crossing slip bands and microtwins, resulting in stress concentration at 
the intersection of the γ’ precipitates. This stress concentration reduces 
elongation and fracture susceptibility when the material is subjected to 
high-temperature tensile stress. However, it has been observed in pre -
vious studies that microtwins improve high-temperature elongation by 
facilitating tensile strain [29] . Microtwins in the material allow for more 
effective stress and strain distribution, reducing the likelihood of 
localized deformation and crack formation. Additionally, the twinned 
regions act as barriers that impede the motion of dislocations, thereby 
reducing the rate of creep deformation and enhancing the resistance to 
creep [29, 30 [43] ]. Adding Mo to the IN738LC alloy brings about 
favorable changes in its deformation behavior and high-temperature 
performance. The changes include the formation of unidirectional slip 
bands and microtwins, as discussed earlier, which improved mechanical 
properties, including creep resistance. The presence of Mo lowers the 
SFE, which promotes microtwinning, resulting in enhanced strain ac -
commodation and a more uniform distribution of stress and strain 
throughout the alloy. 
4. Conclusions 
The present study has developed a modified, economically feasible 
variant of the IN738LC superalloy, demonstrating enhanced high- 
temperature elongation properties. The key findings in this study are 
as follows. 
1. The Mo-IN738LC alloy exhibits enhanced mechanical properties, 
including high-temperature elongation compared to the IN738LC 
alloy. This is achieved by reducing expensive Co (~ 50%) content 
and increasing cheaper Mo (~20%) content, which reduces the 
overall material cost. 
2. The microstructure analysis of the two alloys revealed that the γ’ 
precipitates were uniformly distributed, but their size in the Mo- 
IN738LC alloy was larger (~21%) than that in the IN738LC alloy.
