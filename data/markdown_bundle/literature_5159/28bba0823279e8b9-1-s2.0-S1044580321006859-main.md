ABSTRACT 
In this paper, six alloys with different Co contents (Nb-16Si-20Ti-4Cr-1.5Al-4Hf-4Zr-1Ta-xCo (x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 
and 5)) were prepared by copper water-cooled crucible arc-melting. The effects of Co on phase selection, 
microstructure, room-temperature fracture toughness, and compressibility were investigated. The six alloys are 
composed of Nbss, 
α-(Nb,X)5Si3, γ-(Nb,X)5Si3, and Cr2(Nb,X) phases. The addition of Co element promotes the 
precipitation of Tiss phase. Primary β-(Nb,X)5Si3 phase was stabilized at room temperature by adding more than 
3% Co. Alloy changes from a near-eutectic to a hypereutectic by the addition of Co. The bulk of primary silicide 
phase deteriorates the room-temperature fracture toughness. At the same time, Co element solid solution in Nbss 
phase reduces the Si content in Nbss phase and increases the K
Q value of alloy. Therefore, the KQ value of 1Co 
alloy reaches 13.14 MPa⋅m1/2. The maximum compressive strength of 5Co alloy is 2155 MPa, which is mainly 
due to the solid solution strengthening of Co element and the highest silicide phase content. 
1. Introduction 
To satisfy the needs of the next generation of engines, the steep 
temperature of the hottest locations for gas turbine engines approaches 
1200–1400 ◦C [1,2]. Nb-Si based in-situ composites have been widely 
investigated as a potential replacement for commonly employed Ni- 
based superalloy [3–5]. Numerous previous studies have shown that it 
performs well at high temperatures ranging from 1200 to 1400 
◦C [6–8]. 
However, there are still two main impediments to the development of 
Nb-Si based in-situ composites, low room-temperature fracture tough
-
ness and insufficient oxidation resistance [9,10]. 
The eutectic point of Nb-Si based in-situ composites is 18.7% [11]. 
The primary phase shifts from Nbss to Nb5Si3 as Si element increases. It 
is also reported that other elements such as Ge and Mo exhibited similar 
properties, in addition to Si [12–14]. Nb-Si based in-situ composites 
contain ductile niobium solid solution (Nbss) phase and high- 
temperature reinforcement silicide phase [15]. Nbss is a tough phase, 
sensitive to crack bridging and trapping during propagation [16]. As a 
result, an increase in the volume fraction of Nbss phase is an effective 
way to enhance room-temperature fracture toughness of Nb-Si based in- 
situ composites [17]. Nbss phase can dissolve a wide range of alloying 
elements, among which Si element has the most significant impacts on 
the room-temperature fracture toughness [18]. Therefore, the reduction 
of Si content in Nbss phase can improve room-temperature fracture 
toughness. Nb
5Si3 phase contains three crystal structures: α-Nb5Si3 (I4/ 
mcm), β-Nb5Si3 (I4/mcm) and γ-Nb5Si3 (P63/mcm) [19]. The structure 
of the three silicide phases can be influenced by various alloying ele -
ments: Al can promote α-Nb5Si3 phase formation [20]; Cr, Fe, and Mo 
can promote β-Nb5Si3 phase formation [21,22]; Ti, and Hf can promote 
γ-Nb5Si3 phase formation [23,24]. Notably, the room-temperature 
fracture toughness of Nb-Si based in-situ composite will be greatly 
reduced due to the excessive size of silicide phase. 
Alloying elements can improve the properties of Nb-Si based in-situ 
composites. For example, W and Mo can reinforce the high-temperature 
strength; Cr, Ti, Hf, Zr and Al can enhance the oxidation resistance; Ti, 
Hf, and Zr can increase the room-temperature fracture toughness 
[25–28]. As a result, the six-element alloy Nb-Si-Ti-Cr-Al-Hf has become 
the traditional system, with reasonable low-temperature toughness, 
high-temperature strength, and medium-to-high temperature oxidation 
resistance. According to literature, the addition of Co to Ti-Al alloys can

2. Experimental procedures 
The nominal compositions Nb-16Si-20Ti-4Cr-1.5Al-4Hf-4Zr-1Ta- 
xCo (x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5) (at.%, similar hereinafter) were listed in 
Table 1. The button ingots were prepared using a water-cooled copper 
crucible with a non-consumable electrode arc-melting in an argon at -
mosphere. Hereinafter, the alloys are represented by 0Co, 1Co, 2Co, 
3Co, 4Co, and 5Co in this study. The selected materials are all high- 
purity bulk raw materials, including Nb blocks (>99.95 wt%), Si 
blocks (>99.99 wt%), Ti sponge (>99.4 wt%), Al particles (>99.95 wt 
%), Ta particles (>99.95 wt%), Zr sponge (>99.4 wt%), Hf particles 
(>99.7 wt%), Cr particles (>99.7 wt%), and Co particles (>99.95 wt%). 
Each alloy ingot was melted six times to ensure uniformity. 
The block samples with 10 × 5 × 10 mm were machined by electrical 
discharge machining (EDM) to analyze phase composition and micro
-
structure. The block samples were mechanically polished by different 
types of SiC paper in turn and polished by water. The phase composition 
was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean) using Cu K
α ra-
diation. The microstructure and element distribution was observed 
using Quanta 200F scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with back
-
scattered electron (BSE) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), 
respectively. The micrographs of a fracture surface for the alloy were 
observed in the secondary electron mode attached to Merlin Compact. 
Transmission electron microscope (FEI, TEM, Talos F200X) and energy 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FEI, EDS) were used to analyze the 
crystal structure and elements distribution of different phases. The area 
fraction of the constituent phases in all alloys was determined using 
Image-Pro Plus 6.0 high-resolution imaging analysis software, and three 
measurements were taken at the same magnification (500×) for each 
sample. 
A three-point bending specimen with a size of 20 (length) × 4 
(width) × 2 mm (thickness) and compression test samples with Ø4 × 6 
mm were prepared by EDM. Room-temperature compression and frac
-
ture toughness experiments were conducted with an AG-X Plus 250 kN/ 
50 kN electronic universal material testing machine. The span of the 
three-point bending specimen was 16 mm, and the loading rate was 0.2 
× 10
 3 m/min. Young’s modulus (En) and nano-hardness (Hn) of 
continuous phase of all alloys were tested by the Table Top Nano -
indentation Tester (G200). The loading and unloading rates of the bulk 
sample nanoindentation test were the same (40.00 mN/min), and a 
maximum constant load was 20.00 mN (for 5 s). The representative 
load-displacement curve is shown in Fig. 1. Mechanical property data 
was obtained throughout three tests, and the average values were 
calculated.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
The room-temperature fracture toughness KQ values of six alloys are 
shown in Fig. 9. The KQ values increase and then decrease as Co content 
increases. The KQ value of the 0Co alloy is 11.82 MPa⋅m1/2. The KQ value 
of 1Co alloy is the highest (13.14 MPa⋅m1/2), rising by 11% compared to 
0Co alloy, while the KQ value of 5Co alloy is the lowest, just 9.2 MPa⋅m1/ 
2, reducing by 30% compared to that of 1Co alloy. 
The crack propagation behaviour of the three-point bending frac -
tured specimens is shown in Fig. 10. The crack propagation 
behaviour of 
1Co, 3Co, and 5Co alloys can be clearly distinguished. Crack deflecting 
occurs in alloys with high KQ values. It is worth noting that secondary 
cracks occur in 1Co alloy, as shown in Fig. 10(a). At the same time, the 
smaller the deflection of crack propagation is, the lower KQ value of the 
alloy is. 
Fig. 11 shows the room-temperature compressibility of different al-
loys. The maximum compressive strength increases as Co content in -
creases. Especially, when the Co content exceeds 3%, it could reach 
2089 MPa. 5Co alloy has the highest compressive strength (2155 MPa), 
while 0Co alloy has the lowest compressive strength (1929 MPa). The 
maximum is 9.66% (1Co), and the minimum is 6.68% (5Co). 
Fig. 12 displays the fracture surfaces of all alloys. The smooth plane 
forms by silicide, and the tear ridge forms by Nbss phase. As Co content 
increases, the smooth cleavage steps gradually increase. Fig. 12(g) and 
(h) show that 
when Co content is less than 3%, a large number of fine 
tearing ridges form in the eutectic structure. There are a lot of cracks in 
the silicide phase in 4Co and 5Co alloys (Fig. 12(i) and (j)). A river 
pattern was found in 1Co alloy (Fig. 12(b’)), which is a typical brittle 
cleavage fracture. In short, adding Co has no effect on the fracture mode, 
and all alloys in this study are brittle quasi-split fractures. 
Table 5 shows the En and Hn of Nbss, α-(Nb,X)5Si3 and β-(Nb,X)5Si3 
phase in all alloys. The Hn of Nbss phase in 0Co alloy is 5.968 GPa. With 
the increase of Co content, the nano-hardness value gradually increases, 
and 5Co alloy reaches 6.704 GPa. The Young’s modulus (En) increased 
from 152.819 (0Co) to 166.940 GPa (5Co). The result shows that Co is a 
strengthening element to Nbss phase. With the increase of Co content, 
Hn of 
α-(Nb,X)5Si3 and γ-(Nb,X)5Si3 gradually increase, from 13.918 and 
15.556 GPa to 14.314 and 17.724 GPa, respectively. In all alloys, the 
nano-hardness of silicide phase is more than twice that of Nbss phase. 
The main reason is that silicide is an intermetallic compound, and the 
crystal structure is more complicated [39]. En of 
α-(Nb,X)5Si3 and β-(Nb, 
X)5Si3 remains nearly constant with the change of Co content, which is 
mainly due to the intrinsic brittleness of silicide phase (less slip system 
and close packed planes). Due to the small size of γ-(Nb,X)5Si3 phase, it 
cannot be measured, meanwhile, due to the similar contrast, α-(Nb, 
X)5Si3 and γ-(Nb,X)5Si3 of 3Co, 4Co and 5Co cannot be measured. Hn of 
β-(Nb,X)5Si3 is higher than that of α-(Nb,X)5Si3 phase. The main reason 
is that the content of Ti and Al (reduce nano-hardness of silicide phase) 
in β-(Nb,X)5Si3 phase is low [33]. The results show that with the increase 
of Co content, both Nbss and silicide phase have solid solution 
strengthening.

5. Conclusions 
(1) The six alloys are composed of Nbss, α-(Nb,X)5Si3, γ-(Nb,X)5Si3, 
and Cr2(Nb,X) phases. The addition of Co element promotes the 
precipitation of Tiss phase, and the primary β-(Nb,X)5Si3 phase is 
formed when the Co content exceeds more than 3%. 
(2) The lattice constant of the Nbss phase and the c/a values of the 
α-(Nb,X)5Si3, β-(Nb,X)5Si3 and γ-(Nb,X)5Si3 phases all decrease 
with the addition of Co. 
(3) The alloy changes from a near-eutectic structure to a hypereu -
tectic structure when the Co content exceeds 3%. 
(4) The KQ value increases and then decreases as the Co content in -
creases, and 1Co alloy has the highest KQ value of 13.14 MPa⋅m1/ 
2, Which is due to the solid solution strengthening of Co and the 
high silicide phase content. The maximum compressive strength 
of 5Co alloy reaches 2155 MPa.
