The evolution of misﬁt dislocation network at γ/γ′phase interface and tensile mechanical properties 
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys at various temperatures and strain rates are studied by using 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. From the simulations, it is found that with the increase of loading, 
the dislocation network effectively inhibits dislocations emitted in the γ matrix cutting into the γ′phase 
and absorbs the matrix dislocations to strengthen itself which increases the stability of structure. Under 
the inﬂuence of the temperature, the initial mosaic structure of dislocation network gradually becomes 
irregular, and the initial misﬁt stress and the elastic modulus slowly decline as temperature increasing. 
On the other hand, with the increase of the strain rate, it almost has no effect on the elastic modulus 
and the way of evolution of dislocation network, but contributes to the increases of the yield stress and 
tensile strength. Moreover, tension–compression asymmetry of Ni-based single crystal superalloys is also 
presented based on MD simulations.

1. Introduction
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys have been widely used as 
crucial materials for turbine blades in aeroengines due to their 
superior mechanical strength and high temperature creep resis-
tance [1]. It was found that in newly developed Ni-based super-
alloys, the γ′ precipitate fraction has reached 70 vol%, and for a 
higher volume fraction of γ′ precipitate, an obvious increase of 
creep rupture life is presented [2]. Moreover, due to the lattice 
mismatch of the γ and γ′phases, an eigenstress ﬁeld is present. 
After a long aging time at elevated temperature, the dislocation 
network appears to accommodate the misﬁt strain [3,4]. In fact, 
the morphology and evolution of the dislocation network plays a 
key role in material mechanical properties. It is well known that 
the role of interfacial dislocation networks is very complex under 
various temperatures and loading conditions, which can not only 
stabilize and strengthen the interface, but also enhance the creep 
resistance through preventing the matrix dislocation from cutting 
into the γ′precipitate [5]. Therefore, it is quite necessary to dis-

Over the past few decades, numerous experiments have been 
conducted to reveal the evolution and role of the interfacial dis-
location networks and their inﬂuences on the creep behaviors of 
the Ni-based single-crystal superalloys [6–9]. These experimental 
studies indicated that the formation and role of the interfacial 
dislocation networks during creep had a close relationship with 
creep mechanical properties of alloys. The yield strength and creep 
strength of alloys were not only dependent on the morphology of 
the γ′ precipitates but also dependent on the evolution of the 
dislocation networks at the γ/γ′ phase interface under various 
temperatures and loading conditions.
Due to the scale of the interfaces is on the nanoscale, molecu-
lar dynamics (MD) and discrete dislocation dynamics (DDD) have 
become very useful tools for studying the morphology and evo-
lution of the interfacial dislocation networks under the load and 
temperature, and exploring the relationship between the damage 
of dislocation networks and mechanical properties of alloys from 
an atomistic standpoint. Yashiro et al. [10]p r o p o s e d a back force 
model to simulate dislocations cutting into a γ′ precipitate by 
DDD, which could give an explanation to the dependence of the 
cutting resistance on the spacing of dislocations in the interfacial

MD simulations were carried out by using a simple two-layer

To give a real image of geometry and physical processes of mi-
crostructure evolution, Zhu et al. [20] and Li et al. [21]d e v e l o p e d 
a three-dimensional (3D) cubic unit model to observe the evolu-
tion of misﬁt dislocation network at γ/γ′ phase interfaces, Li et 
al. [21]f u r t h e r discussed the stress distribution characteristics of 
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys during the microstructure evo-
lution based on this 3D cubic unit MD model. Using MD to simu-
late the evolution of dislocation networks and dislocation motion 
at the γ/γ′phase interface, the construction of an atomic model 
has a great inﬂuence on the microstructure evolution and mechan-
ical properties, and the evolution of dislocation network and the 
relevant mechanical properties are strongly dependent on temper-
ature and loading conditions of alloys. Therefore, more detailed 
studies on the deformation and damage of interfacial dislocation 
networks under various temperatures and loading strain rates are 
necessary and important for studying the mechanical properties of 
the Ni-based single-crystal superalloys. Moreover, very few works 
have been done about the relationship between the microstructure 
evolution and the mechanical properties of alloys.
In this work, the MD model uses a three-dimensional cubic 
unit and ensures the volume fraction of γ′ is about 70%, which 
is similar to the structure of the actual materials. Uniaxial tensile 
MD simulations are performed to investigate the evolution of dis-
location network and dislocation motion at γ/γ′ phase interface 
of Ni-based single crystal superalloy at the temperature from 0 
to 1200 K and strain rate from 2.0 × 108 s−1 to 1.0 × 1010 s−1. 
The objective of the present work is to determine the inﬂuences 
of the temperature and strain rate on the evolution of dislocation 
network and mechanical properties, to explore the relationship 
between the microstructure evolution and the mechanical prop-
erties of alloys. In addition, the tension–compression asymmetry 
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys based on MD simulation is 
discussed at the same temperature and loading strain rate.

periodic boundary conditions to eliminate the surface effect. Be-
fore the external loading, the system is relaxed by running 20000

formed using the open-source Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Paral-
lel Simulator (LAMMPS) code [26]w i t h an integration time step of

study the effects of temperature and strain rate on the microstruc-
ture deformation. The atomic conﬁgurations and their evolutions 
are analyzed by centro-symmetry parameter (CSP) proposed by 
Kelchner et al. [27], and the resulting structures and deformation 
mechanism are visualized by using visualization software OVITO 
[28]i n the atomistic simulations, which provides a built-in CNA 
tool.

3.2. Effect of temperature
When the strain rate of 1.0 ×109 s−1 remains unchanged, the 
effect of temperature on mechanical properties of Ni-based super-
alloys is investigated using MD simulation. At ε = 3%, the mor-
phologies of the dislocation networks with the temperature rang-
ing from 0 to 1200 K are presented in Fig. 4. Compared with dif-
ferent temperatures, the mosaic structure is the most regular and 
stable at 0 K as shown in Fig. 4a. At 300 K, the dislocation network 
becomes irregular, and has a tendency to a cube which is indi-
cated by the right lower two horns changing into two right angles 
as illustrated in Fig. 4b. At 500 K, it is observed that the mosaic 
structure becomes unstable and generates a local damage, some 
lines have disappeared which cause the structure incompleteness 
and chaos (see Fig. 4c). When the temperature rises up to 800 and 
1200 K, the dislocation network at γ/γ′interface has completed 
damaged or basically disappeared, as shown in Fig. 4d and e.
Figs. 5 and 6 show the stress–strain curves and yield strength 
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys at various temperatures. It is 
obviously observed that the average stress of atoms decreases as 
the temperature increases at the same loading strain rate. Com-
paring these curves at various temperatures, the maximum yield 
stress and yield strain are 17.78 GPa and 16.8% at the temper-
ature of 0 K, respectively. With the temperature increasing, the 
yield stress decreases gradually as shown in Fig. 6. In addition, 
the values of elastic modulus of Ni-based single crystal at 300 K, 
500 K, 800 K and 1200 K are 72.485 GPa, 69.39 GPa, 56.89 GPa 
and 41.34 GPa, respectively, which indicate that there is a slight 
decline trend on elastic modulus with the increase of the temper-
ature. Moreover, the stresses decrease slowly and irregularly after 
exceeding the tensile strength, the initial misﬁt stress is smaller at 
a higher temperature. It reveals that the mosaic structure of the

3.4. Tension–compression asymmetry
In order to investigate the tension–compression asymmetry of 
alloys, tension and compression simulations are performed at the 
temperature of 0 K and the strain rate of 1.0 ×109 s−1. As shown 
in Fig. 10, although the duration of elastic deformation is so brief 
in compression stage compared with that in tensile stage, the 
elastic modulus remains unchanged basically. Moreover, the max-
imum tensile strength of Ni-based single crystal superalloys is up 
to 17.784 GPa which is larger than the maximum compressive 
strength and show an obvious tension-compressive asymmetry. Be-
fore the damage of this alloy in compression stage, there is a 
period of time that the stress has no obvious change as the loading 
continues, and then the alloy begins to damage. It is a great dif-
ference between tensile and compression that the formation and 
evolution of dislocation network can relieve the coherency stress, 
and the direction of the tensile stress in accordance with the com-
ponent of the coherency stress tensor, while the compressive stress 
is opposite to the direction of the component of the coherency

4. Discussion
The formation and evolution of interfacial dislocation network 
is one of the important factors that affect the mechanical proper-
ties of Ni-based single crystal superalloys. In the service of super-
alloys, the formation of the dislocation network effectively reduce 
the mismatch stress, prevent dislocation cutting into γ′phase and 
enhance creep resistance of superalloys [4,7]. In this paper, the 
evolution process of interfacial dislocation network is presented in 
detail at the temperature of 0 K. In the early stage of loading, no 
dislocation can be observed in the γ and γ′phases. As the loading 
continues, dislocations emit ﬁrst in the γ matrix phase. Then the 
dislocations in the γ matrix phase gradually increase and move 
to the γ/γ′ interface, eventually accumulate at the γ/γ′ inter-
face, which plays a role in absorbing dislocations and inhibiting 
sliding dislocations cut into the γ′ precipitates. When the stress 
exceeds the critical point that the dislocation network could bear, 
the dislocation network ﬁrst appears local destruction, at this case, 
the damaged networks lose the co-coordinating role of maintaining 
dynamic equilibrium. From the stress–strain curve in Fig. 3, it also 
can be seen that the superalloys failure is a process of a long time, 
the dislocation network is not suddenly and completely destroyed, 
but a local damage ﬁrstly occur and cause stress concentration, 
dislocations in the γ matrix then cut into the γ′precipitates from 
the interfaces where dislocation network is damaged.
Moreover, according to the study on the evolution process of 
interfacial dislocation network at various loading strain rates and 
temperatures, it is found that the morphology of dislocation net-
work has a change tendency from an original tetrahedron to a 
hexahedron. The dislocation network becomes more unstable and 
irregular with temperature increasing even if at lower stress levels. 
The dislocation network will generate a local damage if a suﬃcient 
high temperature is applied. As a result, the slip dislocations in the 
γ matrix will cut into the γ′precipitates from the γ/γ′interface 
due to the local damage of the dislocation network. By compar-
ing the stress–strain curves at various temperatures, it is found 
that the initial mismatch stress decreases, and the yield stress and 
the elastic modulus also decrease with the increase of the tem-
perature. On the other hand, it is found that the change of the 
loading strain rate does not affect the morphology of initial inter-
facial dislocation network and the loading strain rate has almost 
not change the elastic modulus of the alloy, but the yield stress 
and tensile strength of the alloy signiﬁcantly increase with the in-
crease of the loading strain rate. In summary, the temperature and 
loading are the key factors that affect the motion and evolution 
of dislocation, meanwhile, the dislocation motion and evolution 
of dislocation network are different at various temperatures and 
loading strain rates, resulting in the difference in the mechanical 
properties of superalloys.
5. Conclusions
The effects of temperature and strain rate on the evolution of 
dislocation network and mechanical properties are investigated by 
using MD simulations. The main conclusions are presented as fol-
lows:
(1) At the initial stage of loading, the dislocation network ba-
sically keeps the regular mosaic structure, dislocations are not 
emitted in the γ matrix. As the loading continues, the disloca-
tion network inhibits dislocations emitted in the γ matrix cutting 
into the γ′ phase by gathering at the {111} phase, meanwhile it 
absorbs the matrix dislocations to strengthen itself which facili-
tates the stability of structure and increases the critical value of 
maximum stress.
(2) As the temperature increasing, the initial misﬁt stress and 
the elastic modulus decrease, the mosaic structure of dislocation 
network at the γ/γ′interface become irregular and gradually gen-
erate a local damage. Only a ﬁner interfacial dislocation network 
can effectively prevent the slip dislocations cutting into the γ′
phase.
(3) The change of loading strain rate almost has no inﬂuence on 
the elastic modulus as well as the way of evolution of dislocation 
network, but contributes to the change of yield stress and plastic-
ity. The yield strength increase, while the plasticity and ductility 
decrease with the increase of strain rate.
(4) An obvious tension–compression asymmetry of Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys is found based on MD simulations. The 
tensile strength of Ni-based single crystal superalloys is larger than 
the compressive strength at the same temperature and strain rate.
