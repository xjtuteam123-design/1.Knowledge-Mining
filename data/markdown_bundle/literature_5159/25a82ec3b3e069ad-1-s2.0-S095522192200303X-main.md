1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) composed of five or more equimolar or 
near-equimolar metal elements have been favored by researchers in the 
past few decades owing to their excellent comprehensive mechanical 
properties [1 – 5] . The development of HEAs has facilitated the applica -
tion of the high-entropy concept in other fields. Rost [6] et al. firstly 
produced the high-entropy ceramic (HEC) of (Co
0.2
Cu
0.2
Mg
0.2
Ni
0.2

transition metal carbides [7,8] , oxides [9,10] , nitrides [11] , and silicides 
[12] have been successfully fabricated and investigated in detail. Of 
note, the HECs have abundant lattice distortion, which makes their 
properties different from that of the single-component ceramic. More 
precisely, the HECs exhibit good mechanical properties, excellent 
corrosion resistance, and electrical conductivity [6 – 12] , leading them to 
be potential materials in the fields of aerospace and electronics. How -
ever, the fabrication of HECs is difficult, especially for the large-sized 
and complex-shaped components. To deal with this problem, the reli -
able joining of the HECs is proposed to expand their engineering 
applications. 
Active metal brazing is a well-established method to join ceramic- 
based materials and the bonding properties of the joint could be 
adjusted by designing different brazing alloy systems [13 – 15] . Similar to 
other ceramics, the good wettability of liquid brazing alloy is funda -
mental to the active metal brazing of HECs. Yang et al. [13] studied the 
wetting mechanism of liquid TiNi-20 Nb (at%) filler alloy on the 
SiO
2
-BN ceramic and found that the chemical reactions between the 
active element Ti in the filler alloy and the SiO
2

the ceramic promoted the wetting process. Thus, the addition of active 
elements in the brazing alloy is significant to the wetting and metal -
lurgical bonding of the ceramic substrate. On the other hand, lots of 
brittle intermetallic compounds (IMCs) might be generated in the 
brazing seam due to the interfacial reactions that occurred at the 
ceramic surface, which was detrimental to the joint strength [16 – 18] . To 
inhibit the IMCs formation and improve the mechanical performances of 
the ceramic brazed joints, eutectic alloys systems, such as Si-Zr [19,20] , 
Ti-Si [21] , Zn-Sn [22] , Ag-Cu [23] , Ti-Ni-Nb [24,25] , were reported to 
active brazing ceramics. Ag-Cu eutectic alloy with a small amount of 
active Ti element was most widely used to braze ceramic to itself and 
metals, and the joints exhibited satisfactory properties at service tem -
peratures below 400 
◦
C. To enhance the high-temperature properties of 
the brazed joint, Ti-based eutectic alloy with a high melting point was 
applied to braze ceramics. Qi et al. [26] successfully brazed SiC ceramic 
using Ti-24Ni eutectic alloy, obtaining brazed joints with the shear

reacted with the ceramic. Therefore, (Ti, Nb)C phase formed adjacent to 
the ceramic substrate due to the negative Gibbs formation energies of 
TiC and NbC [32] . The reaction equation can be illustrated below: 
(HfTaZrNbTi)C
5

The cation-carbon bonds in the ceramic were broken due to the 
above reaction occurred at the ceramic side and then Hf, Ta and Zr 
atoms escaped from the ceramic and remained in the liquid phase. Ac -
cording to the results reported in Refs. [33,34] , the order of the diffusion 
coefficients of metal atoms in the (HfTaZrNbTi)C
5 
ceramic was Ta < Hf 
< Zr < Nb < Ti. The diffusion of Ta and Hf was the slowest while that of 
Ti was the fastest. Therefore, a small amount of Hf and Ta elements were 
detected in the (Ti, Nb)C reaction layer due to their lower diffusion 
coefficients, and Zr element was detected in the β Nb phase away from 
the ceramic, as shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1 . The distribution of the metal 
elements in the joint was consistent with the order of their diffusion 
coefficients. The formed (Ti, Nb)C reaction layer acted as the barrier to 
retard the reaction between the active Ti and Nb in the liquid phase and 
the dissolved C from the HEC. Consequently, the width of the (Ti, Nb)C 
layer was thin. Meanwhile, the composition of the liquid TiNi-Nb alloy 
was changed due to the consumption of the Ti and Nb elements, leading 
to the formation of (Ti, Nb)
2
Ni layer along with the (Ti, Nb)C layer. 
Subsequently, TiNi + Nb eutectic microstructure solidified from the 
remained liquid phase during the cooling stage and the metallurgical 
bonded HEC brazed joint formed via TiNi-Nb eutectic alloy.

3.3. Mechanical properties of the HEC brazed joints 
The variation of the shear strength of the brazed HEC joints 
depending on the oxidation times is displayed in Fig. 11 . The black line 
is the shear strength of the brazed joints at different oxidation times and 
the blue line shows the thickness of the oxide layer of the HEC substrate. 
The measurement position of the oxide layer thickness is marked by the 
red square in Fig. S3 . The joint strength of HEC brazed at 1220
◦
C/

◦
C 
and RT undoubtedly demonstrated the excellent properties of the TiNi- 
Nb eutectic alloy, which is consistent with the microstructural stability 
of the HEC brazed joint. However, the joint shear strength was decreased 
when the oxidation time was prolonged. The joint shear strength was 
maintained at 164 MPa after oxidation for 2 h, and it just further 
decreased 22 MPa when the oxidation time was prolonged to 8 h, which 
indicated that the HEC brazed joint using TiNi-Nb eutectic alloy 
exhibited poor high-temperature endurance. Additionally, the joint 
shear strength was decreased as the oxidation layer of HEC thickened. 
This phenomenon suggested that the oxidation damage of the HEC 
substrate was responsible for the decreased joint strength of the HEC. 
The fracture mode and microstructure of the HEC brazed joints were 
investigated to analyze the effect of the oxidation treatment on the 
mechanical performance of the joint, as shown in Fig. 12 . The fracture in 
the HEC substrate meant that the weakest area of the joint was the 
ceramic itself when the brazing parameter was chosen as 1220
◦
C/ 
10 min, as seen in Fig. 12 (a-b). Although the oxidation resistance of the 
HEC was poor, the existence of the brazing fillet inhibited the aggression 
of oxygen to the brazing seam. Meanwhile, the width of (Ti, Nb)C re -
action layer neighboring the HEC did not change, which was crucial to 
the bonding properties of the joint. Therefore, the joint shear strength 
was high after the HEC joint oxidized for 2 h. Further increasing the 
oxidation time to 6 h, the fracture still occurred at the HEC side, but 
partial (Ti, Nb)
2

the fracture, as illustrated in Fig. 12 (c-d). Compared with the original 
joint, the HEC substrate was damaged after long-time oxidation treat -
ment, leading to the decrease of the strength performance [48,49] . 
Hence, the strength of the joint was just 22 MPa. In short, the HEC 
brazed joint with TiNi-Nb eutectic alloy possessed a high shear strength 
both at RT and elevated temperature for a short time.

entropy (HfTaZrNbTi)C

reinforced joint with high shear strength was obtained through inter -
diffusion and chemical reactions between the HEC and the TiNi-Nb 
liquid alloy. The main conclusions can be drawn as follows: 
1. The brazing seam of the HEC brazed joint via TiNi-Nb alloy at
