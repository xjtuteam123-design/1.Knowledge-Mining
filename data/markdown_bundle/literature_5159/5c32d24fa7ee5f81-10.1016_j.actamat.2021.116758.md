An analytic model describing substitutional solid solution strengthening in body-centered cubic (BCC) 
complex, concentrated alloys (CCAs) based on a/2 < 111 > screw dislocation mobility is developed and pre- 
sented. At lower temperatures it is assumed that dipole dragging is the rate limiting strengthening mech- 
anism, and as the temperature increases this changes to a jog dragging mechanism. While both mecha- 
nisms are contained in Suzuki’s classical model for substitutional solid solution strengthening developed 
for BCC alloys signiﬁcant modiﬁcations are required to apply such a model to the refractory CCA’s. First, 
two approaches for incorporating the net effects of the complex solute chemistry are developed and 
contrasted using a ternary NbTaTi alloy and four quaternary alloys, MoNbTaTi, WNbTaTi, CrMoNbTi and 
CrMoTaTi . Our model is extended to higher temperatures and applied to analyze experimental yield data 
as a function of temperature (0.15–0.7T L , where T L is the absolute liquidus temperature) and strain rate 
(10 −5 to 10 −2 /s) in a range of BCC chemically complex alloys including stoichiometric NbTiZr and HfNbTa- 
TiZr. We ﬁnd that the model, based on just these two mechanisms, gives excellent agreement with exper- 
imental yield data in both these BCC CCAs, NbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr. The experimental yield data of these 
two BCC CCA’s as well as NbTaTi and four quaternaries are also analyzed using Maresca’s edge dislocation 
model developed for these types of alloys. The screw model presented is useful in predicting BCC CCAs 
with adequate high temperature strength, which will accelerate development of high temperature, high 
strength BCC alloys for aerospace applications.

1. Introduction 
Refractory metal Complex Concentrated Alloys (RCCAs) have 
demonstrated some attractive properties for high temperature 
aerospace applications [ 1 , 2 ]. Experimental results show that some 
single-phase BCC multicomponent alloys retain their strength to 
very high temperatures (~0.6T L ), as compared to simple BCC met- 
als [2-4] . The strength of these alloys have been modeled based on 
screw dislocation mobility [5-7] as well as edge dislocation mobil- 
ity [8] . Previously we have proposed a model for BCC RCCA’s, based 
on screw mobility, that was best suited for low to intermediate 
temperatures (i.e. below 10 0 0 °C), before the experimental yield 
data deviate from model predictions through a precipitous drop 
in strength with temperature [ 9 , 10 ]. In this work we extend the 
previously described low temperature dipole dragging yield model 
to higher temperatures. This approach assumes that yield is con-

2. Solid solution strengthening model for the yield behavior of 
BCC chemically complex alloys based on screw dislocation 
mobility 
Here we present a new version of the Suzuki model for describ- 
ing low and high temperature yield behavior of BCC chemically 
complex alloys [ 18 , 19 ]. The strengthening of such alloys is assumed 
to be mainly due to substitutional and interstitial solid solution 
strengthening. The low temperature portion of the model has re- 
cently been applied to describe yield behavior in RCCAs [5] as well 
as describing interstitial solid solution strengthening in simple BCC 
metals [11] . The low temperature model considers substitutional 
solid solution strengthening arising from two factors: a) stress to 
move kinks across solute obstacles at the screw dislocation core 
and b) stress to bow screw dislocations between interstitial and 
vacancy dipoles formed on the dislocation line due to kink-kink 
collisions − the dipole dragging stress. At high temperatures, the 
interstitial and vacancy dipoles collapse [10] due to climb onset 
by strong self diffusion. At these temperatures, it is proposed that 
a vacancy concentration gradient develops at the jogs formed on 
the screw dislocation due to kink-kink collisions and the screw 
dislocation drags such jogs as it moves [17] . In the high temper- 
ature model, the dipole dragging stress from the low tempera- 
ture model is replaced by the jog dragging stress. In general there 
will be contributions from dislocation forest strengthening, Hall- 
Petch strengthening at yield, and in general the effects of chemical 
short range order should be considered. However these contribu- 
tions are expected to be small in the alloys considered here and 
have been neglected in this analysis. Both the low and high tem- 
perature models assume that yield is controlled by the mobility 
of screw dislocations in these alloys. There is signiﬁcant transmis- 
sion electron microscopy evidence in both HfNbTaTiZr and NbTiZr, 
that this indeed is the case [20-22] . We start by recapitulating and 
reﬁning the low temperature dipole dragging model and later on 
give the high temperature modiﬁcation to such a model.

2.1. Low temperature dipole dragging model 
As a kink moves on a screw dislocation, the number of so- 
lute atoms at the core of the dislocation can permanently change. 
Suzuki considered the core of the screw dislocation in a BCC struc- 
ture to involve 6 atomic rows (ﬁrst and second neighbor atoms) 
right at the center of the compact core [ 5 , 6 , 11-16 ] where the 
solute-dislocation interaction energy is high and almost equal. The 
change in solute-dislocation interaction energy from the rest of 
the atoms around the core is assumed to be minimal. Later on in 
this manuscript, we relax this constraint and extend it to consider 
solute-dislocation interactions for solutes farther from the disloca- 
tion core. In this geometry, for a unit step ‘b’ of kink movement 
along the dislocation line, 3 atoms leave the core and 3 new atoms 
enter the core. For a kink movement of distance ‘z’ along the dis- 
location line the number of atoms leaving or entering the core is 
giving as N = 3z/b. For a concentration “c” of substitutional solute 
the mean value can then be written as n = c ∗N = 3cz/b. Since the 
solute atoms are assumed to be distributed at random at the core 
of the dislocation, the number of strongly interacting substitutional 
solute atoms at the core of the dislocation during a movement of 
‘z’ along the dislocation line may permanently change by a value, 
m. The activation enthalpy for such a transformation of the dislo- 
cation core is then simply [ 5 , 6 , 11-16 ]

5. Summary 
A model for describing substitutional solid solution strengthen- 
ing in BCC CCAs over a wide range of temperatures is developed 
and presented. The low temperature portion of model is termed 
the dipole dragging model and the high temperature version, jog 
dragging model. Both models are based on the mobility of screw 
dislocations in BCC solid solutions. The low temperature model as- 
sumes that the screw dislocations move by dragging vacancy and 
interstitial dipoles whereas the high temperature model assumes 
that screw dislocations move by dragging atomic jogs. The dipoles 
or jogs form as a result of kink-kink collisions occurring on the 
screw dislocation in these alloys. At high temperatures, the dipoles 
collapse due to signiﬁcant climb velocity aided by vacancy diffu- 
sion. At these temperatures, a chemical force is exerted on the 
screw dislocation due to the development of a vacancy concentra- 
tion gradient at the jogs. In both cases, an activation barrier ex- 
ists for kinks to overcome solute obstacles in between the dipoles 
or the jogs, derived from Suzuki’s model of substitutional solid 
solution strengthening for BCC alloys. Two schemes for summing 
up the contributions from more than one solute were presented: 
a phenomenonlogical approach (Model 2) and a direct approach 
(Model 1). Such a model is used to analyze experimental yield data 
as a function of temperature (0–1200 °C) and strain rate (10 −5 to 
10 −2 /s) in stoichiometric NbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr as well as yield 
stress vs. temperature data (0–800 °C) in the ternary alloy, NbTaTi 
and four quaternary alloys, MoNbTaTi, WNbTaTi, CrMoNbTi and Cr- 
MoTaTi. It is shown that the model results are in fairly good agree- 
ment with experimental yield data in all these BCC composition- 
ally complex alloys. The phenomenological and direct approaches 
of summing up the contributions from various solutes give re- 
sults within 20% of each other. The experimental yield data for 
the alloys considered in this manuscript were also analyzed using 
Maresca’s edge model. It is shown that excepting for CrMoTaTi and 
CrMoNbTi, edge dislocations have a fairly high mobility in all other 
alloys. The yield behavior in these alloys is controlled by screw 
dislocation mobility. The screw model presented should be useful 
in developing and designing high temperature, high strength BCC 
CCAs for aerospace applications. It should also be useful in evalu- 
ating the creep strength of single phase BCC CCAs.
