abstract
The strength of a Fe–Al-based alloy containing small additions of Mo, W and C has been determined from
room temperature up to 800 /C14 C, and the strain rate dependence of strength examined. Strength of the
as-cast material is maintained at above 600 /C14 C, but it is lost at higher temperatures, especially at slow
strain rates. This behaviour is largely explained by solute hardening effects, with no sign of any
precipitates forming. After a solution treatment, annealing material at 800
/C14 C leads to the appearance of
Fe–Mo–W carbides which provide better strength under conditions of high temperature and slow strain
rate. The possibilities for improving high-temperature strength and creep behaviour by the formation of
carbide or intermetallic precipitates are discussed.
/C2112008 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.1. Introduction
Iron aluminides are interesting engineering materials since they
can be regarded as light steels or as high-temperature oxidation/
corrosion resisting materials. Conditions for processing these
materials have developed over the past years, and room tempera-
ture strength and ductility are both essentially acceptable for many
commercial applications. Poor strength at high temperatures,
above 600 /C14 C, and poor creep behaviour remain, however, as major
obstacles to applications for the temperatures where the good
oxidation/corrosion behaviour is beneﬁcial.
Many studies of creep behaviour of iron aluminides have been
carried out. These range from basic studies of simple binary alloys
at very high temperatures [1–3] to studies of alloys prepared by
special methods such as rapid solidiﬁcation [4,5] or mechanical
alloying for the introduction of stable oxide dispersions [6–9].
Solution hardening is seen to achieve some useful strength
increases at high temperature [10,1 1], but much more signiﬁcant
creep strength increases are possible by the introduction of stable
particles [10–16].
Strengthening achieved at high temperatures by additions of
stable dispersoids [17–19], by carbides [20–25], or by intermetallic
precipitates [26–28], has been extensively researched over the past
few years, and signiﬁcant improvements in strength demonstrated.
In these cases, precipitation hardening is achieved during heat
treatments following casting, that is the precipitates are produced
from an initially solutionised state. Major increases in high-
temperature ﬂow strength and creep strength have also been
shown to be possible by the introduction of particle dispersions, for
example of complex Laves phases, directly during the casting stage
[29–34]. While such particles often show great thermal stability,
a disadvantage of such cast-in particles is that they are usually
distributed as brittle intermetallic networks that will reduce
ductility to very low levels [29,35]. Some attempts have been made
to disperse such brittle networks by thermomechanical processing
[36], but these have generally been unsuccessful unless very high
processing strains are imposed.
The present paper examines the strengthening which can be
achieved, and its high-temperature stability, by the addition of
small amounts of Mo and W with C to the Fe–Al–Cr base alloy. Such
additions are expected to produce carbides under suitable heat
treatment conditions [37,38], with a signiﬁcant amount of Mo and
W in the carbides, or possibly to produce Laves phase precipitates
[39], depending on the alloy compositions and heat treatments
imposed.

therefore somewhere near 1 h. Samples were removed quickly

out at 600 /C14 C and 700 /C14 C using a variety of strain rates, covering the
range from about 10 /C0 6/s to 10 /C0 3/s. A few additional tests were

out at room temperature on round cross-section samples with
a gauge of 3 mm in diameter and 20 mm in length.

Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Transmission Electron Microscopy
(TEM) on electropolished disk samples. These were prepared using

1

200 type).
3. Results
3.1. Examination of as-cast materials
All three alloys showed the columnar-grain morphology of
width 200–300
mm in the test samples, and had a dendritic struc-
ture of secondary dendrite arm spacing of about 100 mm. Inter-
dendrite regions were covered with an almost continuous ﬁlm rich
in the transition metals. Chemical analysis by EDS conﬁrmed
the nominal compositions of the three alloys studied, with the
interdendritic ﬁlm being transition metal boride, or possibly bor-
ocarbide after high-temperature heat treatment, see Table 1 .
Compositions of light elements were not quantitatively deter-
mined, but EDS analysis conﬁrmed a signiﬁcant C content in the
alloys (B in interdendritic ﬁlms). The volume fraction of phases seen
after suitable heat treatments was consistent with the nominal
compositions for each alloy. Note that the chemical composition
measured for matrix and any second phase particles was essentially
the same for the two Mo–W–C containing alloys.
Results of compression testing as a function of test temperature
are shown in Fig. 1 for the three alloys. Plastic deformation began
smoothly, with no sign of a yield discontinuity, and the ﬂow stress
measured at 0.2% plastic strain has been measured to characterise
the given material and test condition. The stress–strain curves
generally showed parabolic work hardening with ﬂow stresses
increasing rapidly at low-test temperature, and weaker work
hardening at higher temperatures. As such, at 700–800
/C14 C, there
was essentially no work hardening and a steady ﬂow stress was
maintained as the strain level increased. Tensile testing carried out
at room temperature conﬁrmed the ﬂow stress values, and showed
approximately 3% ductility for the ternary alloy and 2% ductility for
the two alloys with additions of Mo, W and C. Such moderate
ductility levels could surely be improved by better casting proce-
dures or through thermomechanical processing. For all three
materials the ﬂow stress is relatively constant from room temper-
ature to 500–600
/C14 C, and thereafter falls rapidly at higher
temperatures. The ternary Fe–Al–Cr alloy begins to lose strength
already above 500
/C14 C, while the two alloys with Mo–W–C additions
retain their strength up to about 600 /C14 C. As will be seen throughout
this study, the strength of the Fe-18–5–2Mo–1W–1C alloy and the
Fe-18–5–2Mo–2W–1C alloy is relatively similar at the high
temperatures, although the Fe-18–5–2Mo–2W–1C alloy is some-
what stronger at temperatures below 600 /C14 C.

Dislocation structures observed after deformation to 2% strain at
room temperature and at 700 /C14 C are illustrated in Fig. 6 for the Fe-
18–5–2Mo–1W–1C alloy. Very similar dislocations were seen for
the other Mo–W–C containing alloy. After room temperature
deformation, Fig. 6a, dislocations were arranged in planar arrays, as
is typical for alloys containing short-range order [43]. These
dislocations appear to be mostly near-screw dislocations of Burgers
vector 1/2 C111D type. The ﬁrst dislocations of a group were often
arranged in loose pairs, with subsequent dislocations widely
distributed in the same slip plane. Some of the dislocations appear
also to leave a weak fault trail after their movement, suggesting
a subtle change of the order/solute state. The present alloys are
expected to be disordered, based on their Al content [44,45], but
the onset of short-range order and a tendency to long range
ordering may be enhanced by the presence of Mo and W in solution
[46,47]. Following deformation at high temperature, the disloca-
tions remained as single 1/2 C111D type, now much more randomly
arranged, see Fig. 6 b. There was no sign of short-range order
dislocation arrays, and no sign of precipitation at any test temper-
ature in any of the alloys.
3.2. Annealing behaviour of cast alloys
Microstructures of as-cast materials were examined after
deformation at temperatures in the range of room temperature to
800
/C14 C (which involved a total time at temperature of about 1 h).
No sign of any precipitation, neither at grain boundaries nor within
the grain interiors, was found. Further studies of possible precipi-
tation were therefore carried out by ﬁrst giving a solution treat-
ment of 1 h at 1 150
/C14 C, followed by a water quench, and then ageing
at temperatures in the range of 500–800 /C14 C. The ternary Fe–Al–Cr
alloy essentially showed no changes during quenching or ageing,
and will not be discussed further. The two alloys containing Mo–
W–C additions showed very similar microstructures during these
treatments and a detailed presentation of only the Fe–Al–Cr–2Mo–
2W–C alloy will be given.

3.3. High-temperature mechanical behaviour of pre-aged materials
As has been shown in the previous sections, precipitates were
observed after suitable solutionising, quenching and ageing treat-
ments, but were not formed during the short high-temperature
exposures of the as-cast materials corresponding to the mechanical
testing itself. Essentially the same behaviour was seen for both
alloys containing Mo–W–C additions. A set of samples of the Fe-18–
5–2Mo–2W–1C alloy was therefore prepared by solutionising for
1 h at 1 150
/C14 C, water quenching, and ageing for 1 h at 800 /C14 C, and
the mechanical behaviour was examined. When tested at the
standard strain rate of 2 /C2 10
/C0 4/s at room temperature as well as at
600–700 /C14 C, the ﬂow stress was seen to have decreased consider-
ably from the values for the as-cast material shown in Fig. 1. Figs. 15
and 16 give ﬂow stress data for tests over a range of strain rates at
both 600 /C14 C and 700 /C14 C as well as a comparison between the pre-
aged material and the as-cast material. Fig. 15 conﬁrms that the
pre-aged materials are weaker at high strain rates, but show a lower
stress dependence on strain rate, such that the material maintains
its strength better at slow strain rates. This behaviour is conﬁrmed
in the ﬂow stress against strain rate to the power 1/4 plot shown in
Fig. 16, from which values of the threshold stress can be deduced as
the intercept with the ordinate axis. At 600 /C14 C the material shows
extremely little strain rate dependence, corresponding to values of
threshold stress close to the applied stress values, as already seen in
Fig. 5 .A t7 0 0 /C14 C, however, the threshold stress is raised signiﬁ-

seen that the high-temperature ﬂow stresses under conditions of
high strain rate may be reduced by the pre-ageing treatment, but
will be signiﬁcantly increased under slow strain rate or creep
testing conditions.

the cubic matrix and, thus, similarly closely-spaced precipitate

the matrix spots) instead of the 5 times closer spacing seen here.

and this may be responsible for the appearance of the stacking
faults or early precipitates in the present work.
The addition of large amounts of Laves-forming elements to
ferritic steels leading to the appearance of Laves phase precipitates
at intermediate temperatures has been considered on several
occasions [53–56]. These precipitates may have better thermal
stability under such conditions than the carbides which generally
reinforce steel. The present alloy and its behaviour bears many
similarities to such alloys, which are typically based on Fe–12%Cr,
with signiﬁcant additions of elements such as W. The most
important difference, of course, is the presence of 18%Al in the
present alloys, which ensures that no austenite phase appears, and
no strengthening martensitic reactions are possible. The last quoted
studies, e.g. [55,56], make it clear that additions of several percents
of Mo and W, and 1%C, as here, will lead to the appearance of both
carbides and Laves phase below temperatures of 700–750
/C14 C, and
to the presence of carbides alone at temperatures in the range
about 700–750
/C14 C to 900–1000 /C14 C. However, the kinetics of reac-
tions involving signiﬁcant movement of W are very slow [55]. Thus,
while carbides may form relatively rapidly at suitable tempera-
tures, the ﬁrst appearance of Laves precipitates may require hours
at a temperature of about 780 /C14 C, but will require many days –
towards years – at temperatures of about 650 /C14 C. The carbides
expected may be either M 23C6 or M 6C, depending on precise alloy
and annealing condition [56]. These results are consistent with the
observations made here on the Fe–18%Al–5%Cr based alloy – the
carbides seen on annealing at 800 /C14 C would be stable at lower
temperatures, but the appearance of any Laves phase requires
much longer annealing times than those considered here.

following pre-annealing at 1 150
/C14 C/800 /C14 C may be explained by the
loss of solute and the formation of the carbide precipitates. The loss
of solution hardening is thus responsible for the fall in ﬂow stress
from room temperature to 700
/C14 C, when materials are tested under
the standard strain rate (2 /C2 10/C0 4/s), as seen in Figs. 15 and 16. For
slow strain rate testing at high temperatures, the presence of the
intragranular precipitates is important since this leads to the
creation of a larger threshold stress. This larger threshold stress
leads to a greater value of the intercept with the ordinate at 700 /C14 C
seen in Fig. 16 for the pre-aged material and the lower ﬂow stress
dependence on strain rate seen in Fig. 15.

The work described here has resulted in only small improvements
in mechanical strength under these high temperature, slow strain
rate conditions, i.e. close to slow creep conditions. It is nevertheless
considered that the alloy system examined shows good promise for
signiﬁcant further strength improvement. It may be possible to
increase volume fractions of precipitates by changes in alloy
composition, but it is also clear that signiﬁcant further work is
required to conﬁrm precipitate stability and optimise particle sizes
and distributions. However, based on the fact that the carbide
precipitates remain relatively small after exposures of 1–10 h at
800 /C14 C, it appears that such particles should retain usefully ﬁne sizes
for extended times at potential application temperatures in the range
650–700
/C14 C. The possibility of subsequent Laves precipitate forma-
tion during such long, intermediate temperature exposure may also
offer hopes for further strength increases at such temperatures.
The present study has produced a material with poorer creep
strength than other Fe–Al alloys, for example the Fe–Al–Zr alloys
[27,28] as seen in Figs. 4 and 5 . The fact that this material is heat
treatable by suitable solutionising and ageing treatments, however,
and the likelihood that further strength improvements are possible
by optimisation of composition and heat treatments, means that
the present alloy system is one worthy of further study.
As a ﬁnal point, the role of small B additions to alloys such as the
present ones may be considered. A small amount of B was added to
the present alloys, with some Zr, in order to reduce the grain size, or
retain moderate grain size by avoiding grain coarsening during
high-temperature exposure. The precipitate particles found after
ageing at 800
/C14 C were clearly conﬁrmed to be carbides (not borides
or borocarbides). The observation of stacking faults associated with
some partial dislocations after annealing at 600–700
/C14 C may,
however, be associated with the presence of a small amount of B in
solution, as found by Pang and Kumar [49–52]. Such faults may thus
represent a precursor stage to subsequent carbide precipitation,
and play a role in determining the size and distribution of these
carbides. This aspect also requires further evaluation.
5. Conclusions
Changing the composition of Fe
3Al-base Fe–Al–Cr alloys by
reducing the Al content to 18% has been shown to improve the
room temperature ductility, while small additions of Mo, W and C
have led to improvements in ﬂow stress from room temperature up
to 800 /C14 C. In the as-cast state the ﬂow stress is increased by solution
hardening, with no sign of any precipitation during the w1h
exposure occurring during mechanical testing. Solutionising and
ageing treatments have been shown to lead to the appearance of
dislocation loops and stacking faults at 600–700
/C14 C, and to the
appearance of M 6C needle particles at 800 /C14 C. These precipitates
notably increase the ﬂow stress at high temperatures and slow
strain rates. There is considerable room for further improvement in
high-temperature mechanical behaviour by optimisation of alloy
composition and of heat treatments.
