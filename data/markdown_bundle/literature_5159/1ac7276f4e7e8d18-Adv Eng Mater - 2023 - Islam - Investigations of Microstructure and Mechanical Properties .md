1. Introduction
With an increasing awareness of global
warming, environmental pollution, and
the scarcity of fossil fuel resources, many
researchers and manufacturers have been
looking for sustainable solutions to
improve fuel consumption ef ﬁciency.[1]
Higher operating temperature and reduced
rotor weight in combustion engines, such
as gas turbines, can improve energy ef ﬁ-
ciency, which is bene ﬁcial to fuel economy
and environmentally friendly. [2] Nickel-
based superalloys are used at high temper-
atures but their application is limited by
solvus and melting point.[3] Due to the high
melting point, refractory metals and alloys
are considered for high-temperature appli-
cations. Refractory niobium (Nb) and its
alloys are promising candidates in high-
temperature applications since they have
demonstrated high ductility, [4] high corro-
sion resistance, [5] moderate strength, and
favorable creep strength. [6] Nb is the light-
est refractory metal with good thermal con-
ductivity, and owing to its body-centered cubic (BCC) structure, it
has a high solubility of alloying elements. Zirconium (Zr) pro-
vides solid-solution strengthening to the Nb matrix, increasing
high-temperature strength while maintaining ductility. [7] Nb-
1 wt% Zr alloy (NbZr1) has high ductility, high stability against
irradiation, and high-temperature strength compared to other
refractory metals. These unique properties make NbZr1
attractive for potential applications in nuclear power systems
as fuel cladding, structural components, chemical, and aerospace
industries.[6]
Due to high melting temperatures and poor oxidation resis-
tance, traditional melting and casting methods are inef ﬁcient
for the fabrication of refractory metals. Vacuum arc melting
furnaces are required to melt Nb and its alloys. However, extra
precautions, such as intensive water cooling, are required
because NbZr1 is prone to arching against the crucibles.
Powder metallurgy with high-temperature vacuum sintering is
generally used to manufacture Nb alloys. [8] In contrast, Ingot
metallurgy is more cost-effective because it produces less impu-
rity and prevents embrittlement. However, thermodynamically
stable and chemically inert mold materials are also needed to

Herein, the feasibility of the gas tungsten arc welding-based wire þ arc additive
manufacturing process for fabricating thin wall structures of niobium-1 wt%
zirconium (NbZr1) alloy is investigated. Three different heat input conditions
(low, medium, and high) have been selected for fabricating it. The microstructure
is characterized by using optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, X-ray
diffraction, energy-dispersive spectroscopy, and electron backscattered diffrac-
tion (EBSD). The microstructure shows the columnar dendritic structure elon-
gated in the build direction. No cracks or porosity are observed in the structure.
Average Vickers hardness for low, medium, and high heat input conditions are
146.6, 162.1, and 163.5 HV, respectively. There is an increasing trend of
microhardness value along the deposition height, which can be attributed to the
difference in secondary dendritic arm spacing and the formation of precipitates.
The tensile strength of the specimen is comparable to the conventional and
additively manufactured structures. EBSD analysis con ﬁrms that possible
subgrains are responsible for good mechanical properties at room temperature.
In the majority of the tensile samples, the failure mechanism has been identi ﬁed
as a ductile fracture. The mechanical characteristics ﬂuctuate with locations in
each of the thin walls, suggesting anisotropy in the deposits.

metallurgy, casting, and ingot metallurgy, similar to other

including a high buy-to- ﬂy (BTF) ratio that leads to material

Due to the advantages of metal additive manufacturing (AM),
such as the ability to produce near-net-shape, complex geometry,
and sustainability, it is being considered a promising technology
for manufacturing refractory alloys. Directed energy deposition
(DED) is a metal AM process that utilizes focused thermal energy
to fuse materials as they are deposited. [10] According to the feed-
stock used, it is further categorized into powder- and wire-fed
techniques.[11] Wire-fed DED techniques can be categorized as
laser beam, electron beam, or arc-based depending on their
power source.[12] The arc-based technology that uses wire as feed-
ing materials is known as wire þ arc additive manufacturing
(WAAM), and it can be further classi ﬁed as the gas metal arc,
gas tungsten arc (GTA), [13] or plasma arc welding. [14]
Advantages of WAAM include a high deposition rate, minimal
system setup costs, a wide range of wire materials that can be
used, and the ability to build large-scale metal structures. [15]
In addition, compared to subtractive machining methods, it
can minimize manufacturing costs by reducing material waste
(i.e., lower BTF). [16] Due to the advantages mentioned earlier,
WAAM has acquired increasing research interest in recent
times.
However, unwanted characteri stics (e.g., microstructural
heterogeneity), defects (e.g., voids), and residual stress are
generated by the AM layer-by-layer stacking mechanism and
nonequilibrium heating and cooling cycles. Due to the
absence of AM understanding fo r refractory alloys, particu-
larly Nb alloys, these undesired features, and ﬂaws might be
exacerbated compared to standard alloys (e.g., Inconel 625).
In addition, due to the high melting temperature of Nb
(2477 °C), the residual stress will be more critical compared
to AM for nickel-based superalloys. Nevertheless, insuf ﬁcient
knowledge of the AM process dynamics signi ﬁcantly
limits the application of additively manufactured NbZr1
(AM-NbZr1) alloys. Given the great potential, efforts must
be undertaken to advance the related knowledge for many
applications. But so far, only a few studies have been done
on AM-NbZr1. To the best of the authors ’ knowledge,
no published literature is a vailable in the case of WAAM
NbZr1.
The main objective of the paper is to investigate the feasibility
of the GTA-WAAM process to fabricate a refractory NbZr1 thin
wall structure. Comprehensive analysis of the associated micro-
structure and mechanical characteristics have been conducted
and correlated to understand the impact of different heat input
conditions. The structure of the paper is as follows: in Section 2,
the related works and background of the research will be inves-
tigated; Section 3 will introduce the experimental procedure,
including the WAAM setup, sample preparation, and analysis;
in Section 4, the microstructure analysis and mechanical prop-
erty results will be presented; in Section 5, the results will be
discussed comprehensively; and Section 6 will present conclu-
sions and the future path of the study.
2. Related Works
Welding of Nb and its alloys is challenging due to its high melt-
ing point, thermal conductivity (53.7 W m /C0 1 K), and reactivity
with environmental oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen at high tem-
peratures. Therefore, inert gas shielding or a high vacuum is
needed during welding. [17] There have been different studies
regarding the electron beam welding (EBW) of pure Nb.
Potukuchi et al. [18] studied the superconducting properties of
pure Nb and concluded that properties do not degrade due to
the EBW. Saha et al. [19] examined the feasibility of fabricating
a superconducting accelerator cavity. Das et al. [17] investigated
the in ﬂuence of different heat input conditions on the micro-
structure and mechanical properties of Nb-welded joints by
changing the beam current. The study found that the low-heat
input condition yielded better mechanical strength compared
to the other two conditions. Hajitabar and Naffakh-Moosavy [20]
studied the effect of welding current variation on the microstruc-
ture and mechanical properties of the NbZr1 joints. The tensile
strength of the weld joints was found to be 281 MPa, which is
almost half of the base metal. Gupta et al. [6] investigated the
microstructure and mechanical properties of the laser-welded
joint of NbZr1 with the addition of 0.1 wt% C. The welded
samples with larger laser power and welding speed exhibited
better strength and ductility.
There have been different studies on the fabrication of ultra-
ﬁne grain (UFG) Nb alloys due to their bio-compatible properties.
Niendorf et al.[5] investigated the relationship between the micro-
structure and mechanical properties of UFG NbZr1, focusing on
its biomedical applications. The study utilized coarse grain thick
hot-rolled NbZr1 and processed it by equal channel angular
extrusion. They found that the process increased the strength
and ductility of NbZr1. Rubitschek et al. [21] studied the micro-
structural stability of Nb alloys at elevated temperatures. They
investigated the effects of annealing treatment and high-
temperature low-cycle fatigue on the microstructural evolution
of NbZr1 and NbZr2.3. Another study [22] also investigated the
corrosion fatigue behavior of UFG NbZr2.3 alloy in body ﬂuid.
The alloy performed better compared to the common implant
material. The anisotropic behavior and crack propagation of
UFG NbZr2.3 alloy were studied at different temperatures by
Toker et al. [23] They considered impact strength and ductile to
brittle transition temperature as the main contributors to the
fracture behavior of the material and concluded that anisotropy
signiﬁcantly in ﬂuences the impact energy.
Limited research has been conducted on AM-Nb and its alloys.
Terrazas et al. [24] investigated the fabrication of high-purity Nb
using electron beam melting (EBM). The structure achieved
99.7% relative density, and both columnar and equiaxed grains
were observed perpendicular and parallel to the build plate,
respectively. The mechanical strength of EBM-Nb and wrought
reactor-grade Nb matched closely. Martinez et al. [25] investigated
the feasibility of producing pure Nb parts using EBM.
Microstructure characterization showed the presence of colum-
nar grain extending along the build direction and the presence of
higher dislocation density when compared with annealed Nb
sheet. In another research, Griemsmann et al. [26] manufactured
Nb using laser-based powder bed fusion (LPBF) considering dif-
ferent build-up rates. They were successfully able to manufacture

3. Experimental Section
3.1. Thin Wall Deposition
The experiments were performed using an in-house developed
GTA-WAAM system, as shown in Figure 1. It includes a six-axis
Fanuc ArcMate 120iC robot arm with a Fanuc R-J3iB controller, a
Miller Dynasty 400 GTA welding power source, and a generic
wire feeder. The torch travel speed (TS) was controlled by the
welding robot. The power source and wire feeder modulated
the welding current and the wire feed speed (WFS), respectively.
NbZr1 alloy wire (ASTM B392) with a diameter of 0.95 mm was
used for the deposition. The chemical composition of the NbZr1
wire is provided in Table 1. The welding voltage depends on the
spacing between the electrode tip and the substrate. To maintain
uniformity of the voltage value during the deposition, a constant
spacing of 5 mm was considered. Fixed process parameters
during the deposition are summarized in Table 2 .
To deposit thin walls, current, TS, and WFS were considered.
The near-optimal process parameters were identi ﬁed for deposit-
ing multilayer thin walls based on the trial-and-error analysis.
Argon– helium mixture was used as the shielding gas because
helium develops a stable arc. In addition, the arc energy is higher
when the argon– helium mixture is used compared to pure argon,
which results in higher heat input.[29] After the deposition of each
layer, the top surface was allowed to cool down to 100 °C before
depositing the next layer. The temperature was measured by a
hand-held device with a type K thermocouple by placing it on
the surface of the deposited layer. TS and WFS were kept con-
stant, while the arc current was changed. The process parameters
are summarized in Table 3 . The equations presented below are
utilized for quantifying energy density and heat input. Based on
their values, deposition conditions are categorized as low-heat
input (LHI), medium-heat input (MHI), and high-heat input
(HHI). Figure 2 shows the images of the WAAM NbZr1 deposits.
A total of 50 layers were deposited for each condition. The sub-
strate length was 80 mm. The height of the thin wall was 45, 40,
and 38 mm for LHI, MHI, and HHI conditions, respectively.
To measure the width, deposits were scanned using Hexagon
Romer Absolute Arm with an integrated laser scanner, which is a
portable coordinate measuring machine (CMM). The scanned
CMM data were converted to xyz format using the PC-DMIS
2020 R1 software. Then the data were analyzed using the
CloudCompare 2.7 software. From each deposit, cross-section
data were taken at eight locations at a spacing of 10 mm.
Within each cross-section, the data were measured at 10 loca-
tions. A total of 80 measurements were taken in a single deposit
and the data were averaged. [30]

3.2. Sample Preparation
To study the microstructural features and microhardness, ﬁrst,
the samples were cut using the metallurgical saw. Second, the
surfaces of the samples were ground using SiC abrasives (grit
sizes of 240 – 1200). Then metallographic polishing was per-
formed with a sequence of 3 and 1 μm polycrystalline diamond
suspensions. Afterward, they were polished with 0.05 μm colloi-
dal silica To reveal the microstructure, etching was performed
using a solution of 10 mL nitric acid (HNO 3), 10 mL hydrogen
ferricyanide, and 30 mL lactic acid. Nikon SMZ 1500 microscope
was utilized for low-magni ﬁcation images (up to 50 /C2 ), and a
Nikon Epiphot inverted microscope was used for higher magni-
ﬁcation images during OM. FEI Quanta 200 scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDX) and Hitachi SU 7000 ﬁeld-emission-SEM,
equipped with EDX, were used for the microstructure, chemical
composition, and fracture surface analyses. For the X-ray diffrac-
tion (XRD) analysis, a Rigaku Ultima IV machine was utilized.
Zeiss EVO MA15 equipment with a Bruker eFlash electron back-
scattered diffraction (EBSD) detector was used for the EBSD
analysis. For postprocessing and analysis of the EBSD data,
ATEX software [31] was used.
Microhardness tests were performed along the build direction
of the polished specimens at 0.5 mm intervals from bottom to top
with a 500 g load (Vickers diamond indenter) and a dwell time of
10 s. Buehler Wilson VH1202 microhardness tester machine
equipped with an integrated high-resolution camera and
Diamet software was used for the test. Mechanical strength
was investigated using the computer-controlled, uniaxial tensile
testing system (TestResources 810 E4 Electrodynamic Test
Machine). The ﬂat dog-bone-shaped subsized tensile specimens
were prepared from the deposited wall using wire electrical dis-
charge machining following the steps and dimensions speci ﬁed
in the literature [15] having a gauge dimension of (10.16 /C2 3.175
/C2 2m m3). For each condition, four samples were tested with
an elongation rate of 0.01 mm s /C0 1, which corresponds to approx-
imately 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1 engineering strain rate at room temperature.
Before the tensile test, the samples were polished to a grit size
of 600 to remove surface defects that may lead to the stress
concentration (localization) during the tensile test. To determine
the failure characteristics and evaluate the presence of anoma-
lies, the fractured locations and the failure modes were investi-
gated using the SEM.

Analysis
4.1. Microstructure Characterization
Figure 3 displays the OM images of MHI WAAM NbZr1. Large
columnar grains are formed along the build direction and span
several layers. The grains are also directed toward the center of
the deposit. Figure 3b– d shows the top, middle, and bottom parts
of the microstructure as indicated in Figure 3a. At the bottom
part of the deposit, a ﬁne cellular dendritic structure is formed.
Secondary dendritic arms (SDAs) are not well developed in that
region. In the middle and top sections of the deposit, a columnar
dendritic structure is present. However, columnar dendrites are
more elongated in the middle of the deposit compared to the top.
This occurs due to thermal gradient and heat accumulation in the
deposit during the WAAM process. Conduction through the sub-
strate is the major mode of heat transfer in the WAAM process,
which results in a higher cooling rate near the substrate region
and promotes ﬁne structure. As new layers are deposited, there is
a buildup of heat in the middle region making it dif ﬁcult for the
heat to dissipate. The cooling rate is slow and promotes a coarse
dendritic structure. However, the top layers do not get remelted
and dendritic growth is inhibited, which promotes smaller den-
drites. The yellow-marked region shows the microstructure of
individual beads at higher magni ﬁcation in Figure 3e. The blue
dotted line shows the layer interface between two individual
layers. Figure 3f shows the microstructure of the layer interface
and interlayer region at higher magniﬁcation. Layer interface and
interlayer regions can be identi ﬁed in the microstructure due to
their distinct morphology. Higher magni ﬁcation images of inter-
layer regions and layer interface marked in black are shown in
Figure 3g,h. The interlayer region consists of a dendritic
columnar structure. The primary dendritic arm and SDA are
prominent in that region. The layer interface consists of a ﬁner

To estimate the hardness, a Vickers microhardness test was per-
formed. The results are shown in Figure 8 . The hardness value
was measured from the substrate to the top of the deposit. The
black, red, and blue line represent the hardness of LHI, MHI,
and HHI conditions, respectively. The lowest hardness for
LHI, MHI, and HHI are 127.5, 131, and 132.5 HV respectively.
The highest values are 178.5, 185, and 198.5 HV respectively. The
hardness increases with minor ﬂuctuations in all three heat input
conditions as the deposition height increases. These drops and
peaks may be due to the microstructural changes at the layer
interface and interlayer region. The average hardness for the
LHI case is 146.6 /C6 13.1 HV, as shown in Figure 8b. In MHI,
the average hardness is 162.1 HV with a standard deviation

163.5 /C6 19.5 HV, which is the largest. To compare the mechani-

NbZr1 thin plate (R04261 ASTM B393) was also collected and
tested. Microhardness in WAAM specimens is higher than
the conventionally fabricated NbZr1, which is 121.5 /C6 2.4 HV.
However, a higher standard deviation in WAAM deposits for

5. Discussion
5.1. Correlation between Process and Microstructure
GTA-WAAM was employed to deposit 50 layers using three dif-
ferent heat input conditions. Although the same number of
layers was deposited, the wall height and width varied with heat
input conditions, as shown in Figure 2. As the heat input
increases, the molten metal temperature increases; surface ten-
sion and viscosity decrease. As a result, the molten material can
spread out more, resulting in a constant increase in bead
width.[35,36] It also results in narrower layer thickness as the
deposited material amount is kept constant. Due to the reason
mentioned earlier, the wall height was the largest in the LHI,
and the widest bead was the HHI.
Epitaxial columnar grain morphology aligned parallel to the
build direction is a common microstructure feature observed
in metal AM parts. This study also observed a similar microstruc-
ture, which is validated by EBSD analysis, as shown in Figure 6.
The binary Nb – Zr alloys form an isomorphous system, which
means Nb and Zr are completely miscible in both liquid and
solid states. As a result, only a single solid-solution phase grows
from the molten alloy in dendritic morphology. [37] Without suf-
ﬁcient solutes present, heterogeneous nucleation does not occur
in the solid/liquid interface, leading to epitaxial grain growth. [34]
Consequently, Zr is unable to re ﬁne Nb grains due to faster cool-
ing rates associated with the WAAM process. This phenomenon
could also be attributed to the directional heat transfer and large
temperature gradient along the deposition direction. When sub-
sequent layers are added, the molten pool comes in contact with
earlier deposited layers, and the heat ﬂows from the deposited
weld bead to the substrate, the direction of the maximum
temperature gradient. Therefore, the growth direction of the den-
drites is opposite to the direction of heat ﬂow, forming columnar
dendrites.[11] In addition, due to repeated thermal cycles, the den-
drites of prior deposited layers are partially remelted and grow in
size.[38] This repeats throughout the deposition process, forming
large dendritic columnar grains. Martinez et al. [25] also observed
columnar grain structures during the fabrication of Nb compo-
nents using EBM. When pure Nb was deposited by EBM,
Terrazas et al. [24] obtained a similar result with elongated colum-
nar grains extending across 20 layers. In welding cases, such as
EBW of the NbZr1 alloy, columnar grains are also developed. [20]
The layer interface depicted dissimilar microstructural
features compared to the interlayer region. This is due to the
repeated melting and cooling of the layer interface. Primary
and secondary dendrites are formed in the interlayer region
during solidi ﬁcation. Secondary dendritic arm spacing (SDAS)
has been measured using the following equation [39]

Figure 11 summarizes the calculated dendritic arm spacing at
different locations. Measurements are taken from the middle to
the top part of the deposit as SDA is not well developed at the
bottom. This is because the substrate act as a heat sink and heat
transfer at the bottom region is fast. This fast cooling rate
promotes cellular dendritic structure. [32] It has been identi ﬁed
that SDAS increases with the increase in deposition height.
This phenomenon is associated with the solidi ﬁcation process.
As more layers are deposited, there is a temperature build-up
along the deposition height, which lowers the cooling rate. As
a result, SDA becomes larger. [40] This ﬁnding is similar to the
other studies where dendritic arm spacing generally increases
with deposition height.[41] However, the top layers of the deposits
are not remelted and experience less thermal cycle. As a result,
continuous dendritic growth is opposed, resulting in the forma-
tion of smaller dendrites. Because of this characteristic, SDAS
decreases at the top of the deposits. The average SDAS also
decreases with the increase of heat input, as shown in
Figure 11. This may be due to the decrease in deposition height
with the increase of heat input. The thin wall height is 45 mm for
LHI and 38 mm for HHI. Due to comparatively lower height,
heat can dissipate faster through the substrate, which leads to
a lower SDAS value.
The EDS analysis in Figure 5 con ﬁrms that Nb is homo-
geneously distributed in the microstructure, and Zr is segregated
at the interdendritic region. Figure 12 shows the SEM image of
the interdendritic region at higher magni ﬁcation. Bright spots
can be identi ﬁed as precipitates in these regions. EDS elemental
mapping shows that there is a higher concentration of oxygen (O)
and Zr in these regions, which can be characterized as ZrO 2 pre-
cipitates. At higher temperatures, refractory metals are highly
prone to oxidation. In the WAAM process, even though the pro-
cess is inert, dissolved oxygen in the substrate and wire can form
oxides. These oxides can contribute to the strengthening effect of
the deposits. The XRD patterns mainly consist of pure Nb peaks.
Other studies of NbZr1 have also found similar results, and only
pure Nb peaks were found. [20,24]
5.2. Correlation between Microstructure and Mechanical
Properties
The hardness test provides a good indication of the localized
mechanical strength. The largest hardness value was found in
HHI, followed by MHI and LHI, as shown in Figure 8. There
is a relationship between SDAS and hardness value. As SDAS
decreases, the mean free path for the dislocation movement
decreases. Therefore, by the Hall – Petch effect, hardness is
expected to increase as SDAS decreases. [42] The average SDAS
was the lowest for HHI, as shown in Figure 11, and the highest
average hardness value is found in HHI. A similar attribute is
also observed in MHI and LHI deposits. In addition, there
has been an increasing trend of hardness value in the build direc-
tion as the wall height increases. Figure 12a – d con ﬁrms that
ZrO2 precipitates are formed in the microstructure. Due to
the thermal gradient, the cooling rate reduces as the deposition
height increases. As a result, more precipitates could be formed
with deposition height. These nanoscale precipitates increase the
hardness.[43] In addition to the increasing trend in the hardness
value, there is a signi ﬁcant disparity related to the heterogeneous
grain size formed within the deposited parts. The nonequilib-
rium thermal cycle, which involves repeated heating and cooling,
produces inhomogeneities. [44,45] However, this is not in the
scope of this study and will be investigated in future studies.
The mechanical properties of crystalline materials are strongly
related to the dislocation motion in the solids. Grain boundaries
act as a barrier to dislocation propagation. [46] The grain bound-
aries are almost parallel to the applied stress in the vertical tensile
specimens. Hence, the dislocation motion is not signi ﬁcantly
deterred by the grain boundaries. As a result, low yield strength
and extremely high ductility are expected. In this case, similar to
the Voigt model, the uniaxial tension along the vertical direction
applies almost the same deformation (elongation) to the vertically
columnar grains. Therefore, it will be an upper bound for the
stress level.[47,48] As for the uniaxial tension along the horizontal

6. Conclusion
In this study, the NbZr1 thin wall for three different heat input
conditions has been successfully fabricated using the GTA-
WAAM process. The processing, microstructure, and mechani-
cal behavior have been thoroughly investigated. The following
results are summarized: 1) In each heat input condition, colum-
nar dendritic grains are formed, and the grains are elongated
toward the build direction. No microstructural defects like poros-
ity and cracks are found in the microstructure indicating near
optimal process parameter has been achieved; 2) Nb is homo-
geneously distributed in the microstructure. There is composi-
tional variation across the dendritic and interdendritic regions.
Zr has been found segregated as ZrO 2 in the interdendritic
region; 3) The average microhardness increases with heat input
condition and the highest average value of 163.5 HV has been
measured in the HHI condition. There is an increasing trend
in the average microhardness value according to deposition
height. This is correlated with the SDAS; and 4) WAAM
NbZr1 shows a good synergy of strength and ductility compared
to the conventional and AM-NbZr1 structures. For the majority
of the tensile specimens, the fracture mechanism has been
identiﬁed as a ductile failure. The presence of subgrain has
contributed to the strength of the WAAM-NbZr1 structure.
It has been found that the mechanical properties vary consid-
erably in three of the deposits, which con ﬁrms anisotropy in the
structure. In addition, residual stress is also present since some
deformations were observed at the bottom of the substrate. An
in-depth study regarding anisotropic behavior investigation and
residual stress measurement is needed, which is the future scope
of the study.
