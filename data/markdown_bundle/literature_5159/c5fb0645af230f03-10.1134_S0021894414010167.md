HIGH-TEMPERA TURE MET AL MA TRIX COMPOSITES
UDC 539.4S. T. Mileiko
Abstract: This paper presents an overview of studies of creep—one of the most important charac-
teristics of high-temperature materials. A structural model of creep and methods of accelerated creep
tests are considered, and the eﬀect of structural parameters on the creep resistance is studied. The
problem of fracture toughness of metal matrix composites is analyzed. The possibility of designing
composites with the necessary balance of high-temperature strength, fracture toughness, and oxida-
tion resistance (heat resistance), in particular, composites operated at temperatures of 1400–1600◦C
is demonstrated by the example of a molybdenum oxide composite.
Keywords: metal matrix composites, creep, creep-rupture strength, high-temperature strength,
fracture toughness, heat resistance, oxide ﬁber, nickel superalloys, molybdenum.

INTRODUCTION
In a monograph [1], Rabotnov summed up his investigations of the creep of structural elements, including
experimental validation of the constitutive equations and solutions of typical problems of solid mechanics, charac-
terized by the creep property, which allowed the development of methods for evaluating the stiﬀness, stability, and
creep-rupture strength of structural elements and members. In a paper [2], written mainly by Rabotnov, the same
problems are solved for structural elements made of materials characterized by steadycreep. In essence, a paper [2]
is a course in the strength of materials that have the creep property.
It should be noted that in the 1960s–1970s, structural material was a kind of black box for experts in solid
mechanics. Rabotnov, discussing the future of solid mechanics, argued that it is associated with the ﬁlling of this
area of mechanics with a physical content.
The mid-1960s are characterized by the active development of materials science, relatively new interdisci-
plinary scientiﬁc ﬁeld which combines solid-state physics, solid mechanics, and solid state chemistry. In this period,
R. Cahn created the ﬁrst materials science magazine–the Journal of Materials Science, and A. Kelly performed
the ﬁrst studies of metal matrix composites, whose results, published in the Journal of Mechanics and Physics of
Solids [3, 4], laid the foundation for the science of composites (composite science).
It has been found that composites can be designed based on the properties of their components (ﬁbers,
matrices, interfaces between ﬁbers and matrix) and structural models that deﬁne the dependence of mechanical
properties (strength, fracture toughness, creep resistance, fatigue, etc.) on the structural parameters of the com-
posite. Constructing structural models has become one of the major problems of solid mechanics. Elastic models
of composites assuming an ideal interface and the absence of a layer between the ﬁber and the matrix were quickly
constructed; they set out in a number of well-known monographs, among which we note the classical works of Van
Fo Fy, summarized in [5]. Of great importance are the results of studies of the fracture toughness of composites,
especially composites with brittle ﬁber and brittle matrix (including reinforced plastics) performed in [6].Current

3. OXIDE-NICKEL COMPOSITES
In designing high-temperature composites, one should use matrices based on nickel alloys and ﬁbers having
rather high strength at temperatures up to 1200◦C [see Eq. (1)], and heat resistance, and also compatible with the
matrix, i.e., stable in a nickel matrix. Among such ﬁbers are oxide ﬁbers. However, polycrystalline oxide ﬁbers,
for which the production technology is well developed, do not have suﬃcient high-temperature strength. Single-
crystalline and eutectic ﬁbers obtained by melt crystallization using technologies based on the well-known Stepanov
concept [20] have a very high cost due to the extremely low rate of the corresponding technological processes carried
out at temperatures of about 2000
◦C.
Thus, the ﬁrst problem to be solved was to produce oxide ﬁbers with a high-temperature strength from melt.
Furthermore, the production technology should provide a suﬃciently high speed of production of ﬁber and, hence,
their low cost to allow their use in structural materials.
The internal crystallization method, involving crystallization of oxides in the channels of a molybdenum ma-
trix, has been developed for model composites [21], but later on it was used as the basis to develop a method for pro-
ducing ﬁber with a speed of one to two orders of magnitude higher than that using the well-known technologies [20].
The main mechanical characteristics of the ﬁbers (strength at room temperature and high-temperature
strength) are given in Figs. 3 and 4. It should be noted that, ﬁrst, the short-term strength of oxide ﬁbers is retained
at very high temperatures. Second, applying a coating to the surface of the ﬁber increases its strength severalfold
(see Fig. 3); the coating heals the possible surface defects of the ﬁber. Third, the high-temperature strength of
single-crystal ﬁbers of yttrium–aluminum garnet (YAG) and mullite, continuing up to a temperature of 1600
◦C, is
signiﬁcantly higher than the high-temperature strength of Nextel 720 polycrystalline ﬁber [22]. Fibers of this type
are used to produce nickel oxide composites.
The second problem arising in the preparation of oxide ﬁbers from melt is the interaction of sapphire ﬁbers
with the melt of the nickel matrix and their possible degradation [23].

As noted above, from the results of strength tests of the initial ﬁbers and ﬁbers etched from the matrix,
it was concluded in [23] that sapphire ﬁber may degrade in the melt of the nickel matrix. Therefore, the authors
of [23] erroneously believed that oxide ﬁbers cannot be used as a reinforcing material for nickel. Actually, surface
defects formed in the ﬁber due to its interaction with the melt are healed by the solidiﬁed matrix, provided that
the bond on the interface is stable, and therefore, the strength of the interface is great (α→ 1) [27].
The dependence of the creep resistance of nickel-oxide composites on the ﬁber volume fraction has a maxi-
mum. The task of designing the composites is to increase the eﬀective strength of the ﬁber and shift the maximum
toward larger ﬁber volume fraction by providing a strong bond at the interface. This leads to an increase in the
creep resistance and a decrease in the density of the composite, which is an essential parameter in the case of using
the composite as the material of a gas turbine rotor blade.

4. MOL YBDENUM OXIDE COMPOSITES
Initially, molybdenum oxide composites produced by internal crystallization [21] were considered only as
model structures; moreover, they contained oxide ﬁbers [20] used in refractory matrices (see Section 3). Later, it
has been found that if ﬁber composites contain some chemical substances, e.g., yttrium, heating of composites of
this type in air leads to the formation of a protective ﬁlm consisting of yttrium molybdate, whose vapor pressure
is several orders of magnitude lower than that of molybdenum oxide [30]. This results in a sharp decrease in the
oxidation of molybdenum (Fig. 8). This phenomenon can be used to produce heat-resistant and high-temperature
composites based on refractory matrices. In this case, the problems of balance between the high-temperature
strength and fracture toughness, topical for alloys based on molybdenum and niobium, become less signiﬁcant due
to the above-mentioned features of the fracture of composites with a metal matrix and brittle ﬁbers.
Figure9showscurvesofthecreep-r upturestrengthofacompositewitheutecticﬁbersandMo–Si–Balloy[31].
It is evident that at a fracture time of 100 h, the creep- rupture strength of the composite is signiﬁcantly higher
than the creep-rupture strength of the alloy, and the diﬀerence grows with increasing fracture period. Apparently,
at times of the order of several thousand hours, the creep-rupture strength of the composite and alloy will diﬀer by
a factor of approximately two. The relatively weak dependence of the fracture stress of the composite on time is
determined by the signiﬁcant contribution of the ﬁber strength to the creep resistance. Given that the density of
the composite is lower than the density of molybdenum alloys, its advantage over the alloys increases.
At suﬃciently high temperatures, compositesofthis type cannot be used in an oxidizingenvironmentwithout
a protective coating, similarly to nickel alloys. However, the composites considered are fundamentally diﬀerent from
molybdenum alloys: protected from the gas environment by the self-regulating coating, they can form compounds
that heal coating defects. The development of protective coatings for molybdenum oxide composites is a separate
problem, which is beyond the scope of this paper.

CONCLUSIONS
High-temperature metal matrix composites were considered. The results of the strength analysis of these
composite show that the use of structural models of creep of composites accelerates the design of high-temperature
composite structures due to the choice of the real parameters of the structure, acceleration of creep tests, and
optimization of the structure.
Currently developed composites with oxide ﬁber and a nickel-based matrix allow an approximately 100◦C
increase in the operating temperature compared to the corresponding temperature of existing and developed
nickel superalloys.
Metal matrix ﬁber composites used at high temperatures provide the necessary balance of the main charac-
teristics: high-temperature strength, fracture toughness at normal temperatures, and oxidation resistance at high
temperatures. The latter is particularly importantin the case of materials based on refractory metals.
Use ofa refractorymatrix andhigh-strengthﬁberscontainingchemicalagentsthat protectthe matrixagainst
oxidation allows an increase in the operating temperature of heat-resistant and fracture-tough composites to 1300
◦C
(in the future, to 1600◦C).
