ABSTRACT
Linear friction welding is a solid-state, near-net shape manufacturing method for metallic alloys which is
characterised by complex deformation and metallurgical actions at the weld interface. However, a lack of un-
derstanding of the welding parameter interaction and subsequent welding mechanisms is hindering the joint
integrity enhancement of dissimilar linear friction welding. In this study, we investigated the influence of various
process parameters on macro/micro-formation, microstructural evolution, and properties to establish optimal
welding conditions for the sound linear-friction-welded joint integrity of dissimilar superalloys, IN718, and the
powder metallurgy FGH96. Increased oscillation frequency or decreased applied pressure promoted continuous
dynamic recrystallisation and grain refinement, although discontinuous dynamic recrystallisation remained
dominant. Enhanced dissolution of the strengthening phases ( γ ′ phase on the FGH96 side and δ phase on the
IN718 side) was observed from the thermomechanically affected zone to the interface. The subsequent corre-
lation between the microstructure and mechanical properties indicated that solid – solution strengthening was the
dominant mechanism for enhancing interfacial bonding, which was promoted by mutual material deformation
on both sides. Accordingly, to achieve synergistic plastic deformation in dissimilar linear friction welding, an
optimisation strategy of welding parameter combination was proposed and validated by investigating hot
compressive dissimilar Ni-based superalloys. The results of simulations of sub-size workpieces showed that using
linear friction welding to manufacture bimetallic bladed disks, from conception to completion, was feasible. The
paper offers an integrated solution for the full-scale manufacturing of an IN718/FGH96 blisk using linear friction
welding based on microstructure – property interactions and relevant simulations, which can ideally serve as the
basis for future bimetallic bladed disk manufacturing.
1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys have become pivotal materials in hot-end
aerospace industry components, such as aero-engine turbine discs and
blades, owing to their excellent mechanical performance at elevated
temperatures [ 1 ]. Near-net-shape manufacturing technology, such as
additive manufacturing (AM) and linear friction welding (LFW) on
Ni-based superalloys, has received increasing attention in recent years.
AM is characterised by a relatively high processing temperature, an
intense temperature gradient, and rapid cooling, which can generate
large dendrites and metallurgical defects, such as pores, nonuniform
residual stresses and distortions, and cracks [ 2 ]. As a solid-state joining
process, LFW effectively overcomes the above shortcomings appearing
in AM and traditional fusion welding, enabling it to produce a refined
microstructure by its thermomechanical coupled effect without melting,
potentially providing improved strength at the welding line relative to
the base metals [ 3 ]. Moreover, differently from friction stir welding,
which requires a more rigid tool than welding material, or rotary friction
welding, designed for welding revolution components, LFW is charac-
terised by a periodic reciprocating linear motion. LFW has proved to be a

such as bladed disks (blisks) [ 4 ] ( Fig. 1 a). In Fig. 1 a, each blade structure
is joined to the hub separately by LFW, which significantly reduces the
material waste. In addition to the cost-effectiveness and high quality of
LFW, another major advantage of using LFW to fabricate blisks is its

LFW can be divided into the initial, transition, equilibrium, and final
alignment/cooling phases, as shown in Fig. 1 b. In the initial phase, two
workpieces are forced together, and the relative rubbing causes asper-
ities to wear down and become flattened under frictional heat, enabling
intimate contact to be achieved. With more heat generated via friction,
the interface temperature rises rapidly, leading to a decrease in the
material strength, resulting in slight plastic deformation in the transition
phase. As the friction process proceeds, a quasi-steady characteristic is
achieved in the equilibrium phase owing to thermal balance by
continuous heat generation and thermal dissipation near the interface.
This characteristic manifests as the continuous plasticised material
appearing and extruding as a flash, with the aid of the oscillatory motion
and applied pressure. Once a prescribed friction time or axial shortening
length has been achieved, two workpieces are aligned quickly with a
forging force applied, followed by dwelling for a certain time to
consolidate the joint. Along with all other friction welding processes, the
welding condition and resulting quality can be controlled by altering the
LFW process ’ s main welding parameters (pressure and oscillatory pa-
rameters). Further parameter combination optimisation can also result
in less material waste and higher overall efficiency. However, it is
impractical to optimise LFW conditions when manufacturing full-scale
blisks, suggesting the importance of fundamental research at the labo-
ratory scale. LFW investigation at the laboratory scale has mainly
focused on joint macro-formation, microstructure formation, mechani-
cal properties, and process optimisation ( Fig. 1 c). To date, researchers
have performed numerous fundamental studies on LFW of metal alloys,
including titanium alloys [ 5 , 6 ] and Ni-based superalloys [ 7 , 8 ].
Compared with using titanium alloys, using Ni-based superalloys can
realise reliable blisk service under more extreme, complex working
conditions. Therefore, an in-depth analysis of superalloys ’ LFW mech-
anism has gradually become a research focus in blisk manufacturing to
improve aero-engine performance. Over the past decade, several studies
have reported on the process – microstructure – property interaction [ 9 ,
10 ] and the underlying thermomechanical coupling mechanism [ 11 , 12 ]
in the LFW of the same Ni-based superalloy, offering valuable guidance
for LFW blisk fabrication.
Nevertheless, compared with same-metal welding, achieving suc-
cessful dissimilar welding is generally more difficult because of the

the thermomechanical conditions for each workpiece material under

dissimilar metals is required. In particular, a comprehensive under-
standing of the macroscopic and microscopic joint integrity for different
parameter combinations in a laboratory-scale study is of great impor-

Considering the above facts, in this study, we systematically and
thoroughly investigated the formation of macro-/micro-
structure – property interactions in dissimilar Ni-based superalloy weld-

investigated the effects of the processing parameters on the joint for-
mation, grain structure, twin evolution, and dissolution/precipitation of
the strengthening phases. The theoretical section describes the hot-
compression physical and numerical simulations conducted based on
newly discovered phenomenological features. Particular attention was
paid to certain aspects. First, the deformation mechanism pertaining to
the dissimilar joint integrity and the interfacial bonding mechanism for
dissimilar joining in LFW were elucidated. Second, the recrystallisation
and strengthening behaviours were identified, and their effects on the
joint ’ s mechanical properties were evaluated. Third, the transferability
of the optimised LFW conditions was assessed from laboratory scale to
full scale.

2.2. Linear friction welding
To join rectangular block workpieces of two Ni-based superalloys
with a height of 35 mm, a length of 16 mm, and a width of 8 mm, hy-
draulic drive LFW equipment (HSMZ-30) developed by the Harbin
Welding Institute, China, was used. Fig. 3 illustrates the experimental
setup for the LFW process. Fig. 3 a shows the LFW equipment compo-
nents and the magnified welding region where the reciprocating motion
with high frequencies ( f ) of the oscillatory part and the stable linear
feeding motion with high applied pressures ( P ) of the stationary part
were realised and controlled by the hydraulic servo system. In the cur-
rent study, IN718 and FGH96 superalloys were clamped on the sta-
tionary and oscillatory sides, respectively. In the current equipment
system, to avoid disturbances from unstable vibrations under different
combinations of applied pressure and frequency, a constant amplitude of
2 mm was recommended. As shown in Fig. 3 c, the time module precisely
controlled the change in welding parameters in different welding stages
and at the end of the welding process. The friction time was increased to
the set value, followed by the final alignment and pressure holding for
10 s in all conditions. Because the current study highlighted the effects of
different combinations of applied pressure and frequency, we did not
consider multi-stage pressures or different friction times. Five specified
welding parameter combinations were adopted based on a preliminary
study ( Table 2 ). The total shortening lengths for the three duplicate
welded joints were averaged.

3. Results and analyses
3.1. Macrostructure
Fig. 4 a shows the macro appearance of the joint for different welding
parameter combinations. The flash size increased with the increase in
applied pressure and oscillation frequency because of the increased heat
input. Regardless of welding parameters, the dissimilar IN718/FGH96
LFW joint showed a double-curved flash structure, and a representative
dissimilar LFW process shown in Supplemental Video SV1 exhibited its
formation. The flash surface changed from rough to smooth with an
increase in the shortening length, indicating the formation of a fresh
welding interface.
Fig. 4 b shows a cross-sectional view of the welded joint along the
oscillation direction. For the LFW joint of IN718/FGH96, the welding
zone of the joint could be divided into the welding centre zone (WCZ),
thermomechanically affected zone (TMAZ), and base metal (BM). The
heat-affected zone (HAZ) between the TMAZ and BM is usually not
marked because of its extremely narrow size [ 17 ]. The width of the
welding zone was smaller on the FGH96 side than on the IN718 side. At
higher pressures and frequencies, the widths of the TMAZ and WCZ
decreased because of the accumulated extrusion of the softened mate-
rials from both sides in the welding zone. A larger flash always occurred
on the IN718 superalloy side than that on the FGH96 superalloy side,
regardless of the welding parameters. In Fig. 4 b, the sectional centreline
parallel to the lateral centreline of the stationary FGH96 workpiece
exhibited a perpendicular relationship to the welding line in most cases;
however, inaccurate alignment resulted in slight tilting and misalign-
ment of the two workpieces, which was more pronounced at higher
pressures.
Fig. 4 c shows the ratio of the shortening length at each side to the
total shortening length in different conditions. The lower high-
temperature strengths of IN718 alloy resulted in more plastic deforma-
tion than the FGH96 alloy. However, the shortening length ratio of
FGH96 under the S1 condition accounted for ~14% of the total short-
ening length, lower than the S4 condition, although their total short-
ening lengths were close. This phenomenon was associated with
different thermomechanical histories caused by variations in applied
pressure and frequency combinations. The S1 condition with the lowest
specific power input ( Supplemental Table S1 ) would produce a lower
temperature rise rate than other cases. However, the interfacial peak
temperature and high-temperature duration to allow metal deformation
also affected the shortening length at each workpiece. As reported in
LFW of various metal alloys previously [ 18 – 20 ], a higher pressure with a
low frequency usually results in a low deformation temperature of the
interfacial zone, suggesting a lower interface temperature in the S1
condition than in the S4 condition. The two abovementioned aspects
were responsible for the lower shortening length ratio of the FGH96
alloy in the case of S1 at the same limited friction time. From another
perspective, the results suggest that the S4 condition facilitated the
occurrence of plastic deformation on both sides, whereas in the S1 joint,
the incomplete extrusion of the original material was also evident
( Fig. 4 a).

3.3. Properties
Fig. 9 shows the microhardness profile across the interface of the
welded joint obtained using different welding parameters. The average
microhardness of BMs was approximately 334 ± 16 HV for IN718 and
495 ± 10 HV for FGH96. Overall, the hardness values decreased from
the BMs to the minimum in the region of the TMAZ and then sharply
increased from the TMAZ to the welding centreline, reaching the
maximum value in the centreline of the WCZ. On the FGH96 side, the
decrease in hardness was significant in the TMAZ. Altering the welding
parameters did not significantly affect the microhardness of the FGH96
side. However, on the IN718 side, the variation in microhardness in the
TMAZ was minor compared with that in the BM. The minimum hardness
location away from the welding line on the FGH96 side was farther than
that on the IN718 side, aligning with the broader TMAZ area on the
IN718 side of the joint. The maximum values for joints S3 and S4 were
significantly higher than that for joint S1 at the centreline. At the TMAZ,
the S4 joint showed the most significant reduction in microhardness
evolution on the IN718 side.
Fig. 10 a shows the average ultimate tensile strengths for different
welding parameters and relevant fracture positions. Under the adopted
welding parameters, most joints fractured in the base metal of the IN718
side, except for joints S1 and S4. Fig. 10 b displays the macrographs of
the three distinct fractured positions existing in all the tested samples.

4.2. Recrystallisation mechanism and interface bonding behaviour
DRX behaviour is typically regarded as the main grain refinement
mechanism in the LFW of Ni-based superalloys and is mainly divided
into discontinuous dynamic recrystallisation (DDRX) and continuous
dynamic recrystallisation (CDRX). As reported in the literature [ 36 , 43 ],
CDRX usually occurs during the hot working of Ni-based superalloys
with a relatively low SFE, whereas DDRX may also occur when certain
conditions are met, including a specific strain rate and temperature.
CDRX is characterised by the progressive rotation of grains with
numerous LAGBs generated inside the prior grain (i.e., without the grain
nucleation process); however, nucleation is the key phenomenon in
DDRX, where recrystallised grains can exist at the prior grain boundaries
and twin grain boundaries [ 30 ].
Fig. 14 shows EBSD images at the TMAZ of joints S1, S3, and S4 with
point-to-point (local) and point-to-original (cumulative) misorientations
inside the grain. The predominant mechanism of DDRX could be
observed in the grain structure of the TMAZ on the IN718 side, where
the grain boundaries were extensively serrated and bulging, with many
grains consisting of HAGBs. Smaller grains were frequently observed at
the boundaries of larger grains, which are typical necklace-like struc-
tures, supporting the occurrence of DDRX. Nevertheless, the number of
LAGBs inside HAGBs was notably higher in S4 than in joints S1 and S3.
The formation of a considerable fraction of LAGB inside the HAGB is a
prominent sign of CDRX, which is necessary for the progressive rotation
of the grains to form recrystallised grains. This observation was even
clearer from the local and cumulative misorientations of the grains. S4
exhibited a relatively high temperature during welding. Although the
total deformation degree was lower than those of S1 and S3 ( Table 2 ),
the set deformation condition still enabled the co-occurrence of DDRX
and CDRX in S4. Therefore, the temperature played a significant role in
determining the mechanism of DRX during LFW. A similar scenario was
also observed in the interfacial region, where DDRX was the dominant
recrystallisation mechanism, and CDRX became increasingly obvious as
the processing temperature increased ( Fig. 15 ).
The interfacial regions of the two alloys showed similar recrystalli-
sation behaviour regardless of the different phase compositions. The
dissolution of γ ′ was believed to be the main reason for the similar
recrystallisation phenomenon. As reported by Xie et al. [ 30 ], CDRX
played a major role in the deformation of one γ ′ -strengthening super-
alloy at subsolvus temperatures because of the limited ability of grain
boundary migration. The formation of new grain nuclei by DDRX was
enabled by the rapid migration of grain boundaries caused by unpinning
from the γ ′ particles, as shown in Fig. 5 b. To examine the mixing ele-
ments shown in Fig. S3 , the diffusion distance was measured via line
scanning at the interface. It was seen that the diffusion distance of both
S1 and S3 was limited, being within a distance of serval grains ( < 10 μ m);
however, in S4, a diffusion distance of more than 20 μ m was seen,
arising from the enhanced processing temperature, as stated previously.

4.4. Transferability from laboratory-scale results to full-scale
manufacturing
To date, studies have investigated the LFW of various high-
performance alloys for blisk manufacturing and their proc-
ess – microstructure – mechanical property interactions by experimentally
analysing the undersized welding assembly. Determining the optimal
LFW process parameters can provide technical guidance for the fabri-
cation of blisks with large-scale parts using LFW. However, the direct
application of optimal parameters based on these fundamental experi-
ments remains risky. This is because of the influence of the frictional
surface size effect (i.e., the workpiece geometry and the corresponding
relationship between the oscillation amplitude/direction and friction
surface dimensions) [ 12 , 49 ] and the effects of tool behaviour [ 50 ],
which still have not been systematically studied. Numerical simulations
can be of significant assistance for further optimising the parameters for
real blisk fabrication. This necessitates the establishment of a feasible
means of combining basic experimentation and numerical simulations in
the LFW manufacturing of dissimilar Ni-based superalloy blisks, as
shown in Fig. 18 , which are currently under development.
According to the current work and numerical simulations, visualising
the performance process of a full-scale blisk using LFW helps in exam-
ining the effectiveness of the optimum LFW process parameters for the
final tailoring of the relevant parameters. As shown in Fig. 18 , an
experimentally validated, basic computational model coupling heat and
deformation was used to calibrate the reliability of the material
constitutive model, contact and friction model, and boundary condition
loading, as deployed in recent studies [ 12 , 51 , 52 ]. In this study, a
counterfeit blisk structure comprising a hub and multiple blades was
designed. According to the preliminary computational model, the
joining process of one blade onto the hub structure by LFW could be
visualised, and Fig. 18 shows the physical fields involving deformation
and temperature under welding conditions S1 and S3. The
joint-shortening length at S1 was insufficient, lowering bonding quality
owing to the incomplete extrusion of the original oxides and contami-
nates (e.g., S1 in Fig. 18 ) or the incomplete bonding at the joint corner

shown in Fig. 18 . Detailed macro material deformation histories on both

From the present study, it was found that by taking advantage of the
flow stress behaviour at different temperature and strain rates, the
appeared synergistic plastic deformation on both superalloys is benefi-

property. This, together with numerical modelling, would provide a
feasible way to optimise the manufacturing process of bimetallic blisks
with superior properties through LFW. More interestingly, such a
strategy also shows a significant potential in expanding in near-net-
shape manufacturing, especially in circumstances requiring precise
dimension or deformation control. By optimising welding parameters to
realise the synergistic plastic deformation in dissimilar metal friction-
based welding (including LFW and RFW), we can directly control the
deformation on both sides, from which the material waste could be
sufficiently suppressed while maintaining joint efficiency. This approach
can greatly enhance the design capabilities and flexibility in near-net-
shape manufacturing of hybrid structures. Notably, the occurrence of
synergistic deformation in dissimilar metal LFW process has certain
advantage over traditional AM, especially in the manufacturing of large-
scale hybrid structures, because it enables an interfacial zone with a
dense structure and excellent performance. This phenomenon can also
be inferred from a recent publication [ 53 , 54 ] where it was reported that
a refined interfacial microstructure and enhanced joint property were
obtained in LFW of additively manufactured components. Therefore, the
finding can shed light on the development of more efficient, cleaner, and
resilient turbomachinery, which is much needed to address the climate
emergency in areas of transportation or power generation.
5. Conclusions
The conclusions are as follows:
(1) A larger flash size always occurred on the IN718 superalloy side
than on IN718 and FGH96, regardless of the increased applied
pressure or oscillation frequency. A higher applied pressure
resulted in a relatively narrow welding zone owing to the greater
extrusion of materials near the friction interface. Thus, within a
similar axial shortening length, the parameter matching of high
frequency and low pressure was more likely to promote the
plasticised deformation on the FGH96 side.
(2) An increase in the applied pressure or a decrease in the oscillation
frequency led to a decrease in the average grain size at the
interface and a reduction in the dynamic recrystallisation degree.
Discontinuous dynamic recrystallisation was the dominant
mechanism for grain refinement at the welded joint for both al-
loys; however, continuous dynamic recrystallisation became
much more prominent when a lower pressure and a higher
oscillation frequency were applied, leading to a higher interfacial
temperature.
(3) The volume fraction and volume size of the δ phase in IN718 and
the secondary γ ′ phase in FGH96, as the main strengthening
phases in the respective alloys, decreased as the distance
approached the welding line with full dissolution at the interfa-
cial region. The mutual diffusion of elements on both sides was
evident, but a higher temperature, such as that in the joint ob-
tained in this study using 200 MPa and 40 Hz, resulted in a

manufacturing of dissimilar blisks using linear friction welding.

and excellent mechanical strength, so it can serve as a new near-
net-shape manufacturing guidance for hybrid structures, such as
special shape components in turbomachinery.
