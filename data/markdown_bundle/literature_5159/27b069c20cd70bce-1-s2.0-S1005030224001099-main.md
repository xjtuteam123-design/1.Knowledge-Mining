Alloy composition design usually contributes to eliminating cracking in Ni-based superalloys during addi- 
tive manufacturing (AM). However, a detailed understanding of each solid solution element in the crack- 
ing susceptibility of Ni-based superalloys during AM is still missing. Thirteen newly designed alloys are 
considered to investigate the combined effect of solid solution elements on cracking susceptibility. The 
behaviors of solidiﬁcation cracking, liquation cracking, and solid-state cracking were analyzed by the 
microstructural characterization and thermodynamic calculations. The results showed that W and Mo 
cause the formation of high melting-point carbides at grain boundaries (GBs), which increase solidiﬁca- 
tion cracking susceptibility. Moreover, W and Mo lead to a slightly higher solidiﬁcation cracking index 
(SCI) compared to Co, Cr, and Re. In the successive solidiﬁcation and remelting process, the borides en- 
riched in W, Mo, and B around GBs will cause grain boundary segregation and liquation cracking. W and 
Re extend the freezing range (FR) and exacerbate the segregation of Al and Ti in the inter-dendritic re- 
gions, contributing to the formation of eutectics. Similarly, complete or partial melting of the eutectic can 
induce liquation cracking during the thermal cycling in AM. The solid-state cracking susceptibility can be 
reduced by solid solution elements, especially Re and Co. In summary, compared to Co, Cr, and Re, W and 
Mo exacerbate the cracking susceptibility.

1. Introduction 
Ni-based superalloys are extensively utilized in aerospace sec- 
tor, particularly for complex components such as engines and gas 
turbines [1–3] . Additive manufacturing (AM) offers several unique 
advantages over conventional manufacturing, including near-net 
shape manufacturing design freedom, minimal material waste, 
and high ﬂexibility [4] . However, the high cooling rate (10 0 0 to 
10,0 0 0 K/s) [5] during the AM, along with the complex compo- 
sition and high γ′ volume fraction of Ni-based superalloys, make 
them highly cracking susceptibility [6] . This poses challenges for 
the AM of precipitation-strengthened Ni-based superalloys with 
relatively high contents of Al and Ti, resulting in only a limited 
number of Ni-based superalloys having a low cracking susceptibil- 
ity, such as Inconel 625 [ 7 , 8 ] and Inconel 718 [ 9 , 10 ].

Cracking, including solidiﬁcation cracking [ 11 , 12 ], liquation 
cracking [ 13 , 14 ], and solid-state cracking [15] , is a major limitation 
in AM of Ni-based superalloys [16] , and has been extensively in- 
vestigated. Researchers have sought to mitigate cracking by adjust- 
ing process parameters, such as laser power, scanning speed, hatch 
distance, scanning strategies, and layer thickness. Previous studies 
demonstrated that optimizing the process parameters can improve 
microstructure and signiﬁcantly reduce crack susceptibility in su- 
peralloy 247LC [17] , and laser traveled in a tortuous path based on 
the Hilbert or Peano–Gosper area ﬁlling curve reduced crack length 
density in CM247LC [18] . It is noted that the cracking was not 
eliminated. The solidiﬁcation and liquation cracking were signiﬁ- 
cantly reduced by using induction heating to preheat the substrate 
for Mar-M247 and CM247LC alloys. Various studies have also ex- 
plored post-processing treatments like hot isostatic pressing (HIP) 
to eliminate cracks [ 19 , 20 ]. However, Carter et al. [21] highlighted 
the limitations of HIP in eliminating surface cracks. It should be 
noted that the complete elimination of cracks through parameter

optimization and post-processing treatment is not universally ap-

Therefore, suppressing crack formation through composition 
design appears to be a more viable approach. Extensive studies 
have been conducted on the inﬂuence of the γ′ forming elements, 
Al and Ti, in Ni-based superalloys fabricated by AM. LW4275 Ni- 
based superalloy that contained 5.1 wt.% Al but no Ti was printed 
and free of cracks. But the alloy had somewhat inferior creep 
properties, compared to conventional alloys such as IN738 and 
Rene80. A new experimental alloy which contained 1.29 wt.% Al 
and 2.22 wt.% Ti was also printed and free of cracks, but somewhat 
lower creep performance than CM247LC [22] . For precipitation- 
strengthened Ni-based superalloys containing high contents of Al 
and Ti that still suffer from cracks, a tighter design of the alloy 
composition should be considered. The precipitation of γ′ phase 
increases residual stress [23–26] and reduces ductility [27] , mak- 
ing the alloys prone to cracking during AM [28] . 
Another signiﬁcant contribution to high-temperature mechani- 
cal properties derives from solid solution strengthening (SSS). SSS 
occurs when the force required for dislocation to overcome the 
barrier caused by the elastic stress ﬁeld around the solute atom 
[29–31] . Elements such as Re , W, Mo, Cr, and Co have proven to 
be effective in strengthening the γ matrix. While increasing the 
strength of Ni-based superalloys fabricated by AM, it is essential to 
consider the effect of solid solution elements on cracking behavior. 
However, there are limited studies on the combined impact of Co, 
Cr, W, Mo, and Re on cracking behavior. It is necessary to quantify 
the inﬂuence of solid solution elements on cracking susceptibility 
and analyze the mechanisms of crack generation. 
This work aimed to elucidate the mechanism of cracking be- 
havior in Ni-based superalloys with different content of solid so- 
lution elements during AM. The solidiﬁcation cracking index (SCI), 
solid-state cracking (SSC), the solidiﬁcation path, and the number 
of precipitated phases were calculated by Thermo-Calc software. 
The area percentage of cracks in Ni-based superalloys fabricated by 
AM was counted to investigate the effect of Co, Cr, W, Mo, and Re 
on cracks. The results can provide insight into the chemical com- 
position design of Ni-based superalloys fabricated by AM.

2.2. Characterization 
Cube samples with dimensions of 16 mm × 16 mm × 20 mm 
were manufactured by DED to observe the microstructure for each 
alloy. The samples were cut by electrical discharge machining 
(EDM), ground polished, and electrolytically etched by the solu- 
tion system of 170 mL H3 PO4 , 10 mL H2 SO4 , and 15 g Cr2 O3 at 
a 5 V direct current for 5–10 s. The microstructures were ob- 
served by a ﬁeld emission gun scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
TESCAN MIRA) accompanied by an energy-dispersive spectrome- 
ter (EDS, Oxford Ultim MaxN) system. The segregation coeﬃcients 
between the inter-dendrite regions and the dendrite core regions 
were measured using an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, SHI- 
MADZU EPMA-1600) system. Three points were taken for each 
sample at inter-dendrite and dendrite core regions. A Bruker elec- 
tron backscattered diffraction (EBSD, Nordlys Max3) system and 
transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200X) were 
used to identify types of carbides. The EBSD dates were generated 
by HKL Channel 5 Mambo software. 
2.3. Cracks analysis 
The cracks were quantiﬁed using digitized optical micrographs 
(OM, Leica DMC4500). The longitudinal plane, parallel to the de- 
posited direction, was selected to quantify the area percentage of 
cracks. Three planes of each alloy were taken at 2 mm intervals 
from the edge to the center and named as the ﬁrst plane, the sec- 
ond plane, and the third plane. The Image-Pro-Plus was then em- 
ployed to calculate the area percentage of cracks per plane. The 
average of the three planes was taken as the area percentage of 
cracks for each alloy.

3.1. Quantiﬁcation of cracks 
The cutting position and cutting method of the samples are 
shown in Fig. 2 (a). The crack morphologies of longitudinal sections 
for ZGH-5 alloy in three planes are shown in Fig. 2 (b). The crack 
content of each alloy decreases gradually from the edge to the in-

3.2. Detailed characterization of cracking 
In order to conﬁrm the effect of solid solution elements on so- 
lidiﬁcation cracking, ZGH-5 (most cracks) and ZGH-7 (least cracks) 
alloys were selected to analyze. Fig. 3 (a, b) shows the SEM im- 
ages of solidiﬁcation cracks with carbides in ZGH-5 and ZGH-7 al- 
loys, respectively. The size of the carbides in solidiﬁcation cracks 
at GBs is from 2 to 4 μm. Fig. 3 (c) shows the high-angle annu- 
lar dark-ﬁeld scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF- 
STEM) image of blocky carbides in ZGH-7 alloy that are commonly 
found in additive-manufactured Ni-based superalloys. The darker 
carbide (A) enriched the elements of W and Mo is generally found 
around the brighter carbide (B) enriched the element of Ti in the 
corresponding EDS mapping analysis. Selected area diffraction pat- 
terns (SADP) conﬁrm that both the carbides are the MC. Smaller Ti- 
rich carbides ( Fig. 3 (d)) are diffusely distributed in inter-dendritic 
regions and have no effect on crack formation. These carbides with 
the size of 0.5–2 μm are identiﬁed as MC carbides by EBSD analy- 
sis, as shown in Fig. 3 (e). 
EPMA mappings presented in Fig. 4 depict that the liquation 
cracking caused by melted borides at the GBs were decorated by 
segregated liquid ﬁlms enriched with Mo, Cr, W, and B, depleted 
in Co and Cr, and uniformly distributed in Re elements. Cracks 
can nucleate at the interface between the γ phase and eutectic. 
There is a deformation incompatibility due to different lattice pa- 
rameters. The eutectic is not strongly bonded to the substrate and 
is susceptible to liquation cracking owing to thermal stress dur- 
ing the process of AM, as shown in Fig. 5 (a). It can be seen from 
Fig. 5 (b) that the liquation cracking in the inter-dendrite regions 
or at GBs due to eutectics can pass through many cladding layers. 
Fig. 5 (c) indicates that there are signiﬁcant differences between the 
tested alloys (ZGH-7, ZGH-10) with respect to the quantity of the 
eutectic. It is obvious that a substantial amount of the eutectic 
forms in ZGH-10 alloy, which implies that the solid solution ele- 
ments affect the quantity of eutectic. EPMA mappings in Fig. 6 dis- 
play the chain borides enriched with B, Cr, W, Mo, and a small

emerge around the eutectic. The element of Co is enriched in front 
of eutectic and borides.

is ﬁrst proposed in the casting, and then was further applied in the 
Ni system, shown as follows:

where TS is the temperature of onset of solidifying, and TL is the 
temperature of end of solidifying on cooling. The DSC thermogram

4. Discussion 
4.1. Solidiﬁcation cracking 
Solidiﬁcation cracks are caused when high melting-point car- 
bides serve as physical obstacles, restricting liquid ﬂow down liq- 
uid channels to feed shrinkage, and experiencing the forces of so- 
lidiﬁcation shrinkage and thermal contraction at the terminal stage 
of solidiﬁcation [36] . Based on the chemical composition of ZGH- 
5 alloy and ZGH-7 alloy, non-equilibrium thermodynamic calcula- 
tions based on the Scheil–Gulliver model were performed by us- 
ing the Thermo-Calc software to simulate the temperature of pre- 
cipitated phases, including carbides and borides. The solidiﬁcation 
path curves show that the high melting-point carbides precipitate 
in ZGH-5 and ZGH-7 alloy at 1335 and 1305 °C, respectively, as 
reported in Fig. 10 . The higher precipitation temperature of car- 
bides in ZGH-5 alloy than ZGH-7 alloy suggests that carbides have 
a longer time to grow, and act as a barrier earlier, resulting in a 
more pronounced effect in obstructing liquid ﬂow. Moreover, the 
semisolid at the GBs possesses little strength because the grains 
were not yet bonded together ﬁrmly but are still separated by 
the grain boundary liquid, and the semisolid also had little duc- 
tility because the fraction of liquid is not enough at the terminal 
stage of solidiﬁcation. Solidiﬁcation cracking that occurs at GBs can 
seriously deteriorate performance, because they are subjected to 
greater tensile stresses, resulting in greater susceptibility to cracks. 
In this study, the MC carbides to restrict liquid feeding are en- 
riched with Mo and W at GBs. Similar to Ref. [37] , Re is not the 
forming element of MC carbide in the superalloys. 
The solidiﬁcation cracking index proposed by Kou [32] was em- 
ployed for analysis. The criteria take into account the strain rate of 
the solidifying and the feeding rate of the liquid. The SCI can be 
described as follows:

4.2. Liquation cracking 
In Ni-based superalloys fabricated by AM, liquation cracking 
usually occurs in the heat-affected zone [41] . When the component 
is rapidly heated below the liquidus temperature, low melting- 
point phases and constitution are melted such as eutectic causing 
the appearance of continuous and discontinuous liquid ﬁlms [42] . 
During the cooling, the ﬁlm suffered a tensile force due to the so- 
lidiﬁcation shrinkage and thermal contraction of the deposit. Under 
this force, cracks can occur around the liquid ﬁlms [ 43 , 44 ]. 
W and Re can extend the FR, and exacerbate the segregation 
of Al and Ti in the inter-dendritic regions, which promotes the 
amount of eutectics and borides [45] . These low melting-point so- 
lidiﬁcation products can enhance liquation cracking susceptibility. 
Fig. 13 shows the formation process of eutectics and surrounding 
precipitated phases during solidiﬁcation. Al and Ti segregate in the 
inter-dendritic regions to form the Al/Ti-rich aera. In the front of 
the area, the liquid phase is rich in Cr, W, and Mo, which con- 
tributes to the formation of carbides, then borides, and ﬁnally eu- 
tectics [46] . Co is not the forming element in borides and eutec- 
tics, and their fast solidiﬁcation rate prevents Co from diffusing to 
form an elemental enrichment layer around eutectics and borides, 
as shown in Fig. 6 . 
Compared to carbides, borides can be considered as low 
melting-point phases, as seen in Fig. 10 . The borides can precip- 
itate in ZGH-5 and ZGH-7 alloy at 1269 and 1178 °C, respectively. 
It is well known that B can increase GB cohesion and limit damage 
at GBs under creep conditions [47] . However, the borides enriched 
W, Cr, and Mo act detrimental and promote liquation cracking as 
they contribute to liquid ﬁlms at GBs [48] . The segregated ﬁlms 
caused by borides allow the liquid to remain stable to a relatively 
lower temperature, resulting in liquation cracking during the depo- 
sition of the subsequent layers [18] . The schematic illustration of 
liquation cracking formation by borides is shown in Fig. 12 (b). The 
low melting-point borides are precipitated in the residual liquid 
after carbides. During the thermal cycle, the temperature reaches 
the melting point of the borides, resulting in the formation of liq- 
uid ﬁlms. The force accumulated in liquid ﬁlms can be transmitted 
via solids but not by liquids. Additionally, the force becomes larger 
than the yield strength of liquid ﬁlms, resulting in the formation 
of cracking paths [ 43 , 49 ]. 
4.3. Solid-state cracking 
In contrast to the solidiﬁcation cracking and liquation cracking 
which involve both solid and liquid phases, the solid-state crack- 
ing is associated with cracking entirely in the solid state and less 
in quantities. During the AM of Ni-based superalloys, the solidiﬁed 
grains not only suffer rapid-heat-cycles induced thermal stress but 
also considerable phase-transformation stress which is attributed 
to the substantial precipitation of γ’, and other phases. The re- 
peated thermal cycles as the laser beam traverses can be regarded 
as the intrinsic heat treatment promoting the formation of γ’, and 
other phases. The solid-state cracking occurs at a lower temper-

5. Conclusions 
The combined and individual effects of solid solution elements 
on cracking Ni-based superalloys were investigated during the pro- 
cess of AM. The following key conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) W and Mo are prone to the formation of high melting-point 
carbides that have a larger size of 2–4 μm. At the terminal 
stage of solidiﬁcation, when the liquid feeding was obstructed 
by the carbides, and promoted solidiﬁcation cracking formation 
at GBs. 
(2) W, Mo, and Cr can form borides around eutectic and at GBs. 
During the thermal cycle in AM, the temperature reached the 
melting point of the borides, resulting in the formation of liquid 
ﬁlms. During the cooling, the ﬁlm suffered a tensile force can- 
not be transmitted by liquids. Under the force, liquation crack- 
ing can occur at GBs. 
(3) W and Re can exacerbate the segregation of Al and Ti in the 
inter-dendritic regions and extend the FR, and therefore pro- 
mote the amount of eutectic. 
(4) Solid solution elements reduce the SSC and improve the resis- 
tance to solid-state cracking, especially Re and Co.
