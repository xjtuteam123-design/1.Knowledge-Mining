In order to develop reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels potentially suitable for 
fusion reactor applications, the effect of principal alloying elements Cr, W and V on the micro- 
structural evolution, toughness and high-temperature creep strength was systematically investi- 
gated for relatively simple Cr-W-V steels. Based on the results on the simple steels, two kinds 
of martensitic 9Cr steels with low and high levels of W were alloy-designed: 9Cr-IWVTa and 
9Cr-3WVTa. They exhibited excellent toughness and high-temperature strength. Irradiation 
hardening was the smaller for the reduced-activation 9Cr-1 WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa steels than 
for the conventional 9Cr-1MoVNb and it was smaller for the low W steel than for the high W 
steel. It is concluded that from the aspect of toughness the 9Cr-1WVTa steel is the most 
promising alloy for the first wall and blanket structures which will be operated at low tempera- 
tures below 773K (500°C). Another 9Cr-3WVTa steel is the most promising in terms of high- 
temperature creep strength for the future power reactor structures which will be operated at 
temperatures higher than 773 K. 
KEY WORDS: fusion reactors, reduced activation, ferritic-martensitic steels, 9Cr 
steels. microstructural evolution, toughness, high-temperature, strength, creep, irra- 
diation hardening, tungsten additions, vanadium additions, chromium additions 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Ferritic/martensitic steels have been con- 
sidered as candidate materials for the first 
wall and blanket structures of demonstration 
(DEMO) and commercial fusion reactorsc1), 
Primary reasons for use of ferritic/martensitic 
steels are higher resistance to swelling, 
higher thermal conductivity and lower thermal 
expansion compared to type 316 stainless steel 
(SS). 
Since the US Department of Energy (DOE) 
Panel'') on Low Activation Materials set a 
goal of the development of fusion reactor 
materials that satisfy at least the Class C 
limit for low-level nuclear waste in the early 
1980's, the development of low activation 
materials has been promoted in the US, Euro- 
pean Communities and Japan(3). The low- 
level nuclear waste was classified by the US 
Nuclear Regulatory Commission into three 
classes; Class A, B and C. Class A is such 
extremely low activation materials that the 
activation decays to an acceptable level dur- 
ing site occupancy, and involves impurity- 
free silicon carbide. Class B is intermediate 
between Class A and C and involves V 
alloys containing low activation alloying ele- 
ments. Class C waste is defined as the ac- 
tivation which decays to an acceptable level 
in more than 100 yr but less than 500 yr, 
which simplifies special waste storage of 
highly radioactive first wall and blanket 
structure after reactor service. Steels con- 
taining low activation alloying elements ap- 
pears to be handled as Class C waste, because 
solvent Fe meets the Class C limit(4).

4. Alloy Design of Martensitic 9Cr Steel 
for Improving Strength and Toughness 
The creep rupture strength of the 9Cr-W 
steels increased but their toughness decreased 
with increasing W concentration, suggesting 
that the simultaneous optimization of creep 
rupture strength and toughness is very dif- 
ficult by varying the concentration of W alone 
in the framework of the simple 9Cr-W steels. 
It is also noted that the creep rupture strength 
of the 9Cr-V steels was much lower than 
that of the 9Cr-W steels. Therefore, in the 
present research the improvement of creep 
strength was attempted by micro-alloying for 
the 9Cr-1W steel which exhibited an excellent 
toughness and the improvement of toughness 
was attempted for the 9Cr-4W steel which 
exhibited an excellent strength. 
For reducing the microstructural evolution 
in the 9Cr-1W steel during creep for improv- 
ing creep strength, fine MC carbides are pro- 
mising for pinning lath boundaries effectively

Cr-Mo steels, the Va group elements, V, Nb

well known that the addition of small amount

added in the present steels by analogy with 
Ni base superalloys. 
For improving the toughness of the dual 
phase 9Cr-4W steel, the &ferrite should be 
eliminated. According to the phase stability 
diagram in Fig. 2, decreasing the W concentra-

centration from 4 to 3%. Thus the elimina- 
tion of d-ferrite from the dual phase 9Cr-4W 
steel by decreasing W concentration to 3% is 
expected to result in an improved toughness 
without any decrease in creep rupture strength. 
Furthermore, similar to the case of the 9Cr- 
1W steel described before, an addition of 
small amounts of V and Ta for MC precipita- 
tion and B for interfacial segregation along 
lath boundaries would also increase the 
strength of the 9Cr-3W steel.

5. Microstructure and Mechanical 
Properties of Optimized 9Cr-1WVTa 
and 9Cr-3WVTa Steels 
The 9Cr-1WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa steels 
were observed full martensitic. The average 
grain size of prior austenite was ZOpm, the

pm. The carbides of about 0.1pm or less 
were distributed preferentially along the lath 
boundaries, which were similar to those of 
9Cr-1MoVNb steel and were refined compared 
to the simple 9Cr-W steels. The refinement 
of the microstructure was due to the small 
addition of V and Ta which formed the fine 
MC carbides. 
The DBTT was 204 and 226K for the

amount of W. The DBTT of the simple 
9Cr-W steels was 210 and 230K at 1 and 3%W, 
respectively (Fig. 6). The lowered DBTT in 
the 9Cr-1WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa steels was 
due to the refinement of the martensite lath 
subgrains and carbides described above. The

8. Optimum Compositions of Reduced 
Activation Martensitic 9Cr Steels 
for Application to Fusion Devices 
As described in the previous section, irra- 
diation behavior of martensitic steels changes 
from hardening to softening at around 773 K. 
This suggests that the alloy designing concept 
should be in accordance with the operating 
temperature of the first wall and blanket 
structures in fusion reactors, i. e. lower or 
higher than 773 K. Because irradiation harden- 
ing causes an increase in DBTT, enough 
toughness will be the primary factor for the 
steels used at temperatures below 773 K. On 
the other hand, enough high-temperature 
strength will be the primary factor for the 
steels used at temperatures above 773K. 
According to the design concept of Steady 
State Tokamak Reactor (SSTR)(3’) which is a 
demonstration type fusion power reactor de- 
signed at JAERI, the blanket structure is com- 
posed of a reduced-activation martensitic steel 
8Cr-2WVTa which is cooled by pressurized 
water. The temperature of the water at 
the outlet side in the blanket is designed 
at 598K (325°C) and a part of the blanket 
structure reaches as high as 723-773 K (450- 
500°C). Therefore, the primary factor for the 
development of reduced activation ferritic/ 
martensitic steels for the first wall and 
blanket structures should be fracture tough- 
ness. 
The martensitic 9Cr-1WVTa steel showed 
an excellent toughness in the as-tempered 
condition and even after long-term thermal 
aging as well as after neutron irradiation. 
This steel showed much lower DBTT than 
the 9Cr-1MoVNb in the as-tempered condition. 
The high-temperature strength is as high as 
that of 9Cr-1MoVNb. Therefore, it is con- 
cluded that the 9Cr-1WVTa is the most pro- 
mising in view of the toughness. Another 
martensitic 9Cr-3WVTa steel is promising in

the future power reactor structures which 
will be operated at temperatures above 773 K. 
IV. CONCLUSIONS 
(1) In the Cr-2W steels, the optimum con- 
centration of Cr was 9-10% from the 
viewpoints of toughness and high-tem- 
perature strength. The formation of 6- 
ferrite should be avoided, because this 
phase results in the deterioration of both 
toughness and creep strength. 
(2) The creep rupture strengthof the 9Cr- 
W steels increased but their toughness 
decreased with increasing W concentra- 
tion. The 9Cr-V steels exhibited poor 
creep rupture strength far below that of 
9Cr-1MoVNb steel and poor toughness 
after aging. 
(3) Two kinds of martensitic 9Cr-WVTa 
steels with low and high levels of W 
were alloy-designed by taking into ac- 
count higher toughness and creep 
strength ; 9Cr-1WVTa and 9Cr-3WVTa 
steels. The low-W steel 9Cr-1WVTa ex- 
hibited an excellent toughness as shown 
by much lower DBTT than 9Cr-1MoVNb 
and high creep strength as high as 9Cr- 
1MoVNb. The high-W steel 9Cr-3WVTa 
exhibited an excellent creep strength 
much higher than 9Cr-1MoVNb and good 
toughness as high as 9Cr-1MoVNb. 
(4) Irradiation hardening was smaller in 
the reduced-activation 9Cr-1WVTa and 
9Cr-3WVTa steels than in conventional 
9Cr-1MoVNb and it was smaller in the 
low W steel than in the high W steel. 
The recovery of irradiation hardening 
occurred over the temperature range from 
673 to 873 K. 
(5) For low temperature application below 
773K (500°C), the 9Cr-1WVTa steel was 
the most promising from the standpoint 
of enough toughness. For high tempera- 
ture application above 773 K, the 9Cr- 
3WVTa steel was the most promising in 
terms of enough creep strength.
