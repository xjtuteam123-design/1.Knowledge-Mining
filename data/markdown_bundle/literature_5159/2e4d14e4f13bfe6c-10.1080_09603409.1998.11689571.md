The development of high-temperature titanium alloys has been dominated by the requirements of aero­
space industries. Initially, improvements in tensile strength and creep properties were the main interest. 
Gas inlet temperatures in aero-engines have increased continuously and, nowadays certain components 
in gas turbine engines that are made out of titanium alloys, such as compressor discs and rotor blades, 
approach operating temperatures as high as 550°C. As a result of alloy development, near-a alloys 
capable of operating temperatures up to 600°C are commercially available. These alloys are intended to 
partially replace heavier nickel-base superalloys, e.g. in the compressor of gas turbine engines and 
much work has been directed towards understanding high-temperature fatigue, creep-fatigue inter­
action and oxidation behaviour of titanium alloys. It has been recognized that alloy composition, heat 
treatment and microstructure all influence strongly the high-temperature properties of titanium alloys. 
This review focusses on the relationship between microstructure and high-temperature fatigue behav­
iour of titanium alloys. It will cover mainly conventional (a + [3) alloys and near-a alloys which have 
been optimized with respect to both creep and high-temperature fatigue properties.

1. INTRODUCTION 
The development of titanium alloys has largely resulted 
from the aerospace industries' need for new materials 
with higher strength to weight ratios at elevated tempera­
tures. For a recent overview on the use of titanium in the 
aerospace industry see ref. 1. Whereas the targets set for 
early alloy development were modest and mainly 
focussed on tensile and creep strength, increased fatigue 
performance at high temperatures has received more 
attention for the most recent alloys, and titanium alloys 
have advanced from the earlier rather simple (a + 13) 
alloys such as Ti-6Al--4V to modem complex alloys. 
Whereas Ti-6Al--4V has a rather limited maximum use 
temperature en of about 315°C, the most recent near-a 
alloys such as IMI 834 and TIMETAL 1100 were 
designed for a maximum operating temperature of 
6ooocz.3. These alloys were mainly intended to replace the 
heavier nickel-base superalloys, e.g. in the latter stages of 
compressors of gas turbine engines, where both creep and 
fatigue loading during service have to be considered. 
There is a huge amount of literature demonstrating the 
close relationship between microstructure and mechani­
cal properties of titanium alloys. Most of these data have 
resulted, however, from low-temperature tests. The 
approach taken here is first to address briefly the main 
microstructural effects. Features specific for high-tem­
perature fatigue behaviour of titanium alloys are dis­
cussed in more detail later. Next, the cyclic stress-strain 
response and fatigue crack growth behaviour under well 
controlled isothermal high-temperature conditions are 
reviewed. These results are used to draw some first con­
clusions regarding fatigue life. Actual high-temperature

environmental attack and metallurgical instabilities are 
the main reasons why the mechanical strength of high­
temperature titanium alloys cannot yet be fully exploited.

linked to the microstructure. As a result of the allotropic 
transformation from the low temperature, hexagonal 
close-packed (hcp) a-phase to the body-centred cubic 
(bee) 13-phase various microstructures can be obtained,

Based on alloy composition titanium alloys can be

are the (prior) 13 grain size and the volume fraction, size

Generally speaking, finer microstructures reduce the slip

as fracture toughness and tensile ductility are also influ­

to represent such a compromise, as good creep properties 
resulting from a high volume fraction of lamellar trans­
formed 13-phase can be combined with fine primary a­
phase which effectively retards crack initiation and growth 
of small surface cracks 7. However, excellent fatigue prop­
erties can be obtained with 13-processed lamellar 
microstructures as well, provided that a fine 13 grain size 
can be preserveds. A detailed account of processing of 
high-temperature titanium alloys is beyond the scope of the 
present paper. Other aspects relevant for optimum high­
temperature fatigue behaviour of titanium alloys such as 
microstructural stability or oxidation resistance will be 
addressed in later sections. 
As compared to the bee 13-phase, the hcp a-phase is 
rather brittle and highly anisotropic, i.e. elastic modulus, 
yield stress and deformation mode all depend strongly on 
the orientation of the grains with respect to the external 
stress axis. Consequently, texture present in the sample 
can have a pronounced effect on the mechanical proper­
ties, and the effects of texture have been studied exten­
sively6,9-11. If the texture is such that a-grains experience 
high stresses normal to the basal plane, rapid crack 
advance can occur by cleavage fracture on this plane.

Cyclic softening in titanium alloys is generally 
attributed to the formation of narrow slip bands as a 
result of shearing of the ordered Ti3Al precipitates. 
Figure 2 demonstrates that pronounced planar slip is 
seen in the primary a phase of bimodal IMI 834, even if 
the alloy is fatigued at a temperature as high as 600°C. 
The factors influencing slip character have been studied 
in detail in binary Ti-Al alloys and in (a + 13) alloys 
such as Ti-6Al-4 V, see ref. 12. The dislocation slip 
character also influences other properties as will be dis­
cussed in later sections. Aged (a + 13) alloys usually 
show cyclic softening. Cyclic hardening is observed only 
when the 13-phase deforms plastically. For the near-a 
alloy Ti-5522-Si, initial cyclic softening followed by 
cyclic hardening has been reported13. Transmission elec­
tron microscopy (TEM) indicated that while the material 
cyclic strain softened, shearing of Ti3Al precipitates 
caused the formation of slip bands in the primary a 
phase. Cyclic hardening was observed when slip bands 
saturated and dislocation interactions became dominant. 
Recent studies on the near-a alloy IMI 834 show that in 
tests run at 500°C the number of cycles at which the 
transition from initial cyclic softening to cyclic harden­
ing occurs depends strongly on the initial ageing heat 
treatment of the alloy14. Again, TEM indicated that the 
precipitation of Ti3Al promotes the formation of intense 
slip bands on basal planes. The amount of cyclic soften­
ing observed in high-temperature titanium alloys further 
depends on the type of microstructure. Generally speak­
ing, cyclic softening is most pronounced in globular 
microstructures with strong texture, which eases spread­
ing of localized slip. Phase boundaries in texture free 
lamellar structures promote more homogeneous defor­
mation modes and thus tend to show less cyclic softening.

(LCF) behaviour of the (a + 13) alloy Ti-6246, continu­

20-500°C was observed. TEM observations and analysis 
of the hysteresis loops indicated that shearing of Ti3Al 
indeed operates over the whole temperature range. 
However, it was concluded that microscopically different 
mechanisms control cyclic softening. At room tempera­
ture, cyclic softening as a result of particle shearing is

4. FATIGUE CRACK GROWTH AT HIGH 
TEMPERATURES 
A variety of parameters such as microstructure, age­
hardening, oxygen content and texture all affect fatigue 
crack propagation in titanium and its alloys, see refs 6 
and 17. In this review, general trends common to both 
room temperature and high temperature fatigue crack 
growth will be presented first. Note, however, that 
fatigue crack growth at high temperatures is the result of 
complex interactions between fatigue, creep and environ­
mental contributions. Furthermore, emphasis will be on 
those aspects peculiar to fatigue crack propagation above 
about 400°C. Thus, environmental effects that can be 
attributed to hydrogen embrittlement will not be covered 
in detail as these are reported to become negligible in 
most alloys at temperatures above about 300°C. It is 
appropriate to note, however, that recent studies's indi­
cate that hydrogen effects can be present in some alloys 
up to temperatures of about 500°C. The effects of hold 
times and load-time profile on fatigue crack growth rate 
will be addressed later. 
4.1 Effect of phase morphology 
The influence of microstructure on FCGR has been stud­
ied extensively for (a + 13) alloys and near-a alloys. For 
a recent review see ref. 6. As shown in Figure 3, it is 
generally observed that in case of long cracks, coarser 
lamellar microstructures show lower FCGR and higher 
threshold values of the cyclic stress intensity factor as 
compared to fine equiaxed structures. Similar to other 
alloys, microstructural effects on FCGR are more promi­
nent in the near threshold regime than at mid crack 
growth rates. For the sake of clarity, the high crack 
growth rate region, where microstructural effects again 
become apparent has been omitted from Figure 3. The 
large influence of microstructure on FCGR in the near 
threshold regime is closely correlated with dislocation 
slip character and slip length12• The lower fatigue crack 
growth rate in aligned lamellar structures can be rational­
ized as follows. Fatigue crack growth is quite rapid 
within a single colony, where the a-platelets have the 
same crystallographic orientation. However, adjacent 
colonies are often orientated such that the preferred 
crack path differs significantly between the individual 
colonies. This causes crack deflection and results in a 
high amount of crack path tortuosity and thus rough frac­
ture surfaces. It is argued that surface roughness reduces

that this accounts for the large microstructural effects 
seen at low !1K values9. This also explains the effect of

4.2 Environmentally assisted fatigue crack growth 
At temperatures above about 400°C, oxidation processes 
are accelerated significantly and one expects that 
increasing the testing frequency decreases FCGR as indi­
cated in Figure 3. Frequency effects on FCGR of the 
near-a alloy TIMETAL 1100 have been studied recently 
in great detaiP2.24. It was concluded that the effect of 
oxidation on FCGR is established very rapidly, as 
environmental effects were observed even at frequencies 
as high as 10 Hz22. Further, recent work by Parida and 
Nicholas24 indicated that environmental effects are 
absent in this alloy only if tested at frequencies as high 
as 100-200 Hz. As shown in Figure 4 the effects of an 
air environment on FCGR at high temperatures are most 
pronounced in the near threshold regime. Various mecha­
nisms can account for the acceleration of FCGR as com­
pared to an inert environment. It is quite difficult to 
determine which mechanisms actually govern fatigue 
crack growth and often a combination of various mecha­

4.3. Small suiface cracks 
To predict the life of fatigue crack growth limited 
structures, the behaviour of small fatigue cracks has to 
be considered and research efforts have focussed on 
understanding small crack growth effects. Most of the 
results on small crack growth behaviour were obtained in 
room temperature tests. The few studies of small fatigue 
crack growth at high temperatures generally indicate 
behaviour similar to that observed in room temperature 
tests. Firstly, in binary Ti-Al alloys, (a + (3)-alloy and in 
near-a alloys small surface cracks propagate faster than 
long through-cracks do17·26-28. This can be understood 
mainly based on the argument that roughness-induced 
crack closure is absent in small surface cracks. For 
instance, it is observed that crack growth at room 
temperature in the near-a alloy IMI 834 is essentially 
independent of R-ratio which indicates a closure free 
mode of crack growth within the short crack regime27 • 
Similarly, Spence et al. 28 studying small crack growth in 
the near-a alloy IMI 829 found essentially no influence

both small and long fatigue crack growth requires that 
the surface properties of a component are different from 
the bulk properties. Consequently, suitable modification 
of the surface appears as an attractive means of improv­
ing the fatigue behaviour of components. Gray et al. 29 
studied the effect of shot peening on fatigue behaviour 
of the (a + f3) alloy Ti-6Al-4V and the near-a alloy 
Ti-6242. They showed that the increase in endurance 
limit at room temperature results mainly from the drastic 
retardation of the propagation rate of small cracks as a 
result of the compressive stresses introduced in the near 
subsurface layer during shot peening. However, in 
fatigue tests run at 500°C it was observed that small 
crack growth is much faster and endurance limit is lower 
in shot peened samples than in electropolished ones. 
This detrimental effect of shot peening on high tempera­
ture fatigue behaviour can be understood based upon the 
observations: ( 1) that the residual stresses are relieved 
almost completely after an annealing heat treatment done 
at 500°C; and (2) that the high dislocation density intro­
duced during shot peening is not reduced significantly 
during annealing at 500°C. Thus, it can be concluded 
that during fatigue at high temperature the residual com­
pressive stresses are also relieved, and that the high dis­
location density introduced by shot peening accounts for 
the increase in FCGR observed. In contrast, an increase 
of the fatigue strength at 550°C was obtained for the 
near-a alloy Ti-6242 when shot peening was followed by 
a recrystallization anneal. This resulted in a fine 
equiaxed microstructure in the near surface region, 
whereas the bulk material still had a lamellar microstruc­
ture. Thus, an optimized microstructure which has low 
FCGR for small surface cracks can be combined with 
low FCGR for long cracks and good bulk creep properties. 
5. FATIGUE LIFE 
Fatigue life of smooth samples in general can be divided 
into three stages: (1) a crack initiation stage, (2) the 
growth of small surface cracks and (3) the growth of a 
long crack until final failure occurs.

initiation

are normally run in stress control. Titanium alloys tend 
to show their true endurance limits in such tests. The 
fatigue strength is usually governed by the resistance to 
crack initiation and often the influence of microstructure 
on fatigue strength is correlated directly with the slip 
length. This explains why fine microstructures show 
improved HCF fatigue properties as compared to coarse 
lamellar structures. This trend is shown in Figure 5 and

6. CREEP-FATIGUE INTERACTION 
Components used at high temperatures usually experience 
a rather complicated load pattern involving hold periods at 
peak stress or strain. If stresses are sufficiently high, a sig­
nificant amount of creep strain can be accumulated during 
such a loading cycle and both creep and fatigue can con­
tribute significantly to damage. As addressed in the pre­
ceding sections, much work has focussed on understand­
ing the processes involved in fatigue and creep behaviour 
of high-temperature titanium alloys. However, only a 
limited amount of data are available obtained under test 
conditions that closely resemble those experienced by an 
actual high-temperature component. In general, such tests 
indicate that simple models which just sum up the contri-
. butions of fatigue and creep in a linear manner fail to 
predict life time satisfactorily. Thus, more sophisticated 
models that account for the synergistic interaction 
between creep and fatigue are required. 
6.1 Effect of dwell 
The effect of a hold time at maximum load on FCGR 
usually is referred to as the dwell effect. Certain titanium 
alloys show a drastic increase in FCGR during dwell 
periods if tests are run around room temperature. If 
fatigue life is controlled by crack propagation, this dwell 
effect can reduce fatigue life significantly. The effect of 
hold time on FCGR is most pronounced if an aligned 
a-colony microstructure is present, see ref. 36. Such a 
microstructure can result from poor control of processing 
conditions. Further, dwell sensitivity is highest if a 
strong texture is present such that the crack can grow 
predominantly parallel to the basal plane. Dwell effects 
are often attributed to hydrogen diffusing into the crack 
tip region, where high hydrostatic stresses are present 37. 
In situ TEM studies 38 on a-titanium have in fact shown 
that brittle hydrides can form in the stress field ahead of 
a crack tip, and that under suitable conditions crack 
advance through the hydrides occurs by a cleavage 
mechanism. However, this issue is still controversial as 
dwell effects at room temperature have been observed 
under test conditions where a contribution of hydrogen 
was ruled out39. For a more detailed review on dwell 
effects of titanium alloys at around room temperature see 
ref. 40.

6.2 Influence of load-time profile 
In fatigue tests it is usually observed that asymmetric 
'slow-fast' waveforms with slowly increasing load 
portions are more damaging than either symmetric or 
'fast-slow' load-time profiles43. Ghanem and Foerch44 
studying the effects of test frequency on FCGR in the 
near-a. alloy TIMETAL 1100 at 593°C indeed observed 
that 'slow-fast' testing (19 s loading, 1 s unloading) 
resulted in an significant increase in FCGR as compared 
to symmetrical tests with a cycle time of 20 s or 'fast­
slow' (1 s loading, 19 s unloading) testing. The effect of 
load-time profile was most pronounced at low fl.K 
values. It was concluded that during the slowly increas­
ing loading part of the cycle extensive creep damage 
occurs. However, it was also observed that introducing a 
300 s hold period at peak load not only had a much more 
damaging effect but also changed the fracture mode to 
intergranular failure involving prior 13 grain boundaries. 
This change in fracture mode was not seen in the 'slow­
fast' tests. The authors concluded that the creep affected 
zone ahead of the crack tip during a 19 s/1 s fatigue test 
presumably was not large enough to encompass the prior 
13 grain boundaries and only accelerated quasi-cleavage 
failure resulted. Similarly, accelerated FCGR in fatigue 
tests with slow rise time have been reported for Ti-6242 
at 400°C32. However, in tests with a very slow rise time 
of 4 min the crack propagation rate was found to even 
exceed the FCGR obtained in fatigue tests with the 
equivalent hold time. In this case, it was argued that 
cracking of the oxidized surface during loading plays an 
important role.

FATIGUE CONDITIONS 
Operating conditions of high-temperature components 
used in aero-engines often involve complex cyclic thermal 
transients in combination with mechanical strain cycles. 
With increasing operating temperatures the combined 
thermal and mechanical loading cycles already often 
limit the fatigue life of certain turbine components. 
High-temperature titanium alloys are, however, capable

combine high strength with low elastic modulus. Thus, 
for conventional titanium alloys that have a maximum 
operating temperature of about 450 to 500°C, thermal 
stresses induced during a thermal cycle can totally be 
compensated for by elastic strains and should not lead to 
TMF failure. Consequently, previous TMF studies 
involved mainly continuous fibre reinforced titanium 
matrix composites, which have much higher temperature 
capabilities than conventional titanium alloys, for an

addressed46. However, with the advent of high-tempera­

has to be taken into account for more conventional alloys 
as well. Figure 9 shows that the cyclic flow stress of IMI 
834 determined in isothermal tests drops significantly 
when the test temperature is raised to 600°C. Hence, ther­

and thus eventually cause TMF damage of a component.

mechanical strain either increasing (in-phase) or decreas­

amplitude is almost identical to that observed in isother­

isothermal and TMF tests directly results from the fact 
that almost identical microstructures are obtained under 
both test conditions, i.e. a planar dislocation slip mode 
prevailed at any temperature in the TMF cycles. Planar 
dislocation slip also dominates up to 600°C in IMI 834 
with a bimodal microstructure, c.f Figure 2. Conse­
quently, stress-strain behaviour of samples with such a 
microstructure was also found to be similar under TMF 
and isothermal conditions, respectively. However, when 
the maximum temperature in a TMF cycle is increased to 
650°C a distinctly different microstructure is established 
and the stress-strain response in the TMF tests no longer 
equals that seen in isothermal tests run in the corre­
sponding temperature range47. Generally speaking, reli­
able prediction of stress-strain behaviour under service 
conditions from any accelerated laboratory tests requires 
that similar microstructures are established in both cases.

8. FACTORS LIMITING THE APPLICATION OF 
TITANIUM ALLOYS AT HIGH TEMPERATURES 
As a result of the relatively fast cooling rates used during 
processing, high-temperature titanium alloys are nor­
mally not metallurgically stable, and the formation of 
precipitates during long-term high-temperature exposure 
may significantly affect the mechanical properties. 
Further, at temperatures of 600°C, which is the intended 
service temperature of the most recent near-a alloys, 
oxidation becomes important as rapidly growing oxide 
scales form that are non-protective. Degradation of the 
mechanical properties as a result of the reaction with 
oxygen is indeed considered as one of the factors limit­
ing the life time of high-temperature components made 
out of high-temperature titanium alloys if these are used 
at temperatures exceeding about 550°C.

8.2 Microstructural stability 
Even if suitable coatings that could protect the matrix 
material from oxidation were available, the effects of 
long-term ageing on mechanical properties still have to 
be considered. Madsen et al. 42 reported on the 
microstructural changes occurring during prolonged 
ageing of the near-ex alloy TIMETAL 1100 at the pro­
posed service temperature of 593°C. It was observed that 
ageing results in the formation of two types of precipi­
tates. Incoherent silicide (TiZr)6Si3 was found to nucle­
ate predominantly on ex platelet boundaries. Furthermore, 
ordered Ti3Al nucleated homogeneously throughout the 
matrix. During ageing both types of precipitates coarsen 
and it was observed that the Ti3Al precipitates remained 
coherent with the matrix. Similar results have been 
obtained for other near-ex alloys63. Shearing of the co­
herent Ti3Al precipitates leads to the formation of narrow 
intense slip bands which drastically decrease room 
temperature ductility63,64. At high test temperatures, 
however, dislocation cross-slip is promoted and tensile 
ductility is less affected by ageing. As regards fatigue 
crack growth, interpretation of the results obtained is less 
clear. An increase in fatigue crack growth rate at room 
temperature and a slight decrease in fatigue crack growth 
rate at 593°C was observed if averaged samples are 
compared to unaged ones64• This is in contrast to results

The foregoing discussion has shown that the long-term 
use at elevated temperatures of titanium alloys is currently 
limited by both environmental degradation as a result of 
the inward diffusion of oxygen and by microstructural 
changes occurring during ageing. Consequently, long-term 
use of high-temperature titanium alloys is currently limited 
to temperatures of about 550°C. 
9. CONCLUSIONS 
Much effort has been directed towards optimizing tita­
nium alloys for high-temperature applications, and dras­
tic improvements in maximum use temperature were 
achieved over the past decades. High-temperature 
fatigue behaviour became more of a concern for recent 
alloy developments, and the close link between micro­
structure and fatigue properties is now well documented. 
As a result of this research effort, titanium alloys are 
available now that have both excellent creep and fatigue 
properties. If high-temperature titanium alloys are to 
replace heavier nickel-base superalloys for components 
operating for extended time periods at temperatures of 
about 600°C three issues have to be addressed. Firstly, 
environmental degradation as a result of the poor oxi­
dation resistance of most titanium alloys affects crack 
nucleation and can cause premature fatigue failure. 
Microstructural instability appears to be another impor­
tant factor that significantly limits long-term high-tem­
perature use. Finally, damage mechanisms observed 
under creep-fatigue conditions or in thermomechanical 
fatigue tests are often different from those seen in more 
conventional fatigue tests. Hence, the excellent mechani­
cal properties of the most recent high-temperature tita­
nium alloys reported from short-term laboratory tests at 
elevated temperatures are of limited use for predicting 
fatigue life under service conditions.
