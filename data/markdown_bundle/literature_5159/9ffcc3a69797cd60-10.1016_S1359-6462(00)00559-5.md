Introduction
Recently, considerable research has been focused on developing new alloys with higher temperature
capabilities than Ni-base superalloys (1–3). Intermetallic compounds based on the platinum group
metals (PGMs) have a unique combination of environmental resistance and high melting temperatures,
and are useful in attaining these requirements (4). One approach is to engineer alloys with analogous
microstructures to the Ni-base superalloys. In such a microstructure, a face centred cubic (f.c.c) matrix
is strengthened by ordered cubic (L1
2) precipitates (5). Near-coherent interfaces between the two phases
result in excellent creep resistance (6). Alloys based on Pt, were selected for a development programme,
which has, as its main purpose, to develop alloys with exceptional resistance to oxidation and hot
corrosion, and temperature capabilities at least 200°C higher than those of existing Ni-base superalloys.
Research at the National Research Institute for Metals (NRIM) in Japan has recently focused on
developing Ir and Rh-based refractory superalloys, analogous to the Ni-base superalloys (3,7–9). Inter
alia, Ir-Nb and Ir-Zr alloys with two-phase microstructures consisting of an f.c.c matrix and L1
2
coherent precipitates were reported to have flow stresses of around 200 MPa at 1800°C, which are
comparable to the strongest tungsten alloys (8).
During a preliminary investigation into the phase relations and properties of several Pt ternary
systems, it was found that Al additions were essential to protect the alloys from oxidation (1).
Additionally, Pt
3Al, which has an L1 2 crystal structure at high temperature, has been reported to have
a compressive yield strength in excess of 600 MPa at temperatures up to 750°C (10). It may therefore
be possible to engineer Pt-Al based alloys with
g/g9 microstructures that retain high-strengths at
high-temperature. Subsequent work has therefore focused on ternary Pt-Al-X alloys.
The Pt-rich end of the binary Pt-Al phase diagram is shown in Figure 1 (11). A two-phase (Pt) 1
Pt3Al phase field occurs between 76 and 88 at.% Pt. Two different crystal structures of Pt3Al have been
reported. The high-temperature form of Pt 3Al, which is stable above about 1290°C, has been reported
to have an ordered cubic (L12) crystal structure, while the low-temperature allotrope has a tetragonally
distorted L12 structure D09C (12). An intermediate form of Pt 3Al has also been reported, although this
allotrope of the phase is extremely difficult to isolate (13).
This paper reports the effects of various ternary substitutional alloying additions on the high-
temperature compressive strengths of the Pt-Al-X alloys located in the two-phase (Pt) 1 Pt3Al region.

The effects of these additions on the oxidation behaviour of the alloys has been reported elsewhere (14).
Experimental Procedure
The compositions of the alloys that were investigated are shown in Table 1. Ingots of the alloys were
produced by arc-melting under argon. The alloys that were selected were based on research that was
carried out into the phase relations of these systems (1). The amount of Re and Ru added was limited
to about 3 at.%, since the preliminary results indicated that higher alloying additions of these elements
resulted in microstructural instability. The formulations selected were those that provided maximum
room-temperature hardnesses, and resisted coarsening after annealing at 1350°C for up to 400 hours
under argon. The alloys were annealed for 96 hours at 1350°C. Samples for microstructural examina-
tion, and cylindrical compression samples were cut using spark erosion.
The microstructures of the samples were examined optically and using scanning electron microscopy
(SEM). Phase compositions were determined using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) with pure
elemental standards. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out on a Siemens D500 diffractometer with
Mo radiation.

The approach adopted was a stepped compression test, starting at 1350°C, down to 850°C in
decrements of 100°C. The compression tests were conducted under argon on samples in the heat-treated
condition. A 15 minute soak time was applied at each temperature prior to testing. Cylinders of diameter
6mm, and height 10mm were loaded at each temperature at a strain rate of 10
24/s until the 0.2% proof
stress was reached, and then unloaded. This was done to limit the amount of plastic deformation during
testing. Some work hardening is likely to have occurred, which may have increased the strength
readings obtained, especially in the lower temperature range.
Results
Typical microstuctures of all the alloys tested are shown in Figure 2. The nominal Pt 83:Al14:Re3,
Pt82:Al14:Ti4,P t 82:Al14:Ta4 and Pt 82:Al14:Cr4 alloys all had microstructures consisting of coarse,
primary Pt3Al surrounded by a fine two-phase eutectic-like mixture of a (Pt) matrix and fine particles
of Pt3Al. The two-phase structure in the Pt 83:Al14:Re3 alloy (Figure 2a) was considerably finer than in
the other alloys. Fine martensite-like laths were observed in isolated areas of the primary Pt 3Al in this
alloy. The microstructure of nominal Pt 76:Al22:Ru2 (Figure 2e) consisted of a Pt 3Al matrix, with fine
particles of (Pt) dispersed within the matrix. Martensite-like laths were observed within the Pt3Al matrix
in the Pt76:Al22:Ru2 alloy, suggesting that this phase had transformed from the high-temperature cubic
form of the phase (L1 2) to the low-temperature tetragonal crystal allotrope (D0 9c). The proportion of
primary Pt 3Al in the Pt 82:Al14:Ti4,P t 82:Al14:Ta4 and Pt 82:Al14:Cr4 alloys was estimated by image
analysis in the optical microscope to be between 40 and 50%, while the Pt 83:Al14:Re3 alloy had about
25% primary Pt 3Al in its microstructure.

XRD confirmed that all the alloys contained Pt 3Al and (Pt), with the exception of the Pt 76:Al22:Ru2
alloy in which only Pt 3Al was identified, although the microstructure clearly indicates the existence of
a secondary phase. The (Pt) phase in this alloy was probably not picked up by XRD because of the low

Pt
3Al peaks. The high-temperature allotrope of Pt 3Al appeared to have formed in Pt 82:Al14:Ti4, and

Metallographic evidence suggested that the low-temperature allotrope of Pt 3Al had formed in the
Pt83:Al14Re3 alloy, since faint laths were observed within the Pt 3Al phase.

Discussion
A number of microstructural parameters are expected to influence the high-temperature mechanical
response of these alloys. These include [1] the crystal structure of Pt 3Al, [2] the volume fraction of
primary and total Pt 3Al, [3] the phase relationships, and [4] solution strengthening.
With regard to these factors, the higher strengths of the Pt76:Al22:Ru2 and Pt83:Al14:Re3 alloys above
1200 °C can be brought into association with the following parameters. In the case of Re:
c Despite the strength superiority of the Pt 3Al phase over the (Pt) phase, Yamabe-Mitarai et al. (15)
have shown that there exists an optimum proportion of the strengthening phase in the two-phase field
for maximum strength, and above this a decrease in strength results. The Pt
83:Al14:Re3 alloy
evidenced both a lower volume fraction of primary Pt 3Al, and a finer two-phase mix to support such
a premise.
c Voronova et al. (16) have demonstrated the efficacy of Re as a solid-solution strengthener, reporting
an increase in tensile strength from 2 to 30 MPa at 1600 °C with the addition of 6 at.% Re to Pt.
In the case of Ru, a fine two-phase mix was achieved, but, unlike the other alloys, was based on a
discrete (Pt) phase within a Pt 3Al matrix, with as yet unknown implications for the deformation
mechanisms. No coarse primary Pt 3Al was observed within the microstructure, and the fine dispersion
of the two phases is likely to have increased the alloy’s strength. Strengthening of ordered Ni 3Al (g9)
by the precipitation of coherent disordered g particles has been reported (17). In such an alloy
dislocations are attracted to the disordered g phase, and cut through the particles during deformation
(17). Additionally:
c It was not established to what temperature the tetragonal form of Pt 3Al was stabilised, but the less
symmetrical form would be expected to create higher mismatch strain, with an increased resistance
to dislocation movement at these strain rates.
c Ru similarly exhibits potent solution strengthening in Pt, associated with its h.c.p. structure (18).
The optimum volume fraction of Pt 3Al for high-temperature strength has not yet been established.
Our observations suggest that, as in the case of the Ni-base superalloys (6), the strength of the solid
solution phase may be a limiting factor, and that additional attention to solution strengthening is
warranted. Another observation in relation to the nickel-base superalloys is worth noting: The effec-
tiveness of Re as a solution strengthener in Ni-base superalloys has been attributed to the development
of short-range order (SRO) in the (Ni) phase (19). Although no such evidence has been forthcoming to
date for Re in the Pt-base alloys, SRO has been inferred for Pt with ;5wt.% Ru additions (20).
Further work is necessary to elucidate the effects of the ternary alloying additions on the mechanical
properties of Pt-Al alloys. The differences between the microstructures of the alloys tested here made
it difficult to isolate the influences of the ternary additions. The point is worth making that high-
temperature strength does not equate to creep strength. Ternary additions profoundly influence the

Conclusions
The high-temperature cubic allotrope of Pt3Al was stabilised by ternary additions of Ti and Ta, both of
which are soluble in Pt 3Al. In order to prevent the low-temperature transformation of Pt 3Al, alloying
with an element that partitions to Pt 3Al will probably be necessary.
The temperature dependence of the compressive flow stress of ternary Pt-Al-X alloys was measured.
The alloys all had higher strengths above 1150°C than MAR-M247, a Ni-base superalloy. Pt76:Al22:Ru2
and the Pt 83:Al14:Re3 alloys had the highest strengths above 1200°C.
