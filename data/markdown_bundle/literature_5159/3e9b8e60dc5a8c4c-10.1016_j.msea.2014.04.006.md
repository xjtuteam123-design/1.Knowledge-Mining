1. Introduction
Nickel aluminide (NiAl) has received attention as a promising
candidate to replace Nickel-based superalloys in some high
temperature structural applications due to some advantages, such
as high melting temperature, good thermal conductivity, excellent
oxidation resistance and relatively low density. However, low
room temperature toughness and poor high-temperature strength
restrict commercial applications of NiAl alloy [1–3]. Alloying NiAl
to form in situ composite materials may be one way to improve
both these properties. For instance, introducing refractory Cr(Mo)
phase is one method of improving the fracture resistance and
strength. NiAl –Cr(Mo) eutectic systems take on better fracture
toughness (21 :6 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp [4]) and high-temperature strength.
Chen et al. [5] and Misra et al. [6] thought that refractory
Cr(Mo) phase can increase fracture toughness and high-temperature
strength. Taking into account that the volume fraction of Cr(Mo)
phase in hypereutectic system is more than that in eutectic system,
we consider that it is reasonable to pay more attention to the
investigation of hypereutectic alloy. However, previous studies
focused mostly on NiAl –(34/C0 x)Cr–xMo eutectic system, and little
work has been carried out in the NiAl –Cr(Mo) hypereutectic system.
Only Shang et al. [7,8] and Wang et al. [9] investigated the micro-
structures of directionally solidi ﬁed (DS) NiAl –Cr(Mo) or NiAl –Cr
(Mo)–Dy hypereutectic alloy and found that the volume fraction of
lamellar Cr(Mo) phase increases obviously compared to eutectic
alloy. Thus, we will take the NiAl –Cr(Mo) hypereutectic alloy as an
object of study.
Moreover, micro-alloying appears to be bene ﬁcial to further
increase the properties. Previous studies demonstrated that the
element Hf can obviously improve the high-temperature strength
of NiAl –Cr(Mo) eutectic systems by solution strengthening and
precipitation strengthening [10–12]. Unfortunately, the fracture
toughness at room temperature decreases largely due to the
distribution of Hf solid solution along the NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface.
For DS NiAl –28Cr–5.6Mo–0.1Hf alloy [12], the fracture toughness
is lower (6 :24 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp ). Thus, we will decrease the addition of Hf
content and expect that the fracture toughness is not too low, and
meanwhile it can improve the high-temperature strength. Further-
more, it is well known that the rare earth elements may improve
the properties of the alloy. Currently, researchers are only studying
the effect of Ho on fracture toughness and high-temperature
strength of DS NiAl-Cr(Mo)-Hf eutectic alloys. The results show
that the addition of rare earth element Ho can improve the high-
temperature strength, but the change of fracture toughness is
inconsistent. The addition of rare earth element Ho slightly
improves the fracture toughness (about 7 :8 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp for DS
Ni–30.4Al–34Cr–4.3Mo–0.1Hf–0.05Ho alloy [13]), but it may
deteriorate the fracture toughness (about 5 :3 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp for DS
NiAl–31Cr–2.9Mo–0.1Hf–0.05Ho alloy [14]). It is very interesting
that the effect of trace element on fracture toughness of NiAl –Cr
(Mo) eutectic system is inconsistent. In addition, the withdrawal

Previous investigation shows that the change of withdrawal rate

2. Experimental details
The master alloy of Ni –30.95Al–32Cr–6Mo–0.05Hf (at%) –0.1Dy
(wt%) alloy (NiAl –Cr(Mo)–(Hf,Dy) for short) was prepared by
vacuum induction melting. The ∅ 7 mm rods were cut from the
homogenized cast ingot by electro-discharge machining (EDM).
The rod was put in a high purity alumna crucible, which was 7 mm
in inner diameter and 1 15 mm in length for directional solidi ﬁca-
tion in Bridgeman apparatus. The temperature gradient at the
solid–liquid interface was about 200 K/cm. The specimens were
pulled down at the withdrawal rates of 6, 10, 30, 60 and 120 μm/s.
The pulling length of the directionally solidi ﬁed specimen was
80 mm. At the end of the experiment, the crucible was quickly
dropped into the liquid Ga –In–Sn alloy.
The directionally solidiﬁed specimens were cut along the long-
itudinal and transverse directions, and the location of the cross
section was 5 mm below the solid –liquid interface. The sections
were mechanically polished and then etched with a solution of 80%
HClþ20% HNO3 by volume. The microstructures were observed by
optical microscope (OM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and
transmission electron microscope (TEM in Tecnai G2 F30). The
composition of the constituent phase was identi ﬁed by electrical
probe microanalyzer (EPMA-1 720, accelerate voltage¼15.0 kV, beam
size¼ 1 μm). The eutectic interlamellar spacing and the eutectic cell
size were measured by SISCIASV8.0 metallurgical analysis software.
Three-point bending technique was used to determine fracture
toughness. Prior to toughness testing, the specimens were cut from
the DS alloy along the growth direction by EDM, and all the surfaces
of specimens were ground with 2000-grit SiC abrasive (nominal
dimensions, 3 mm/C2 6m m /C2 30 mm). Fracture toughness tests were
performed in air at room temperature in an Instron 3382 universal
test machine and at a cross-head speed of 0.05 mm/min. In our
study, some of the NiAl –Cr(Mo)–(Hf,Dy) alloy specimens were not
broken into two separate halves after three-point bending tests in
order to observe the crack propagation path from the side surfaces.
Tensile specimens having a gauge section of 3/C2 3 /C2 17 mm were c u t
from the DS alloy along the growth direction by EDM, and all the
surfaces of the specimens were ground with 2000-grit SiC abrasive.
Tensile tests were performed at 1253 K and a strain rate of
1 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 on an ultra-temperature tensile testing machine.

increasing withdrawal rate, the RT fracture toughness ﬁrst

changed trend of fracture toughness. The eutectic interlamellar

3.2.1. Extrinsic mechanism
In the NiAl-based eutectic alloys reinforced with a refractory
metal second phase, the RT fracture toughness is enhanced by some
toughness mechanisms compared to monolithic NiAl, such as crack
bridging, crack de ﬂection, crack blunting, interface debonding and
microcrack linkage. In the present investigation, most of these
mechanisms can provide some resistance to crack propagation.
Fig. 6 shows the high magni ﬁcation image of crack propagation of
the higher toughness specimen (30 mm/s). The interface debonding
occurs frequently at the intercellular region, as shown in Fig. 6 (a),
which induced the crack de ﬂection to the loading direction, thus
resulting in an effective increase of fracture toughness. The crack
deﬂection may lead to a series of microcracks. The microcrack
propagation is similar to the main crack propagation, and toughness
mechanism occurs in the same way, such as crack bridging, as
shown in Fig. 6 (a). Johnson et al. [4] thought that the material
between the microcracks should fracture by shear as the micro-
cracks connect with the main crack. Therefore, the linkage of these
microcracks with the main crack may provide further resistance to
crack propagation. Moreover, Fig. 6 (b) shows the tip of crack
propagation for the unbroken specimen after three-point bending
tests. It is obvious that the mechanism of crack bridging occurs
frequently during crack propagation due to the existence of Cr(Mo)
phase. For the present hypereutectic alloy, the volume fracture of Cr
(Mo) phase increases obviously compared to eutectic alloy, as
reported by Shang et al. [7]. Thus, the fracture toughness increases
by enlarging the mechanism of crack bridging. Consequently, these
mechanisms of crack bridging, interface debonding and microcrack
linkage result in the higher fracture toughness of the alloy.

3.2.2. Intrinsic mechanism
In the NiAl-based eutectic alloy, the native features of consti-
tuent phases can be bene ﬁcial to the increase of fracture tough-
ness. A semicoherent interface exists between NiAl and Cr(Mo)
phase due to the very small difference in lattice parameter
between the two phases, as shown in Fig. 8(a), and the dislocation
network is constrained to lie along NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface, thus
strengthening the alloy [4]. In addition, there is evidence to
indicate that NiAl phase has a plastic deformation in the vicinity
of the crack due to a high density of dislocation in NiAl phase, as
shown in Fig. 8 (b). Thus, a part of toughness may be attributed to
the intrinsic toughening of NiAl phase by the generation of a high
density of mobile dislocations. Furthermore, the addition of rare
earth element Dy can reduce the segregation of impurities O, S and
C in the interface, thus enhancing the cohesive strength of phase
or cell interface. Also, since the Dy atom is larger than Ni or Al
atom, electron transfer may occur when the Dy atom segregates at
the phase or cell interface, and such an increased electron density
may increase the interface strength, thus improving the fracture
toughness [22].
In the present investigation, the fracture toughness of NiAl –Cr
(Mo)–(Hf,Dy) alloy ﬁrst increases and then decreases with the
increasing withdrawal rate due to the microstructural evolution.
The relatively large degree of main crack de ﬂection at the side
surface ( Fig. 5 ) and the tortuous fracture surface ( Fig. 7 ) also
conﬁrm that the toughness is relatively high at a moderate
withdrawal rate. Moreover, although the fracture toughness of
the present alloy at a moderate withdrawal rate is higher than that
of NiAl (4 /C0 6 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp [1]) or NiAl –Cr(Mo)–(Hf, Ho) alloys
(5:3 /C0 8:0 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp [13, 14]), the fracture toughness of the present
NiAl–Cr(Mo)–(Hf,Dy) alloy is lower than NiAl –Cr(Mo) eutectic
alloy (21 :6 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp [4]). It is reasonable to consider that the large
Hf solid solution appearing at the eutectic cell boundary may
decrease the fracture toughness, since the crack initiates com-
monly from the precipitated phase at the cell boundary. In
addition, the addition of Hf element may in ﬂuence the solidi ﬁca-
tion process, and thus it decreases the fracture toughness by
disturbing the microstructure (6 :24 MPa ﬃﬃﬃﬃ ﬃmp for DS NiAl –28Cr–
5.6Mo–0.1Hf alloy [12]). For the NiAl –Cr(Mo)–(Hf, rare earth
element) alloy, more work needs to be done to further improve
the fracture toughness in the future.

For the present alloy, the high-temperature strength can reach
438 MPa, which is higher than that of NiAl–Cr(Mo)–(Hf or Ho) alloys
[13,23,24]. Some strengthening mechanisms are also discussed in
the following lines. Firstly, previous investigation[5,6] demonstrates
that Cr(Mo) phase can improve the high-temperature strength,
and the volume fraction of Cr(Mo) phase increases obviously for
the NiAl –32Cr–6Mo hypereutectic alloy [7], thus enhancing the
strength. Secondly, dispersion strengthening is an important
strengthening mechanism in NiAl –Cr(Mo) system. With the TEM
examination of the microstructure, aﬁne array of NiAl particles with
an average size of 20 nm is present in Cr(Mo) phase, as shown in
Fig. 1 1(a), which indicates that Ni and Al have a solubility in Cr(Mo)
phase at high temperature. Some ﬁner spherical Cr(Mo) particles
with a mean size of 2 nm are also observed in NiAl phase, as
displayed in Fig. 1 1(b), which is similar to previous studies [24–26].
The difference is that the size of Cr(Mo) particles is smaller than that
(30 nm) in the previous studies [24,25], thus dispersion strengthen-
ing provides more contribution to the increase of the strength in
the present investigation. Thirdly, precipitation strengthening and
solution strengthening are ubiquitous for NiAl –Cr(Mo)–Hf alloy
[10–12,18,23,24]. Moreover, the addition of rare earth element Dy
can also improve the high-temperature strength by purifying the
matrix and strengthening the interface. Thus, the present alloy has a
better high-temperature strength.
4. Conclusions
The microstructure, room temperature fracture toughness and
high-temperature strength of DS NiAl –Cr(Mo)–(Hf, Dy) alloy at
different withdrawal rates have been studied. The obtained results
are summarized as follows:
(1) The solid –liquid interface changes from planar to cellular, even
dendritic with the increasing withdrawal rate. Correspond-
ingly, the lamella and the eutectic cell re ﬁne gradually, and the
relation between interlamellar spacing and withdrawal rate
can be expressed as λ ¼ 4:55 V /C0 0:40.
(2) The room temperature fracture toughness is relatively high at
a moderate withdrawal rate. This is mainly attributed to some
extrinsic toughening mechanisms, such as crack bridging,
interface debonding and microcrack linkage. The role of the
intrinsic toughening mechanism involving dislocation genera-
tion in NiAl phase is also considered.
(3) For the present alloy, the high-temperature strength at cellular
or dendritic growth rate is higher than that at planar growth
rate. The information of slip band and microcrack can con-
tribute to the higher strength.
