ABSTRACT
The high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) is one of the candidates for next-generation nuclear
reactors. Helium is used as the coolant in the primary circuit of an HTGR. Several superalloys with high
corrosion resistance and superior mechanical strength are potential structural materials for HTGR
intermediate heat exchanger component. However, impurities, such as oxygen and water vapour,
that are inherently present in the helium coolant or from the atmosphere during a loss-of-coolant
accident may lead to serious degradation in these alloys and significantly increase their oxidation
rates at high temperatures. In this work, the oxidation behaviour of the tested alloys was
investigated in high-temperature helium environments. Results showed that relatively thick and
continuous chromium oxide layers were present on the surfaces of all tested alloys. Based on the
results of mass gain and scanning electron microscope analyses, Hastelloy X alloy and Alloy 625
both exhibited relatively better corrosion resistance after all designated corrosion tests.

Introduction
A high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) is cate-
gorised as a Generation IV nuclear reactor, primarily dedi-
cated to the cogeneration of electricity and hydrogen. The
use of helium as coolant and graphite and ceramics as core
structure materials allows an operation temperature at the
core outlet as high as 1000°C, which makes hydrogen pro-
duction easily achievable [ 1]. Accordingly, the alloy material
of the intermediate heat exchanger (IHX) in an HTGR may
be exposed to a high temperature of up to 950°C and should
thus have high degradation resistance and superior mechan-
ical strength over a long lifetime. The helium coolant in an
HTGR inevitably contains low levels of impurities during
reactor operation, primarily consisting of small amounts of
H
2,H 2O, CH 4,C O ,C O 2, and N 2. Impurities in the helium
coolant may decrease the corrosion resistance of structural
alloys and lead to various corrosion problems in these alloys
at high temperatures.
Depending on the impurity level and the helium tempera-
ture, alloys may have various interactions with the impurities
and result in corrosive oxidation, carburisation, or decarbur-
isation. When a nickel-based alloy is exposed to oxidising
helium environments, the alloy would form a chromia
(Cr
2O3) scale on the surface. As the oxidation of the alloy
continues to proceed, it could cause the growth of the chro-
mia scale, internal oxidation of aluminium, slight increase
in carbon content, and development of a carbide-free zone.
On the other hand, the formation of the chromia layer may
instead serve as a barrier to the detrimental effects from the
corrosive coolant gases. In reducing environments, however,
the chromia is unstable due to the presence of dissolved car-
bon inside, some carbon would deposit on the chromia sur-
face, and internal carbides could form [ 2– 7].
A specific process, termed as ‘microclimate reaction ’,
could take place in pores within the oxide layer, rendering
the reduction of chromia into pure chromium (Cr
2O3 +3
C = 3 CO + 2 Cr). [ 8] To avoid the microclimate reaction
that would result in loss of the chromia layer, the CO partial
pressure in the helium coolant must exceed 30 μbar at 950°C
for most nickel-based alloys. A partial pressure ratio of water
vapour to hydrogen gas [P(H
2O)/P(H2)] must be larger than
0.01 to allow the formation of a protective chromia layer. In
the meantime, it is also necessary to maintain a partial
pressure ratio of methane to water vapour [P(CH 4)/P
(H2O)] lower than a critical value of 0.01 to avoid excessive
carburisation and reduction of oxide layers [ 9].
For an HTGR, one important safety design consideration
is the air ingress event, which would usually result from a rup-
ture in the reactor pressure boundary or in the primary cool-
ing system. During an air ingress event, the core held at high
temperature would experience significant oxidation in graph-
ite components and fuel claddings. The air intrusion to the
IHX alloys may cause more serious oxidation and result in
integrity breach of the IHX structure. In the meantime,
ingress of water vapour may also play an important role in
the oxidation of structural alloys. Alloy oxidation rates
would increase with the addition of water vapour into the
atmosphere, in comparison with those under dry conditions.
In addition, adhesion of alumina and chromia scales wor-
sened due to the presence of water vapour [ 10]. For an iron
aluminium alloy, a hydrated alumina might form in the
early stage of oxide growth in wet atmosphere, rendering
the alloy more susceptible to cracking and leading to a large
increase in nodule formation as well [ 11]. At temperatures

In general, the formation of stable oxides is preferred for
preventing continuous degradation of alloy materials, but
unexpected spallation of thick oxides may harm the integrity
and the mechanical strength of the materials during normal
and prolonged operations of an HTGR. Several nickel- and
iron-based alloys such as Hastelloy X, Alloy 617, and Alloy
800H are considered as promising candidates for IHX
materials in an HTGR in consideration of their excellent
creep resistance. Studies over the degradation of IHX candi-
date alloys have been focused on exposure to normal oper-
ation environments in the past. This study instead intends
to investigate corrosion behaviours of candidate IHX alloys
during air ingress events, in which the environments are
usually highly oxidising.
In this study, three nickel-based alloys (Alloy 617, Alloy
625, and Hastelloy X) and one iron-based alloy (Alloy
800H) were selected and underwent corrosion tests. Samples
prepared from these alloys were exposed to helium gas
environments with various amounts of impurities (i.e. oxygen
and water vapour) at 950°C for 48 –144 h. Test results on
mass changes, surface morphologies, and composition of
oxides of the samples are reported and discussed.
Experimental
Sample preparation
Table 1 shows the chemical compositions of the tested alloys
of 800H, Hastelloy X, 617, and 625. These alloys were
machined into samples in square-disc shape with a dimension
of 20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm. Both sides of the samples were
mechanically polished with abrasive paper starting from 80
grit up to 1200 grit. Each sample was then ultrasonically
cleaned in acetone (CH
3COCH3), dried in air, and rinsed in
distilled water for 10 min. No specific thermal treatment on
the samples was carried out before designated corrosion tests.
Corrosion test conditions
A conceptual schematic of the experimental system is shown
in Figure 1 . In each corrosion test, eight samples, two from
each alloy, were simultaneously placed on a ceramic holder
inside a tube furnace and exposed to designated flowing gas
mixtures at 950°C. Four gaseous conditions were specifically
engineered to simulate normal and various air-ingress
environments in an HTGR, including pure dry helium, 90%
helium with 10% oxygen, pure helium with 10% humidity,
and pure helium with 50% humidity. Table 2 lists the impur-
ity levels in pure helium used in this study. Corrosion test
durations varied from 48 to 144 h. Gas input was controlled
using a mass flow controller at a flow rate of 250 mL min
−1.
The samples were heated up at a rate of 15°C min −1 and
cooled down at a rate of 7.5°C min −1 in pure argon at a con-
stant flow rate of 250 mL min −1.
Morphological analyses on tested samples
After the corrosion tests, the mass gain of each sample was
measured using a high-precision electronic balance. The mor-
phology and cross-sectional microstructure of oxides on a
sample were directly observed using a scanning electron
microscope (SEM), and the elemental composition of the
oxide was analysed with energy-dispersive X-ray spec-
troscopy (EDX) and glow discharge spectrometry (GDS).
The crystallinity of the surface oxides was characterised by
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis.

Results and discussion
Mass gain analyses
Figure 2 shows the mass gain of each alloy as a function of
square root of time ( t1/2) after isothermal oxidation at 950°
C up to 144 h in various controlled helium environments.
The oxidation rate of each alloy approximately follows the
well-known parabolic rate law, as shown in Figure 2 . In the
four controlled helium environments, the parabolic rate con-
stants of Alloy 625 and Hastelloy X were almost the same and
comparatively smaller among the four selected alloys. The
parabolic rate constants of Alloy 800H and Alloy 617 were
similar and greater in the two wet helium environments com-
pared to those in the helium environment containing 10%
oxygen. Furthermore, the mass gain of Alloy 800H was the
highest, and that of Hastelloy X was the lowest among the
four alloys under all test conditions.
The influence of environmental conditions on each alloy
was also analysed, as shown in Figure 3 . It was observed
that the mass gain of Alloy 800H was the lowest in pure
helium, and no significant differences in mass gain of this
alloy were observed in the other three helium environments.
A similar behaviour was also observed in Alloy 625. In
addition, the mass gains of Hastelloy X were similar in the
four test environments. For Alloy 617, the mass gain in the
helium environment containing 10% oxygen was slightly
higher than that in pure helium. In the meantime, the mass
gains of Alloy 617 were similar under the two wet helium
conditions.
XRD analysis
Figure 4 shows the XRD results on surface oxides of the four
alloys after being oxidised at 950°C for 144 h in pure helium,
90% helium with 10% oxygen, and pure helium with 50%
humidity environments. The major components in the sur-
face oxide of Alloy 800H were MnCr
2O4/MnFe2O4,C r 2O3,
and TiO 2 in the three controlled helium environments.
Hastelloy X had an oxide film comprising Cr 2O3, MnCr2O4-
/MnFe2O4, and Fe 2O3. Alloy 617 had a similar surface oxide
composition of Cr 2O3, MnCr 2O4, and TiO 2, and Alloy 625
had Cr 2O3, MnCr 2O4, and Ti –Cr oxide. For the three
nickel-based alloys, Cr 2O3 crystals seemed to dominate in
the oxides, while the spinel oxide of MnCr 2O4/MnFe2O4
was seen comparatively more abundant than the others on
Alloy 800H. It was also noted that the selected environmental
conditions did not have a significant influence on each alloy
in terms of variation in the oxide composition.

Oxidation mechanism
The composition of elements plays an important role in
deciding the properties and characteristics of an alloy. To
maintain the long-term integrity of structural components,
the presence of a protective oxide layer on the alloy surface
is essential. When an alloy is exposed to high-temperature
HTGR environments, the Gibbs free energies of formation
of various metal oxides and the diffusivities of corresponding
cations in the substrate and in the oxides must be considered
simultaneously. In this study, the Cr contents in the selected
alloys ranged from 18 to 20% and exceeded the critical
chromium concentration for forming continuous oxide
scales [ 15–18]. Therefore, it was expected that a Cr
2O3
layer would form and become a major component in the sur-
face oxides of the tested alloys at the early stage of the cor-
rosion process [ 19].
In the meantime, the Gibbs free energy of formation of
MnCr
2O4 (ΔGf = −557.78 kJ mol −1) is of the same order as
that of Cr 2O3 (ΔGf = −546.86 kJ mol −1) but a little lower,
and MnCr 2O4 should, therefore, be able to co-exist with
Cr2O3. In all tested alloys, MnCr 2O4 formed at the outermost
layer, since Mn generally diffuses two orders of magnitude
faster than Cr, Fe, and Ni in the Cr 2O3 layer.
Another important alloying element is aluminium. The
Gibbs free energy of formation of Al 2O3 (ΔGf = −858.30 kJ
mol−1) is much lower than that of Cr 2O3. Although the Al

Al2O3 still preferentially formed underneath the Cr 2O3 layer,
leading to reduction in creep resistance of the alloys due to
the resultant tensile stress in the perpendicular direction
[20]. Internal oxidation is the process in which oxygen dif-
fuses inward into the substrate and reacts with a certain active
element in an alloy. Except for Hastelloy X, internal oxides of
Al2O3 could be found near the Cr 2O3-alloy interface of the
other alloys. From a thermodynamic viewpoint, Al 2O3 is con-
siderably more stable than Cr 2O3 [21]. The equilibrium oxy-
gen potential at Cr 2O3 and substrate interface is much higher
than the value required to form Al 2O3. Accordingly, alu-
minium reacts with Cr 2O3 to form Al 2O3 and chromium,
leading to the morphology of internal precipitates [ 22].
Internal oxidation was observed underneath the grain bound-
aries of Alloy 800H, Alloy 617, and Alloy 625 and possibly
resulted in oxide ridges on the surfaces of these alloys.
Alloy 617 has a higher internal oxidation rate due to its
greater aluminium content that promotes the oxidation of
aluminium.
In summary, Cr
2O3 acted as the major protective oxide,
and it was speculated that a greater amount of internal oxi-
dation of Al would result in a higher possibility of alloy
failure. It is worth noting that the corrosion tests were car-
ried out in oxidising environments in this study. Accord-
ingly, the microclimate reaction, which would lead to
decarburisation of alloys and usually occurs in reducing
environments, was not discussed in this paper. The carbon
contents in the selected alloys will be investigated in our
follow-up work.
Effects of humidity
Alloy oxidation becomes faster in the wet helium environ-
ments than in the dry ones. This study shows that the mass
gains of Alloy 617 in the wet helium environments were
greater that in a mixture of helium and oxygen. Alloy 800H
and Alloy 625 had slightly greater mass gains in the wet
helium environments than in the 10% oxygen environment.
Hastelloy X instead showed less mass gains in the wet helium
environments than in the other two dry environments. Alloys
in a humid helium environment may react with steam in sev-
eral oxidising ways as follows.
On the surface of an alloy at high temperatures, steam may
react with metal to produce oxide and hydrogen. Sub-
sequently, the oxide may further react with steam and oxygen

Conclusions
Isothermal oxidation tests on Alloy 800H, Hastelloy X, Alloy
617, and Alloy 625 were conducted at 950°C up to 144 h in
various controlled helium environments. In the mass gain
analyses, the parabolic rate constants of Alloy 625 and Hastel-
loy X were almost identical and comparatively smaller among
the four alloys. The mass gain of Alloy 800H was the highest,
and Hasetlloy X exhibited the lowest mass gain among the
four alloys under every designated condition.
A continuous Cr
2O3 layer was observed on the surface of
every tested alloy and acted as the main protective oxide at
950°C in the four selected environments. Grain boundary
ridges were observed on the surface oxide of Alloy 800H in
the four controlled helium environments. Two-layer oxides
formed with a thin outer layer of spinel MnCr
2O4 and MnFe2-
O4 and a thick inner layer of Cr 2O3 right above the alloy
substrate.
The formation of Al 2O3 internal oxides underneath the
Cr2O3 layer in Alloy 617 was more prominent than those in
Alloy 800H and Alloy 625, and Alloy 625 had the least amount
of internal Al
2O3 of the three alloys. No internal oxides of
Al2O3 were found in Hastelloy X in all environments.
