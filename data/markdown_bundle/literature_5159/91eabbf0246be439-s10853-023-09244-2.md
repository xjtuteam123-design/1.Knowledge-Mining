ABSTRACT
Medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) have recently emerged as potential replacements 
for traditional alloys in high-performance applications. Among the various MEAs 
developed, equi-atomic CoCrNi MEA stands out for its excellent mechanical per-
formance. Nevertheless, the yield strength of this MEA having a single-phase 
FCC structure is not exceptionally high. This study suggests a novel approach 
to strengthen the MEA by adding a small quantity of Zirconium (0.2 at.%). A 
(CoCrNi)99.8Zr0.2 MEA was cast and homogenized, alongside a CoCrNi MEA for 
comparison purposes. The alloys underwent high-pressure torsion followed by 
annealing in a temperature range of 700–1100 °C. In the Zr-doped MEA, anneal-
ing resulted in the recrystallization within the FCC matrix, accompanied by the 
precipitation of finely dispersed particles (Ni7Zr2). The concurrent precipitation 
hindered the grain growth, maintaining refined grain sizes (0.17 – 4 µm) over a 
wide range of temperatures. In contrast, the grain size in the Zr-free counterpart 
increased rapidly above 10 µm at elevated temperatures. The Zr doping substan-
tially increased the yield strength with a minor negative impact on the ductil-
ity. The strength enhancement was due to grain refinement, precipitation, and 
dislocation locking caused by the Zr segregation at dislocation sites. These phe-
nomena and their impacts on the Hall–Petch relationship were investigated and 
discussed. This study proves micro-alloying as a promising strategy for strength-
ening MEAs, making these alloys superior to their traditional counterparts.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT 
Introduction
High- and medium-entropy alloys (H/MEAs) have 
drawn significant attention in the materials science 
community in recent years. Conventional alloying 
design typically involves mixing one principal element 
and other minor alloying elements to form dilute solid 
solutions. In contrast, H/MEAs incorporate multiple 
principal elements that form compositionally com-
plex solid solutions. The general assumption is that 
the high-configuration entropy assists in stabilizing 
solid solutions instead of promoting phase separation 
in H/MEAs [1–6].
Equi-atomic CoCrNi MEA having a single-phase 
FCC (face-centered cubic) structure has been the sub-
ject of numerous recent studies. This alloy exhibits 
remarkable mechanical properties including high 
strength, toughness, and excellent ductility. The 
strength of CoCrNi MEA mainly originates from the 
solid-solution strengthening, which is closely cor-
related with the local crystal lattice distortions and 
high friction stress in the compositionally complex 
solid-solution [7 –14]. In addition, many researchers 
have tried to incorporate additional strengthening 
mechanisms to further reinforce the single-phase 
structure of the MEA. A common approach is to 
introduce other alloying elements (such as Nb, Ti, 
and Ta) [15– 19], aiming at promoting the formation 
of secondary phases in large volume fractions. Nev-
ertheless, this approach adds more complexity to 
the bulk composition and has the potential to form 
undesirable and brittle phases that compromise the 
material’s ductility. Furthermore, the formation of 
secondary phases depletes the solid solution of the 
principal elements, therefore largely diminishing the 
solution strengthening as the essential characteristic 
of H/MEAs.
Grain refinement is an alternative method to 
improve the strength of metallic materials without 
requiring any chemical change in the material’s com-
position. Ultrafine-grained metals, typically character-
ized by grain sizes below 1 µm, are often fabricated by 
applying severe plastic deformation (SPD) followed 
by annealing conducted at low temperatures and/or 
for short durations [20–23]. Yoshida et al. [11, 12] suc-
cessfully used this method to substantially increase 
the strength of H/MEAs by reducing their grain sizes 
down to the sub-micrometer scale.

In the current study, we have employed the SPD 
and annealing processing, combined with the concept 
of micro-alloying , to effectively refine the microstruc -
ture of the CoCrNi MEA. Micro-alloying refers to 
the small addition of alloying elements with specific 
impacts on microstructure and mechanical properties. 
A traditional example of this strategy is micro-alloyed 
steels, which contain trace amounts of elements such 
as Ni, V, and Ti (typically below 0.1 wt.%) [24–27]. The 
primary role of the micro-alloying elements is to refine 
the grain size and/or accelerate precipitation strength-
ening, resulting in high strength and toughness in the 
micro-alloyed steels. In our study, we chose Zirco-
nium (Zr) as a micro-alloying element to be added 
to the CoCrNi MEA. As a refractory metal, Zr usu-
ally improves the thermal stability and performance 
of alloys at high temperatures [28, 29]. Zr is known 
for its capability to form nano-sized precipitates in 
FCC alloys [30–32]. Nano-precipitates with high ther-
mal stability and resistance to growth are beneficial 
for grain refinement during the thermo-mechanical 
processing of alloys. In this research, we have added 
0.2 at.% Zr to the composition of the CoCrNi MEA, 
and then processed the material by SPD followed 
by annealing at various temperatures. Our aim is to 
investigate the effect of the Zr micro-alloying on the 
microstructural refinement and mechanical properties 
of the CoCrNi MEA.
Materials and methods
Vacuum arc-melting was used to prepare as-cast 
ingots (30 g) of (CoCrNi) 99.8Zr0.2 along with equi-
atomic CoCrNi which served as a reference material 
for comparison. Pure metals with purity exceeding 
99.9 at.% were melted inside a water-cooled copper 
mold in an argon atmosphere. To ensure composi-
tional homogeneity, the ingots were flipped and re-
melted at least five times. The as-cast materials were 
cold-rolled with 80% reduction in thickness and subse-
quently homogenized at 1100 °C for 24 h in a vacuum 
(~  10–3 Pa) furnace. Discs with a diameter of 10 mm 
and a thickness of 0.8 mm were cut from homogenized 
materials. High-pressure torsion (HPT) was used to 
severely deform the discs to five rotations (1800°). The 
HPT process was performed at room temperature, 
applying a pressure of 6 GPa and a rotation speed 
of 0.2 rpm (rotation per minute). The HPT-ed discs 
were annealed in a vacuum furnace, in a temperature 
range of 700–1100 °C for different periods, and then 
quenched in water.
For microstructural observation, the annealed speci-
mens were mechanically polished using sandpapers 
up to 4000 grit size. This was followed by electropol-
ishing in a solution of 10 vol. % HClO4 and 90 vol. % 
 C H3COOH, applying a voltage of 15 V at room tem-
perature for 25 s. Microstructural observations were 
performed using a field-emission scanning electron 
microscope (FE-SEM), JEOL JSM-7800F, equipped 
with a backscattered electron (BSE) detector. Obser-
vations were conducted on a section normal to the 
radial direction at a position of 3 mm radial distance 
from the center of the HPT disc. The acceleration volt-
age was set at 20 kV, with a working distance of 5–10 
mm. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectros-
copy (EDS) was employed for compositional analy-
sis. EDS measurements were carried out in a FE-SEM 
(JEOL JSM-7100F) equipped with an EDS spectrom -
eter. The acceleration voltage and working distance 
for EDS measurements were set at 20 kV and 15 mm, 
respectively. Microstructure characterizations using 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) 
together with STEM-EDS were performed in JEOL 
JEM-2100F operated at 200 kV. For STEM observations, 
thin foils having an initial thickness of 1 mm were cut 
at a 3 mm radial distance from the disc center. After-
ward, sandpapers were used to thin the foils down to 
0.1 mm. Finally, the foils were ion-milled using JEOL 
EM-09100IS ion-slicer until a hole was created in the 
center of the thin foils. Final ion milling was conducted 
at a low voltage (2 kV) to minimize the damage cre -
ated by the ion milling.
Additional phase identification was conducted 
using a synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) facil-
ity (BL46XU) at Super Photon ring–8 GeV (SPring-8) 
located in Hyogo prefecture (Japan). An incident X-ray 
beam, with energy of 30 keV (wavelength of 0.0413 
nm) and spot size of 0.2 mm × 0.5 mm, was directed 
perpendicular to the surface of target specimens with 
0.5 mm thickness. Transmission-diffracted X-ray 
beams were detected by a one-dimensional semicon-
ductor detector (MYTHEM, Dectris). The exposure 
time was set to 120 s to achieve a sufficient signal-to-
noise ratio. The ICDD (International Center for Diffrac-
tion Data) database was used for phase identification.
Tensile tests were performed on a universal ten-
sile test machine (SHIMADZU, AG-100kN Xplus) 
at room temperature using a quasi-static strain rate 
of 8.3 ×  10–4 s −1. Miniaturized tensile test specimens

Mechanical property
Figure 8a and b shows engineering stress-engineer-
ing strain curves of the CoCrNi and (CoCrNi) 99.8Zr0.2 
MEAs having various grain sizes, respectively. The 
curves were obtained by uniaxial tensile tests at room 
temperature. In both materials, the yield strength 
increased with decreasing the mean grain size, while 
the total elongation decreased owing to the strength-
ductility trade-off. Additionally, a transition in the 
yielding behavior from continuous yielding to discon-
tinuous yielding became apparent as grain refinement 
occurred. Interestingly, the Zr addition promoted the 
discontinuous yielding phenomenon. This is evi-
dent in Fig. 8b as the upper yield points became pro-
nounced, particularly when the grain size was reduced 
below 0.3 µm.

the deformation behavior of the Zr-added speci-

deformation [34]. Figure 10b shows a STEM micro-

dislocations were connected to the precipitates, sug-

for the nucleation of precipitates. It should be men-

within a grain that was larger than the average grain 
size of the UFG specimen. This is because disloca-
tions in other regions with smaller grain sizes were 
scarce. Figure 10c shows the stress–strain curve of 
an interrupted tensile test, in which the specimen 
was unloaded just after the discontinuous yielding 
happened (ε  ~ 0.02). The microstructure observation 
was conducted inside the strain-localized region 
(band), as shown by the illustration in Fig. 10c. The 
STEM micrograph in Fig. 10d represents the defor-
mation microstructure after unloading. The precipi-
tates acted as obstacles to the dislocation motion, as 
dislocations were piled up and bowed around the 
precipitates. It seemed unlikely that dislocations 
were able to cut (penetrate) the precipitates by dislo-
cation shearing mechanism. The latter is expected to 
happen when the precipitate size is extremely small 
[35]. Therefore, we assume that the by-pass (disloca-
tion looping) mechanism [35] is the dominant inter-
action between dislocations and precipitates. Later 
in the discussion part, the contribution of such a 
mechanism to the material’s strength is quantified 
and discussed.
Discussion
Zr is known as an essential alloying element in refrac-

Various microstructures with a wide range of 
grain sizes and diverse distributions of precipitates 
were fabricated by HPT followed by recrystallization 
annealing at temperatures of 700–1100 °C (Fig. 6). 
Owing to the high stored energy after HPT, the recrys-
tallization started immediately and was completed 
within less than a minute (Fig. 2d). It remained unclear 
whether the precipitation of nano-size particles, char-
acterized as Ni7Zr2 (Figs. 4, 5), started before or con-
currently with the recrystallization. However, it was 
evident that a notable fraction of precipitates evolved 
after the recrystallization and during the subsequent 
grain growth stage (Figs. 2e–f, 3a). The fraction of pre-
cipitates varied in relation to the annealing tempera-
ture. Initially, the fraction increased with increasing 
the temperature up to 900 °C, owing to the enhanced 
kinetics of precipitation (Fig. 7b). However, at higher 
temperatures of 1000–1100 °C, the fraction decreased, 
suggesting a slight enhancement in the solubility of Zr.
The Zr addition proved to be very efficient at 
retarding the grain growth during the annealing pro-
cess (Fig. 7a). The grain size upon Zr addition stayed 
refined (< 4 µm) even after annealing at a high tem-
perature of 1100 °C for 1 h. Such exceptional resistance 
to grain growth is mainly due to the Zener pinning 
effect [43–45]. That is, the finely dispersed precipitates 
impose pinning pressure opposing the grain growth. 
Additionally, trace amounts of Zr solute atoms that 
stay in the solid solution can also play a part as these 
solute atoms can potentially segregate to grain bound-
aries causing a solute drag effect. Both phenomena, 
i.e., Zener pinning and solute drag effects, have been 
reported upon Zr addition in nanocrystalline FeNiZr 
and FeZr alloys, resulting in an exceptional thermal 
stability of the nanocrystalline structures at elevated 
temperatures [31, 46].
Precipitates can grow especially at elevated temper-
atures (Fig. 7c) over extended annealing time (Fig. 3b). 
The underlying mechanism can be explained by the 
well-known Ostwald ripening mechanism [47, 48]. 
That is, smaller particles dissolve while larger particles 
grow due to the diffusion of solute atoms (Zr) from 
smaller particles to larger ones. Overall, the coarsen-
ing of precipitates can reduce their ability to impede 
the migration of grain boundaries. Nevertheless, the 
precipitates exhibited a relatively slow coarsening 
behavior in the current material since many precipi-
tates remained nano-sized over extended annealing 
times. One contributing factor to the slow coarsen-
ing can be the scarcity of Zr solute atoms in the solid 
solution. In addition, precipitates were mainly located 
inside grains rather than along GBs (Fig. 4). The coars-
ening of in-grain precipitates is expected to be slower 
than the coarsening of GB precipitates. This is due to 
the slower diffusion of Zr atoms through the crystal 
lattice, necessary to reach the in-grain precipitates, in 
contrast to the faster diffusion that occurs along GBs.

nificant impact on the strength of the CoCrNi MEA

to some extent via precipitation hardening (Fig. 9b) 
mechanisms. The strengthening by grain refinement is 
widely known as the Hall–Petch (HP) effect [49–51], in 
which the yield stress (σ YS) of a polycrystalline metal 
is described by the Hall–Petch equation:
where σ0 is the friction stress, k is a constant (the HP 
slope), and d is the mean grain size of the material. 
This empirically established relation predicts that 
the strength (or hardness) of a polycrystalline mate-

boundaries act as obstacles to the dislocation move-
ment; therefore, dislocations encounter more GBs as 
the grain size is reduced. This makes the movement 
of dislocations more difficult, and such resistance to 
plastic deformation leads to a higher strength of the 
material.

certain value (1–2 µm). The extra hardening that was 
realized as the higher HP slope is closely related to 
the discontinuous yielding behavior (Fig. 8), indicat-

Such behavior is a characteristic of ultrafine-grained 
metallic materials [20, 51, 52], and it has been also 
reported in the CoCrNi MEA by Yoshida et al. [11,

Summary and conclusions
In this research, CoCrNi and (CoCrNi) 99.8 Zr0.2 
medium-entropy alloys were cast, homogenized, and 
subsequently processed by high-pressure torsion fol-
lowed by annealing at temperatures ranging from 
700 to 1100 °C. Our primary focus was to assess the 
impact of Zr doping on the microstructure evolution 
and mechanical properties of the MEA. The key find-
ings are summarized as follows:
1. The addition of 0.2 at.% Zr led to the formation of 
a (Zr, Ni)-rich secondary phase in the inter-dendritic 
regions of the solidified microstructure. Cold-rolling 
followed by prolonged homogenization dissolved the 
second phase although a small fraction of the second-
phase particles remained, indicating the limited solu-
bility of Zr in the FCC solid solution.
2. High-pressure torsion led to extensive grain 
refinement (d  ~ 23 nm) in the FCC matrix, while the 
pre-existing particles underwent severe fragmenta-
tion. Subsequent annealing at 700–1100 °C led to the 
recrystallization and grain growth within the FCC 
matrix, accompanied by the formation of finely dis-
persed precipitates (Ni7Zr2). Exceptional resistance to 
grain growth was realized due to the Zener pinning 
imposed by the precipitates as well as the solute drag 
effect. In the Zr-doped MEA, the grain size remained 
refined ranging from 0.17 to 4 µm. In contrast, the 
Zr-free MEA underwent rapid grain growth, with 
the grain size exceeding 10 µm as the temperature 
increased.
3. The fraction of precipitates increased with 
increasing the temperature up to 900 °C and then 
decreased due to the increased Zr solubility at higher 
temperatures. Most precipitates, particularly those 
formed at lower temperatures, were nano-sized 
ranging from 20 to 150 nm. Oswald ripening growth 
was observed at higher temperatures over extended 
annealing periods. However, the precipitate coarsen-
ing was sluggish due to the Zr scarcity and slow dif-
fusion of Zr solute. 
4. The yield strength of the Zr-doped MEA substan-
tially increased (around a few hundred megapascals) 
with a small reduction in ductility (10–15%) compared 
to the Zr-free counterpart. The primary contribution 
of Zr to the materials’ strength was due to the grain 
refinement. Furthermore, Zr doping promoted the dis-
continuous yielding behavior specifically in ultrafine-
grained specimens, leading to a higher slope (extra 
hardening) in the Hall–Petch plot. This finding was 
attributed to (1) precipitation strengthening provided 
by the dense nano-sized precipitates dispersed within 
the UFG structures, along with (2) strengthening 
caused by the Zr segregation at dislocations, which 
could trap dislocations and reduce their mobility.
