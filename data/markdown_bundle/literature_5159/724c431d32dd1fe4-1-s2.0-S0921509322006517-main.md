induced by residual nano-sized Cr precipitates

ABSTRACT 
The mechanical performance of Cu – Cr alloy severely deteriorates at high temperatures due to the Cr precipitates 
coarsening rapidly or dissolving in Cu matrix again. This study achieved the supersaturated solid solution Cu-5wt 
%Cr alloy (S1) with Cr solubility of 0.94 wt% by combining mechanical alloying and spark plasma sintering. The 
effect of residual Cr precipitates on the high temperature hardness, softening resistance and microstructures were 
investigated. The results showed that S1 alloy exhibited an excellent hardness of 106.27 HV at 500 
◦
C, 23.14% 
higher than the common sample. The softening temperature of S1 alloy is 800 
◦
C (0.74 T
m
), and the average grain 
size of S1 alloy annealed at 800 
◦
C is 0.69 μ m, which increased by only 0.11 μ m. The microstructural evolution 
showed that the high thermal stability is mainly attributed to two kinds of residual Cr precipitates in the matrix. 
The larger ones act as obstacles to inhibit the motion of Cu grain boundaries, and the fine precipitates disperse in 
Cu matrix to fix the dislocations. Combining these two types of residual Cr precipitates act as very powerful 
obstacles to inhibit the motion of dislocations and grain boundaries. Also, the increment in yield strength at 
elevated temperature (300 – 700 
◦
C) deduced from the residual Cr precipitates strengthening ranges from 320 
MPa to 337 MPa. 
1. Introduction 
Cu-based binary alloys are widely used in the electrical industry due 
to their excellent mechanical properties and electrical conductivity 
[ 1 – 5 ], which can be divided into two kinds based on the strengthening 
effects: solid solution strengthening and precipitation hardening. For 
solid solution strengthening Cu alloys, such as Cu-Ag [ 6 ], Cu-Mg [ 7 ], 
and Cu – Sn [ 5 ] alloys, etc., it is difficult to keep high strength and high 
electrical conductivity simultaneously owing to the influence of solid 
solution atoms in Cu matrix. The precipitation hardening Cu alloys, such 
as Cu-Cr [ 8 – 11 ], Cu-Fe [ 12 – 14 ] and Cu – Ni [ 15 – 17 ] alloys, etc., exhibit 
better comprehensive properties than the solid solution strengthening 
type due to the contribution of Orowan strengthening mechanism of the 
precipitates [ 18 ]. Especially Cu – Cr alloys have been paid more attention 
due to their being widely used in the electrotechnical industry [ 9 , 
19 – 21 ]. Although the electrical conductivity of Cu – Cr alloys could reach 
80% IACS (International Annealed Copper Standard defines 17.241 n 
Ω m as 100%), the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is less than 500 MPa 
for Cu – Cr alloy after aging treatment [ 22 , 23 ]. Besides, Cu – Cr alloys 
exhibit poor mechanical properties at high temperatures ( ≥ 300 
◦
C) due 
to the rapid coarsening or re-dissolving Cr precipitates [ 7 , 24 – 26 ], which 
significantly limit their applications under elevated temperatures. 
Therefore, improving Cu – Cr alloy ’ s high temperature mechanical 
properties is highly desirable but still challenging. 
Various methods have been exploited to improve Cu – Cr alloy ’ s high 
temperature mechanical properties in recent years. One common way 
applied is by introducing alloying elements into Cu matrix, such as Mg, 
Nb, Yb, Zr, Si, Sn, Ag and Ti [ 18 , 27 – 32 ]. Ti, Si Cr and Yb can improve the 
strength under elevated temperatures but significantly reduce the elec -
trical conductivity of Cu – Cr alloys. What ’ s more, element Ag is high 
cost, and Zr is easily oxidized, leading to the uncontrolled composition 
and the embrittlement of the alloy in the air atmosphere [ 27 ], all of 
these factors limit the development of Cu – Cr – Ag or Cu – Cr – Zr alloy. On 
the other hand, technological methods such as severe plastic deforma -
tion (SPD), multi-step solution, and aging treatment were introduced to 
improve high temperature strength [ 11 , 33 – 35 ]. Purcek et al. [ 35 ] re -
ported that the ultrafine-grained Cu-based alloys have a softening 
resistance temperature of up to 450 
◦
C. However, the manufacturing 
process of these alloys contains solution treatment, SPD process and 
aging treatment, and the preparation method is discontinuous [ 33 ].

Consequently, exploring new strategies to simultaneously achieve Cu – Cr 
alloy with excellent high temperature mechanical properties and high 
electrical conductivity is essential. 
Our previous study discussed the Cr solubility extension in Cu matrix 
and the structural evolution of nanostructured Cu – Cr alloy powders 
induced by mechanical alloying (MA) [ 36 ]. MA is an effective way to 
manufacture nanostructured materials with excellent homogeneity, and 
it has been widely used to fabricate Cu – Cr alloys [ 37 – 39 ]. Previous work 
showed that the Cr solubility of 0.94 wt% could be achieved, higher than 
0.7 wt% (the solubility at the eutectic temperature), indicating that the 
supersaturated solid solution was realized [ 36 , 40 ]. The current work 
achieved the supersaturated solid solution Cu-5 wt% Cr block composite 
(S1) by combining MA and spark plasma sintering (SPS). SPS method is 
an attractive option for the consolidation of MA powders due to its 
shorter consolidation times than conventional sintering methods, thus 
ensuring better retention of nanocrystalline [ 41 ]. After aging treatment, 
the dissolved Cr (0.94 wt%) could precipitate all out of the Cu matrix. 
When the aging treated Cu – Cr alloy was annealed at high temperature 
( ≥ 300 
◦
C), the Cr precipitates could dissolve into Cu matrix again. It 
should be noted that a maximum of 0.7 wt% Cr can re-dissolve into Cu 
matrix. Therefore, the residual Cr precipitates (at least 0.24 wt%) could 
also act as hard dispersion particles to fix the dislocation at high tem -
peratures. The high temperature (300 – 700 
◦
C) hardness and softening 
resistance tests were carried out in this study. Additionally, the pre -
cipitates microstructure evolution and grain sizes were also character -
ized. Based on the examinations, the strengthening effects at high 
temperatures induced by the residual Cr precipitates were quantitatively 
evaluated from the increment in yield strength by the Orowan rela -
tionship, which could provide new elevated temperature strengthening 
approaches for precipitation hardening alloys. 
2. Experimental procedure 
2.1. Preparation of Cu – Cr alloy 
Commercial copper (99.5%, < 75 μ m) and chromium (99.5%, < 75 
μ m) were used as the initial materials according to the designed nominal 
compositions, as shown in Table 1 . The composite powder preparation 
details could be found in previous work [ 36 ]. After milling, composite 
Cu – Cr powders were consolidated in a vacuum in a LABOX-325S SPS 
System (SINTER LAND INC. Japan). Specifically, the powders were 
placed into cylindrical graphite die with an inner diameter of 30 mm and 
uniaxially loaded with a pressure of 42 MPa. The sintering temperature 
was 750 
◦
C, and the specific sintering process is shown in Fig. 1 . For 
comparison, S2 alloy with 5 wt% Cr was also prepared, composed of 
pre-milling Cu powders and pre-milling Cr powders, as shown in 
Table 1 . It should be mentioned that the S2 alloy was solution treated 
(ST) at 980 
◦
C for 1 h followed by water quenching, and the Cr solubility 
of 0.65 – 0.7 wt% in S2 alloy was achieved [ 2 , 18 , 30 , 40 , 42 , 43 ]. In addi -
tion, the aging treatment (AT) for S1 and S2 alloys was carried out at 
450 
◦
C for 2 h. 
2.2. Morphology and microstructure characterization 
Microstructural evolution was conducted on a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM, Gemini 500) equipped with an electron backscat -
tering diffraction (EBSD). More detailed microstructural evolution of 
Cu – Cr alloys was further characterized by a transmission electron mi -
croscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200). The average grain size and distribu -
tion were determined based on the TEM and EBSD data. The TEM foils 
were ground to 20 μ m and then punched into a disk with 3 mm diameter, 
followed by ion beam polishing (EM, RES102). 
2.3. Mechanical performance and electrical conductivity 
Vickers microhardness measurements of these alloys were conducted 
using a HV1000z Microhardness Tester with a load of 100 g and a 
loading time of 10 s, and the average value of five measurements was 
taken. In addition, the high temperature hardness measurements were 
investigated by Vickers Hardness Tester System (HTV-PHS30) under 
argon atmosphere and in a range from 300 
◦
C to 700 
◦
C within a 100 
◦
C 
interval. The applied load and dwell time were 1000 g and 10 s, 
respectively. The electrical conductivity was performed at room tem -
perature using Sigma Portable digital conductivity Meter equipment 
(FQR-7501), and five values were measured for the average.

wt% and 5.49 wt%, respectively. In addition, the corresponding element

be seen that many twins are formed during the milling process, marked 
with red arrows in Fig. 3 (a) and (b). The width and length of the twins 
are about 50 – 100 nm and 300 – 500 nm, respectively. These twin 
boundaries could effectively hinder the motion of dislocations and act as 
obstacles during dislocations slip, like grain boundaries [ 44 , 45 ]. In

has excellent thermal stability. Fig. 8 (d) shows the SEM image of S1 
alloy, and two types of Cr precipitates exist in Cu matrix, coarse pre -
cipitates with the size of ~30 nm and fine precipitates with ~10 nm. The 
size distribution of these two kinds of precipitates is displayed in Fig. 8 
(e). The size of the coarse precipitates ranges from 20 to 45 nm, and the 
size of fine precipitates is less than 20 nm, and the number fraction of 
fine precipitates with the size of less than 20 nm is nearly 51%. The 
average diameter of the coarse and fine precipitates is 11.58 nm. 
Combining these two kinds of residual Cr precipitates may be very 
effective in hindering the movement of the dislocations and grain 
boundaries. 
To investigate the interaction of two types of residual Cr precipitates 
with Cu grain boundaries, TEM measurements were carried out, as 
shown in Fig. 8 (f). It can be observed that the coarse precipitates marked 
with blue arrows with the size of ~25 nm as an obstacle to inhibiting the 
movement of Cu grain boundaries. The accumulation of stress and dis -
locations causes the dark regions near the grain boundaries marked by 
white rectangle because the motion of the grain boundaries is inhibited. 
Also, the fine precipitates marked with red arrows disperse in Cu matrix, 
which could fix the dislocations in the grain to enhance the strength of 
alloy. Therefore, the softening temperature improvement of S1 alloy 
mainly contributes to two types of Cr precipitates in Cu matrix. The 
combination of these two types of precipitates acts as very powerful 
obstacles for the movement of dislocations and grain boundaries, which 
could enhance the strength of Cu matrix.

4. Conclusions 
In this study, the supersaturated solid solution Cu-5wt% Cr alloy 
with nano-structure was prepared by combining MA and SPS, and 0.94 
wt% Cr solubility was achieved. The effect of residual Cr precipitates on 
the high temperature hardness, softening resistance and microstructures 
were investigated. The results showed that S1-AT alloy exhibits an 
excellent hardness of 106.27 HV at 500 
◦
C, 23.14% higher than common 
sample. The softening temperature of S1-AT alloy is 800 
◦
C (0.74 T
m
). 
The average grain size of S1 alloy annealed at 800 
◦
C is 0.69 μ m, which 
increased by only 0.11 μ m, and the microstructural evolution showed 
that the high thermal stability is mainly attributed to two kinds of re -
sidual Cr precipitates in the matrix. The larger ones act as obstacles to 
inhibit the movement of Cu grain boundaries, and the fine precipitates 
disperse in Cu matrix to fix the dislocations. Combining these two types 
of residual Cr precipitates act as very powerful obstacles to the move -
ment of dislocations and grain boundaries. Also, the increment in yield 
strength at elevated temperature (300 – 700 
◦
C) deduced from the re -
sidual Cr precipitates strengthening ranges from 320 MPa to 337 MPa. 
This strengthening mechanism at high temperature of the residual pre -
cipitates is expected to provide new insight into the precipitation rein -
forced metal matrix alloys.
