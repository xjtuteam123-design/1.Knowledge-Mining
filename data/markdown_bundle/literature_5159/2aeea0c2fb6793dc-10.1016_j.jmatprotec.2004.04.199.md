Effects of casting process variables on the residual stress
in Ni-base superalloys
H. Farhangi∗, S. Norouzi, M. Nili-Ahmadabadi
Department of Metallurgy and Materials Engineering, Faculty of Engineering, University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran
Abstract
Quality of castings is partly affected by the type and extent of residual stresses, which may lead to undesirable distortions and dimensional
changes in critical parts and components. The effect of pouring temperature on residual stress in Ni-base superalloys, IN713C and U500,
was investigated using a cutting technique, followed by measurements of the change in the distance between prescribed markings. For this
purpose, rectangular and triangular shape samples were cast under vacuum using investment casting process. It was found that increasing
melt superheat temperature leads to higher levels of casting residual stresses. The extent of residual stresses were also found to depend
on the casting shape, being higher in the triangular sample due to local variations in casting modulus. Furthermore, it was found that
higher levels of residual stresses are developed in U500 compared to IN713C, at constant melt superheat temperatures. The differences are
explained in terms of variations in the freezing range and thermo-mechanical properties with alloy chemical composition.

1. Introduction
Residual stresses in cast components are elastic stresses
remaining in the castings after they have been removed from
the mould [1–3]. The stresses are basically generated be-
cause of non-uniform expansion and contraction rates from
point to point within castings. Dimensional changes and dis-
tortions resulting from casting residual stresses can particu-
larly affect the quality of near net shape castings. In addition,
the total stress state acting on an engineering component
bearing residual stresses is composed of loading stresses
and the residual stresses. Thus, residual stresses can have a
beneﬁcial or detrimental effect on the function of the com-
ponent, depending on their sign. Accordingly, the service
performance and useful life of engineering components can
be inﬂuenced by the type, extent and distribution of residual
stresses.
Various techniques broadly deﬁned as destructive and
non-destructive have been used for measuring residual
stresses [4]. The cutting technique is a common destructive
method utilized for bulk residual stress measurements. This
method is based upon releasing the locked up stresses by
subjecting the component to a cutting procedure. The re-

2.2. Residual strain measurement method
The magnitude of residual stress in the central bar of the
respective sample castings was evaluated using cutting tech-
nique. Two markings were inscribed on the central bar prior
to cutting process using wire cut machine. The spacing of
the markings was set at 200 and 140 mm for the rectangu-
lar and triangular samples, respectively. The increase in the
spacing of the markings was then measured using a coordi-
nate measurement machine.

3.2. Comparison of freezing ranges
The freezing range for U500 and IN713C superalloys are
shown in Table 2. It is seen that IN713C has a much shorter
freezing range compared to U500. A shorter freezing range
tends to promote uniform and planar solidiﬁcation behav-
ior, and reduces the extent of mushy zone. As a result, the
liquid pool at the solidiﬁcation front can more effectively
compensate the contractions resulting from the solidiﬁcation
process.
Moreover, given that the differences in thermal expan-
sion coefﬁcient and elastic modules of U500 and IN713C
[8,9], over an extended temperature range in the solid state,
are only about a few percent, then at an assumed constant
temperature difference of /Delta1T, the variations in the casting
residual stresses of the superalloys should be related to the
differences in the their ductility as shown by Eq. (2). De-
creasing ductility is expected to result in higher levels of
residual stress. Since IN713C exhibits much lower ductility
at failure compared to U500 [8], by as much as a factor of
2 at some temperature intervals, the higher casting residual
strains measured for IN713C at any given pouring tempera-

3.3. Effects of mould shape
Figs. 2 and 3 also show that higher levels of residual
stresses are generated using triangular sample castings com-
pared to the rectangular castings. This can be attributed to:
(a) the large difference between the casting modulus of cen-
tral bar compared to the sidebars in the triangular castings,
which leads to lower cooling rates in the central bar and
therefore, higher levels of residual stresses and (b) the more
rigid structure of the triangular castings which prevents over-
all distortion and shape change and consequently retains
more residual stresses.
4. Conclusions
1. Increasing pouring temperature results in higher levels of
residual strain in both triangular and rectangular castings
of U500 and IN713C Ni-base superalloys.
2. At constant melt superheat temperature, the magnitude
of residual strain is higher in U500 compared to IN713C.
This effect could be related to the more extended freez-
ing range and mushy solidiﬁcation behavior of U500
superalloy, despite its greater ductility compared to
IN713C.
3. Increasing melt superheat temperature induces higher
thermal gradients and cooling rate differences be-
tween the members, resulting in higher casting residual
stress.
4. Higher levels of residual stresses are generated in trian-
gular sample castings, under similar casting conditions.
