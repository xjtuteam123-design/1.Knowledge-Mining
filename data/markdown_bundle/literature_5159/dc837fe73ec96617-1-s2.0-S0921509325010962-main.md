ABSTRACT
Dispersion strengthened (DS) alloys are used in critical high temperature applications due to their excellent creep 
strength. Finer particles than in precipitation hardened (PH) alloys can be used due to the thermal stability of the 
dispersoids. During recent years, models for the creep strength in PH-alloys have been developed. They have 
successfully been able to describe the creep strength of austenitic stainless steels and copper alloys. These models 
are referred to as time controlled climb (TCC) because the time it takes a dislocation to climb across a particle 
plays a crucial role. In the paper, TCC is applied to DS-alloys for the first time. A copper alloy and a ferritic steel 
are considered. Many DS-alloy are associated with a threshold stress. It is demonstrated it can be computed from 
a dislocation density that is generated during the creep process. The commonly used R ¨osler-Arzt (RA) model 
(1990) is analyzed. Several proposals are made to allow the RA-model to be applied to a wider spectrum of 
applications.
1. Introduction
Precipitation hardening is an effective way to raise the creep strength 
in metallic alloys. Small particles are introduced in the matrix in a heat 
treatment process. The presence of particles blocks the motion of the 
dislocations by forcing them to climb across the particles. In this way, 
the creep strength is increased. The higher the volume fraction and the 
smaller the precipitates are, the larger is the effect. However, fine pre -
cipitates are usually not thermally stable. They tend to coarsen in 
particular at high temperatures, which makes them ineffective. The 
creep strength typically decreases exponentially with increasing tem -
perature. There are different mechanisms that enables dislocations to 
pass particles. Very small particles can be cut by the dislocations. 
However, this is rarely of importance during creep since fine particles 
can freely be climbed. At large stresses, Orowan bowing of dislocations 
is also of importance. The dislocations circumvent the particles by 
forming loops. A more precise description of the active mechanisms will 
be described later in the paper. A general text on precipitation hardening 
can be found in the book by Martin [ 1 ]. Reviews on precipitation 
hardening during creep are available in the books by Zhang [ 2 ], Kassner 
[ 3 ] and Sandstr ¨om [ 4 ].
To avoid the problem with thermal coarsening, fine dispersoids can 
be introduced. They are usually oxides but carbides, nitrides and 
intermetallics can also be considered. Finer particles than for precipi -
tation hardening can be used, which makes it possible to significantly 
raise the creep strength. This type of alloy is referred to as dispersion 
strengthened (DS) or when oxides are used oxide dispersion strength -
ened (ODS). Many types of base alloys such as Ni-base, Fe-base, Al and 
Cu-alloys are applied. The main method for producing DS-alloys is 
mechanical alloying where metal and oxide powders are mixed in a high 
energy mill [ 5 ]. Another common procedure is internal oxidation, where 
metal powder is oxidised and compacted. Mechanical alloying in general 
gives incoherent particles, where the lattices of the matrix and the 
particles do no match. Internal oxidation can generate coherent particles 
[ 6 ]. The latter type of particles can provide a higher strength 
contribution.
DS-alloys have characteristic creep properties. Due to the presence of 
fine particles, the creep rate is low even at high temperatures. The sta -
tionary creep rate varies rapidly with stress. The stress exponent is 
10 – 20 or even higher. Values up to at least 175 have been reported [ 7 ]. 
This should be contrasted to values for PH-alloys, which are in general 
lower. In many cases, the stress exponent is constant over a number of 
decades of strain rates. The stress exponent often decreases with 
increasing temperature. This is particularly noticeable if a wider range 
of temperatures is considered. This indirectly means that the creep 
activation decreases with stress. Nominally, quite high activation

The traditional way of analysing creep rate data of DS-alloys has 
been to introduce a threshold stress [ 34 ]. To be precise a strict threshold 
stress means that no creep takes place below the threshold stress. 
However, this is very difficult to verify experimentally and the threshold 
stress is in many cases simply a limit of observed creep. The threshold 
stress should be contrasted to the strength contribution from particles. 
Particles of all types reduce the creep rate, which can be expressed as a 
strength contribution. The strength contribution depends on the applied 
stress and temperature. The introduction of a threshold stress makes the 
apparent stress exponent become smaller. It is often below 10 that is 
typical for dislocation creep in particle free materials. It was originally 
thought that the particles themselves could provide the threshold stress. 
However, it was early on recognized that the intrinsic barrier against 
climb is much smaller than the typical magnitude of the threshold 
stresses observed [ 35 ]. It is of course easy to understand that if the stress 
exponent is high, say 20, an apparent threshold stress is present. If the 
lowest minimum creep rate recorded is what the testing equipment can 
cope with, a further reduction of the stress by only 5 % from the 
threshold value, makes the creep rate no longer detectable.

A simple model of DS-alloys is to assume that it is the Orowan 
bowing stress from the particles that gives the creep strength [ 27 ]. 
Although it is used in a number of papers, it is not a good solution 
because it gives the wrong temperature dependence. The Orowan stress 
shows a weak temperature dependence whereas the creep strength of 
DS-alloys is typically quite pronounced. The Orowan stress often over -
estimates the creep strength in particular at high temperatures.
In recent years, significant progress in modelling of the creep 
strength in precipitation hardened (PH) alloys has taken place. These 
models are based on a simple principle. It is the time it takes for a 
dislocation to pass a precipitate that controls the strength. This type of 
model is referred to as time controlled climb . Accurate representation of 
PH have been obtained for austenitic stainless steels and Cu-alloys, see 
for example [ 38 – 40 ]. On the other hand, DS-alloys are typically 
analyzed with the help of a threshold stress or the RA-model as 
mentioned above. Thus, PH- and DS-alloys are handled with different 
types of models. However, there is nothing in the derivation of the 
models, which prevents them to be applicable to both types of alloys. 
The open question is whether there are mechanistic differences between 
PH- and DS-alloys. It does not seem to be much discussed in the litera -
ture. In summary, there are three main models that will be considered: 
the threshold model, the RA-model and time controlled climb. These 
three models will be applied to both DS- and PH-alloys to illustrate the 
virtues and the limitations of the models.
In section 2 , a summary of possible dislocation barriers against climb 
over particles is given. In PH-alloys, particles have typically a size dis -
tribution that plays a major role for the creep strength. This is discussed 
in section 3 . A model for a threshold stress is presented in section 4 . It is 
based on a back stress from the dislocation network. In section 5 , the 
particle strengthening due to time controlled climb is applied to DS- 
alloys. The RA-model is analyzed in section 6 . The model is general -
ized to allow it to be applied to more types of alloys.
2. Dislocation barriers
There are several ways that dislocations can pass particles. The dis -
locations can cut through the particles, flow around or climb across 
them. Particle cutting is usually considered only at modest tempera -
tures. It requires quite small particles and they can easily be climbed 
across at elevated temperatures. This will be analyzed in section 5 . 
Particle cutting will not be considered in this paper. Mechanisms for 
particle cutting are described in Ref. [ 1 ]. Orowan bowing is important 
both at ambient and elevated temperatures.

To reach a secondary creep rate, a balance between work hardening 
and recovery must take place. For particle free and PH-alloys, the creep

At low temperatures, some dislocations are not exposed to static 
recovery [ 55 ]. The reason is that during the formation of substructure, 
dislocations with opposite Burgers ’ vector or opposite orientation move 
in different direction during the deformation. This creates a polarised 
dislocation structure that is much more stable against recovery than an 
unpolarised structure giving a higher creep strength [ 56 ]. It creates a 
back stress that is crucial for the influence of cold work on the creep 
strength and why creep curves at close to ambient temperatures have the 
same appearance as at elevated temperatures [ 57 , 58 ].
It is well established that a significant dislocation density can be 
formed in DS-alloys [ 21 , 59 ]. The simplest assumption would be that 
they are geometrically necessary dislocations that are created due to the 
presence of all the dispersoids. However, that would generate a dislo -
cation strength that is far larger than the observed creep strength. That 
would also be the case if the dislocations would be generated through 
cold work.

6. The R ¨osler-Arzt (RA) model
There are many papers in literature on DS-alloys that discuss the 
R ¨osler-Arzt (RA) model [ 36 ]. The model is based on the stress that a 
climbing dislocation stress needs to exceed to leave a particle, the 
so-called detachment stress σ
d
, eq. (11) . The detachment stress is due to 
the reduction in line tension when the dislocation is in contact with the 
particle. The reduction is proportional to the relaxation parameter k
A
. 
The model is accurately derived based on physical principles. From the 
model, high creep stress exponents and creep activation energies are 
typically obtained. These are quite attractive features, which are diffi -
cult to obtain from other approaches. Further developments of the RA 
model have been proposed [ 87 ] but have not received very much 
attention in the literature. A modification has also been proposed to 
handle stress drop tests [ 88 ].
The weak point in the model is that k
A 
is an adjustable parameter, 
which means that the model is not predictable. The only established way 
of finding k
A 
is to fit the model to a full set of creep data. In addition, 
there are three different ways of fitting k
A 
to the creep data: i) the 
detachment stress, eq. (11) ; ii) the stress exponent, eq. (13) and iii) the 
activation energy, eq. (12) . Ideally, the three ways of fitting should give 
the same values but that is typically not the case. In the literature, it can 
be difficult to find out which of the three fitting alternatives that have 
been considered.
This is not a very satisfactory situation and to avoid it, it will be 
assumed that k
A 
is a given parameter. Detailed fitting of k

is only 
presented in a limited number of papers, for example [ 9 , 18 , 63 ]. Further

7. Discussion
The strength of DS-alloys should be controlled primarily by particle 
hardening. In addition, there is strengthening from dislocations. A 
network of dislocations is always formed during creep. This means that 
models for PH-alloys should be applicable. In principle, DS-alloys should 
be simpler to model than PH-alloys, because there is typically also a 
major contribution from solid solution hardening. Its relative impor -
tance is in general smaller in DS-alloys.
The creep strength of PH-alloys was first modelled by assuming that 
the operating mechanism was Orowan bowing, because this was the 
only mechanism that was known. However, this does not give a satis -
factory result. The temperature dependence is not correct. The creep 
strength of high temperature alloys decreases typically exponentially 
with increasing temperature whereas the Orowan mechanism gives 
approximately a linear decrease due to the temperature dependence of 
the shear modulus, eq. (2) . Orowan bowing overestimates the contri -
bution to the strength, since the dislocations can pass the particles by 
climb at lower stresses in particular at high temperatures.
There is general agreement that small particles can be passed by 
climb. For a long time, it was assumed that there is a significant stress 
barrier, which gives the creep strength. However, attempts to compute 
the barrier gave smaller and smaller values until it became evident that 
it is insignificant [ 35 , 64 ]. The resulting stress barrier is provided in eq. 
(9) .
To resolve this problem, the time it takes for a dislocation to climb 
across a particle must be considered [ 89 ]. This is referred to as time 
controlled climb, which is described in section 5 . The basic assumption 
is that small particles can be climbed across freely. Only when there is 
not time enough, climb cannot take place. Larger particles must be 
passed by Orowan bowing. This simple concept has turned out to be 
quite successful. Two examples are shown in section 5 for P92 and 
Super304H. A number of examples have also been published in the 
literature, in particular for austenitic stainless steels [ 38 , 39 , 47 , 66 ]. One 
difficulty with these materials is that several strengthening mechanisms 
are involved, not least solid solution hardening. The final verification of 
the approach could be made by applying it to CuCo-alloys where solid 
solution hardening is negligible [ 40 ]. Several heat treatment conditions 
could successfully be described.
It is well known that PH-alloys can have considerably higher creep 
activation energies than the value for self-diffusion [ 59 , 90 ]. The acti -
vation energies from the dislocation barriers in eqs. (8) and (12) or (39) 
represent a possible explanation to the high activation energies. Eq. (8)
has been used in section 5 when deriving the creep rates. Eqs. (8) and 
(39) have quite similar appearance. They are both proportional to the 
particle radius and their values are not too different. The problem is that 
you expect that a finer particle distribution that gives a higher creep 
strength would also be associated with a higher activation energy, but 
that is not the case according to the equations. This problem remains 
unsolved at present.

A threshold stress is common for DS-alloys. An example was illus -
trated for an Al2.6Al2O3 alloy in section 4 . The threshold stress was 
modelled as a back stress that can appear in alloys at lower temperatures 
[ 91 ]. This back stress is generated during the creep process. If the stress 
exponent is high, the back stress can compensate for the rapidly 
increasing true stress with strain. In Cu at close to ambient temperature, 
the stress exponent could be as high as 30 to 50. Without a major back 
stress, it would be impossible to explain why creep strain versus time 
curves appear in much the same as at much higher temperatures [ 57 ]. 
This effect is likely to be the same for DS-alloys with high stress 
exponents.
The back stress is modelled by introducing a separate dislocation 
density [ 56 ]. This dislocation density is polarized. Dislocations of 
opposite Burgers ’ vector or orientation always move in different di -
rections in a stress field. This means that these two types of dislocations 
end up in different positions. The dislocations become polarized. It is 
very likely that this is the case also for DS-alloys. The development of the 
dislocation density for the back stress is modelled with the same type of 
creep equation as other dislocations except that the static recovery term 
is missing. This must be the case because dislocations of opposite sign 
and type are not located at the same position. The absence of the static 
recovery term implies that the dislocation density can reach high values 
which results in a large back stress. In alloys close to ambient temper -
atures, it gives a huge effect. If it is neglected, it can generate large er -
rors. The back stress is a possible origin of the threshold stress in many 
DS-alloys.
It has been seen in section 5 , that time controlled climb can represent 
the creep strength of DS-alloys. Results were presented for the alloy

stress. As demonstrated in Fig. 9 , high stress exponents of up to 30 can be 
reached. This is essential when describing DS-alloys. This is the same 
effect that gives rise to high stress exponents in ordinary PH-alloys. 
However, there are limitations. It requires a high effective stress. 
Sometimes, the effective stress is low for DS-alloys and then the model 
does not give high stress exponents. The alloy IN9021 in Fig. 12 is an 
example of that.
Procedures for extrapolation for conventional creep resistant alloys 
seem to work for DS- alloys as well. This has been demonstrated for 
martensitic 9Cr-ODS steels. For example, the Larson-Miller parameter 
was applied [ 92 ]. In Ref. [ 26 ], Θ projection has been applied to a 
13CrODS steel.
The model of R ¨osler and Arzt (RA-model) [ 36 ] is well-known and 
discussed in many papers on DS-alloys. It has several valuable aspects. It 
can give the high stress exponents and creep activation energies that are 
often observed. The RA-model is semi-empirical since it assumes that the 
relaxation parameter k
A 
that describes the interaction between a dislo -
cation and a particle that it is climbing is an adjustable parameter. The 
only well-known way of finding k
A 
is to fit it to a full set of creep data for 
an alloy. It is found out in the present paper that it is not necessary to 
have k
A 
as a fitting parameter, because most values found in the liter -
ature lie in a narrow range between 0.9 and 0.95 with preference for 
0.95. So for most purposes a value of k
A 
= 0.95 has been chosen. This has 
the advantage that the model is now predictable and the model can be 
applied to a much wider set of alloys.
There are, however, limitations with the RA-model. The maximum 
stress that the model can handle is the detachment stress. For k
A 
= 0.9 to 
0.95, the detachment stress is 10 – 20 % of the Orowan stress, eq. (11) . 
This is often lower than some applied stresses, so the model can only be 
used for some of the test data. This problem can be reduced by 
decreasing the k
A 
value but then the predicted activation energy and 
stress exponent would typically be too high. It has turned out that it is 
even difficult to find data sets where the detachment stress is well above 
the applied stresses. To extend the use of the RA-model, it is suggested 
that average values over the valid stress range are used instead, eqs. (39) 
and (40) .
In the derivation of the RA-model, there is nothing that restricts it to 
DS-alloys. It should be applicable to PH-alloys as well but no such ap -
plications have been found in the literature. The particles are typically 
larger or more widely spaced in PH-alloys than in DS-alloys. This re -
duces the magnitude of the Orowan stress and that makes it even more 
difficult to fulfil the criteria for the detachment stress. This is illustrated 
for Super304H in Fig. 15 . This can be assumed to be the main reason 
why applications to PH-alloys are lacking.
8. Conclusions
⋅ Barriers against climb of dislocations over particles are summarized. 
For the relevant barriers, the stress barriers are small but they can 
give a significant contribution to the creep activation energy.
⋅ It is illustrated that particle size distribution in PH-alloys often show 
an exponential distribution. Size distributions for DS-alloys are 
available to a limited extent but a similar behaviour can be expected.
⋅ Many DS-alloys have a high stress exponent and show a threshold 
stress. In PH-alloys, a back stress is formed at higher stresses and 
lower temperatures. This a due to a polarization of the dislocation 
density. Dislocations of opposite Burgers ’ vector and type will move 
in different directions in a stress field. These polarized dislocations 
are not exposed to static recovery, which can generate a high back 
stress. It is very likely that the same effect appears in DS-alloys 
although it has not been explicitly demonstrated. The modelled 
back stress at low stresses has been used to model the value of the 
threshold stress.
⋅ Models for particle hardening of PH-alloys should be applicable to 
DS-alloys. In recent years, models for precipitation hardening based 
on time controlled climb (TCC) has been successful in modelling the 
stress and temperature dependence of precipitation strengthening of 
austenitic stainless steels and copper alloys. The model is based on 
the existence of a critical particle size that is temperature and stress 
dependent. Particles smaller than the critical size can freely be 
climbed whereas larger particles have to be passed by Orowan 
bowing. To find the critical particle size, the particle size distribution 
must be known.
⋅ Examples of the TCC model are given in the present paper for the 
martensitic steel P92 and the austenitic stainless steel Super304H. 
For the first time the TCC model has also been applied to two DS- 
alloys. It is demonstrated that the model can handle stress expo -
nents of at least up to 30.
⋅ The R ¨osler and Arzt (RA) model has been widely discussed in the 
literature in connection to DS-alloys. The model has several
