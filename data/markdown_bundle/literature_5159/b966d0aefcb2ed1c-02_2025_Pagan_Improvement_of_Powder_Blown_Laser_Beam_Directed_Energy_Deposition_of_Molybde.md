Molybdenum (Mo) is comparable in weight, has higher thermal conductivity,
costs less, and can sustain higher mechanical loads at high temperatures than
niobium, motivating the development of additive manufacturing (AM) of Mo
structural components as complements and/or replacements to niobium-base
refractory alloys for high-temperature applications. Toward enabling AM of
Mo components an investigation of directed energy deposition AM using a
laser beam energy source and blown powder feeding (DED-LB-BP) was per-
formed. Mo powders mixed with La
2O3 ceramic particles were printed and
showed grain reﬁnement compared to pure Mo depositions. Pure Mo samples
exhibited grains that grew several millimeters, signiﬁcant porosity, and
intergranular cracking. Additions of 0.7–3.5 wt.% La
2O3 particles result in
printed densities greater than 99% corresponding with 100- to 300- lm-sized
grains, limited porosity, and reduced cracking. Furthermore, previous work
suggested that laser powers greater than 4500 W may be required for DED-
LB-BP of Mo; in this work, materials were fabricated and improved using
laser powers less than 2000 W.
INTRODUCTION
High melting temperatures, creep resistance,
thermal conductivity, and resistance to radiation
are some of the main attractions that make molyb-
denum (Mo) and other refractory alloys desirable for
use in critical high-temperature applications such
as reactors, aerospace turbine engines, space
nuclear systems, and missile systems.
1–4 Of the
refractory metals, Mo is one of the lightest and
lowest cost. It also exhibits high thermal conductiv-
ity and, when protected from oxidation or used in
vacuum, it retains mechanical strength at temper-
atures above 1000 /C176C. However, it also exhibits a
high ductile to brittle transition temperature of up
to 300/C176C depending on the strain rate,
5–7 resulting
in poor room-temperature mechanical properties
and manufacturing challenges. Commercially pure
molybdenum is also difﬁcult to weld as it experi-
ences signiﬁcant grain size coarsening when heated
to its recrystallization temperature, around 1100–
1500/C176C depending on impurity levels. 8,9 During
welding, its grain boundaries are rapidly enriched
with impurities, such as C, N, and O, which result
in grain boundary fracture from brittle molybde-
num oxide and carbide formation. Due to the low
fabricability of molybdenum components, they are
typically manufactured via powder metallurgy tech-
niques, which limits the attainable size and geo-
metric complexity.
10 The development of new
manufacturing methods and molybdenum alloys
which overcome these limitations is desired.
Additive manufacturing (AM) represents a signif-
icant disruption to traditional manufacturing as
complex, near-net-shape components can be created
in a fraction of the time or cost from traditionally
manufactured components.
11–13 The development of
AM for Mo has been pursued with powder bed
fusion (PBF) and both wire and blown powder
directed energy deposition (DED) methods. PBF

13 However, cost and powder require-

DED can be larger, as material is only deposited
where needed.
21 Therefore, creating molybdenum
structural components using DED metal AM pro-
vides signiﬁcant cost savings and production capa-
bilities.13,21 Stawovy22 demonstrated that pure Mo
could be fabricated using wire feed-directed energy
deposition electron beam (DED-EB), although the
components contained coarse millimeter-sized
grains with signiﬁcant porosity due to impurity
elements. Johnson and Palmer
23 demonstrated the
feasibility of DED-LB-BP using Mo, although they
concluded that high power inputs ( > 6000 W) were
required to adhere the powders to room-tempera-
ture substrates, while large grains, signiﬁcant
porosity, and residual stresses were still present.
Wire arc DED has also been used for creating
molybdenum components, although large columnar
grains, weak grain boundaries, and brittle fractures
occurred due to molybdenum oxide formation.
24
Some established strategies to improve the room-
temperature mechanical properties of Mo are to
facilitate dislocation motion, reduce impurity con-
tent in grain boundaries, and reinforce the material
with a second phase to reﬁne the grains and
restrain recrystallization.
25 Several types of disper-
soids have been used for Mo alloys, including
lanthanum, titanium, and zirconium compounds.
Although these could pin dislocations, only certain
concentrations can produce beneﬁcial results.
26–28
Rhenium, osium, iridium, and platinum alloying
have also been investigated to improve the room-
temperature properties of molybdenum, but their
limited natural availability and high costs limit
commercial use.
29,30
Lanthana (La 2O3) additions to Mo (MoLa) can
increase the mechanical and corrosive properties of
Mo while also increasing its recrystallization tem-
perature. The La 2O3 compound also absorbs impu-
rities and oxides away from the molybdenum during
processing.
26,30,31 Coarse micron-sized La 2O3 is
detrimental to the mechanical properties, 25,32,33
while nanosized particles have been found to main-
tain smaller grain size and increase the plasticity of
the alloy. 34,35 Strengthening effects from La 2O3
have been associated with both room-temperature
and high-temperature mechanical strengthening,
increased creep resistance, and fracture toughness
enhancements.
35 This occurs through several mech-
anisms such as pinning dislocations to hinder
gliding and climbing. 36 While the decreased Mo
grain size leads to Hall-Petch strengthening, there
is strong evidence that Orowan strengthening plays
a major role in the improved mechanical
properties.
33,37
Here, we present work to advance DED-LB-BP
AM of Mo by investigating the use of La 2O3
additions which are known to be beneﬁcial in
traditionally manufactured Mo alloys to improve
the mechanical properties, only here we investigate
their ability to reﬁne grain sizes during DED-LB-BP
AM processing. We also investigate the ability to
develop process parameter sets using lower laser
powers (< 2 kW) than the existing state of the art
(4.5–9 kW).

Additive Manufacturing (AM)
AM was performed within an ultra-high purity
inert argon environment of an Optomec LENS 860
hybrid manufacturing system. Readings of the
machine oxygen sensors did not exceed 45 ppm of
oxygen during manufacturing. The process used a
nLight 1070-nm ﬁber laser with a maximum power
of 2000 W, a gaussian-shaped spot size of 600 lm
diameter, and a focal length of 200 lm. Powder was
deposited using ultra-highpurity argon carry gas to
blow powder through 4 discrete coaxial copper
nozzles, each with an aperture diameter of
1.3 mm. The powder deposition rate, or mass ﬂow,
was kept constant throughout the duration of each
print, including within the interlayer dwell time, to
minimize interruptions of the powder ﬂow stability.
The mass powder ﬂow rate was calculated using a
correlation between powder hopper auger speed and
measured molybdenum powder collection averaged
over 100 s. All the printed samples in these exper-
iments were wall samples created using a scanning
strategy of longitudinal printing scanning in the +
X direction of 25.4 mm, immediately followed by
scanning in the – X direction of 25.4 mm with a
corresponding increase in layer height + Z of
0.3 mm. This created thin-wall samples with widths
ranging from 1.0 to 1.5 mm. A lead in and out
distance was employed for each layer at the begin-
ning and end of the deposition track to negate the
acceleration effects of the CNC bed, removing the
overbuild otherwise seen at the ends of the track.
This pattern was repeated for 25 layers, designed to
build a 7.5-mm-tall wall, as shown in Fig. 1. All the
samples were removed from the baseplate after
printing using standard wire electrical discharge
machining operations.
Design of Experiments
Previous process parameter studies for this con-
ﬁguration of DED-LB-BP system are limited, and
therefore we initially used one factor at a time
(OFAT) methodology to study the effects of varying
the laser power, scan speed, mass ﬂow rate, layer
height, interlayer dwell time, powder carrier gas
ﬂow rate, print head center gas ﬂow rate, initial
working distance, and lead in/out distance using
pure Mo powder. From the initial OFAT testing, we
chose to ﬁx the powder carrier gas ﬂow rate to 5.1
LPM, print head center gas ﬂow rate to 21.2 LPM,
initial working distance to 5.715 mm, and lead in/
out distance to a 2.54-mm z step between layers to
0.3 mm and to have a 1-s pause between layers.
A central composite design (CCD) response sur-
face methodology (RSM) was used to sequentially
search for the region of the parameter design space
which resulted in the highest density for 2.1-wt.%
MoLa powder. These parameters were then used to
deposit pure Mo for a direct comparison of the
beneﬁts of adding lanthana ceramic to deposit Mo in
DED-LB-PB.

Density Testing
After the manufactured samples were removed
from the build plate, they were tested for density
using the Archimedes’ Principle, following ASTM
B962. The inﬂuence of open porosity was mitigated
by vacuum impregnating the samples with Mobile
DTE 24 oil. Mass measurements of the green mass,
the oil-impregnated mass, and the mass submerged
in distilled water were taken on a Mettler Toledo
XPR 225 scale and density kit. Water temperature
measurements were taken during the measure-
ments to apply appropriate correction values.

powder with La
2O3 were collected prior to manu-

particle size, ﬂowability, and skeletal density. Pow-

Powder samples were laid on a copper tape that

X-ray Fluorescence
Elemental distribution of La in the powders and
prints was determined using micro x-ray ﬂuores-
cence (l-XRF). Powder samples were adhered using
tape to the sample stage prior to analysis. Thin wall
samples were prepared in a similar manner to SEM

The measurements used a rhodium x-ray source
with a voltage of 50 keV and a current of 600 lA.
The La distribution maps were captured by expos-

between pixels. Elemental quantiﬁcation was per-
formed on the Bruker M4 Tornado software using
the oxide conﬁguration software.

CONCLUSION
Molybdenum alloys are limited by difﬁculties in
traditional manufacturing due to their poor room
temperature ductility and rapid oxide embrittle-
ment. Metal AM through directed energy deposition
(DED-LB-BP) provides the ability to rapidly exam-
ine a variety of processing parameter conditions and
screen alloy compositions, while also creating large
complex structural components. The deposition of
pure molybdenum using DED-LB-BP typically
exhibits large grains, signiﬁcant porosity, and
intergranular cracking. Introducing 0.7- to 3.5-
wt.% La
2O3 to molybdenum signiﬁcantly decreased
the grain size, porosity, and cracking. The average
grain size in the build direction decreased from
several millimeters (the size of the printed sample)
down to 200 lm or less. Within the range of
investigated dispersoid concentrations, the 2.1-
wt.% La
2O3 minimized the grain size without
detrimentally affecting geometric ﬁdelity. More-
over, statistical design pointed toward a promising
processing zone for the Mo 2.1-wt.% La
2O3 where
greater than 99% density was achieved as compared
