1. Introduction
The development of high-performance metallic structural materials 
has always aimed to achieve an optimal balance between strength, 
ductility, and low density. With the increasing performance demands of 
modern industry, traditional single-principal-element alloys — such as 
steels, aluminum alloys, magnesium alloys, and titanium alloys — are 
gradually reaching their limits in strength, ductility, corrosion resis -
tance, and high-temperature stability. These limitations have driven 
researchers in materials science to explore novel structural metallic 
materials with superior comprehensive properties, capable of meeting 
engineering requirements in extreme environments. Consequently, 
developing new alloy systems that combine low density, high strength, 
good ductility, and high-temperature stability has become a key focus in 
materials science research.
In recent years, lightweight refractory complex concentrated alloys 
(LRCCAs) have emerged as a research hotspot in the field of metallic 
structural materials due to their unique compositional flexibility and 
exceptional performance potential [ 1 – 8 ]. Compared to traditional re -
fractory alloys (e.g., W-, Mo-, and Nb-based alloys), LRCCAs incorporate 
lightweight elements such as Ti, Al, and Zr to significantly reduce den -
sity while maintaining high-temperature strength through high-melting- 
point components like V and Nb [ 9 – 14 ]. This achieves a promising 
balance between lightweight design and high-temperature performance 
[ 15 – 18 ]. However, the practical application of LRCCAs still faces major 
challenges. First, while refractory elements enhance high-temperature

3. Results
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of the alloy under different processing 
conditions. The results indicate that both the HR alloy and HRA740 alloy 
consist solely of a single BCC solid solution phase. However, in addition 
to the BCC phase, the HRA580 alloy shows detectable precipitation of an 
HCP phase.
Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 presents the EBSD characterization results of the 
LRCCAs under different processing conditions. The HR alloy exhibits a 
microstructure composed of fibrous grains and near-equiaxed grains 
distributed along the fibrous grains. Shear bands extending from the 
center to both sides are also observed. After annealing at 580 
◦
C 
(HRA580 alloy), the fibrous grains in the center become more elongated 
with reduced fraction, while the fraction of near-equiaxed grains in -
creases. Meanwhile, some deformation bands still remain. When the 
annealing temperature increases to 740 
◦
C (HRA740 alloy), the fraction 
of fibrous grains further decreases with a corresponding increase in 
near-equiaxed grains, this indicates a further increase in the extent of 
alloy recrystallization, which is consistent with the results obtained from 
the Grain Orientation Spread (GOS) mapping ( Fig. 3 ). Notably, some 
equiaxed grains show significant growth, indicating enhanced recrys -
tallization. The microstructural difference between the central layer and 
outer regions in the HR alloy originates from thermal gradients during 
processing. The surface layers experience greater heat loss due to con -
tact with relatively cooler rollers, while the center maintains higher 
temperatures. Consequently, the surface undergoes more severe shear 
deformation, resulting in higher defect density and greater recrystalli -
zation driving force [ 29 ]. In contrast, the central layer retains elongated 
deformed grains due to its higher temperature, lower recrystallization 
driving force, and stronger recovery tendency. The kernel average 
misorientation (KAM) maps reveal that near-equiaxed grains in the HR 
alloy show higher KAM values than central fibrous grains, confirming 
more intense shear deformation in surface-near regions. However, after 
annealing, these near-equiaxed grains display lower KAM values than 
fibrous grains, reflecting their stronger recrystallization driving force 
compared to fibrous grains.

Fig. 6 presents SEM fractography results of the tensile fracture sur -

figures of HR alloy and HR740 alloy show that necking occurred during 
tensile deformation of the alloys, and HR740 alloy is more resistant to 
necking than HR alloy. In contrast, HRA580 alloy shows a relatively flat 
fracture surface without noticeable necking. Higher-magnification SEM 
observations demonstrate distinct fracture characteristics, the central 
area displays numerous dimples and microporous, accompanied by 
fatigue-like features at the edges. However, the central region of HR740 
alloy is relatively flat, and tear ridges are observed, while the periphery

contains abundant dimples with some microcracks, and the peripheral

their specific fracture features varied according to their respective pro -

structural evolution induced by each thermal – mechanical treatment.

However, partially coherent interfaces exhibit dual characteristics by 
combining both coherent and incoherent regions. For strength 
enhancement, the coherent regions hinder dislocation motion through 
elastic strain fields, producing strengthening effects similar to fully 
coherent interfaces. Meanwhile, the incoherent regions create stronger 
obstacles via interfacial dislocation networks. This hybrid mechanism 
forces dislocations to alternately overcome different types of interfacial 
resistance, significantly increasing energy dissipation during dislocation 
motion and resulting in superior strengthening. Regarding plastic 
deformation behavior, partially coherent interfaces demonstrate unique 
dislocation-interface interactions. When dislocations encounter 
coherent regions, they can transmit across the interface through dislo -
cation reactions or decomposition, maintaining continuity of plastic 
deformation [ 51 – 54 ]. In contrast, incoherent regions cause dislocations 
to either pile up at the interface or undergo cross-slip. This differentiated 
dislocation behavior leads to more uniform deformation and delays the 
formation of localized strain concentrations. More importantly, the 
incoherent regions at interfaces can act as dislocation sources, emitting

4.2. Effect of annealing on strength-ductility trade-off
The present LRCCAs achieve a remarkable strength-ductility com -
bination through a carefully designed process combining hot rolling and 
annealing, where multiscale microstructural control enables promising 
matching between strengthening and plasticizing mechanisms. During 
hot rolling, high-temperature plastic deformation introduces high- 
density dislocations and deformation substructures that not only 
significantly enhance alloy strength through dislocation strengthening 
but also store sufficient deformation energy to drive subsequent 
annealing processes. The low-temperature annealing plays a pivotal role 
through two complementary effects: moderate thermal activation pro -
motes dislocation rearrangement and reorganization to form stable 
dislocation cells and sub-grain boundaries, creating an optimized 
dislocation configuration that maintains considerable strengthening 
while reducing stress concentration to improve plastic deformation 
capability. Simultaneously, the low-temperature thermodynamic con -
ditions induce precipitation of nanoscale secondary phase particles from 
the supersaturated solid solution, which produce notable precipitation 
strengthening effects while their fine and uniform distribution avoids 
excessive obstruction of dislocation motion. The improved ductility 
stems primarily from optimized dislocation structures where annealing- 
induced recovery rearranges dislocations into low-energy configurations 
that preserve high initial dislocation density while enhancing mobility 
through reduced stress concentration, coupled with partially coherent 
interfaces between HCP precipitates and BCC matrix that permit dislo -
cation transmission across boundaries via decomposition to maintain 
plastic deformation capacity. Most importantly, the retained high- 
density dislocations and nanoscale precipitates develop a synergistic 
interaction where dislocation networks provide preferential nucleation 
sites for precipitates while the precipitates in turn stabilize dislocation 
structures through pinning effects, creating a mutually constrained yet 
reinforcing microstructural characteristic that enables sustained work 
hardening during deformation to simultaneously ensure high strength 
and prevent localized necking through uniform plastic deformation, 
ultimately achieving an promising balance between strength and 
ductility.
4.3. Heterostructure enhanced strength-ductility trade-off
Microstructural analysis reveals that the alloy develops a heteroge -
neous grain structure consisting of “ fibrous unrecrystallized grains +
equiaxed recrystallized grains ” after hot rolling. Following subsequent 
annealing at 580 
◦
C, this heterogeneous structure is not only preserved 
but further enhanced by the precipitation of secondary phases, ulti -
mately forming a multiscale “ fibrous unrecrystallized grains + equiaxed 
recrystallized grains + HCP precipitates ” architecture that synergisti -
cally improves both strength and ductility through coupled multiscale 
mechanisms. The strengthening-toughening mechanism of this hetero -
geneous structure operates at multiple levels: the significant deforma -
tion incompatibility between fibrous unrecrystallized grains and 
equiaxed recrystallized grains creates strain gradients and back stress 
strengthening through GNDs networks at their interfaces, where the 
density gradient induces back stress that simultaneously elevates yield 
strength and delays necking through sustained work hardening 
[ 6 , 55 – 57 ]. Concurrently, the multiphase interface cooperative slip 
mechanism emerges through partially coherent interfaces between 
nano-sized HCP precipitates and BCC matrix, where periodic dislocation 
networks exert dual effects by both obstructing dislocation motion to 
produce precipitation strengthening and permitting dislocation 
decomposition (e.g., a/2 < 111 > BCC → a/3 < 11 – 20 > HCP + a/6 <
11 – 23 > HCP) to enable cross-interface slip, while the recrystallized 
grains act as “ soft phases ” that preferentially activate dislocation sources 
and the unrecrystallized regions serve as “ hard phases ” that maintain 
work hardening through dislocation storage [ 6 , 57 ].
5. Conclusions
In this work, systematic investigations were conducted on Ti-V-Al- 
Zr-Nb LRCCAs subjected to hot rolling plus annealing treatments, 
focusing on phase composition, microstructure, and mechanical prop -
erties under different processing conditions, with exploration of 
strengthening mechanisms. The main conclusions are summarized as 
follows: 
(1) The HR alloy and HRA740 alloy consist of a single BCC solid 
solution phase, while the HRA580 alloy comprises a BCC matrix 
with HCP precipitates. The HCP precipitates are enriched in Ti 
and Al elements, the BCC matrix is rich in V and Nb elements, and 
Zr elements are uniformly distributed.
(2) Both HR alloy and HRA740 alloy exhibit a heterogeneous grain 
structure composed of “ fibrous grains + equiaxed grains ” . With 
increasing annealing temperature, the proportion of fibrous 
grains decreases while that of equiaxed grains increases. The HR 
alloy contains a high density of dislocations, which undergo re -
covery during high-temperature annealing. In contrast, low- 
temperature annealing maintains a relatively high dislocation 
density while simultaneously promoting HCP precipitation.
(3) All annealed alloys show improved strength-ductility combina -
tions. The HRA740 alloy exhibits the highest fracture strain of
