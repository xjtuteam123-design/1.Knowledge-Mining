ABSTRACT
Several fast-spectrum nuclear reactors designed to generate high power (~450 MWe) rely on forced convection
of media such as supercritical CO
2
, sodium, or liquid lead to cool the nuclear core, operating at temperatures up
to 600
◦
C. Cost-effective, high-strength Fe-based alumina forming austenitic (AFA) alloys are a promising
candidate for the fabrication of critical nuclear components. This study investigated laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF) processing of an AFA alloy composition optimized for improved creep resistance. Electron microscopy
revealed an elongated grain structure along the build direction with a fine sub-grain cellular structure decorated
with (Cr,Fe,Nb)
23
C
6
carbide precipitates at the intercellular boundaries. At temperatures of 20 – 900
◦
C, the LPBF
alloy ’ s superior tensile properties compared to its arc-melted counterpart and other advanced steels (e.g., SS316)
were attributed to the distribution of nano-sized carbide precipitates, whereas the high ductility was attributed to
the LPBF alloy ’ s elongated grain structure.
High performance automotive engines and advanced nuclear reactor
designs are taking advantage of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) additive
manufacturing because of its high geometric resolution ( < 200 µ m ),
making manufacturing geometrically complex components a reality [ 1 ,
2 ]. Besides the geometric freedom, LPBF is host to some of the fastest
cooling rates ( > 10
6
K/s [ 3 , 4 ]) in any bulk manufacturing process. The
rapid solidification of high-temperature commercial alloys such as
stainless steel (SS) 316, advanced austenitic steel HK30Nb, or Ni-based
Inconel 625 and 718, results in a high degree of solute segregation, often
forming a sub-grain cellular structure found within a heterogenous grain
structure [ 4 – 11 ]. The unique microstructures in LPBF-processed mate-
rial can be tailored to enhance mechanical performance as compared to
cast or wrought counterparts [ 6 , 7 , 12 , 13 ]
Although success has been widely demonstrated in processing alloy
SS316 into desired components by LPBF, it was designed to be mainly
single-phase, with operating temperatures not expected to exceed 500
◦
C. Oxide or carbide dispersion – strengthened versions of LPBF SS316
could result in superior high-temperature mechanical properties
through dislocation and grain boundary pinning, but limited improve-
ments have been reported [ 14 – 16 ]. Moreover, the compatibility of
SS316 with the proposed coolants for advanced fast-spectrum reactors
such as supercritical CO
2
(SCO
2
), sodium, or liquid lead also drastically
limits operating temperatures [ 17 – 21 ]. Ni-based superalloys such as 718
or 625 offer superior strength and corrosion resistance as well as exhibit
high LPBF processability [ 22 , 23 ], but the high cost of Ni limits their
deployment [ 24 ].
Alumina forming austenitic (AFA) alloys are a class of alloys that
have been developed over the past 20 years as a cost-effective alterna-
tive to Ni-based superalloys [ 24 , 25 ]. AFA alloys offer superior corrosion
resistance in environments such as humid air, SCO
2
, and liquid metals
(e.g., lead) because of the slow growth of Al
2
O
3
scales, which are more
stable at high temperatures than the Cr
2
O
3
scales that form on alloys like
SS316 or HK30Nb [ 26 , 27 ]. As a class of wrought and cast alloys, AFA
alloys can be tailored to the application ’ s operating conditions, with
additions of solid solution strengthening elements (Mo, W, etc.) or the
formation of strengthening precipitates such as NiAl, M

Pre-alloyed gas-atomized powder was acquired from the Powder Alloy
Corporation (PAC) and was processed with a Renishaw AM250 LPBF
system, equipped with a 400 W Yb fiber pulsed laser which has a spot
size of ~130 µ m . All builds were performed in an inert Ar atmosphere,
keeping the O

feedstock powder, the resulting LPBF material, and the cast counterpart
are reported in Table 1 . Both the arc-melted and LPBF material met the
specification set for the desired composition determined by Gwalani
et al. [ 33 ], but with a slightly lower Y content potentially lost due to
splatter of oxide particles.
An initial processing study was performed to assess the AFA alloy
printability. The LPBF process parameters chosen were based on the
Renishaw recommended parameters for SS316: laser power ( P ) of 195
W, point distance ( PD ) of 60 µ m, dwell time ( DT ) of 80 µ s, hatch spacing
( HS ) of 110 µ m , and layer thickness ( T ) of 50 µ m . These most influential
parameters are often normalized with the energy density ( ED ) term
expressed as follows:

Process mapping studies often involve varying the five energy den-
sity parameters to some combination, thereby varying the total energy
input of the system. Results from two different studies with 18 samples
each (36 in total) were used to explore the effects of the energy density
parameters as reported in Table 2 . Laser power and scan speed — varying
dwell time — were explored first because these parameters directly affect
the molten melt pool formation [ 4 ]. The point distance and hatch
spacing were kept constant at 60 and 110 µ m, respectively. Once a laser
power was determined over a given scan speed range, the layer thickness
was investigated since powder layer thickness has been shown to play a
significant role in the solidification microstructure and defect evolution
[ 3 , 4 ]. Simple rectangular samples with dimensions of 5 × 10 × 12 mm
3
were printed, and the different parameter combinations were assigned
to individual samples. No contours or remelting strategies were used,
and a consistent 67
◦
scan rotation was maintained between layers.
After being removed from the build plate, the bulk density of the
samples was measured using the Archimedes immersion method as
described in ASTM B962. The samples were immersed in ethanol, and
three measurements were taken for each sample. Select samples were
then sectioned along the build direction (BD) and metallographically
prepared. Since it was not feasible to perform metallography on all the
samples, only 12 were selected. Optical microscopy analysis was per-
formed using a Keyence VHX-6000 to analyze the defect formation and
to confirm density. Image analysis of the flaws and defects was per-
formed with ImageJ; the procedure is described in our previous work
[ 4 ]. Image analysis was used to confirm the optimized parameter chosen
based on the Archimedes immersion data. Comparisons for the density
of the select cube samples between that measured by image analysis and
Archimedes immersion is reported in Fig. S1 in Supplementary Material.

The observed texture along the BD is (001) 〈 100 〉 , as shown in Fig. 3 c,
with a texture index near 6. The texture index represents the randomness
of a particular texture throughout a map, with an index near 1 being a
completely random isotropic material. Therefore, an index of 6 repre-
sents a strong preference towards a given orientation. Observation of the
lower strength in Fig. 2 at room temperature for the samples tested along
the BD vs. samples perpendicular to the BD indicate that slip was easily
activated along the BD due to the elongated grain structure [ 35 ];
enhanced slip allows for the increased movement of dislocations, thus
lowering the strength and increasing the ductility. Although the KAM
magnitude is relatively low, the vast network suggests the presence of a
large dislocation density. Grain mapping performed at high magnifica-
tion as shown in Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material indicates the
presence of multiple misorientations within a single grain, typically a
result of high deformation [ 36 , 37 ].

counterpart. It has been commonly reported that industry standard
