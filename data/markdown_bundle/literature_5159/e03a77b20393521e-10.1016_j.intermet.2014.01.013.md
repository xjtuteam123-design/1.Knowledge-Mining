abstract
TieAleNb-based alloys have shown promise for high temperature applications, however limited
research has been conducted in g(TiAl) þ s(Nb2Al) alloys that are aimed towards increasing strength and
operating temperatures through microstructural control. Alloys with less than 30% s-phase have been
investigated, exploring relationships between microstructure and deformation mechanisms. An alloy
with composition Ti e45Ale14Nbe5Cre1Mo has been produced, characterized, and tested at high
temperature under compression at strain rates ranging from 10 /C0 3 to 10/C0 5 s/C0 1. The ﬂow behavior of this
alloy shows strain rate and temperature dependence with constant strain rate sensitivity at each tem-
perature in the testing regime. Microstructural analysis reveals the
g-phase is primarily responsible for
the alloy ’s ability to accommodate deformation to large strains under compression.

1. Introduction
Advancements in the research and development of lightweight
intermetallics have led to increases in the overall ef ﬁciency and
performance of jet turbine engines [1e5]. Improvements in
g/C0 TiAl þ a2/C0 Ti3Al alloys have increased high temperature strength
while demonstrating excellent properties below 700 /C14 C, owing
primarily to alloying and microstructural control [2,4,6e9]. Alloy-
ing with elements such as Cr, Mo, Nb, and Ru improve the me-
chanical properties by modifying phase transformations and the
microstructure [7e10]. In particular, additions of Nb in concentra-
tions up to 10 at.% have resulted in microstructural re ﬁnement of
g þ a2 alloys, as well as improvement in strength, ductility [6] and
oxidation resistance [1 1]. Furthermore, increasing the Nb content
leads to the development of the s-Nb2Al phase, which can be uti-
lized to improve high temperature properties of titanium alumi-
nides [1,12e14].
It has been shown that two-phase microstructures with coarse
connected s-phase and disconnected g-phase exhibits high tem-
perature strength and creep resistance superior to that of g þ a2
alloys [1,12], but have poor room temperature ductility and fracture
toughness [14]. Recent work has shown that by re ﬁning the
microstructure, Ti eAleNb alloys with g þ s microstructure can
produce desirable properties at room temperature [10,15,16]. The
development of an ultra ﬁne g þ s microstructure with g-phase
matrix and disconnected s-phase precipitates improves strength
and ductility through grain re ﬁnement and precipitation
strengthening [16]. Additionally, these precipitates may help to pin
grain boundaries at elevated temperatures, reducing the extent of
grain boundary sliding, which has been found to dominate defor-
mation in the creep regime [12,17,18]. In order to produce this ul-
traﬁne microstructure, alloying additions of Cr and Mo are
necessary to modify phase stability and transformation kinetics
which can in turn be exploited to control microstructural scale
[10,15,19,20]. The addition of Cr stabilizes the b-phase which is
favorable for microstructure development [10], but the retention of
this phase can be detrimental for high temperature properties
[7,1 1]. Meanwhile, it has been shown that addition of Mo improves
creep resistance of g þ a2 alloys by partitioning between the g and
a2 phases [8]. While preliminary testing of alloys with ultra ﬁne
g þ s microstructure at room temperature has shown promising
strength and fracture toughness [16], their high temperature
properties are unknown.
Deformation of g þ s alloys with 0.6 volume fraction (V f)o f s-
phase is dominated by interfacial sliding of the connected s-phase
[1], so it is expected that reducing the amount of Nb (and therefore
s-phase) to produce a disconnected g þ s microstructure should
change the deformation mechanisms operant at high temperature.
Deformation should be more heavily in ﬂuenced by the connected
g-phase, but the importance of competing deformation

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure and mechanical properties
Solution treatment in the single-phase b solid solution region
[15,16,20,21] and subsequent quenching retains the b-phase,
limiting the formation of g-TiAl and allowing for control over
microstructural scale [10,15,20]. Aging in the two-phase
g/C0 TiAl þ s/C0 Nb2Al region, produces a homogenous ultra ﬁne
microstructure made up of continuous g-phase with disconnected
s-phase [10,16,20,21]. A representative microstructure and XRD
analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2 . Quantitative microscopy was per-
formed using the ImageJ software to characterize s-phase V f and
relative stability after high temperature deformation. The g-phase
ranged from 0.1 to 1 mm in diameter and approximately 0.13 Vf of s-
phase particles, ranging in size from approximately 50 e500 nm.
3.2. Compression testing
As shown in Fig. 3 , all ﬂow curves show a linear elastic region

4. Discussion
4.1. Deformation mechanisms
Analysis of ﬂow curves obtained at varying strain rates and
temperatures can lead to insight into the deformation mechanisms
that may be active in these alloys. Work hardening decreases with
temperature but increases with strain rate, as described by the
equation _ε ¼ bry [22]. As strain rate ð_εÞ increases, dislocation
density ( r) or velocity ( y) must also increase in order to accom-
modate deformation, assuming a constant burger’s vector (b). Since
dislocation velocity is controlled by vacancy diffusion, at constant
temperature the density of mobile dislocations must increase with
increasing strain rate. The effect of strain rate on work hardening is
evident at 700 and 800 /C14 C. At higher temperature, vacancy diffusion
is easier so dislocation velocity can be higher resulting in a lower
work hardening rate, as fewer dislocations are necessary to main-
tain the given strain rate. Stress exponents calculated at 700 and
800 /C14 C fall into the range of dislocation recovery mechanisms,
which include dynamic recovery, dynamic recrystallization, inter-
facial sliding, and diffusion-assisted dislocation motion [6]. This is
supported by the ﬂow curves, which indicate that dynamic recov-
ery and recrystallization play important roles in deformation.
Evidence of dynamic recrystallization can be seen most promi-
nently in the ﬂow curves of this alloy tested at 800 /C14 C and lower
strain rates. These characteristics include a peak stress value fol-
lowed by ﬂow softening and oscillations in the ﬂow stress [23].A t
3 /C2 10/C0 5 s/C0 1, multiple oscillations in the ﬂow stress can be seen as a
function of increasing strain due to the nucleation and propagation
of dynamic recrystallization waves through the material.
Dislocation-free grains are formed upon recrystallization. As the
material is strained further, its dislocation density increases in
these grains up to a critical value, at which point recrystallization is
initiated once again [23]. Since at this strain rate one wave of dy-
namic recrystallization can be fully completed before next wave
begins, oscillations in the ﬂow stress are evident. At higher strain
rates, the critical dislocation density required to nucleate dynamic

Previous work with alloys containing 0.6 V f s-phase has shown
that dynamic recrystallization occurs in both ﬁne and duplex g þ s
microstructures, with TEM analysis showing g-phase deformation
by dislocation glide and twinning [1]. Due to the connected nature
of the s-phase in the 0.6 V f alloy, creep and high temperature
deformation was controlled by s/s grain boundary sliding [1,14],
but by reducing the phase fraction and forming a discontinuous s-
phase, deformation is instead controlled by the g-phase in the al-
loys currently under study, as evidenced by analysis of the
deformed microstructure, which will be discussed in further detail
in the following section. At 800 /C14 C the s-phase acts as a hard phase,
as it has been found in literature [1,24] that it undergoes a transi-
tion to a deformable phase at temperatures above 900 /C14 C.
While deformation of this alloy is controlled by the properties of
the continuous g-phase, the disconnected s-phase particles
contribute to strengthening and limit the size of recrystallized
grains. Similar to microstructure development upon aging of the
alloy, the dispersion of ultra ﬁne s-phase particles will pin the g-
phase grain boundaries and limit coarsening [23]. Another bene ﬁt
of s-phase pinning is that the particles at the g grain boundaries
may reduce the amount of g/g interfacial sliding at elevated tem-
perature. Since the g/s interface is not fully coherent, as disloca-
tions attempt to slide along the g/g interface they are impeded by
s-particles which they can overcome through dislocation climb or
Orowan looping. These processes are more dif ﬁcult in this tem-
perature regime when compared to g/g interfacial sliding, resulting
in increased work hardening and retention of strength.
Another factor that can affect the dynamic recrystallization
behavior of this material is alloying additions. If alloying additions
are in solid solution, such as Cr and Mo in this alloy system, the
solute drag effect may hinder the growth of recrystallized grains in
addition to grain boundary pinning from the s-Nb2Al particles.
Particle stimulated nucleation (PSN) of dynamic recrystallization
is a phenomenon observed in precipitation- and dispersion-
strengthened alloys, though it is unlikely to contribute signi ﬁ-
cantly to nucleation of recrystallization in this two-phase alloy. It
has been shown [23] that second phase particles should be
approximately 1 mm or larger to initiate recrystallization, and the
largest s-phase particles in these microstructures are approxi-
mately 500 nm, with many smaller particles in the regime of 10 e
50 nm. However, the presence of the ultraﬁne s-phase particles may
have the effect of reducing the amount of strain required to initiate
recrystallization by increasing strain in the matrix even though local
dislocation densities are not large enough at the precipitate-matrix
interface for nucleation at the particles. Since particles smaller than
1 mm have been found to retard recrystallization under certain
conditions, systematic study would be required to determine the
regimes of particle size and spacing in which recrystallization would
be retarded or accelerated. At this point the effect of the s-phase
particles upon recrystallization is unclear, but it has been seen that
they have a strong inﬂuence in the recrystallized g-phase grain size
through their pinning effect.

In compressive fracture of a brittle material, microcracks align
with the direction of maximum compressive load, which in single-
phase samples is the loading axis [25]. It can be seen in Figs. 5 and 6
that this is not the case for deformation of this two-phase material,
but instead there is uniform microcracking throughout the micro-
structure. Additionally, ﬂow curves indicate pseudoplastic
behavior. In ceramics or intermetallics, such uniform cracking is
usually only seen when there is suf ﬁcient con ﬁning stress to pre-
vent axial splitting or shear failure [25e27].
Since this alloy was tested only in uniaxial compression with no
external conﬁning stress, this behavior may be attributed to the re-
distribution of stresses due to the reinforcement phase, as well as
the small grain size. In this two-phase microstructure, con ﬁnement
stress is provided locally by the hard reinforcement phase, which
imposes a triaxial stress state due to strain compatibility constraint
of the g/s interface [16,28]. This also changes the stress state such
that the loading direction is not necessarily that of maximum
compressive stress, therefore modifying the orientation of micro-
cracks relative to the sample geometry.
No fragmentation or cracking through the s-phase was present
in this alloy, in contrast with the damage observed in studies ofg þ s
microstructure with higher V f s/C0 phase [1]. As expected, a contin-
uous ultra ﬁne g-TiAl matrix with disconnected s-Nb2Al results in
increased ductility [16]. This is evidenced by the high amount of
compressive strain, more than 40% strain at 800 /C14 C, accommodated
by the material with no evidence of large-scale cracking.
In samples tested at 700 /C14 C, there is not a clear indication of the
g-phase morphology based solely on microcracking, as they do not
appear to predominantly open up at g/g or g/s interfaces. The
majority of cracking is branching within the g-phase and is more
severe in coarser regions of the microstructure, consistent with
localization of strain in the g-phase also seen at 800 /C14 C. By contrast,
alloys with a connected s-phase matrix show very poor ductility,
even at elevated temperatures, suffering brittle failure in
compression testing at 700 /C14 C [1].
5. Conclusions
High temperature compression of Ti e45Ale14Nbe5Cre1Mo at
700 and 800 /C14 C indicates good high temperature strength and large
compressive strain (40%) without sustaining macroscopic fracture.
Strain rate sensitivity and analysis of theﬂow curves indicate thatg/g
interfacial sliding and dynamic recrystallization of theg-phase are the
predominant deformation mechanisms at 800/C14 C, especially at slower
strain rates. Microstructural analysis of deformed samples supportsg/
g interfacial sliding, with microcracks opening primarily atg/g grain
boundaries and no cracking of the s-phase. At 700 /C14 C, localization of
strain within the g-phase is apparent and higher strength and work
hardening rates may be attributed to the lack of recrystallization
behavior in this temperature regime. The impact of dynamic recrys-
tallization behavior at lower strain rates warrants further study in
order to determine this material’s potential under creep loading.
