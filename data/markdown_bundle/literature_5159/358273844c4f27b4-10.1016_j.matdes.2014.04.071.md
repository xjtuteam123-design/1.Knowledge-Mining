abstract
The Mg–7Y–4Gd–1Zn (wt.%) alloy was prepared by hot extrusion technology, and the microstructure,
tensile properties and superplastic behavior have been investigated. The extruded alloy possesses high
tensile strength both at room temperature and 250 /C176C, and especially the yield strength can remain above
300 MPa at 250 /C176C. The outstanding microstructure, i.e. bent 18R long period stacking ordered (LPSO)
strips and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) Mg grains containing ﬁne lamellae with 14H LPSO or stacking
fault structures, is responsible for the excellent mechanical properties, and it is considered that the inte-
grated performance can be further improved by controlling the size of LPSO phase. The alloy shows the
maximum elongation of 700% at 470 /C176C and 1.7 /C2 10
/C0 4 s/C0 1. The predominant superplastic mechanism is
considered to be grain boundary sliding assisted by lattice diffusion. The fracture of superplastic defor-
mation is related to the microstructure evolution, i.e. the disappearance of LPSO phase and the formation
of cubic phase. Both high temperature and stress contribute to the phase transformation.

1. Introduction
It is well known that rare earths (RE) have a favorable effect on
magnesium (Mg) alloys, and Mg-RE-based alloys have attracted a
great deal of attention as light structural materials due to their
low density, high strength and good heat-resistance [1–6]. Previ-
ous literatures have reported that Mg-RE1-RE2(–Zr) (RE = Gd, Y,
Nd, Dy, Er, etc.) series alloys represented by Mg–Gd–Y(–Zr) alloys
show superior strength and heat resistance due to the age harden-
ing response of b
0 (b00 + b0 or b0 + b1) nano-precipitations [2,4,7,8].
While at present, research interests about the mechanical prop-
erty improvement of Mg alloys are mainly focus on the Mg–RE–Zn
alloys (RE = Y, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm) strengthening by the long
period stacking ordered (LPSO) phase [9–20]. In 2001, a
Mg97Y2Zn1 (at.%) alloy prepared by rapidly solidiﬁed powder met-
allurgy (RS P/M) exhibits super high tensile yield strength of
610 MPa and elongation of 5% at room temperature, which is
mainly attributed to the ﬁne grains combined with LPSO structure
lamellae at nanometer scale [9,10]. However, the RS P/M technique
is extremely complicated. Subsequent researches showed that
LPSO structures could also be formed in ingot metallurgy (IM)
and extrusion/rolling processes. Yamasaki et al. [11], and Zhu
et al. [12] studied the detailed crystal structure and stacking
sequence of different kinds of LPSO structure. Yamasaki et al.
[13], Kawamura and Yamasaki [14], Liu et al. [15], Zhang et al.
[16,17] and some other researchers exhibited the high tensile
properties of Mg–RE–Zn alloys with different LPSO phases fabri-
cated by IM process with the hot extrusion. Zhang et al. [6], Chino
et al. [18], Itoi et al. [19], and Shao et al. [20] found the high elastic
modulus, hardness and deformation kinking of LPSO phases.
According to our recent research, we suggest that it also should
be noted the great inﬂuence of the morphology and distribution
of LPSO phases on the mechanical properties.
Superplastic forming is a high-efﬁciency method of fabricating
Mg alloys into complex shapes. Until now, many researches have
focused on the superplastic behavior of Mg alloys [21–26], how-
ever, only few studies were related to that of Mg–Gd–Y-based
alloys [27,28]. Zhang et al. investigated the superplasticity of the
Mg–9Gd–4Y–0.4Zr (wt.%) alloy and reported the maximum elon-
gation of 410% and 380% for the extruded bar and rolled sheet,
respectively [29,30]. Considering the relatively good plasticity
and coherent interface of LPSO intermetallics compared with that
of intermetallics with common crystal structure [6,20], there is
reason to believe that LPSO phase contributes more to the ductility

2. Experimental procedures
The investigated alloy with nominal composition of Mg–7Y–
4Gd–1Zn (wt.%) was prepared from commercial pure Mg
(99.95 wt.%), Zn (99.95 wt.%), Mg–20Y (wt.%) and Mg–20Gd
(wt.%) master alloys in an electric resistant furnace. The ingot
was obtained from a water-cooled metal mould. After homogeniz-
ing at 500 /C176C for 12 h, the ingot was extruded with an extrusion
ratio of /C24 17. The extrusion temperature and extrusion ram speed
were about 460 /C176C and 1 mm s /C0 1. The actual chemical composi-
tions were determined using inductively coupled plasma (ICP)
analysis and the result was listed in Table 1 . The microstructure,
phase structure and composition were characterized by the scan-
ning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with X-ray energy-
dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and the transmission electron
microscope (TEM). The thin foil samples for the TEM observation
were prepared using the argon ion thinning technique. The speci-
mens for tensile test were dumbbell pieces and the length direc-
tion was parallel to the extrusion direction. The tensile tests
were carried out using an Instron-5569 materials testing machine,
and the tensile tests referred to ASTM: E8/E8M-13a and ASTM:
E21-09. Every test was repeated at least four times.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure
Fig. 1 (a) shows the microstructure of extruded Mg–7Y–4Gd–
1Zn alloy parallel to the extrusion direction. After hot extrusion
process, the main secondary phase presenting lamellar morphol-
ogy is elongated along the extrusion direction and the length
ranges from a few microns to tens of microns. The morphology
and shape of intermetallics in this extruded alloy is different from
that of common intermetallics. In general, intermetallic com-
pounds are hard and brittle, thus they would be broken into small
particles during extrusion. Therefore the lamellar phase in the
extruded Mg–7Y–4Gd–1Zn alloy possesses relative good plasticity.
Fig. 1 (b) shows the SEM image of extruded alloy perpendicular to
the extrusion direction. It can be seen that besides the strip-like
phase, there seem to be many ﬁne dynamic recrystallization
(DRX) grains with ﬁne lamellae within them.

In order to further conﬁrm the structure of main strengthening
phases observed under SEM, the phase morphology and structure
were characterized by TEM. Fig. 3 (a)–(c) shows the morphology
of bent strips. It can be seen that the bending angle of the LPSO
strip is close to 90 /C176, and subboundaries are found at the bent
region, which is considered to contribute to the plastic deforma-
tion of the long strip. Thus, the LPSO strips were reﬁned and
obtained work hardening due to their bending deformation during
hot extrusion. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern
for the strip-like phase is shown in Fig. 3 (d). In the SAED pattern
there are additional spots at positions n/6 (where n is an integer)
of the (0 0 0 2)a diffraction ( a represents Mg matrix), which is the
common evidence to prove the existence of 18R LPSO structure
[12]. Fig. 3 (e) reveals the ﬁne DRX grains with size of only 5–
7 lm in the extruded alloy, within most of which ﬁne lamellae
are observed. Through careful observation, these ﬁne lamellae
can be divided into two types. As shown in Fig. 3(f), some lamellae
with width ranging from 50 to 100 nm are distributed throughout
a whole DRX grain. They are identiﬁed to be 14H-type LPSO phase
by SAED pattern ( Fig. 3g). Others with length of about 200–400 nm
and width of only /C24 10 nm have more ﬁne morphology ( Fig. 3h). In
the SAED pattern shown in Fig. 3(i) there are no obviously periodic
extra spots but streaks between the diffraction spots parallel to the
[ 0002 ]a direction. Thus, these ﬁne lamellae are considered to be
the basal plane stacking faults (SFs) [6,11,20,38].

3.4. Superplastic behaviors
The superplasticity tests were carried out with the temperature
ranging from 350 /C176C to 500 /C176C and the strain rate ranging from
1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 to 1.0 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1. Fig. 6 (a) shows the typical unde-
formed and fractured tensile specimens under the strain rate of
1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 with the temperature ranging from 350 /C176Ct o
500 /C176C. The maximum elongation of 700% is achieved at 470 /C176C
as shown in Fig. 6 (b), in which the diffusional necking takes place
within the whole gauge length. When the temperature is up to
500 /C176C, the elongation reduces ( Fig. 6 a) due to the rapid growth
of grains and the instability of microstructure at too high
temperature.
Fig. 7 (a and b) shows engineering stress–strain curves and cor-
responding true stress–strain curves with the stable strain rate of
1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 at various temperatures, respectively. Fig. 7 (c and
d) shows engineering stress–strain curves and corresponding true
stress–strain curves with the stable temperature of 470 /C176C at vari-
ous strain rates, respectively. It is worth noting that this alloy
exhibits great superplastic deformation ability with the maximum
elongation of 700% tested at 470 /C176C and 1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1, whose
stress–strain curve presents stable steady state ﬂow behavior. Fur-
thermore, the elongation is more than 180% at the strain rate of
1.0 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1 under 470 /C176C test temperature indicating that high
strain rate superplasticity (HSRSP) can be achieved in extruded
Mg–7Y–4Gd–1Zn alloy.

3.5. Microstructure of samples after superplastic deformation
Fig. 9 shows the microstructure of extruded Mg–7Y–4Gd–1Zn
alloy after tested at 470 /C176C with the strain rate of 1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1.
As shown in Fig. 9(a–d), after the maximum superplastic deforma-
tion, there are lots of cubic phase distributing in the matrix near
the crack tip of the specimen, and these cubic phase is identiﬁed
as Y-rich phase by EDS analyses, just as the same as cuboid-shaped
phase in the undeformed sample, while at the same time the LPSO
phase almost completely disappear. As seen from Fig. 9 (d), there
exists irregular-shaped phase around the cubic phase, it is maybe
the transition-state before the formation of the Y-rich cubic phase.
However, the microstructure for the regions about 1.5 cm ( Fig. 9e)
and 2.5 cm ( Fig. 9 f) far away from the cracked tip of the specimen
is that both Y-rich cubic phase and LPSO phase coexist. It is easy to
deduce that the fracture of superplastic deformation is related to
the evolution of microstructure, i.e. the disappearance of LPSO
phase and the formation of cubic phase.
There is no doubt that the high temperature contributes to the
main secondary phase transforming from LPSO phase to cubic
phase. However the observation result from Fig. 9 (a–f) indicates
that stress is another factor accelerating the phase transformation,
and accordingly the following process is supposed. During the plas-
tic deformation, it is unavoidable that the cross-sectional area at a
certain region would become smaller than that of other regions;
subsequently this region bears stronger stress, which accelerates

4. Conclusions
In this study, the microstructure, tensile properties and super-
plastic behavior of Mg–7Y–4Gd–1Zn alloy prepared by hot extru-
sion were investigated. The main results are summarized as
follows.
(1) The extruded alloy is mainly composed of 18R LPSO strips,
DRX Mg grains and very few amounts of cubic phase. Most
of the DRX grains contain lots of ﬁne lamellae with 14H LPSO
or stacking fault structures. The outstanding microstructure
is responsible for the excellent mechanical properties of this
alloy.
(2) The extruded alloy exhibits high tensile strength at room
temperature and 250 /C176C, and especially the tensile yield
strength can keep above 300 MPa at 250 /C176C. The integrated
performance of LPSO-containing Mg alloys can be further
improved by controlling the size of LPSO phase.
(3) The maximum elongation of 700% is achieved after super-
plastic deformation at 470 /C176C with the strain rate of
1.7 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1. The m-value is measured to be /C24 0.55. The
activation energy is determined to be 200.5 kJ/mol. The pre-
dominant superplastic mechanism is considered to be grain
boundary sliding assisted by lattice diffusion.
(4) The fracture of superplastic deformation is related to the
microstructure evolution, i.e. the disappearance of LPSO
phase and the formation of cubic phase. Both high tempera-
ture and stress contribute to the phase transformation.
