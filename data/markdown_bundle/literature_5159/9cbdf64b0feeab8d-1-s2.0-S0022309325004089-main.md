ABSTRACT
Bulk metallic glasses (BMGs) exhibit unique properties, including ultra-high strength, exceptional hardness, and 
superplasticity in the supercooled liquid region, due to their defect-free atomic structure. In this study, the su -
perplasticity of BMGs was exploited to achieve high-strength bonding between high-temperature alloys. A 0.5 
mm-thick Ni
62
Nb
38 
amorphous interlayer was fabricated using vacuum die-casting technology and employed for 
joining TiAl and GH4099 alloys. The results demonstrated that the interlayer produced via vacuum pressing 
exhibited superior stability and performance consistency compared to the amorphous ribbon solder prepared by 
conventional single-roll melt spinning. The shrinkage behavior of interfacial pores in the supercooled liquid 
region, along with the subsequent atomic diffusion and microstructural evolution at elevated temperatures, was 
analyzed via X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM), Transmission Electron Microscope 
(TEM), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) techniques. The findings revealed the formation of a robust 
metallurgical bond, where the joint consists of the γ -TiAl/Ti ₃ Al/AlNi ₂ Ti/NiNb and Ni ₃ Nb/ γ -Ni phases. Shear tests 
performed at room temperature and 750 
◦
C were employed to assess the bonding strength, revealing that the 
joint achieved shear strengths of up to 315 MPa at room temperature and 250 MPa at 750 
◦
C.
1. Introduction
Bulk metallic glasses (BMGs) exhibit exceptional physical and me -
chanical properties, such as high strength, hardness, wear resistance, 
and corrosion resistance, rendering them highly promising for a broad 
range of applications [ 1 – 3 ]. To date, researchers have developed over 
500 bulk amorphous alloy compositions, encompassing various systems 
including Ti-based, Fe-based, Zr-based, Cu-based, and Ni-based alloys 
[ 4 ]. However, despite their remarkable properties, amorphous alloys 
have yet to achieve widespread industrial adoption. A key limiting 
factor is their intrinsic brittleness at room temperature, which presents 
significant challenges for their utilization in engineering applications.
To fully unlock the performance potential of bulk amorphous alloys, 
Santoni et al. leveraged the superplastic properties of these materials in 
their supercooled liquid state, achieving successful application in micro- 
molding technology for the precision manufacturing of micro-devices 
[ 5 – 7 ]. Since plastic deformation in materials is primarily concentrated 
on the microscale, plastic micro-forming technology demands materials 
with excellent plasticity and low deformation resistance. Numerous 
experiments have demonstrated that amorphous alloys exhibit 
exceptionally high plasticity and excellent flowability in the supercooled 
liquid region, making them ideal candidates for plastic micro-forming 
and the precision manufacturing of micro-components. Compared 
with micro-casting and powder-forming techniques, plastic 
micro-forming operates at lower temperatures and requires shorter 
forming cycles, thereby facilitating the fabrication of amorphous 
micro-components with uniform microstructures and stable perfor -
mance [ 8 , 9 ]. Furthermore, amorphous alloy components produced by 
this process exhibit high dimensional precision, superior surface quality, 
and minimal requirement for subsequent processing. Currently, this 
technique stands as the most promising approach for fabricating 
micro-scale amorphous alloy components, while laying a solid founda -
tion for their application in micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) 
and optical devices.
Schroers et al. also developed a novel "maximum diameter method" 
to evaluate the microformability of amorphous alloys [ 9 ]. Wang et al. 
employed a hot embossing technique to imprint a specially shaped lat -
tice structure with characteristic dimensions in the tens of micrometers 
onto the surface of a Pt-based amorphous alloy, and derived the rela -
tionship between filling height and various processing parameters based

Another notable application of amorphous alloys is their utilization 
as solders. As an emerging material with substantial development po -

composition, excellent formability, and superior toughness. Yuan et al. 
employed a Ni-based amorphous solder to join duplex stainless steel via 
transient liquid phase (TLP) diffusion bonding and achieved promising 
results [ 12 ]. Xu et al. utilized a boron-containing Cu-P-based amorphous 
solder to bond copper and found that trace amounts of boron acted as a 
surface-active element, enhancing both wettability and joint perfor -
mance [ 13 ]. Ganjeh et al. reported that when elements such as Cu, Zr, 
and Ni are combined with Ti, they can form thin-strip brazing materials 
characterized by low melting points and good fluidity. These materials 
enable effective joining of substances like Si ₃ N ₄ ceramics, pure Ti, and 
Ti-6Al-4 V, yielding excellent results [ 14 ]. Zhao et al. applied a 
quenched Al-Si-Cu-Zn filler metal for brazing pure aluminum and 6063 
aluminum alloy, and observed that the quenching process significantly 
lowers the alloy ’ s melting temperature and narrows its melting range, 
while enhancing wettability [ 15 ]. Lee et al. employed a Zr-Ti-Cu-Ni-Be 
amorphous alloy to bond Ti and Ti-Cu dissimilar materials, while 
investigating the resulting microstructural evolution and joint strength 
[ 16 ].
Despite their advantages, amorphous solders frequently incorporate 
brittle elements such as Si, B, and P. These elements impede conven -
tional processing techniques like stamping and cutting — especially in 
the case of thin strips with thicknesses of ≤ 0.03 mm [ 17 , 18 ]. This 
constraint substantially limits their wider application in the brazing of 
high-temperature alloys.
To tackle this challenge, the present study integrates the 
superplasticity of amorphous alloys — suitable for plastic micro-for -
ming — with the favorable diffusion properties of their metastable 
structure for brazing. A binary Ni ₆₂ Nb ₃₈ metallic glass thin sheet with a

successfully applied to join TiAl and GH4099 alloys. The crystallization 
behavior and filling process were analyzed using the Kissinger equation 
and the Hagen-Poiseuille law, respectively. The microstructural features 
and atomic diffusion characteristics within the joint were investigated 
via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy- 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and trans -

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Superplastic filling of heterogeneous interfaces
Figs. 4(a) and 4(b) present the SEM images of the interfaces between 
the base alloys and the Ni-Nb bulk metallic glass (BMG) interlayer. As 
can be observed from Fig. 4(a) , after undergoing a period of heat pres -
ervation in the supercooled liquid phase, the thickness of the amorphous 
alloy intermediate layer is significantly thinner than its initial thickness. 
This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the fact that the amorphous 
alloy exists in a viscous flow state under such conditions; under the in -
fluence of external forces, a portion of the amorphous alloy then over -
flows from the contact interface between the base material and the 
intermediate layer. Notably, a 30-minute isothermal treatment in the 
supercooled liquid region under applied pressure, the BMG completely 
infiltrated and eliminated interfacial defects, including cracks and voids, 
at both the TiAl- and Ni-based alloy interfaces. To characterize the 
structural evolution of the interlayer during bonding, X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) was performed, and the corresponding pattern is embedded in 
Fig. 4(b) . The XRD pattern shows no sharp peaks associated with 
crystalline phases, indicating that the filler metal retained its amorphous 
structure after superplastic filling.

3.3. Mechanical properties of the joint
Microhardness and elastic modulus of different phases within the 
joint were shown in Fig. 12(a) . The microhardness of the weld joint is 
measured via nanoindentation. The sample is first polished to a mirror 
finish and then chemically etched to reveal the microstructure clearly. 
After preparation, the sample is mounted on the nanoindenter ’ s stage. 
Using the instrument ’ s integrated optical microscope or scanning probe 
microscope (SPM), the macroscopic region of the weld joint is located 
under low magnification. Higher magnification is subsequently 
employed to accurately identify distinct structural zones based on 
etching-induced contrast variations. A single-point testing method is 
then applied to measure each region individually. The maximum 
indentation load is set to 150 mN, with a hold time of 20 s to minimize 
creep effects and ensure stabilization of the unloading curve. Nano -
indentation mapping demonstrates a 35 – 45 % increase in interfacial 
hardness (from 4.2 ± 0.3 GPa to 6.1 ± 0.4 GPa) attributable to γ’ pre -
cipitation and Al-Ni-Ti intermetallic formation at the TiAl/interlayer 
interface. The phases with high elastic modulus and microhardness 
exhibited low plastic deformation capability. During loading, the joint is 
prone to cracking in places with high hardness. The room-temperature 
and elevated-temperature shear strength of the joint bonded with 
different parameters were shown in Fig. 12(b) . As shown in the picture, 
the shear strength of the joint at both room temperature and 750 
◦
C 
increases with the elevation of bonding temperature and the extension 
of holding time. Specifically, the joint achieves a maximum shear 
strength of 315 MPa at room temperature and 250 MPa at 750 
◦
C. These 
values are slightly lower than the corresponding strengths of the TiAl 
alloy base material, which are approximately 480 MPa at room tem -

interlayer material [ 37 , 38 ]. The enhanced joint performance across 
both ambient and elevated temperature regimes arises from the syner -
gistic effects of (i) microstructural refinement and (ii) the high 
melting-point characteristics of the filler metal. The absence of 
liquid-solid phase transformation during bonding results in significantly 
finer phase formation within the joint compared to conventional liq -
uidus joining methods. This microstructural refinement, consistent with 
Hall-Petch strengthening mechanisms, enhances the joint ’ s mechanical 
properties through grain boundary strengthening [ 39 ]. Furthermore, 
analysis of the temperature relationship presented in Fig. 14(b) reveals a 
direct correlation between filler metal melting point and maximum 
service temperature. Notably, while brazed joints are strictly limited to 
service temperatures below the brazing temperature, superplastic 
interlayer diffusion bonded joints demonstrate potential for 
high-temperature applications exceeding their bonding temperature.
5. Conclusion
This study presents an innovative joining strategy that synergistically 
combines vacuum die-casting technology with superplastic bonding to 
improve the high-temperature reliability of TiAl/Ni dissimilar joints. 
The approach capitalizes on the unique superplastic deformation char -
acteristics of bulk metallic glass (BMG) in its supercooled liquid region. 
Utilizing a high-melting-point Ni-Nb amorphous filler, we achieved 
defect-free metallurgical bonding via solid-state diffusion while main -
taining processing temperatures below the TiAl alloy ’ s phase trans -
formation point. Compared to conventional diffusion bonding 
techniques, this method demonstrates three significant advantages: (1)
reduced sensitivity to surface preparation requirements, substantially 
simplifying processing; (2) refined joint microstructure with enhanced 
phase stability; and (3) superior mechanical reliability of the crystallized 
interlayer. The successful integration of vacuum die-casting for BMG 
interlayer fabrication with superplastic diffusion welding establishes a 
groundbreaking solution for joining traditionally incompatible high- 
performance metal systems.
