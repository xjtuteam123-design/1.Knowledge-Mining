1. Introduction
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as a 
research focus in materials science due to their exceptional performance 
characteristics. Systematic investigations have been conducted across 
multiple dimensions, including compositional optimization, advanced 
processing techniques, microstructure-property correlations, multiscale 
computational modeling, and fundamental theoretical innovations
[1 – 4] . Among these, refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) composed 
of high-melting-point elements (Nb, Mo, Ta, W, V) have attracted sig -
nificant attention since their initial discovery. Most RHEAs predomi -
nantly consist of a single body-centered cubic (BCC) phase or a BCC solid 
solution matrix with secondary phases such as Laves, hexagonal 
close-packed (HCP), or face-centered cubic (FCC) structures [5] . Exper -
imental results demonstrate that representative RHEA systems (e.g., 
HfNbTaZr and CrTaVW) achieve remarkable room-temperature yield 
strengths up to 2300 MPa [6] . NbMoTaTi RHEA, fabricated via additive 
manufacturing, achieves a compressive strength of 651 MPa at 1000
◦
C
[7] . It also has excellent development potential, offering a new option 
for studying and creating new high-temperature resistant materials for 
high-performance aerospace hot-end components [8,9] . Nevertheless, 
the practical implementation of conventional RHEAs faces challenges 
due to their inherent high density ( > 10 g/cm³) and the elevated costs 
associated with precious metal constituents (e.g., Ta, Hf). To address 
these limitations, researchers have proposed lightweight RHEA design 
strategies through the incorporation of low-density elements (Al, Ti) and 
compositional refinement, aiming to achieve material lightweight while 
maintaining superior high-temperature performance [10,11] .
A growing number of researchers are researching the selection of 
elements appropriate for producing lightweight HEAs in order to create 
high-performance and lightweight HEAs [12] . The elements Al, Ti, and V 
are good choices for creating lightweight HEAs because they are 
reasonably light and have comparable atomic sizes, electronegativity, 
and strong chemical stability. Therefore, people have made multiple

Qiu et al. introduced an AlTiVCr alloy with a density of 5.06 g/cm³ . The

strategy is to augment the proportion of light refractory elements (e.g., 
Zr, Cr.) [20 – 22] . This procedure not only diminishes the alloy ’ s density 
but also conserves costs and minimizes expenditures. Our team previ -
ously investigated the influence of the V element on AlMoNbTiVZr 
RHEAs. The augmentation of the V element positively influences the 
mechanical properties of this alloy series. The alloy exhibits low density 
and high strength at room temperature with an additive concentration of 
0.5. However, its plasticity at room temperature is moderate. The alloy ’ s 
microstructure contains a significant amount of Zr-based intermetallic 
compounds, adversely affecting its ductility [23] . Extensive research has 
demonstrated that Zr, as a high-melting-point transition metal, plays 
multiple roles in refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs). First, Zr can 
regulate phase stability and promote grain refinement, thereby influ -
encing the phase composition and microstructure of the alloy. For 
example, the addition of Zr introduced a hard disordered BCC phase 
embedded in a soft intermetallic B2 matrix in the brittle (TaMoTi) ₉₂ Al ₈ 
RHEA, leading to enhanced room-temperature plasticity. [24] Addi -
tionally, Zr contributes to mechanical property enhancement through 
solid-solution strengthening and precipitation hardening. Yurchenko 
et al. [25] found that the strength of AlNbTiVZrx alloys exhibits a sig -
nificant dependence on Zr content. However, excessive Zr promotes the 
formation of brittle intermetallic compounds, such as Laves phases or 
other detrimental phases, which compromise ductility and fracture 
toughness [20] . Therefore, our study investigates the effect of different 
Zr concentrations on the organization and properties of alloys in order to 
achieve an optimal balance between strengthening and toughness.

4.3. Fracture characteristics
Observations of the longitudinal sections of the alloy after 
compression at 800
◦
C and 1000
◦
C reveal that the cracks can be classified 
into two distinct types based on their location. The first type, referred to 
as Class I cracks, originates from edge defects and propagates inward. 
The second type, designated as Type II cracks, initiates within the 
interior of the alloy and gradually extends outward. Fig. 15 presents two 
representative examples of these crack types. In Fig. 15 (a), the majority 
of cracks originate at the specimen ’ s surface and exhibit a tendency to 
extend along the edges. These cracks are relatively small and densely 
distributed. In contrast, the crack morphology in Fig. 15 (b) is markedly 
different, characterized by a single, larger crack with a jagged propa -
gation pattern extending into the material. Adjacent to this primary 
crack, another crack intersects it, leading to further material separation. 
These cracks are stress-induced and result from localized plastic defor -
mation insufficiency during high-temperature compression. They pre -
dominantly form in regions of strain localization, particularly at the 
specimen ’ s edges, where stress distribution is non-uniform, making 
crack initiation more likely. Fig. 15 (c, d) illustrates the propagation of 
internal cracks within the alloy. In Fig. 15 (c), the primary crack extends 
along the dendrite boundary and is accompanied by fine secondary 
cracks. Several cracks are observed in the vicinity, originating from pre- 
existing voids and gradually expanding under the influence of applied 
stress. Beneath the main crack, additional microcracks can be observed, 
which have not yet fully developed but appear to be induced by the 
stress concentration from the propagating cracks on both sides. The 
cracked region in Fig. 15 (d) exhibits a wide opening, with certain areas 
displaying cavity-like fracture features, and the crack predominantly 
propagates along the two-phase boundary. This behavior suggests that 
localized brittle fracture or void coalescence may contribute to crack 
formation and propagation. The primary factors contributing to internal 
cracking during high-temperature compression can be summarized as 
follows [41,50,51] : (i) Internal stress concentration: Due to the inherent 
inhomogeneity of the alloy ’ s microstructure, localized stress concen -
trations may arise during deformation, leading to the initiation of 
microcracks, which subsequently propagate under continued deforma -
tion. (ii) Effect of phase distribution: The presence of brittle or hard 
phases within the alloy can act as preferential sites for crack initiation, 
as cracks may nucleate and propagate along the interfaces of these 
phases. In other words, the formation of internal cracks is closely asso -
ciated with localized phase distribution, microporosity, or inclusions, all 
of which can serve as nucleation sites for crack initiation and further 
propagation during high-temperature deformation [52] . In future 
studies, appropriate homogenization treatment should be applied to 
minimize elemental segregation and enhance the microstructural uni -
formity of the alloy, thereby alleviating stress concentration and mini -
mizing the formation of brittle cracks.
