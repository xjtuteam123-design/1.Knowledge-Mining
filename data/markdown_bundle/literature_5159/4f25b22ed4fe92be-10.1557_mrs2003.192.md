Introduction
High-temperature materials have made
substantial contributions to performance
improvements of both aircraft engines and
land-based gas turbines.
1 Advances in 
Ni-based superalloy airfoils have enabled
metal surface temperatures of advanced
turbine-engine airfoils to approach
/H110111150/H11034C. However, increases in the tem-
perature capability of Ni-based superalloys
beyond that of third-generation single-
crystal alloys (3GSXs) will be very difficult
to achieve because most advanced super-
alloys soften above 1150 /H11034C and melt at
/H110111350/H11034C.
High-temperature airfoil materials that
can operate above the present temperature
limit are desired for advanced gas turbines.
Designers can generally exploit an increase
in airfoil material temperature capability
in several ways, including life extension,
higher combustion temperature, or a com-
bination of the two. Turbines for either air
or surface applications have a range of
performance requirements, such as fuel
efficiency in producing power or thrust,
weight, and reliability. Reducing the
amount of cooling air increases efficiency
because less of the work done in com-
pressing the air for combustion is lost to
cooling. If the airfoil mass can be reduced
significantly, the centrifugal stress on the
rotor is reduced, and a smaller, lighter
rotor can be employed.
Consideration of the Nb-silicide-based
composites for high-temperature struc-
tural applications began about 10 years
ago.
1–5 The Nb-silicide-based composites
showed early promise, based on a good
balance of high-temperature strength and
low-temperature damage tolerance. The
lower density of Nb-silicide-based systems
(/H110117 g/cm
3, as compared with /H110119.2 g/cm3
for Ni-based superalloys)5–9 was also con-
sidered a significant advantage over nickel-
based superalloys. Oxidation and creep
performance were areas where significant
improvement was required. The substan-
tial progress made previously in the im-
provement of oxidation performance of
monolithic Nb alloys
1,2 suggested that
analogous alloying schemes could be em-
ployed for Nb silicide composites with
similar results. Hence, a series of recent
programs has explored the effect of alloy-
ing additions on the balance of mechani-
cal and oxidation properties; elements of
these programs are described here.
Nb-silicide-based composites show sub-
stantial promise because of their low den-
sity, high fracture toughness, and good
fatigue behavior. In addition, they can be
cast and machined. The preliminary creep
and oxidation properties indicate that Nb-
silicide-based composites could, with signifi-
cant further development, be integrated
into blade designs with substantial payoffs
in weight and cooling-air savings, relative
to third-generation Ni-based superalloy
blade designs. There have been several re-
cent extensive reviews of progress in the
development of Nb-silicide-based compos-
ites
1,2,9 as well as technical papers on phase
equilibria, mechanical properties, and oxi-
dation behavior of these alloys.
10–14 This
article provides a brief review of the most
recent developments in Nb silicide com-
posites. In the following section, some of
the basic principles of Nb-silicide-based
composites are described; subsequent

ical properties, oxidation performance,

Abstract
This article reviews the most recent progress in the development of Nb-silicide-based
in situ composites for potential applications in turbine engines with service temperatures
of up to 1350/H11034C.These composites contain high-strength Nb silicides that are toughened
by a ductile Nb solid solution. Preliminary composites were derived from binary Nb-Si
alloys, while more recent systems are complex and are alloyed with Ti, Hf, W, B, Ge, Cr,
and Al. Alloying schemes have been developed to achieve an excellent balance of room-
temperature toughness, fatigue-crack-growth behavior, high-temperature creep perform-
ance, and oxidation resistance over a broad range of temperatures. Nb-silicide-based
composites are described with emphasis on processing, microstructure, and perform-
ance. Nb silicide composites have been produced using a range of processing routes,
including induction skull melting, investment casting, hot extrusion, and powder
metallurgy methods. Nb silicide composite properties are also compared with those of
Ni-based superalloys.
Keywords:casting, composites, creep, extrusion, fatigue, jet engines, Nb silicide,
niobium, oxidation, superalloys, turbines, ultrahigh-temperature materials.

Processing Techniques for Nb-
Silicide-Based Composites
Nb-silicide-based composites have been
fabricated using a range of processes, in-
cluding non-consumable tungsten electrode
arc melting, plasma arc melting, induction
skull melting (ISM), ingot casting plus
thermomechanical processing,
2,8,10,12 direc-

From a commercial point of view, invest-
ment casting of near-net-shape components
of Nb silicide composites offers substan-
tial potential because of its proximity to
existing airfoil manufacturing practices.
However, investment casting techniques
are not well developed for Nb silicide
composite airfoils. The reactivity of the Nb
alloy melts limits the use of ceramic-based
melting systems. Recently, the feasibility

Summary
Nb silicide in situcomposites are promis-
ing candidates for future high-temperature
turbine-engine applications. Nickel-based
superalloy airfoils presently have maxi-
mum operating surface temperatures of
/H110111150/H11034C; future turbine designs require
materials that can operate at temperatures
above 1300 /H11034C. Recent Nb-silicide-based
composites have shown excellent progress
in the development of processing tech-
niques, rupture resistance, fracture and
fatigue-crack-growth performance, oxida-
tion resistance, and coatings, including both
bond coats and TBCs. Nb silicide compos-
ites offer higher operating temperature ca-
pability together with lower density.
Key technical challenges are in the areas
of processing, long-term oxidation resis-
tance, and creep-rupture resistance. Most
of the research on Nb silicide composites
has focused on developing a fundamental
understanding of the mechanical behavior
and oxidation resistance; there have been
essentially no engineering alloy and proc-
ess development efforts of the magnitude
performed for nickel-based superalloys.
This is a significant program deficiency.
In the area of processing, investment
casting, directional solidification, induction
skull melting, and hot extrusion have all
been used successfully to produce Nb sili-
cide composites. Recent investment-casting
mold developments have shown excellent
potential for thin-walled airfoil casting.
The specific creep-rupture capability of
the Nb silicide composites exceeds that of
2GSX Ni-based alloys, because Nb silicide
composites have a density /H1101120% lower
than the most recent superalloys. How-
ever, a further increase of /H110112 Larson–Miller
parameters is required to satisfy the future
turbine requirements. The oxidation resis-
tance of present Nb silicide composites is
sufficient to qualify for near-term engine
testing, but further improvements are re-
quired to satisfy the long-term design re-
quirements. Achievement of the long-term
oxidation goal (/H1102125 
/H9262m in 100 h of oxida-
tion exposure at 1315/H11034C) while also main-
taining the required balance of mechanical
properties is still required, unless airfoil
reliability can be ensured simultaneously
with a greater dependence on the coating.
In addition, the unique bond coats for 
Nb-silicide-based in situ composites have
demonstrated excellent oxidation resis-
tance at temperatures up to 1370/H11034C. TBCs
have also been explored, and thermal
cyclic oxidation testing has shown excel-
lent retention/survivability of the TBC at
temperatures of up to 1650 /H11034C in a JETS
test. More detailed coating and airfoil de-
sign studies are required to explore the fu-
ture design practices for coated Nb silicide
composite airfoils.
Further research is required to improve
the understanding of the mechanisms that
control properties, such as fracture tough-
ness, fatigue-crack initiation and growth,
tensile strength, creep performance, and
stress-rupture performance, before these
composite systems can be considered for
service. Manufacturing complex-shaped
Nb silicide components poses significant
equipment and process challenges and sub-
stantial further process developments are
required to manufacture complex articles
with satisfactory properties. Investment
casting of Nb-silicide-based composites
offers a relatively low-cost processing
approach for fabricating near-net-shape
components such as blades and vanes.
However, the casting methods for Nb
silicide composites are still in their infancy,
and extensive further efforts are required.
