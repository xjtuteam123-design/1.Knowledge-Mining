A comparative analysis was conducted on the equiatomic MoNbVTi model
high-entropy alloy and its carbon-incorporated counterpart, (MoNbVTi) 95C5.
The research indicates that the presence of secondary carbides at grain
boundaries exerts varying effects on the mechanical properties of the (MoN-
bVTi)
95C5 alloy under different temperature conditions. At room temperature,
these carbides manifest as a brittle phase, which detrimentally affects both
the strength and plasticity of the alloy. Conversely, under high-temperature
conditions, the carbon-containing (MoNbVTi) 95C5 alloy demonstrates en-
hanced strength and improved compressive plasticity. It is proposed that, in
thermal environments, unique physicochemical interactions occur between
the carbides and the matrix, leading to the formation of ceramic-strengthen-
ing phases that contribute to an increase in alloy strength. Density functional
theory (DFT) simulations estimated an upper limit of /C240.85 at.% for carbon
concentration in the bulk phase, revealing carbide formation at grain
boundaries. Future research will focus on in situ characterization of carbide
compositions/properties at elevated temperatures, exploring the intrinsic
correlation between carbon content and alloy mechanical behavior, and elu-
cidating the underlying mechanisms.
INTRODUCTION
The term high-entropy alloy (HEA) refers to
materials that exhibit distinctive structural charac-
teristics owing to their compositionally complex
nature, leading to the simultaneous emergence of
topological order and chemical disorder.
1 Currently,
state-of-the-art nickel-based superalloys struggle to
maintain sufﬁcient strength above 1200 /C176C, primar-
ily due to their limited compositional ﬂexibility and
the inherently low melting points of constituent
elements. Consequently, the majority of these alloys
experience signiﬁcant softening at temperatures
ranging from 0.5 to 0.6 Tm.
2 The growing demand
for high-temperature structural applications has
spurred intensive research into refractory metals
and their complex alloys, particularly refractory
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs).
3 RHEAs are a novel
class of multi-component solid solutions character-
ized by four key features: (1) composition compris-
ing 4–9 refractory metal elements selected from the
Cr-Mo-Nb-V-Ta-W-Hf-Ti-Zr series; (2) near-equimo-
lar elemental concentrations (5–35 at.%); (3) body-
centered cubic (BCC) crystal structure formation;
and (4) random atomic arrangement of constituent
elements at lattice sites.
4 RHEAs typically exhibit a
BCC crystalline structure as their dominant phase,
with a secondary B2 phase and topologically close-
packed Laves phases occasionally observed.
5,6 Since
the pioneering report of refractory high-entropy
alloys by Senkov in 2010, substantial break-
throughs have emerged in alloy design, 7,8
microstructure engineering, 9 and property opti-
mization,10 while innovative processing

The deﬁning characteristic of RHEAs, compared
to other classes of HEAs, lies in their exceptional
mechanical properties at temperatures exceeding
1000 /C176C, coupled with outstanding structural sta-
bility, which renders them highly competitive can-

notable high-temperature strength retention, high-

mally stable materials for extreme environment
applications. For most RHEAs, achieving and
retaining sufﬁcient strength at elevated tempera-

example, in our prior research on the MoNbVTi

conditions revealed that the equiatomic MoNbVTi

literature. However, a crucial temperature-depen-
dent transition occurs: when the temperature sur-
passes 800 /C176C, its yield strength experiences a
signiﬁcant decline, dropping to approximately
600 MPa. The persistent issue of thermal softening
effects in metals and alloys, especially those char-
acterized by the BCC phase, has long been acknowl-
edged, particularly in the realm of high-
temperature structural materials. According to the
mechanism of solid-solution strengthening, intersti-
tial atoms not only contribute to the enhancement of
alloy strength but also play a crucial role in
improving various other physical properties.
16
The addition of interstitial elements such as
carbon and nitrogen has been widely recognized as
a particularly effective and economical approach for
strengthening steels and other conventional alloy
systems.
17–19 Researchers have employed a similar
doping strategy to enhance the overall properties of
multi-principal component alloys. Numerous exam-
ples can be found in face-centered cubic (FCC)-
structured HEAs, which primarily incorporating
carbon or boron as interstitial strengthening ele-
ments.
20,21 Researchers have endeavored to adopt a
comparable approach by introducing interstitial
atoms to systematically modify the properties of
refractory multi-component alloys. Tao et al. suc-
cessfully synthesized a series of NbHfTiVC
x RHEAs
(x = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3), which simultaneously exhibited
outstanding strength and excellent ductility, while
compressive tests demonstrated that the yield
strength increased from 958 MPa to 1115 MPa;
furthermore, the fracture mechanism transitioned
from ductile fracture to a mixed mode of ductile and
brittle fracture as carbon content increased. Xu
et al. employed a carbon microalloying strategy to
enhance the room-temperature brittleness
resistance of the NbMoTaW alloy fabricated via
laser powder bed fusion. The incorporation of 0.5
at.% C resulted in excellent formability and signif-
icant microalloying effects. Its yield strength, ulti-
mate compressive strength, and ductility were
improved to 1725 MPa, 1782 MPa, and 7.0%,
respectively.
Previous studies on doping strategies for multi-
component refractory alloys, as documented in the
literature, have predominantly focused on low-con-
centration doping regimes. In fact, the majority of
research efforts have adopted analogous low-con-
centration approaches, while systematic investiga-
tions into high-concentration doping effects remain
notably scarce. Therefore, we attempted to intro-
duce carbon atoms at elevated concentrations (5
at.%) into a Mo-Nb-V-Ti RHEA matrix, aiming to
characterize the resultant inﬂuences on its
microstructure, mechanical properties, and other
relevant attributes.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
High-purity titanium (99.95 wt.%), molybdenum
(99.95 wt.%), vanadium (99.95 wt.%), and niobium
(99.95 wt.%) were chosen as raw materials, and
high-purity carbon particles (99.99 wt.%) were
utilized as dopant. The equiatomic MoNiVTi HEA
and its counterpart (MoNbVTi)
95C5 alloy were fab-
ricated via vacuum arc-melting within a water-
cooled copper mold. Both ingots were cast into
rectangular shapes and ﬂipped over and re-melted
at least 7 times to ensure chemical homogeneity
within a high-purity argon atmosphere.
The microstructure characterization of the alloys
was investigated using a ﬁeld-emission scanning
electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7800F; JEOL)
equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) on non-etched surfaces. The global crystal
structure of the alloys was determined using a
powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert PRO;
PANalytical) with Cu K a monochromatic radiation
in the range of 2 h = 10–90/C176with a scanning rate of
5/C176/min.
Uniaxial compression tests are conducted to eval-
uate plastic deformation by subjecting cylindrical
specimens to axial loads until failure, with stress–
strain data analyzed to determine yield strength
and work-hardening characteristics. A comparative
study was conducted to investigate the mechanistic
aspects of plastic deformation in the as-cast MoN-
iVTi and (MoNbVTi)
95C5 alloys. Room-temperature
compression testing was performed using a comput-
erized electronic universal testing machine
(UTM5305SYXL) with an engineering strain rate
of 1 9 10
/C03 s/C01. High-temperature compression
testing was performed using a Gleeble 1500 system
with a true strain rate of 1 9 10/C03 s/C01. Quasi-static
uniaxial tensile tests were carried out at room
temperature and at 1173 K on an AGXplus50KN

C at the bulk and grain boundary regions were
approximated as the average of these absorption

Phase Identiﬁcation and Surface Morphology
Analysis

the XRD data, it was observed that, apart from the

the XRD spectrum of the (MoNbVTi)
95C5 specimen.
These minor peaks were apparently the result pf
precipitated carbides which are further identiﬁed by
the SEM image in Fig. 2c. Figure 2a, b, c, and d
displays the micrographs and element mapping
carried using SEM-EDX for the as-cast MoNbVTi
and etched (MoNbVTi)
95C5 specimens. Figure 2a
depicts a surface average structure of the model
MoNbVTi alloy, which displays a typical single-
phase structure with a coarse grain size. The
spatially distributed concentration of the
constituent alloying elements, as demonstrated by
the EDX elemental mapping in Fig. 2b, indicates an
even chemical distribution. Figure 2c (left) depicts
the SEM back-scattered electron (BSE) image of a
carbon-doped (MoNbVTi)
95C5 alloy that has under-
gone etching treatment using a mixture of nitrohy-
drochloric acid. The BSE morphology demonstrates
a widespread distribution of dark spots across the
etched surface of the (MoNbVTi)
95C5 specimen. It
can be speculated that these dark spots are indica-
tive of carbon precipitates which arise from a
supersaturated solid solution. Surface analyzing
without etching treatment was also conducted using
EDX for comparison, as demonstrated in Fig. 2c
(right), which incorporated an overlapping image of
both metallic elements and the carbide precipitate,
showing a notable phenomenon of a great number of
carbide precipitates near the grain boundaries. This
clearly shows the EDX morphology of the chemically
etched and non-etched carbon-containing specimens
exhibiting similar characteristics, indicating that
the doping level of carbon in the (MoNbVTi)
95C5 was
excessively high, leading to the segregation of a
carbide phase. Additionally, the EDX element dis-
tribution mapping depicted in Fig. 2d demonstrates
that the introduction of carbon results in a promi-
nent heterogeneous distribution of the constituent
elements.

Theoretical analysis suggests that the observed

interrelated mechanisms. First, carbide precipita-

strength and toughness. It can be concluded during
room-temperature processing and forming that
excessive dissolution of carbon should be avoided
in the alloy. In other words, precise control over the
carbon content is critical in composition design.
The elevated-temperature compressive mechani-
cal properties of the MoNbVTi RHEA and its
carbon-modiﬁed counterpart, (MoNbVTi) 95C5, were
systematically evaluated at 900 /C176C to elucidate the
microstructural inﬂuences of carbon addition on
high-temperature deformation resistance. In con-
trast to its ambient-temperature compression
behavior, the carbon-containing (MoNbVTi) 95C5
RHEA exhibited superior resistance to high-tem-
perature softening during the thermomechanical
compression. Notably, this alloy demonstrated
enhanced elevated-temperature yield strength,
approximately 100 MPa greater than its carbon-
free MoNbVTi counterpart. This improved perfor-
mance is attributed to the synergistic effects of
carbon-induced solid-solution strengthening and
dynamic precipitation hardening, which effectively
impede dislocation motion and delay dynamic recov-
ery/recrystallization processes under high-tempera-
ture deformation conditions. In this study, the high-
temperature strength of the carbon-containing
MoNbVTi alloy reached approximately 1000 MPa.
Could a further increase in carbon content enhance
its resistance to high-temperature softening? Luo
et al. reported a high-temperature strength of
2.19 GPa at 1200 /C176C in carbide-containing MoN-
bW(TaC)0.9, where microstructural analysis showed
a BCC phase matrix with ﬁne lamellar eutectoid
structures, enabling dual strengthening mecha-
nisms through second-phase reinforcement and
grain reﬁnement. 24 Wan et al. reported a novel
high-entropy composite (NbMoTaW) 100-xCx com-
posed of HEAs and high-entropy ceramics (HECs).
Appropriate regulation of carbon addition signiﬁ-
cantly enhanced the compressive strength (2.5-fold
enhancement in the NbMoTaWC alloy) and ductil-
ity through a combination of BCC HEA and FCC
HEC microstructures. 25 The carbon enrichment in
our specimens suggests that in situ formation of
ceramic phases likely occurred during thermal
testing, thereby improving the alloy’s resistance to
high-temperature softening. As highlighted in the
referenced studies, precisely regulating the carbon
concentration within the alloy matrix emerges as a

Computation Results of Absorption Energy
of Carbon Atom
Figure 4 illustrates the atomic geometries of pre-
built grain boundary models for the simulations, as
well as relative C adsorption energies for the bulk
and grain boundary areas. Here, the C adsorption
energy was calculated by DE
ads = EM-C /C0EM /C0EC,
where EM-C is the electronic energy of the HEA
containing a C atom, and EM and EC are the
energies of a clean alloy and a C atom, respectively.
EC is taken as the per-atom energy of a graphite
geometry. Within such a deﬁnition, more negative
absorption energy results in a more energetically
favorable C adsorption process. Our simulation
results show that, for all the grain boundary
supercells except for the R5[110]/(110), C adsorption
at lattice sites in the bulk phase are generally more
thermodynamically stable than at the grain bound-
ary (Fig. 4a), conﬁrming the favorable C element
distribution within the bulk area in the metal
rather than gathering at the grain boundaries. For
R5[110]/(110), adsorption of C at the grain boundary
is slightly more favorable, indicating the existence
of more C-afﬁnitive sites for such a geometry
compared to the bulk phase.
To estimate the upper limit of C concentration
within the bulk region, we deﬁned an extra C
concentration at the bulk phase (cbulk) which was
calculated by counting the number of adsorption
sites in the bulk area whose C binding strengths
were stronger than the most favorable binding site
within the grain boundary. In other words, carbon
atoms tend to occupy lattice sites with more nega-
tive adsorption energies, and a higher cbulk sug-
gests a higher C solution in the bulk phase before
accumulating in the grain boundary region. The
calculation process of the bulk as well as the total
number of adsorption sites in the bulk and bound-
ary regions are displayed in Table S2 in the
Supplementary Information. As shown in Fig. 4b,
for R5[110]/(110), R5[110]/(1–12), and R5[111]/(111),
more favorable adsorption sites in the bulk phase
compared to the boundary region are identiﬁed,
with cbulk ranging from /C240.35 at.% to /C242 at.%,
indicating that such HEA models can yield a
relatively high carbon solution in the bulk phase.
For the R5[110]/(/C0111) and R5[111]/(1–21) systems,
we spotted an adsorption site at the boundary
region with a C adsorption energy lower than in
the bulk region. However, considering the relatively
lower averaged C binding energy, as well as the
higher number of adsorption sites in the bulk area
(Fig. 4a), the averaged C concentration within the
grain boundary region should be smaller compared
to the bulk phase. By averaging the cbulk values
over the grain boundary models, we estimated an
overall upper limit of /C240.85 at.% carbon concen-
tration in the bulk phase before this impurity
accumulated within the grain boundary. In this
study, the carbon content in the MoNbVTi alloy
reached 5 at.%, signiﬁcantly exceeding the solid
solubility limit predicted by the theoretical model,
resulting a supersaturated state, which induced
extensive carbide precipitation along the grain
boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 2c. It is well
established that the carbide-ceramic phase plays a
critical role in enhancing material strength. Never-
theless, whether the carbide precipitates observed
at the grain boundaries can be classiﬁed, as ceramic
phases require detailed compositional and struc-
tural characterization. The atomic-scale structural
features of carbide ceramic/matrix interfaces and
the volume fraction of these phases exert a profound
inﬂuence on the mechanical performance of metallic
systems. Precise control over phase morphology and
chemical composition is therefore critical to attain
an optimal strength-toughness balance in multi-
component refractory alloys.

CONCLUSION
The equiatomic MoNbVTi alloy and the carbon-
doped (MoNbVTi) 95C5 alloy were both subjected to
microstructural and mechanical analyses in a
comparable manner to address the impact of the
carbides. The SEM and BSE results revealed that
the carbon precipitate ph ase was preferentially
segregated along the grai n boundaries. According
to DFT calculations, the maximum solid solubility
of the carbon atoms in the MoNbVTi alloy was
approximately 0.85%. The doping level of carbon
atoms signiﬁcantly surpasses the established
threshold, resulting in the aggregation of carbides
at the grain boundaries. In ambient conditions, the
carbides exist as brittle phase and weaken the
binding force of boundaries, consequently leading
to a decline in the mechanical properties. However,
at elevated temperatures, the carbides probably
evolved into a ceramic-strengthening phase, lead-
ing to increased resistance to high-temperature
softening. Precisely controlling the concentration
of the carbon atoms and modifying the interface
structure by appropriate thermal processing
maybe a promising way towards required RHEA
materials.
