Abstract: The morphological evolution of the ’ precipitates has been studied in a second 
generation nickel-based single crystal superalloy d uring isothermal creep straining at 1093°C 
under multiaxial stress state. For this purpose, a specific V -shaped specimen has been 
designed to induce an evolving multiaxial stress state along its thickness. These stress 
conditions are quantified by the stress triaxiality obtained using viscoplastic finite element 
simulations. channel width and ’ rafting orientation ha ve been analyzed along the 
thickness for several triaxiality values, using Rose diagrams and specifically developed image 
processing tools . The ’ rafting process is shown to be highly sensitive to the sign and 
magnitude of the stress triaxiality. Finally, it is shown that the /’ topological inversion 
development is faster under compression.

1) Introduction 
Nickel-based single crystal superalloys are widely used for the design of airfoils in the hottest 
sections of aero -engines and industrial gas turbines (e.g. high and intermediate pressure 
turbine blades, vanes and s hrouds) due to their outstanding mechanical properties at 
temperature up to ~ 1150°C [1,2]. These excellent mechanical performances are inherited 
both from the absence of any deleterious grain boundaries and from a high volume fraction 
(~ 70% in most of the commercial alloys up to 800°C) of strengthening ’ precipitates having 
a L12 crystallographic structure, coherently merged in the disordered fcc  matrix [1–4]. High 
pressure turbine blades of the most advanced aero -engines and industrial gas turbines 
generally have complex shapes, with a hollow profile due to internal cooling channels and 
also, complex profiles to optimize the external hot gas flow. Such complex geometries, in 
addition to through thickness thermal gradients, leads to complex multiaxial stress states, 
which in turn may affect the morphological evolution of the strengthening ’ phase of these 
alloys [5–8]. Moreover, during service operation s, the viscoplastic behavior of these alloys 
may lead to stress redistribution close to notches, affecting the local multiaxial stress state. 
The kinetics of ’ directional coarsening are affected by the sign of the applied stress, by the 
sign and magnitude of the /’ lattice misfit value and by the differences in elastic constants 
of the two phases [9–14]. More generally, the application of a macroscopic external load 
involves an anisotropic misfit relaxation led by dislocations generated during creep test s 
[10,15]. When loaded in tension along the <001> crystallographic direction, directional ’ 
rafting is developed perpendicular to the loading direction for negative misfit alloys , known 
as N -type rafting . However, when loaded in compression along the same direction , ’ 
coarsening is developed parallel to load ing axis, known as P -type rafting . Véron et co-

in compression compared to tensile creep by a factor of 4 . In the case of multiaxial stress

<001> direction) provides a more isotropic’-rafting. 
In the literature, the impact of a complex stress state on the ’ rafting in Ni -based single 
crystal superalloys has been studied performing tests using notched specimens [18–20]. 
Cormier et al. [21] also studied the ’ rafting mechanisms in the vicinity of large casting pores 
during creep in the high temperature/low stress regime of the MC2 Ni-based single crystal 
superalloy. They showed that both the void diameter and morphology affect the ’ rafting 
kinetics and orientation which are highly dependent on the local stress field . Similar results 
were also observed by Wang et al. [22] during isostatic pressing of turbine blades made of 
GTD 111 Ni-based superalloy. 
Touratier et al. [23] studied the ’ rafting microstructure during creep at very high 
temperature using “TV-shaped” notched MC2 sp ecimens. In this kind of geometry, a strong 
creep strain localization is observed in the vicinity of notch es where ’ precipitates are 
oriented at 45 degrees away from the macroscopic loading axis. To extend such a multiaxial 
effect to a wider extent , le Graverend et al. [24] designed an asymmetric dual-notched 
specimen involving in the minimum bearing section a strong multiaxial stress field, during 
high temperature creep test. In th is area of the specimen , ’ rafts mean plane was observed 
to deviate from a mean plane perpendicular to the tensile loading axis, due to a lattice

2) Numerical analysis of V-shaped specimen 
Contrary to other notched specimen observed in the literature, the proposed V-shaped 
specimen allows to reproduce the stress relaxation in the concave side in addition to an 
evolution of the triaxiality along the thickness of the specimen in order to affect the ’ rafting 
morphology during isothermal high temperature creep test. Before an y creep experiment 
using such a specimen, a numerical analysis aimed at optimizing the specimen geometry and 
applied load during the tests has been performed. 
The geometry has been adjusted to introduce the desired magnitude and variation of stress 
triaxiality across the thickness of the specimens. Indeed, the α angle and the local radius R 
(shown in Fig. 1 ) control both the multiaxial stress state and the stress relaxation during 
creep deformation.

loads using such a specimen geometry has been performed using Abaqus CAE (version 6.12) 
for an α angle of 75° and a tip radius R of 2 mm. Numerical simulations comprised two 
successive steps; after a first fully elastic loadin g step, a creep step has been simulated for 
100 hours. C3D20R reduced integration element s were used for the meshing of the 
specimen, ensuring a faster convergence during the creep loading step [25]. A convergence

3) Experimental Procedure and image analysis 
3.1. Material and specimens 
Specimens were machined from nearly <001> CMSX -4 rods (deviation less than 5 degrees) , 
which had received a standard heat treatment comprised of a solution heat treatment of 2 
hours at 1315°C/air quench (AQ) followed by two aging heat treatments of 4 hours at 
1100°C/AQ and 20 hours at 870°C/AQ. The nominal chemical composition of CMSX-4 alloy in 
weight percent is given in 
2. To achieve the specific shape of the specimen, electric discharge machining was used to 
machine the gage section of the specimen, followed by low-stress mechanical polishing up to 
a 4000 SiC paper grade . Given the very low thickness used for such specimens, careful

3.2. Creep experiments 
Several interrupted isothermal creep tests were performed at 1093°C, adjusting the applied 
load (given small variations in specimens’ thickness) to achieve a local principal stress σ1 = 
150 MPa in the most heavily loaded point in the concave side of the s pecimen (side A in Fig. 
3 a). These structural creep experiments were performed using a radiant furnace and the 
temperature was controlled using an S -type thermocouple spot welded in the middle of the 
gage section, on the convex side of the specimen (side B in Fig. 3 a). The elongation of the 
specimen between each head has been followed using a laser beam extensome ter. The 
following procedure has been used for each experiment: heating from room temperature up 
to 1093°C at 100°C/min, soak time for 5 minutes to ensure temperature homogeneity in the 
gage section of the specimen and then, application of the tensile load . As shown in Fig. 7, 
several experiments have been performed to track the evolution of the ’ morphology at 
different level of creep elongation. Experiments have been interrupted after 5, 24, 100 and 
300 hours, including first and second stage of creep . The interruption after 300 hours under 
these conditions corresponds to the very beginning of the tertiary creep stage. Interruptions 
of these creep tests have been performed under load, with a cooling rate of near ly 10°C/s 
between 1093°C and 900°C. The interested reader is referred to a previous publication for 
further details on the experimental set -up [27]. It is worth mentioning that we have paid a 
special attention to remain under small creep strain conditions (i.e. below ~ 1% creep strain) 
to limit the contribution of the lattice rotation to the morphological evolution of the ’

3.3. Microstructure investigations 
 After each experiment, a longitudinal cut of the specimen has been done, using a precision 
saw. The specimens were then mounted in a conductive resin and mechanically polished up 
to a mirror finish (final polis hing with a 1 µm diamond paste). They were th en etched with a 
solution made of 1/3 HNO 3 and 2/3 HCl (vol. parts) at 4°C for 8 to 10 seconds . Using such a 
chemical etching, the ’ phase is dissolved, hence revealing the /’ microstructure (i.e. the ’ 
phase appears in black in the scanning electron microscope –SEM- pictures presented in the 
following of this paper). 
The /’ microstructure has been observed using a JEOL 6400 scanning electron microscope 
operating at 25 kV, with a working distance in the 6 – 10 mm range. Several SEM 
observations have been performed all along the thickness of specimen in the area close to 
the V notch (starting from concave side and moving toward the convex zone as shown in Fig. 
11 for section S-L reported in the square). Doing such, the main aim is to better understand 
the /’ microstructure evolution as a function of the local stress triaxiality, as shown 
previously in Fig. 6. According to Fig. 6, 25 µm wide images have been taken to achieve a 
statistically relevant discretization of the specimens’ thickness, supposing that the stress 
triaxiality is constant in each image field of view (i.e. a mean value of stress triaxiality is 
used). It is also worth mentioning that microstructure observation s have been performed 
mainly in the primary dendrite arm spacing, and far from pores and residual eutectics pools, 
to avoid any possible contribution of these casting defects to the lo cal precipitation 
morphology [21].

3.4. Image analysis of ’ rafting 
The  channel width and ’ raft orientation evolutions as a function of the creep time have 
been analyzed by image analyzes using algorithms specifically developed for this study in 
Matlab. After image segmentation,  channel widths were calculated as the difference 
between two co nsecutive points intercepting the  phase, by using a rotating line intercept 
method, varying the orientation of the lines within a range 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. Fig. 8 presents a 
typical example showing how the algorithm works: for any θ angle, a set of parallel lines 
starting from the image boundary (this depends on the theta value) intercepting coordinates 
of the  channel perimeter provides us all distances of θ orientation, in the overall image. 
Fig. 9 shows an example of channel distance calculation as the difference between initial 
coordinate (bl ue) and final coordinate (red) for θ = π/6. Three alternating colors ( purple, 
yellow and blue) represent all calculated distances. 
These results are then used to build Rose histograms, depicting the mean  channel width 
versus θ angle. This representation, most ly used to represent microcrack and damage 
density detection [28–34], allowed us to quantify the morphology evolution of the /’ 
microstructure during isothermal creep test. To avoid any oxidation effect in the analysis of 
the /’ microstructure evolutions with respect to the local mechanic al state, areas far from 
the oxidation-affected layers [35–39] have been used to track the microstructure 
observations (i.e. typically, 50 to 80 µm below the surface, depending on the creep 
duration).

4) Results and discussion 
4.1. Typical /’ morphological evolutions 
As shown in Fig. 11 for a 100 hours creep test , the ’ rafting is affected by the local stress 
triaxiality, both concerning  channel width and orientation. F or a high positive level of

near 1/3 uniaxial case (Fig. 11 d), a standard N -type rafting is observed, like in the case of

occurs perpendicularly to the <001> direction. An abrupt microstructure morphology change

4.2. Time dependence of the ’-rafting process as a function of the stress triaxiality 
4.2.1. Stress triaxiality  ≈ 0.6 
Fig. 12 depicts the ’-rafting process between 5 and 300 hours with correspond ing  channel 
width Rose histograms for a stress triaxiality value ≈0.6 (Fig. 11 close to 10 % of thickness) . 
At the beginning of the creep deformation (5 hours, Fig. 12a), a ’ coalescence perpendicular 
to the loading axis is observed . Surprisingly, despite a local 0.6 stress triaxiality (i.e. a 
multiaxial stress state), the microstructure develops a N -type rafting similar to the one 
obtained in the case of uniaxial tensile creep loading using smooth specimen having a <001> 
orientation. According to pioneering results from Tien & Copley [12,13] and by many other 
authors later on (e.g. [1,15]), a similar ’ morphological is observed during creep at the 
beginning of high temperature/low stress tensile creep t ests performed using smooth 
specimens oriented along the <001> direction. Is known that the main driving forces 
governing the ’ rafting process are the inhomogeneity in elastic stiff nesses of the two 
phases [9], the magnitude and sign of the /’ lattice misfit [15] and the amount of creep

During subsequent creep deformation, a generalized tilted ’ rafting is observed, oriented at 
45 degrees of the loading axis and confirmed by the Rose diagrams (Figs. 12 c and d). 
According to Gaubert et al . [44], taking in account the viscoplasticity is mandatory to

non-symmetric way. When ’ shape evolves, dislocations move across the  channel width

the one observed in Fig. 12b, c d. At the same time, the microstructure evolution is also

results in an increased stress triaxiality (see Fig. 6) in the convex side, thereby leading to a 
misoriented ’ rafting due to this increased level of stress multiaxiality.

4.3. /’ topological inversion 
According to the Rose diagrams presented in Fig. 15, a progressive  thickening along the +/-
π/2 direction (i.e. formation of horizontal  channels) is also observed , a thickening starting 
earlier for ≈-0.6 in comparison to ≈-0.33. This “lateral”  thickening, beginning after 100 
hours in creep for a -0.6 stress triaxiality, corresponds to the creation of horizontal  
channels with respect to the <001> macroscopic loading direction, i.e. a breakdown of the P-
type ’ rafting. This clearly means that the / ’ topological inversion is taking place, a 
microstructure evolution often considered as controlling the leading to the creep rate 
progressive increase [40,41,47]. This microstructure evolution usually occurs in the last stage 
of the secondary creep. It leads to a microstructure where ’ particles surround the  matrix 
(while in the undeformed state, ’ cuboids are embedded in the  matrix). This process is

In the present work, the influence of the stress triaxiality on the morphological evolution of 
the /’ microstructure of second generation Ni -based single crystal superalloy has been 
studied during high temperature/low stress isothermal creep test. A V-shaped specimen has 
been specifically designed by 3D FEM analysis to achieve controlled evolutions of the stress 
triaxiality and stress redistribution along its thickness. Microstructure evolutions have been 
quantified through the use Rose diagrams, plotting the 2D spatial evolution of the  channel 
mean width. 
From the present study, the following main conclusions can be established: 
1) The ’ rafting orientation correlates in a very satisfying way to the spatial evolution of 
the local stress triaxiality obtained by using a viscoplastic finite element simulation of 
the stress field evolution in the creep specimens. 
2) The  channel width increases almost continuously for all stress triaxiality values 
under these creep conditions. 
3) For a stress triaxiality of 0.6, the ’ rafting morphology evolves from plates 
perpendicular to the macroscopic stress axis (after 5 hours in creep) to a 45 degrees 
orientation. For a stress triaxiality of 0.3 3, the microstructure is identical to the one 
observed during uniaxial tensile test (triaxiality = 1/3 in uniaxial tension using smooth 
specimen), showing a classical N-type ’ rafting. 
4) For stress triaxialities of -0.33 ad -0.6, a P-type rafting is observed. 
5) Positive values of stress triaxiality increase both the ’ rafting and  channel widening 
kinetics.
