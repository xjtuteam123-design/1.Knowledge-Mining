abstract
Austenitic, ferritic, and duplex Fe – 17Cr–6Mn–0.45C–xNi–yAl (3Ni alloy: x¼ 3, y¼4 and 9Ni series: x¼9,
y¼ 0,4,7) cast stainless steels were obtained by adjusting the Al and Ni concentrations. The Al-free steel
containing 9 wt% Ni exhibited an austenitic microstructure. The addition of 4 wt% Al to the 9Ni series of
alloys retained the microstructure austenitic. Increasing the Al content to 7 wt%, however, led to the
development of a fully ferritic matrix with a high hardness and poor ductility. The alloy containing 4 wt%
Al but a reduced Ni content of 3 wt% exhibited a duplex microstructure consisting of nearly 20% ferrite in
an austenitic matrix. Dilatometry indicated unusually high thermal expansion coef ﬁcients for the 7 wt%
Al ferritic steel at temperatures above the Curie temperature of the alloy. This anomaly was interpreted
in terms of the formation of high concentrations of thermal vacancies possibly due to the presence of
intermetallic compounds. The tensile tests conducted at room temperature indicated a concurrent
enhancement of tensile strength and total elongation of the Al-free austenitic alloy by the addition of
4 wt% Al. This advantage is magni ﬁed by the lower density of the Al-alloyed variant. The extended
elongation and the high strength were achieved in spite of the decreasing work hardening rate of the
Al-alloyed steel variant at high strains, interpreted as a gradual transition from planar to wavy glide
mode of dislocations. The high glide planarity at low strains of the Al-alloyed austenitic alloy was
conﬁrmed by the TEM observation of dislocation pile-up arrangements. Substantial strengthening
compared to austenitic steels was achieved in the duplex steel. This strengthening was associated with a
marginal loss of ductility as 20% ferrite was introduced in the microstructure.

1. Introduction
Lightweight steels have received an increasing attention in the
past decades due primarily to their superior combination of
ductility and strength [1–6]. Alloying with aluminum can increase
the strength by means of solid solution hardening [7], formation of
quenched-in vacancies [8], precipitation hardening [9,10], and
short-range ordering [1 1,12]mechanisms. Alloying with Al is an
approach to the manufacture of steels with higher strength to
weight ratios in order to respond to the competition from light-
weight non-ferrous alloys such as those based on magnesium and
aluminum [13].
In austenitic steels, Al addition has been found to improve the
wear resistance of high-Mn Had ﬁeld steels [14] and the resistance
to hydrogen embrittlement of Fe–Mn–C [15]and Fe–Cr–Mn–Ni–C [16]
alloys. Furthermore, Al is known to increase the stacking fault energy

The SFE is not the only parameter determining the degree of
glide planarity. Planar glide of dislocations has been also observed
during tensile testing of Al-alloyed high Mn steels with SFEs in
excess of 70 mJ/m 2 [23,24]. Gerold et al. [22] explained this
phenomenon in fcc solid solutions and precipitation-hardenable
fcc alloys in terms of “glide plane softening ”. This condition arises
due to interactions between dislocations and short-range cluster-
ing (SRC) or short-range ordering (SRO). In the above mechanism,
the irrecoverable destruction of the short-range order with a
leading dislocation facilitates motion of the following dislocations
thus encouraging planar glide [22]. The enhancement of planar
slip in aged Fe –Mn–Al–C steels has been attributed to the forma-
tion of ordered shearable (Fe,Mn) 3AlCx κ-carbides [9].
There are recent efforts to add Cr and Ni to ternary Fe –Mn–Al,
and quaternary Fe –Mn–Al–C alloy systems [16,25–28]. For
instance, the mechanical properties and microstructure of
Fe–20Mn–(10–14)Al–(0.75–1.8)C–5Cr (compositions always in wt%)
steels with densities below 7.0 g/cm 3 were investigated by Sutou
et al. [26]. Addition of Cr proved effective as the strength and
ductility of the Cr-containing Fe –20Mn–10Al–5Cr–1.5C alloy were
superior to those of the Cr-free Fe –20Mn–10Al–1C alloy. This
indicates the prospect of alloying Al-containing austenitic steels
with still higher amounts of Cr. In the presence of suf ﬁciently high
Cr levels, Al-alloyed stainless steels are obtained. The addition of Al
to austenitic stainless steels can be used as a novel method of
designing lightweight stainless steels. For example, the tensile
properties at /C0 50 1C of an Al-alloyed austenitic stainless steel with
the chemical composition Fe –13Cr–10Mn–8Ni–3Al–0.12C were
comparable to the AISI 316 stainless steel [16]. The high total
elongation of the Al-containing alloy at /C0 50 1C, which was attrib-
uted to its high resistance against the strain-induced martensitic
transformation, warrants further attention to the potential of Al
addition to stainless steels.
The present study demonstrates the microstructural changes
caused by the Al addition to Fe –Cr–Mn–Ni–C stainless steels. The
mechanical properties and the work hardening behavior at room
temperature of the alloys are subsequently correlated with the
microstructure. The bene ﬁts of Al addition to stainless steels in
view of the strength to weight ratio and ductility are highlighted.
The results obtained with a variety of Al-alloyed stainless steel
microstructures (ferritic, duplex, and austenitic) are particularly
relevant to future developments and modi ﬁcations.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure
To obtain lightweight stainless steels, various amounts of Al
were added to the base compositions of approximately Fe –17Cr–
3Ni–6Mn–0.45C (3Ni4Al) and Fe –17Cr–9Ni–6Mn–0.45C (9Ni ser-
ies). In these alloy systems, some of the elements such as Ni and
Mn thermodynamically stabilize the γ-austenite phase, while
others such as Cr and Al tend to stabilize the α-ferrite phase in
the microstructure [16]. The addition of high carbon contents was
intended to counterbalance the ferrite stabilizing effect of Al and
ensure that the Al-alloyed steels did not always become ferritic
and that austenitic steels could be obtained too. Fig. 1 shows the
pseudo-binary phase diagrams of the alloys calculated by Thermo-
Calc. The position of each alloy is indicated by a labeled line.
Fig. 2 shows the optical micrographs of specimens quenched in
He after annealing at 1200 1C for 5 min. In agreement with the
Thermo-Calc predictions, the Al-free 9Ni0Al variant was found by
microscopy and magnetic saturation measurements to develop a
fully austenitic matrix. For the 9Ni4Al alloy, an austenitic –ferritic
microstructure was predicted by Thermo-Calc. In practice, how-
ever, magnetic saturation measurements yielded a ferromagnetic
phase content of almost zero indicating the absence of ferrite in
the microstructure of the 9Ni4Al alloy. Microstructural examina-
tions aided by magnetic saturation measurements con ﬁrmed the
development of a fully ferritic matrix in the 9Ni7Al alloy. The
3Ni4Al steel which may be regarded as a low Ni variant of the
9Ni4Al alloy was found to have a duplex ferritic –austenitic
microstructure.
The optical micrographs in Fig. 2 conﬁrm the presence of
undissolved carbides in the microstructure of all alloys. This is in
line with the Thermo-Calc predictions ( Fig. 1 ) indicating the

3.1.1. Austenitic alloys: 9Ni0Al and 9Ni4Al
Fig. 3 shows EBSD orientation maps of 9Ni0Al and 9Ni4Al
steels. Small misorientations within the coarse austenitic grains
reﬂect the lattice rotations caused by light compression of alloys.
The 9Ni4Al alloy also features a pattern of subgrain regions with
an equiaxed morphology. The subgrains delineated by low-angle
grain boundaries are expected to exist even in the absence of prior
deformation. The low-angle grain boundaries which are hard to
identify in the optical micrographs of solution treated specimens
become visible by low-temperature aging treatments as the
preferential formation of carbides in such boundaries facilitates
their identi ﬁcation. Metallography of aged specimen also revealed
that the distribution of subgrain boundaries was not uniform
throughout the specimens and that they were more densely
located in regions with a ﬁner austenite grain size. The difference
in the subgrain structure of 9Ni0Al and 9Ni4Al alloys likely
originates from their mode of solidi ﬁcation. Whereas the 9Ni0Al
alloy solidi ﬁes austenitic and remains so at lower temperatures,
the solidi ﬁcation of the 9Ni4Al alloy begins with the formation of
ferrite. The sequence of transformations during cooling from the
liquid state (L) of the 9Ni4Al alloy can be summarized as L -

Furthermore, the absence of extra spots in the SAD patterns taken

spinodal decomposition was reported to occur even during rapid
cooling from high temperatures [36]. Aging of the 9Ni4Al steel for
times up to 8 h at 450 and 550 1C did not increase the hardness
signiﬁcantly implying the unlikelihood of κ carbide formation by
aging of stainless steels with a high Cr/C ratio. This is due to the
high stability of Cr-rich carbides so that the formation of any κ
carbide is preceded by the formation of Cr-rich carbides.
3.1.2. Duplex alloy: 3Ni4Al
The duplex microstructure of the 3Ni4Al steel is shown in the
EBSD IPF map and the corresponding phase map of Fig. 5 . The
ferrite volume fraction was determined to be 19.4% closely
matching the volume fractions based on magnetic saturation
measurements of 19.8%. As with the 9Ni4Al austenitic alloy,
equiaxed subgrain regions with small misorientations were also
observed inside the γ grains of the 3Ni4Al steel. Similar to the
9Ni4Al alloy, this may be attributed to the formation of a
signiﬁcant fraction of
δ-ferrite during primary solidi ﬁcation and
its subsequent transformation to γ at lower temperatures. The
higher ferrite stability, on account of a lower Ni content, of the
3Ni4Al steel compared to the 9Ni4Al steel supports this mechan-
ism as a high fraction of primary δ-ferrite, prerequisite for this
mechanism, will be available according to Fig. 1b.
The TEM bright ﬁeld image in Fig. 6 shows an austenite –ferrite
grain boundary containing an array of precipitates. The high Cr
content of ferrite and the higher diffusivity of Cr in the ferrite than
in the austenite make ferrite a particularly good supplier of Cr in
the process of chromium carbide formation. Given the low
solubility of C in the ferrite, the C required for the chromium
carbide formation is supplied by the austenite. This makes the α/γ
boundaries particularly favorable sites for the formation of Cr-rich
carbides in duplex stainless steels.

A striking feature of the 9Ni7Al ferritic alloy was its high
hardness of 424 HV 10, signi ﬁcantly higher than the hardness
values of 175 HV 10 and 165 HV 10 measured for the 9Ni0Al and
9Ni4Al austenitic alloys, respectively. This hardness level is also

solution strengthening effect of Al. In binary Fe –Al alloys, the
strengthening of ferrite by Al addition has been attributed to both
the solid solution strengthening effect of solute Al and the

signiﬁcant quantities of alloying elements in the 9Ni7Al alloys

3.2. Dilatometry
In order to study phase transformations, alloys were subjected to
dilatometry experiments in the temperature range of 25 –118 01C.
Fig. 8 shows the length changes during heating to 1 180 1Co fa l l o y s
and the corresponding ﬁrst derivative plots (heating segment only)
denoted incremental coefﬁcient of thermal expansion (CTE). Heat-
ing of the fully austenitic alloys with a high CTE at room tempera-
ture caused a gradual increase in the incremental CTE which
accelerated at temperatures above approximately 950 1C. Further
dilatometry investigations and microstructural examinations con-
ﬁrmed that no phase transformation occurred in the austenitic
alloys until 1 1801C.
The CTE of the duplex 3Ni4Al steel increased steadily with
temperature. The temperature dependence of CTE in the duplex
alloy was higher than that of the fully austenitic steels. This is
consistent with the greater temperature dependence of CTE in
ferritic steels compared to austenitic steels [40]. At temperatures
above approximately 950 1C, the incremental CTE of the 3Ni4Al
alloy almost matched that of the austenitic alloys implying that
the CTE of the ferrite contained in the alloy had reached levels
close to that of the austenite. To con ﬁrm that ferrite persisted in
the microstructure of the duplex alloy at temperatures as high as
1200 1C, the ferrite fraction in specimens quenched from tem-
peratures between 600 1C and 1200 1C was determined by mag-
netic saturation measurements and quantitative metallography.

As the Ni content of the 9Ni4Al alloy was decreased to 3 wt%,
i.e. the Ni concentration in the 3Ni4Al alloy, the yield and tensile
strengths increased appreciably with a marginal loss of ductility.
This strengthening was caused by the presence of nearly 20%

accordance with the observations made for Al-added duplex
stainless steels with various ferrite contents [28]. The strengthen-
ing of austenite by the introduction of ferrite is consistent with the
higher solid solution strengthening effect of common alloying
elements and Al in the ferrite than in the austenite [47]. Magnetic
saturation measurements before and after tensile tests con ﬁrmed
that no deformation-induced martensite formation occurred in the
austenite phase.

the 9Ni4Al stainless steel implies that in both cases, Al changes the
deformation processes in such a manner that an initial increase in
the work hardening rate is followed by a decrease at higher

onset of wavy glide, seems to correlate with the uniform elonga-

should be related to the presence of ferrite in the microstructure.

4. Conclusions
The microstructure and mechanical properties of Fe –17Cr–
6Mn–0.45C–xNi–yAl (x¼3, y¼4 and x¼9, y¼0,4,7) stainless steels
were studied. The following conclusions were drawn:
1. Alloying with Al leads to a variety of stainless steel micro-
structures including alloys with ferritic, duplex, and austenitic
matrix microstructures. The Al-free variant of the 9Ni series of
alloys developed an austenitic matrix after annealing at
1200 1C. The addition of 4 wt% Al to the 9Ni series of alloys
retained the microstructure austenitic. Increasing the Al con-
tent to 7 wt% led to the development of a fully ferritic matrix.
The alloy containing 4 wt% Al but a reduced Ni content of 3 wt%
exhibited a duplex microstructure consisting of nearly 20%
ferrite in an austenitic matrix. Undissolved precipitates were
found in the microstructure of all alloys. Some of the coarse
precipitates were identi ﬁed to be M
23C6 and M 7C3 types.
2. Dilatometry investigations of the 9Ni4Al ferritic alloy showed a
rapid rise in the CTE at temperatures above the Curie tempera-
ture. This phenomenon was interpreted as the formation of
high concentrations of thermal vacancies aided by the forma-
tion of intermetallic compounds in the ferritic matrix. The
relatively high Curie temperature of the 9Ni7Al alloy which
remained almost equal to that of the 3Ni4Al alloy with a lower
Ni and Al content may refer to a higher depletion of Cr and
other alloying elements in the 9Ni7Al alloy. This can arise due
to the presence of a higher fraction of carbides and possibly the
formation of FeAl-type intermetallic compounds with a B2
structure.
3. As the aluminum content was increased to 4 wt% in the 9Ni
series of austenitic alloys, a simultaneous rise occurred in the
UTS and total elongation. Substantial strengthening compared
to austenitic steel variants was achieved in the duplex steel.
This strengthening was associated with a marginal loss of
ductility as 20% ferrite was introduced in the microstructure.
The stress –strain behavior of the ferritic alloy could not be
determined as tensile specimens of the 7% Al alloy underwent
fracture in the elastic deformation range.
4. The Al-free austenitic stainless steel exhibited near-linear work
hardening until necking. The linearity of work hardening
behavior decreased in the Al-alloyed austenitic alloy. The work
hardening behavior of the latter was characterized by an
increase in the work hardening rate at low strains which
transitioned to a regime of decreasing work hardening rate at
true strains in excess of 0.17. The transition to a regime of non-
linear hardening implies a gradual establishment of wavy glide.
The high planarity of glide in the early stages of deformation of
the Al-alloyed austenitic alloy was con ﬁrmed by the TEM
observation of planar dislocation arrangements.
