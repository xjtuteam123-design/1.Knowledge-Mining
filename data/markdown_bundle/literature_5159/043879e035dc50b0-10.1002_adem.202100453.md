L12-Strengthened Co-Rich Alloys for High-Temperature
Structural Applications: A Critical Review
Boxuan Cao, Yilu Zhao, Tao Yang,* and Chain Tsuan Liu*
1. Introduction
Higher ef ﬁciency for the engines working at elevated tempera-
tures puts forward an urgent request for structural materials with
superior temperature capabilities.[1] Material selection criteria for
such a demanding environment are the combination of excellent
thermal stability and superior high-
temperature strength. Precipitation-
hardened superalloys are extensively
developed and widely used to cope with
aggressive environments with both high
temperatures and stresses. [2] The secret
for advanced high-temperature alloys to ful-
ﬁll the demanding requirements lies in
their microstructures: homogeneously dis-
tributed high-density γ 0 precipitates with
an ordered crystalline structure (L1 2-type
structure) embedded in the solid-solution
γ matrix (face-centered cubic [FCC] struc-
ture) by keeping coherent γ/γ 0 interfaces.[3]
Continuous efforts have been devoted to
pushing the applicability of Ni-based super-
alloys toward higher temperatures, which
drive the development and evolution of
Ni-based superalloys. For example, the Re
addition, which is known to substantially
enhance the creep resistance, leads to the
development of the second- and third-
generation Ni-based superalloys. [4] The
Ru element, capable of suppressing the
microstructural instability induced by heavily alloyed refractory
additions, has found its place in the fourth- and ﬁfth-generation
Ni-based superalloys. [5,6] Nevertheless, Ni-based superalloys are
generally believed to have approached their temperature limits
due to the narrow gap between the γ 0-solvus temperature and
the melting point. [7] Consequently, this dilemma motivates the
search for new class structural materials with higher upper
temperature.[8,9]
Previous studies indicated that the creep rupture life among
the Ni-based superalloys demonstrated a strong correlation with
the γ 0-solvus temperature, which suggests that a higher γ 0-solvus
temperature is bene ﬁcial for the enhanced creep resistance by
retaining a higher γ 0 volume fraction at operating tempera-
tures.[3] However, since the γ 0-solvus temperature is approaching
the melting temperatures of the Ni-based superalloys, the service
temperature capabilities for the Ni-based superalloys have nearly
been maximized. Alternatively, due to the fact that the melting
point of Co is 40 /C14 C higher than that of Ni, Co-rich alloys are
thereby recognized as promising alternatives for the well-
developed Ni-based superalloys with enhanced applicability at
elevated temperatures. Therefore, the advantages in the melting
temperature justify the motivation to pursue potential high-
temperature applications within Co-rich alloy systems.

(low stress) components in gas turbine engines. [10] As they rely
on carbide precipitation and solid solutes for strengthening, their

These elements tend to promote high stacking-fault energy
(SFE) in the FCC lattice and suppress the propensities for stack-
ing faults and associated hexagonal structure formation. As a
result of the improved FCC stability, the γ-Co phase can be
retained as the equilibrium phase even at room temperature
via controlled alloying additions, which provides the opportunity
to introduce ordered precipitates for strengthening within Co-
rich alloys. In the following, Co-rich alloys with ordered L1 2-type
precipitates for strengthening are critically reviewed.
2. L1 2-Strengthened Co-Rich Alloys
2.1. Co –Al–W Ternary Alloy System
In 2006, Sato et al. [7] reported the formation of the L1 2-type γ 0-
Co3(Al, W) phase with an ordered structure within the ternary
Co–9Al–7.5W alloy (in at%, and this unit is also applicable in
the following content) after aging at 900 /C14 C for 72 h. The ordered
L12 precipitates are coherent with the γ-Co matrix phase by
keeping cube-on-cube orientation relationship and distributed uni-
formly throughout the γ-Co matrix,[13] as shown in Figure 1a. This
kind of microstructure resembles that of the precipitation-
hardened Ni-based superalloys. Therefore, the discovery of the
L12 precipitates in the Co–Al–W alloys demonstrates the possibil-
ity of utilizing the effective ordered precipitation strengthening
within the Co-rich alloy systems for the ﬁrst time.
More surprisingly, the melting point of the Co –Al–W ternary
alloy was found to be higher than those of commercial Ni-based
superalloys by 50 –150 /C14 C.[7] Given the narrow gap between
γ 0-solvus temperature and melting point among the extensively
developed Ni-based superalloys, the remarkable advantages in
the higher melting point of Co-rich alloys immediately render
them as potential candidates to be the new-generation high-
temperature structural materials.
Another notable advantage is that the L12-strengthened Co-rich
alloys display a minor degree of solute segregation during solidiﬁ-
cation, which is bene ﬁcial for single-crystal fabrication as well as
additive manufacturing.[14,15] Ni-based superalloys usually suffer
from freckle defects and misoriented grain formations in the pro-
duction of single-crystal/directionally solidiﬁed components as a
result of severe segregation tendency, [16,17] whereas the Co-rich
alloys generally display superior manufacturability toward
single-crystal/directionally solidi ﬁed components. Additive
manufacturing, which is essentially a repeated welding process,
can also be in ﬂuenced by the solute partitioning behavior during
solidiﬁcation. The localized solute segregation lowers the liquidus
temperature, which creates a solute-enriched liquid ﬁlm in the
mushy zone between the welding pool and the solidiﬁed metal.[18]
It will induce crack formation if the localized strain at the liquid
ﬁlms exceeds a critical threshold during solidiﬁcation. Due to the
severe segregation tendency for refractory solutes during the solid-
iﬁcation process, the Ni-based superalloys usually exhibit poor
weldability and are susceptible to crack formation. [19] In contrast,
the low solute partitioning extent in Co-rich alloys alleviates the
tendency for cracking formation during additive manufacturing.[20]
The L1 2-strengthened Co –Al–W alloys generally show a high
melting point and mild solute segregation; however, several lim-
itations still exist and impede their engineering applications
(Figure 2 ). The ﬁrst drawback of these alloys is that the

density of commercial Ni-based superalloys falls in the range
of 7.9 –8.5 g cm/C0 3. Therefore, exploring a feasible approach to
replace the high-density W is vital for the development of L1 2-
strengthened Co-rich alloys for engineering applications, which
will be addressed in the following section.
2.2. W-Free L1 2-Strengthened Co-Rich Alloys
W is conventionally regarded as an essential element for the sta-
bilization of the γ 0 phase in many Co-rich alloy systems. High
mass density is unfavorable for engineering applications where
the speciﬁc strength is crucial, especially for aerospace engineer-
ing. Considerable efforts have been devoted to the replacement
of the W element with lighter alloying additions without
compromising the γ–γ 0 microstructural stability and high-
temperature deformation resistance.
Preliminary results indicated that the stabilization of γ 0 phase
at high temperatures is feasible via alloying additions, namely γ 0-
stabilizing elements. [30,31] Molybdenum, a transition metal that
belongs to the same VB group as tungsten, is a promising can-
didate for the replacement. Up to 4 at% W can be substituted by
Mo in the Co –Al–W-based alloys without degrading the γ–γ 0
dual-phase microstructure, whereas excess substitutions led to
the formation of a needle-shaped Co 3Mo phase with the D0 19
structure.[32,33] Later, Makineni et al. [29,34] found that Nb addi-
tions help to stabilize the γ 0 phase in the quaternary Co –
Al–Mo–Nb alloys by forming a cuboidal γ 0-Co3(Al, Mo, Nb) phase
(Figure 4a). Although the γ 0-solvus temperature was only 866 /C14 C,
plus the tendency for the phase decomposition from the L1 2 into
D019 structure when isothermally heat treated at 800 /C14 C for the
Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb alloy, it is still the ﬁrst attempt in designing
W-free L12-strengthened Co-rich alloys. Ta addition also demon-
strated a similar L1 2 stabilization effect as the Nb element
in promoting the γ 0 formation in Co –Al–Mo-based alloys
(Figure 4b).[35] These ﬁndings motivate a series of follow-up stud-
ies on searching for the γ 0 phase in the baseline Co-rich alloys via

complicated elemental interactions in the multicomponent alloy
systems are reported to increase the propensity for detrimental
intermetallic phase formations. [25] Moreover, the high-energy
grain-boundary region often acts as a preferential site for a het-
erogeneous intermetallic phase formation, which makes the
grain boundaries more vulnerable during long-term thermal
exposure.[41] In the Co–6Ta–6V alloy, the γ 0 phase was consumed
by the discontinuous C36-type Laves phase at 900 /C14 C, implying

phase at this temperature.[42] The formation of D019-type and B2-

cause severe embrittlement.[23,24] Furthermore, such intermetallic
phases also weaken the effectiveness of solid-solution strengthen-
ing and accelerate γ 0 coarsening by depleting refractory elements
(e.g., Mo, W, Nb, and Ta) from the γ matrix.[25] Consequently,
balancing the concentration of γ 0-stabilizing elements and broad-
ening the γ–γ 0 dual-phase region is vital for the development of
γ 0-reinforced Co-rich superalloys.
2.3. Ni-Containing Co-Rich Alloys
The γ–γ 0 dual-phase region is continuous between the Co –Al–W
and Ni–Al–W ternary alloy systems from the Co-rich side to the
Ni-rich side ( Figure 5 ).[45] The γ 0 stabilizing effect from the Ni
addition as the replacement of Co is not signi ﬁcant on a
per-atomic-percent basis (Figure 3b); however, the large solubil-
ity of Ni in the Co-rich alloys allows for a large proportion of Ni
alloying additions with notable contributions. For instance, in the
Co(–Ni)–Al–W alloys, replacing Co with Ni leads to a cumulative
/C25 200 /C14 C increment in the γ 0-solvus temperature. [31] Therefore,
substituting Co with the Ni addition is capable of broadening
the γ–γ 0 dual-phase region, providing an enlarged γ–γ 0 compo-
sitional choice and a more ﬂexible alloy design space. Given these
beneﬁcial effects, the alloy design space with various alloying
additions in the middle of conventional Co-based alloy and con-
ventional Ni-based alloy is promising. These alloys are also
referred to as the Co-rich high-entropy alloys in this section
because of the multicomponent characteristic in the extensively
modiﬁed alloys.
It has been reported that the metastable γ 0-Co3(Al, Mo, Nb)
phase can be stabilized via various alloying additions. Alloying
30 at% Ni increased the γ 0-solvus temperature to 990 /C14 C and
the γ 0 volume fraction to 76% (800 /C14 C).[34,46] In another modiﬁed
alloy containing Ta, an additional 2 at% Ti content further
increased the γ 0-solvus temperature to 1066 /C14 C.[35] The γ 0 phase
was stable without any phase transformation after isothermal
aging at 950 /C14 C for 100 h with the incorporation of Ni and Ti addi-
tions. By comparison, the metastable γ 0-Co3(Al, Mo, Nb) phase
decomposed into D0 19 phase and γ-Co phase at 800 /C14 Cf o r
merely 35 h. [29] In the ternary Co –Ta–V alloys, the γ 0 precipi-
tates in the Co –6Ta–6V alloy were consumed by a discontinu-
ous C36-Co 3(Ta, V) Laves phase at grain boundaries after 2 h at
900 /C14 C.[42] Surprisingly, 10 at% of Ni combined with a small
amount of Al and Ti content resulted in a stable γ–γ 0 dual-phase
microstructure at 850 /C14 C up to 1000 h in the Co –10Ni–5Al–3Ta–
3V–2Ti–0.04B alloy. [47]
Apart from the γ 0 stabilizing effect, the incorporation of Ni
also manipulates elemental partitioning by shifting solute distri-
bution from the Co-dominate toward the Ni-controlled manner.
In the ternary Co –Al–W alloys, W preferentially partitions to the
γ 0 precipitates with a partitioning coef ﬁcient value (the ratio of
atomic concentration of the element in the γ 0 and γ phases)
of 2.60. [45] Alloying additions of Ni progressively suppress W
from entering γ 0 and monotonically decrease the partitioning
coefﬁcient values of W to 0.84 as the substitution of 70 at%
Co with Ni. [45] The reversed partitioning behavior suggests that
W switches from a γ 0-stabilizing element among Co-rich alloys to
a γ-forming element in the Ni-rich alloys. Additions of refractory
elements in the Co –Al–Mo-based alloy also exhibit similar par-
titioning trends after alloyed with Ni addition, that is, Mo and Ta
are drawn to the γ phase, resulting in a decreased partitioning
coefﬁcient from 3.11 to 1.53 for Mo and 26.43 to 15.78 for
Ta.[35] The interactions between Ni and other alloying additions
give rise to the concentrated refractory additions (e.g., W, Mo,
Nb, and Ta) in the γ-Co matrix, which increases the effectiveness
of solid-solution strengthening as well as the deformation resis-
tance at elevated temperatures. [25]
The γ 0 precipitates in particle-hardened superalloys are known
to grow and coarsen at elevated temperatures, which eventually
leads to mechanical property degradation with a prolonged time.
Therefore, a stable γ/γ 0 microstructure with adequate resistance
toward particle coarsening or, in other words, a minimized coars-
ening rate, is preferred in the development of L1 2-strengthened
metallic materials for high-temperature applications. Extensive
efforts have been devoted to studying the γ 0 coarsening behaviors
in Ni-based superalloys [48]; however, due to the very young age
since the discovery of the γ 0 phase in Co-rich alloys, the

By comparison, the Co –Al–W-based alloy demonstrates a tem-
perature advantage of /C25 210 /C14 C over binary Ni –Al alloy with an
equivalent particle coarsening rate (Figure 6). As the solute dif-
fusion in the matrix phase generally controls the particle coars-
ening, a detailed examination of the matrix composition is
essential in unveiling the origin of the composition-related coars-
ening resistance. Given the enriched Ni and Co element in the
matrix phase, a simpli ﬁed model is ﬁrst utilized by assuming a
binary FCC-structured binary Ni –Co alloy as a substitution for
the matrix phase. Previous studies indicated that solute diffusiv-
ities gradually decreased toward the Co-rich end in the Ni –Co
binary alloys.[64] As a result of the reduced diffusivities, the par-
ticle coarsening resistance can be enhanced by the concentrated
Co content in the matrix phase. It should also be noted that the
unfavorable effect from the Ni addition in accelerating solute dif-
fusion can be partially offset by the enhanced γ 0 phase stability,
permitting higher γ 0-solvus temperature and higher γ 0 phase vol-
ume fraction at elevated temperatures. Still, it is important to
point out that the simpliﬁed model might overlook certain critical
factors in controlling the particle coarsening, for example, the
interface character, including the lattice mis ﬁt and the interfacial
solute segregations. Atom probe analyses indicated that Mo seg-
regation at the γ/γ 0 interface resulted in a decreased interfacial
free energy among a multicomponent Co-rich alloy, leading to a
reduced particle coarsening rate. [25] Moreover, a lattice mis ﬁt
value close to zero also contributes to a reduced lattice mis ﬁt
strain, which enhances the coarsening resistance. [65] Although
the origins underlying the superior coarsening resistance are still
not fully understood at the present time, these preliminary
results indicate a promising outlook for Co-rich alloys to be devel-
oped as new-generation structural materials with an improved
microstructural stability.

3. Conclusion
Alloys with high-strength and high-temperature capabilities
are not merely of academic interest to probe the composition –
structure–processing–property relationship, but also of great
engineering interest for material persons to design new-
generation structural materials with broader industrial applica-
tions. Although inspiring progress and attractive achievements
have been made during the past years, the development of
L12-strengthened Co-rich multicomponent alloys for structural
applications is still at an early stage, and more fundamental stud-
ies and systematic understanding are in urgent demand. Notably,
the following aspects are certainly worthy of detailed investiga-
tion: 1) thermodynamic-guided alloy design via the calculation
of phase diagram (CALPHAD) methods; 2) grain-boundary
characteristics and their correlations with mechanical properties;
and 3) effective hardening of directionally solidi ﬁed and/or sin-
gle-crystal components for engineering applications at elevated
temperatures.
First of all, one of the major challenges among multicompo-
nent-alloy design is the extraordinarily large compositional space
and numerous elemental combinations.[70] CALPHAD-type tools
have been widely used in the design and development of Ni-
based superalloys for advancing their high-temperature proper-
ties.[71] The establishment of the CALPHAD thermodynamic
database requires a careful evaluation of phase equilibria of
the binary and ternary subsets of major alloying additions.
The L12-Co3(Al, W) phase was not present in any evaluated ther-
modynamic database by the time it was discovered, and therefore
CALPHAD tools were not applicable at the initial time. As the
L12-type Co 3(Al, W) phase is a ternary intermetallic phase with-
out extending to any binary subsets (Co –Al, Co –W, and Al –W),
the Co –Al–W ternary system should be considered as the start
point for thermodynamic evaluations. Therefore, the ternary
Co–Al–W system[72] and quaternary Co–Ni–Al–W system[31] have
been carefully assessed to integrate the CALPHAD method into
the Co-rich alloy design in recent years. Several bene ﬁcial
elements should also be considered in the thermodynamic eval-
uations of several Co-rich systems, [73] such as Ti, Ta, Nb, and Cr.
Thus, the relevant subsets contained in the Co –Al–W–Ni–Ti–Ta–
Nb–Cr systems need further evaluations to equip CALPHAD-
type tools with a higher accuracy in guiding the alloy design
to map out the compositional space where the phase equilibria
are of interest in the Co-rich compositional space.
Moreover, it has been of considerable interest for many metal-
lurgists to probe grain-boundary regions, including grain-
boundary structures, solute segregation at grain boundaries,
and grain-boundary migration. [74] Distinctly different composi-
tional and structural interfacial states from those at grain interior
can be stabilized at grain boundaries, whose presence plays a key
role in determining the grain boundary mobility, grain boundary
cohesion, and overall tensile ductility. [75–77] The situation
certainly becomes more complicated when it comes to multicom-
ponent alloys. [78] The unique grain-boundary characters are
expected to alter the microstructure evolutions as well as the asso-
ciated properties. For example, detrimental intermetallic phases
were frequently reported to precipitate out preferentially at grain
boundaries in Co-rich alloys: γ 0 phase was consumed by C36-
Laves phase at grain boundaries during the thermal exposure
at 900 /C14 C in the Co –6Ta–6V alloy [42];D 0 19-type and B2-type
phases were reported to form at grain boundaries in the Co –
Al–W-based alloys crept at 850 /C14 C, together with adjacent L1 2-
precipitation denuded zones along grain boundaries. [79] Apart
from these undesired formation of brittle intermetallic phases,
Al segregation at grain boundaries has also been identi ﬁed via
atom probe analyses in a multicomponent Co-rich L1 2 alloy,[80]
whose presence increased the susceptibility to the moisture-
induced environmental embrittlement with a ductility loss. [81,82]
In contrast, grain-boundary segregating elements, such as B, Zr,
and Hf, have been reported to improve the grain-boundary cohe-
sion and avoid brittle intergranular fracture in polycrystalline
alloys[83]; however, Bocchini et al. [79] noticed the accumulated
grain-boundary damage due to phase decomposition within
the crept B-doped and Zr-doped Co –Al–W-based alloys.
Therefore, more quantitative studies are required for targeting
grain-boundary regions of the polycrystalline Co-rich alloys.
An in-depth understanding of the grain-boundary region is
necessary to further optimize the properties of polycrystalline
Co-rich alloys.
If Co-rich alloys are developed for high-temperature applica-
tions, it is likely to be used in directionally solidi ﬁed and/or sin-
gle-crystal forms. The low segregation tendency among Co-rich
alloys stabilizes against freckling and misoriented grains forma-
tion during solidi ﬁcations, whose presences are detrimental to
the mechanical properties.[14] Therefore, Co-rich alloys are attrac-
tive to produce large-size directionally solidi ﬁed and/or single-
crystal metallic components. Moreover, due to the elimination
of grain boundaries in the single-crystal alloys, the

segregation to planar and linear faults has also been reported
recently via high-resolution scanning transmission electron
microscopy and atom probe analyses, [89,90] together with associ-

their high-temperature mechanical properties.[91,92] Therefore, to
further optimize the Co-rich multicomponent alloys for high-

the intrinsic factors controlling the creep and fatigue performan-

needed.

Engineering at City University of Hong Kong. He received his Ph.D. degree from City University of Hong
Kong in 2021. His research interests are focused on applying advanced characterization methods to
elucidate the composition –microstructure–processing–property relationship among precipitation-
strengthened high-temperature alloys and multicomponent intermetallic alloys.
Yilu Zhao is an associate professor at the School of Materials Science and Engineering at Harbin
Institute of Technology (Shenzhen). Her current research interests are focused on the characterization of
high-entropy alloys and advanced nuclear materials via transmission electron microscope and atom
probe tomography analysis.
Tao Yang is currently an assistant professor at the Department of Materials Science and Engineering at
City University of Hong Kong. His current work is primarily focused on the control of nanoprecipitation,
grain-boundary characters, and atomic structures using multiple state-of-the-art techniques, such as atom
probe tomography, high-resolution transmission electron microscope, and 3D printing.
Chain Tsuan Liu is a university distinguished professor at the City University of Hong Kong. He
previously worked at Oak Ridge National Laboratory as group leader of the Alloying Behavior and Design
Group. His research interests include physical metallurgy and mechanical behavior of alloys,
nanostructured materials, intermetallic compounds, and bulk amorphous alloys, microstructure and
phase transformation, alloy design of high-temperature structural materials, Ti-based alloys, and
innovative materials processing.
