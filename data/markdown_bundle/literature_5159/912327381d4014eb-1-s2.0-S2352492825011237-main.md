ABSTRACT
As a lightweight, high-temperature structural material, Ti
2
AlNb alloys have great application prospects for the 
new generation of high-performance engines. For Ti
2
AlNb alloy, achieving the synergy between the strength and 
plasticity is challenging and critical. In previous work, it was found that high strength-ductility Ti
2
AlNb alloy can 
be fabricated by spark plasma sintering (SPS) and rolling. However, reheating between the adjacent rolling is 
needed, which inevitably decreases the production efficiency. In this work, Ti
2
AlNb alloy sheet is fabricated 
using continuous rolling by cancelling the reheating procedure between the adjacent rolling, trying to increase its 
productivity. Results show that no crack forms on the sheets after a 70 % thickness reduction with two rolling 
passes. Microstructural analysis shows that numerous nano-size O phases form in the Ti
2
AlNb alloy, and the O 
phases near the dislocation aggregation region exhibit larger size. Tensile tests show that the Ti
2
AlNb sheet 
obtained in this work has both high tensile strength and good ductility. This work provides a referential value for 
fabricating high strength-ductility Ti
2
AlNb alloy.
1. Introduction
To meet the increasing demand for lightweight structures in aero -
space field, Ti
2
AlNb alloys, as a representative of new lightweight high- 
temperature structural materials, have attracted wide attention due to 
their excellent properties [1,2] . Ti
2
AlNb phase was discovered by 
Banerjee et al. [3] in 1988 in Ti
3
Al-based alloys. Compared with tradi -
tional Ti
3
Al-based alloys, Ti
2
AlNb alloys not only have excellent 
high-temperature mechanical properties, but also have good ductility at 
room temperature. Compared with Ni-based high-temperature alloys, 
which are most widely used in spacecraft currently, Ti
2
AlNb alloys have 
less than two-thirds the density. Thus, the unique combination of low 
density, high strength, high creep resistance and high oxidation resis -
tance of Ti
2
AlNb alloys makes them to be a strong contender for struc -
tural and functional components in demanding engineering applications 
[4 – 6] .
The improvement of the alloy properties usually depends on the 
change of their microstructure [7] . For Ti
2
AlNb alloy, it consists of three 
phases [8 – 10] : the matrix phase β /B2 (body-centred cubic structure), 
the main reinforcing phase O (orthorhombic structure), and the rein -
forcing phase α
2 
(hexagonal close-packed structure). Among them, B2 
phase has excellent ductility because it has numerous independent slip 
systems [11] . α
2 
phase has few active slip systems and is brittle [2] . O 
phase has better compatibility with B2 phase and it is the key component 
to improve the high temperature performance and reduce the brittleness 
of Ti
2
AlNb alloys [12,13] . The microstructure of Ti
2
AlNb alloys is very 
complex due to different proportions, shapes and sizes of the phases 
[14] .
In the last several decades, researchers have tried many methods to 
produce Ti
2
AlNb alloys with excellent properties [15 – 19] . Wu et al. [20]
obtained Ti
2
AlNb-based alloy sheets in single B2 phase region by heat 
treated it at high temperature for a long period of time. Subsequent 
mechanical tests revealed that the single B2 phase sheets had poor 
plasticity and an elongation smaller than 5 %. Through microstructural 
analysis, it was found that defect such as void tended to appear at the 
grain boundaries (GBs) when the sheets were in the single B2 phase 
region. The defect tend to develop and grow at the GB, therefore the 
bond between the grains was low. By contrast, our earlier study found 
that Ti
2
AlNb alloy prepared by spark plasma sintering (SPS) and rolling 
in the single B2 phase region owned both high strength and ductility 
[21] . The significant improvement in the sheets properties was attrib -
uted to the large amount of nano-sized O phases precipitated in the B2

2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of sheets
Ti-20.3Al-24.7 Nb (at%) pre-alloyed powders, fabricated by gas at -
omization, were used as starting material. As shown in Fig. 1 a, these 
powders were sintered in a ZLY-60 furnace (Jinzhou Hangxing Group 
Co., Ltd.). Detailed sintering process has been reported in our previous 
works [23,24] . After sintering, the compact was cooled to room tem -
perature with the furnace. Then, the compact was cut into tiny blocks 
(28 mm × 28 mm × 3 mm) by wire cutting. The parting agent (ZrO
2
) was 
then coated on the blocks ’ surface to prevent the adhesion between the 
pack and the block during rolling. The pack was made of 304 stainless 
steel. Two blocks were sealed in each pack. Before rolling, the pack was 
preheated to 1030 
◦
C and held for 15 min. The pack was then contin -
uously rolled in two passes using a Two-High Asymmetric Hot Rolling 
Mill machine. The total thickness reduction was approximately 70 %. 
The schematic of the rolling process is shown in Fig. 1 b.
2.2. Microstructural characterization and mechanical testing
The phase composition of the rolled sheets was characterized using a 
D8A25 X-ray diffractometer (XRD). Metallographic samples for electron 
backscatter diffraction detector (EBSD) observation were electrolyti -
cally polished with 6 vol% HClO ₄ + 34 vol% C ₄ H ₁₀ O + 60 vol% CH ₃ OH 
at 20 ℃ . Grains, textures and phases of the sheets were tested using an 
FEI-Quanta 200 F scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with 
EBSD. Specimens for Tolas F200X transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM) analysis were mechanically ground to within 50 μ m and then 
thinned using a GATAN 695 ion thinning equipment. The stress/strain 
state distribution of the sheets during the rolling was investigated using 
Abaqus finite element simulation software. Tensile specimens were cut 
from rolled sheets along the transverse direction (TD) and rolling di -
rection (RD), respectively. A schematic representation process of the 
tensile sample and its geometry is shown in Fig. 1 c. The room- 
temperature tensile properties were tested on an Instron-5569 elec -
tronic universal testing machine using a tensile speed of 0.36 mm/min.

3. Results and discussions
3.1. Morphology and appearance of the rolled sheets
Due to the rapid cooling of the sheets, cancelling the reheating 
process causes the sheet at a quite low temperature at the second rolling 
pass. Typically, Ti
2
AlNb alloys are difficult to deform at low tempera -
tures. Its ductility can not be ensured and it is prone to cracking during 
deformation. This problem can be overcome in this work.

Fig. 4 g-h show the geometrically necessary dislocation density (GND)

Fig. 5 shows the high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image and

The microstructural analysis of the continuous rolled sheet is carried 
out by TEM and the results are shown in Fig. 6 . Fig. 6 a-d show the TEM 
morphologies of the sheet, and a large number of nano-size particles 
precipitated inside the matrix. Fig. 6 e-f show the SAED patterns of the 
precipitated particles (point A) and the matrix (point B) in Fig. 6 b, 
respectively. It shows that the precipitated particles are the O phases and 
the matrix is the B2 phase. The high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) 
morphology of the sheet ( Fig. 6 h) reveals that besides the lattice fringes 
of the matrix phase, there are some internal regions with significantly 
different lattice fringes. The fast fourier transform (FFT) is performed on 
them separately to obtain two sets of patterns. After calibration with the 
standard PDF card, it is confirmed that the matrix phase is the B2 phase 
( Fig. 6 g), and the region with different lattice stripes is the O phase 
( Fig. 6 i). These results further confirm the conclusions obtained in Fig. 5 . 
Fig. 6 a-d show that there are some regions with a large number of 
aggregated dislocations and these dislocations are not uniformly 
distributed. Usually, the sheet is subjected to uneven strain during 
rolling. The region with larger deformation will produce stress con -
centration, causing dislocations to gather in local stress concentration 
region, forming dislocation entanglement. As shown in Fig. 6 , the O 
phases near the dislocation aggregation region show slatterns with 
larger sizes ( Fig. 6 b-c). By contrast, the O phases away from the dislo -
cation aggregation region show acicular or lamellar morphology 
( Fig. 6 a). According to classical kinetic theory of atomic diffusion [46] , 
this can be attributed to the greater atomic activity near the dislocation 
aggregation region, where is easily diffused. This mechanism can pro -
vide a key theoretical basis for the subsequent optimisation of me -
chanical properties.

3.4. Mechanical properties of the rolled sheets
To evaluate the tensile properties of Ti
2
AlNb sheets in this work, 
room temperature tensile tests are carried out along TD and RD di -
rections and the results are shown in Fig. 10 . Fig. 10 a shows the engi -
neering stress-strain curves of the specimens. The ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) and elongation (EL) of the specimen along the two di -
rections are 1113.66 MPa/1100.94 MPa and 9.35 %/7.92 %, respec -
tively. The two curves also show quite obvious plastic deformation 
characteristics. It is worth noting that the tensile strengths of the spec -
imens are similar in both directions, but the elongation along TD is 
larger than that of the RD direction. This can be attributed to the 
anisotropy of the specimens due to the preferential orientation of the 
crystal slip system [29] . As is well-known, B2 phase is the main 
contributor to the plasticity in Ti
2
AlNb alloys. In this work, the volume 
fraction of the nano-size O phase precipitated in the sheets obtained by 
continuous rolling is very small, thus only the slip coefficient of the B2 
phase is considered. From previous studies, it can be found that there are 
three main slip mechanisms in the B2 phase: < 111 > {110} slip, 
< 111 > {112} slip and < 111 > {123} slip. Among them, < 111 >
{123} slip has a high critical shear stress at room temperature, so the 
deformation mechanisms activated in the B2 phase at room temperature 
are mainly < 111 > {110} slip and < 111 > {112} slip [30] . As can be 
obtained from the pole figures ( Fig. 10 c), the rolled sheets have obvious 
selective orientations on the {110} crystal planes, and the orientations 
on the {110} crystal planes are mainly concentrated in TD, which forms 
a strong < 111 > {110} texture. Therefore, the sheet is prone to activate 
< 111 > {110} slip along TD and has better plasticity. Fig. 10 b shows 
the comparison of the sheet properties obtained in this work and with 
other literatures [2,24,31 – 44] , especially for TiAl alloys, Ni-based

decreases, which can be attributed to the higher residual stresses in the 
sheet. The elongation of the sheets does not change significantly. 
Possible reason is that the reheating process promotes the appear and 
growth of defects such as microvoid in the sheets [45] , and its effect on 
the ductility of the sheet is almost equal to the effect of material 
strengthening caused by dislocations. The above-mentioned results 
indicate that the continuous rolling process is a promising method to 
fabricate Ti

deformation during the test. The microscopic morphology of the fracture 
surface is shown in Fig. 11 b, which mainly shows transgranular fracture 
pattern. Only a tiny region presents intergranular fracture. Fig. 11 c- 
d show the enlarged morphology of the two regions, respectively. The 
fracture surface in the intergranular fracture region has a well-defined 
river pattern ( Fig. 11 c), and the fracture surface in the transgranular 
fracture region has a large number of dimples ( Fig. 11 d). The magnified 
morphology of the dimples in Fig. 11 e shows large depth. This result can 
help to explain the high ductility of the sheets.
Fig. 12 shows the fracture morphology of the continuously rolled 
sheets tested at room temperature along RD. Fig. 12 a shows the 
macroscopic morphology of the fracture cross section. Similar to Fig. 11 , 
the elongation of the sheets along RD is high. Thus, necking is easily 
recognized from Fig. 12 a. As shown in Fig. 12 b, the fracture surface is 
inhomogeneous, and both intergranular fracture and transgranular 
fracture patterns are recognized. River patterns as well as smooth grain 
boundaries are observed in Fig. 12 c. It is noteworthy that most of the 
region shows transgranular fracture pattern ( Fig. 12 d). The entire frac -
ture surface is covered with dimples ( Fig. 12 e), which corresponds well 
with good ductility of the sheet.
4. Conclusions
In this work, Ti
2
AlNb alloy sheets with good formability and excel -
lent mechanical properties were successfully prepared using continuous 
rolling process. The microstructure of the sheets was discussed in detail 
by SEM, EBSD, XRD and TEM. The stress/strain state of the sheet was 
analyzed by multiscale finite element analysis. The main conclusions are 
as follows: 
1. The Ti
2
AlNb compact prepared by SPS has good plasticity at high 
temperatures, no crack appears on the compact after two rolling 
passes with a total thickness reduction of 70 %.
2. During continuous rolling in the single B2 phase region, a large 
number of nano-sized O phases precipitate inside the sheets. Due to 
the greater atomic activity in the dislocation aggregation region, the 
O phases nearby are coarsened.
3. Due to the frictional resistance between the sheets and the rolls, the 
sheets are subjected to different stress states in different regions, but 
they are all three-way compressive stresses. After rolling, the sheets 
exhibits thinning along the ND direction and elongation along the RD 
and TD directions.
4. The continuous rolling process improves the rolling efficiency of the 
sheets, without sacrificing the mechanical properties of the sheets.
