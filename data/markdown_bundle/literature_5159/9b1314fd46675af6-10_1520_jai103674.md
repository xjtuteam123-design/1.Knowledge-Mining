Modeling Creep-Fatigue Deformation of Ni-Base Superalloys
Using Crystal Viscoplasticity
ABSTRACT :Crystal viscoplasticity enables one to correctly capture the anisotropic inelastic behavior of
single crystal and directionally solidiﬁed alloys as well as the microstructural sensitivity in polycrystalline
alloys. This paper reviews several physics-based crystal viscoplasticity models that have been established
to predict the deformation response of Ni-base superalloys. The attributes of crystal viscoplasticity models
necessary for predicting the creep-fatigue deformation are identiﬁed. Several new developments in consti-
tutive modeling of Ni-base superalloys are discussed.
KEYWORDS: crystal plasticity, Ni-base superalloy, deformation mechanisms, ﬁnite element analysis
Introduction
Modeling the deformation response of Ni-base superalloys used in hot section components with the aim of
predicting crack formation is challenging for several reasons. The response of these components depends
on the cyclic loading and temperature cycles that often include extended dwells, and the interaction
between environmental reactions and diffusion that affect the local composition and microstructure. These
superalloys are often either a single crystal or directionally solidiﬁed /H20849DS/H20850, and therefore both the elastic
and plastic response is highly anistropic. In addition, the heterogeneity of the microstructure includes local
crystal orientation of DS alloys and distributions of the precipitate phase coupled with compositional
variations between the dendritic and interdendritic regions.
This is a multiscale problem as illustrated in Fig. 1. The elastic response depends on atomic arrange-
ment and orientation while the inelastic response depends on the resistance of dislocation movement
through the microstructure. The direction and planes of dislocation movements depend on the lower length
scales while the coherent /H9253
/H11032precipitates provide resistance to shearing. In DS and polycrystalline alloys,
the grain boundaries provide further resistance to dislocation movement. Ideally, all of these inﬂuences in
dislocation movement should be captured, either explicitly or in a phenomenological manner.

Speciﬁc Flow Rules and Evolution Equations

vastly different loading rates accurately, often over a range of temperatures, to simulate conditions of
typical applications. Most CVP models employ a ﬂow rule that relates the shear strain rate on each slip
system to the viscous overstress deﬁned by the resolved shear stress minus the back stress and threshold
stress. The speciﬁc functional form is aimed to capture the experimentally observed response over the
required temperature range of applicability for a given state of the microstructure which is tracked by the
internal state variables /H20849ISVs/H20850.

Directionally Solidiﬁed and Polycrystalline Alloys
For DS and polycrystalline alloys, the grain interactions play a critical role in the deformation response.
The polycrystalline alloy is modeled by applying a deformation history to a representative volume element
containing several grains. For capturing just the macroscopic response of polycrystalline alloys while
keeping the simulation time small, the Taylor constraint can be used to approximate the intergranular
interactions /H208519/H20852. For the latter, several single element simulations with different orientations corresponding
to the orientation distribution are run assuming all of the elements experience the same deformation and
the stress depends on the aggregate response. For DS alloys, 30 grains were found to be sufﬁcient to
represent the macroscopic response /H208519/H20852. On the other hand, to explicitly model the grain interactions
including size and morphology effects, the individual grains need to be represented in a ﬁnite element
mesh using CVP . With a meshed microstructure, the inﬂuence of intergranular constraints on the local
response can be captured to obtain a real picture of the internal distribution of inelastic strain and stress
state /H2085134,35/H20852and the model parameters for DS and polycrystalline alloys can be reﬁned /H208519/H20852. The predicted
response is always considerably more heterogeneous even in an alloy that is macroscopically isotropic.
The intergranular constraints strongly contribute to the kinematic hardening behavior /H2085136/H20852. In addition,
these intergranular constraints as well as the presence of large carbides or other nonhomogeneous micro-
structural features suggest that the driver for crack formation is related to the localized accumulation of
ratchet strain /H2085137,38/H20852that may be enhanced under creep-fatigue conditions.

orientation for PW A 1480 /H2085139/H20852, which is clearly observed in the uniaxial response shown in Fig. 6. This
leads to formation of internal stresses due to the intergranular constraints even when stresses remain below

Since CVP formulations are rooted in continuum mechanics, they can readily be adopted and used in ﬁnite
element codes, for example through User MA Terial subroutines /H20849UMA Ts/H20850in ABAQUS /H2085140/H20852. The UMA T

non-isothermal/H20850, current values of the ISVs, and the deformation gradient and time increment. It then
returns updated values of the stress, ISVs, and the Jacobian matrix

Parameter Determination
Deriving and hypothesizing the theoretical relationships is only one aspect of implementation. The other,
and perhaps more challenging, is the determination of the parameters. Most of the physics-based param-
eters cannot be measured directly. Hence, an inverse approach must be employed. For isothermal models,
this is usually feasible through a judicious iterative process. However, if one desires a temperature-
dependent model, then the challenge of this task increases by an order of magnitude.
In order to calibrate both the octahedral and cube slip systems, experiments must be conducted in
different orientations. First, the deformation response when only the octahedral slip systems are activated
are conducted and used to calibrate those parameters. For single crystal alloys, the /H20855001/H20856orientation is
suitable and for DS alloys, the longitudinal orientation will work. To determine the cube slip parameters,
an orientation where the Schmid factors are considerably higher on the cube slip systems compared to the
octahedral system, such as /H20855111/H20856, needs to be selected. For DS alloys, loading transverse to the grain
growth direction will activate both cube and octahedral slip.
Experiments need to be conducted at multiple temperatures since the deformation mechanisms are
highly temperature dependent. If the aim is to use the model for low cycle creep-fatigue, then at each
temperature cyclic loadings should be applied at different strain rates with sufﬁcient inelastic strain to
clearly identify yielding and hardening response. An example of a calibration experimental program is
shown in Fig. 11. This protocol will often work with Ni-base superalloys that are cyclically stable, but may
need to be modiﬁed for materials that exhibit considerable cyclic hardening or softening. An iterative
process coupled with using optimization software such as Isight /H2085152/H20852or ModelCenter /H2085153/H20852is used to
identify the material parameters /H2085128,54/H20852.

Summary and Prospects
Creep-fatigue and thermomechanical fatigue life prediction requires highly accurate cyclic deformation
models. The development and improvements in CVP models will enable the engineer and designer to
predict the inﬂuence of the material microstructure directly in the life analysis. Much progress has been
made in the past decade. Enhancements to capture the aging and changes in response due to temperature
exposures and cycling, such as directional coarsening, are needed. Effort must continue to be placed on
reducing the time and effort to calibrate these physics-based and microstructure-sensitive constitutive
models. Protocols for calibration, which may include a hierarchy of experiments coupled with lower length
scale simulations, need to be established. Improvements in the efﬁciency of their implementation including
code parallelization continue to be needed. Moreover, CVP simulations may be used to develop inﬂuence
functions or train artiﬁcial neural networks for including microstructure-sensitive parameters in more
efﬁcient reduced-order, homogenized viscoplasticity models.
