Abstract
In this study, a comprehensive investigation was conducted to explore the microstructural evolution,
texture characteristics, and mechanical properties of extruded Mg-9Gd-3Nd-1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr alloys
with varying extrusion ratios(ERs). Moreover, the high-temperature mechanical properties of the
alloy and analyzed the corresponding fracture mechanisms were investigated. Theﬁndings unveiled a
reﬁnement in grain size and an improvement in homogeneity with increasing ER. Such reﬁnement
coincided with the disruption of coarse Mg
5(Gd, Nd, Zn) eutectic phases, facilitating dynamic
recrystallization. As a result, the extruded alloy manifested small grains, signiﬁcant orientation
difference in grain boundaries, and a weakened texture. In particular, the average grain size in the
extrusion direction (ED) diminished to 13.29μm (ER4), 9.62 μm (ER16), and 4.05μm (ER25). And
the ED texture of the ER4 alloy featured a strong(0001) basal bimodal pattern, whereas the ER16 and
ER25 alloys showcased a random texture. Furthermore, the ER25 alloy displayed notable work
hardening at temperatures of 200°C or below, exerting a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on its stress– strain
curve. The ultimate tensile strength(UTS) of the alloy remained consistently above 275.09 MPa,
demonstrating ductile fracture characteristics characterized by numerous dimples at temperatures of
200 °C or higher. The study identiﬁed the second phase as the primary contributor to enhancing the
high-temperature tensile characteristics of the alloy. In summary, this research developed a unique
high-strength Mg-9Gd-3Nd-1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr alloy characterized byﬁne grain and texture strengthen-
ing. The alloy exhibited remarkable stability at temperatures of 250°C or lower, making it a promising
candidate for applications in high-temperature applications.
1. Introduction
Magnesium alloy is the lightest commercial metal for structural applications due to its signiﬁcant characteristics,
known for its high speciﬁc strength, speciﬁc stiffness, and ease of processing. It is widely used in aviation,
automotive, and electronics industries[1, 2]. However, magnesium alloys have shortcomings in strength and
heat resistance compared to steel, aluminum, and copper alloys, posing challenges in meeting higher strength
requirements and limiting their widespread applications[3]. Therefore, solving the problem of improving the
strength of magnesium alloys has become the most concerned issue, leading researchers to shift their attention
to rare earth magnesium alloys[4– 12]. Mg-RE alloys are preferred for their notably higher yield strength and
superior heat resistance compared to conventional magnesium alloys.
Previous research indicates that introducing heavy rare earth elements into magnesium alloys, particularly
the Mg-Gd and Mg-Y series, has the potential to enhance both creep resistance and mechanical properties at
elevated temperatures [13]. Notably, Mg-RE alloys with elevated Gd or Y content have demonstrated the ability
to withstand service temperatures of up to 250°C, exhibiting improved aging strengthening and higher tensile
properties in speciﬁc investigations [14, 15]. The remarkable combination of low density and impressive
strength, whether at room temperature or high temperatures, has piqued substantial interest in Mg-Gd alloy.
Nevertheless, the relatively high production costs associated of Mg-Gd alloy with high Gd content limit its

Notably, the comprehensive properties of wrought magnesium alloy products, achieved through processes like

effects of various ERs on the microstructure and mechanical properties, laying the groundwork for developing
high-performance wrought magnesium alloys. An in-depth analysis of the enhancement mechanisms through
plastic deformation provides valuable insights and establishes a solid foundation for practical applications.
2. Experimental procedure
In this study, the Mg-9Gd-3Nd-1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr alloys were initially prepared through the casting process, and
their elemental composition were determined using the ICP-MS method, with the results presented in table1.
According to an earlier study[18], the as-cast alloy underwent a homogenization process at 525°C for 10 h,
followed by a cooling phase in hot water at 70°C to enhance its homogeneity. Subsequently, the ingots were
turned form a diameter of 100 mm to 80 mm, and the oxide skin and casting defects were removed from the
material’s surface. These segments were then introduced into an extrusion machine, where they were
transformed into bars with diameters of 40 mm, 20 mm, and 16 mm, all extruded at a rate of 2 m/min. The
resulting samples were labeled as ER4, ER16, and ER25 alloys, respectively. Additionally, the failure modes of the
alloy under different temperature tension are analyzed in terms of material composition, microstructure, and
properties with the help of optical microscopy(OM), x-ray diffraction(XRD), and scanning electronic
microscope (SEM), as shown inﬁgure 1.

Typically, the strength of the basal texture weakens with the addition of RE elements in magnesium alloys,
particularly in wrought magnesium alloys. In this study, the basal texture density of the extruded alloy(0001)
with an ER of 25 measures 2.530, which is weaker than that observed in conventional magnesium alloys. This
phenomenon is consistent withﬁndings by Ball[22], who observed a weakening of the basal texture in WE54
alloy, attributing it to second-phase particles in the alloy that impede the movement of dislocations. These
particles readily deform in the vicinity, consequently promoting the nucleation of recrystallized grains and
contributing to a more random grain orientation, resulting in the weakening of the basal texture. Hutchinson
[23] proposed that RE can alter the critical shear stress of various slip systems, activate non-basal slip systems,
and introduce diversity in the deformation mode. This mechanism serves to weaken the basal texture in wrought
magnesium alloys. Furthermore, Bohlen[24] suggested that owing to the substantial disparity in the atomic radii
of RE and Mg, the diffusion rate of RE within the magnesium matrix is prolonged. This effect exerts signiﬁcant
drag on the migration of grain boundaries, subsequently inﬂuencing the growth of grains with differing
orientations. However, following the extrusion process, the texture strength of Mg-RE alloys tends to increase,
which may be attributed to the exposure of coarse grains. This effect is ampliﬁed when coarse deformed or
recrystallized grains with speciﬁc orientations develop.

3.4. Mechanical properties and high-temperature fracture mechanism of extruded alloy
Tactical missile cabins and various other equipment often operate in short-duration high-temperature
environments with working temperatures exceeding 200°C. Consequently, materials used for these cabins need
to possess speciﬁc characteristics that make them both heat-resistant and high-strength within a brief
timeframe. This is crucial to meet the strength requirements of certain components within the military industry.
Prior literature has offered valuable insights into the microstructure development during the extrusion process
of the ER25 alloy[18]. Building upon this body of knowledge, the present study subjected extruded Mg-9Gd-
3Nd-1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr alloys to high-temperature tensile tests.
Figure 10 illustrates the stress– strain curve for the ER25 alloy at various temperatures. Following extrusion
with an ER25, the UTS, YS, and EL of the homogenized alloy reach 369 MPa, 292 MPa, and 11.44%, respectively,
indicating a noticeable enhancement in the alloy’s strength. At lower temperatures, such as 150°C, the alloy
exhibits a minor softening effect, reﬂecting a short duration of work hardening and softening before achieving a
stable equilibrium state. As the tensile temperature increases, the time required to attain stability extends, and
softening gradually leads to increased elongation. Work hardening at temperatures at or below 200°C effectively
regulates the alloy’s stress– strain curve and maintains its UTS above 275.09 MPa. In comparison to widely used
high-temperature magnesium alloys like WE54 and WE43, this alloy demonstrates good high-temperature
mechanical properties. However, when the temperature exceeds 250°C (with the highest temperature in this
study being 300 °C), the stress immediately drops after reaching its peak value. The YS trend with tensile
temperature closely mirrors that of UTS, with the ultimate tensile strength remaining near 200 MPa.
Nevertheless, elongation steadily increases as the tensile temperature rises, particularly when it surpasses 200°C.
At 300 °C, the maximum elongation reaches 77%, indicating that the extruded bar exhibits higher tensile
strength at 250 °C or lower temperatures, while signiﬁcant softening occurs above 250°C. The reduction in the
spacing between the most closely packed plane and the sub-closely packed plane with increasing tensile
temperature can be attributed, in part, to the increased amplitude of Mg atoms. As the critical shear stress

Overall, the deformation characteristics of magnesium alloys at high temperatures are primarily inﬂuenced
by the movement of dislocations within the grains and the behavior of phases at grain boundaries. Among these,
the interaction between compounds at the grain boundaries stands out as a critical factor. Commercial
magnesium alloys, such as AZ31 and ZK60, are known to exhibit poor high-temperature tensile properties
[25, 26]. This limitation primarily stems from the low thermal stability of grain boundary compounds in these
alloys, which fails to restrain dislocation slip and climb effectively. In the temperature range of 200°C to 250 °C,
there were observed brief river patterns on the cleavage surface, with their size diminishing as the tensile
temperature increased. Simultaneously, the number of tearing edges and dimples increased. At 300°C, the
cracks along the grain boundaries increased, while the cracks within the grains decreased signiﬁcantly. This
observation indicates a signiﬁcant reduction in the strength of the grain boundaries and, consequently, a decline
in the alloy’s overall strength at 300°C. The fracture surface of the alloy exhibited the typical characteristics of
ductile fracture. The formation of dimples typically requires the activation of multiple slip systems, which is a
clear indication that theﬁne-grained extruded alloy experiences non-basal dislocation slip. This can be

4. Conclusion
In this study, the novel Mg-9Gd-3Nd-1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr magnesium alloy was successfully extruded into bars with
diameters of 40 mm, 20 mm, and 16 mm. Extensive investigations were conducted to assess the inﬂuence of ERs
on the alloy’s microstructure and mechanical properties. The results, including insights into the deformation
mechanisms, offer valuable experimental and theoretical insights, paving the way for the reliable use of the alloy
in challenging service conditions:
(1) Extrusion with an ER of 4 resulted in an average grain size of approximately 14 μm for the Mg-9Gd-3Nd-
1Zn-1Sn-0.5Zr alloy, leading to signiﬁcant improvements in both strength and plasticity. As the ER
increased from 4 to 16, the degree of dynamic recrystallization intensiﬁed, leading to a noticeable reduction
in the number of large grains within the alloy and a more uniform grain size distribution. When the ER
reached 25, the dissociation plateau and secondary cracks on the fracture surface diminished, giving way to
the appearance of more tearing edges and dimples, characteristic of ductile fracture.
