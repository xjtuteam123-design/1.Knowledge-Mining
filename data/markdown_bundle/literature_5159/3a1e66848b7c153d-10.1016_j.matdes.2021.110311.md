abstract
Refractory complex concentrated alloys are an emerging class of materials that attracts attention due to
their stability and performance at high temperatures. In this study, we investigate the variations in the
mechanical and thermal properties across a broad compositional space for the refractory MoNbTaTi qua-
ternary using high-throughput ab-initio calculations and experimental characterization. For all the prop-
erties surveyed, we note a good agreement between our modeling predictions and the experimentally
measured values. We reveal the particular role of molybdenum (Mo) to achieve high strength when in
high concentration. We trace the origin of this phenomenon to a shift from metallic to covalent bonding
when the Mo content is increased. Additionally, a mechanistic, dislocation-based description of the yield
strength further explains such high strength due to a combination of high bulk and shear moduli, accom-
panied by the relatively small size of the Mo atom compared to the other atoms in the alloy. Our analysis
of the thermodynamics properties shows that regardless of the composition, this class of quaternary
alloys shows good stability and low sensitivity to temperature. Taken together, these results pave the
way for the design of new high-performance refractory alloys beyond the equimolar composition found
in high-entropy alloys.

and thermal environments. Of interest in this work, refractory
complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) have recently attracted
attention due to superior high-temperature stability and perfor-
mance as compared to commonly used nickel-based superalloys
[1–3]. RCCAs, also sometimes referred to as refractory multi-
principal element alloys (RMPEA), usually consist of three or more
principal elements presenting a large composition space for prop-
erty dependence to be explored. They may also contain non-
principal elements, generally added to further improve properties
of the principal composition, which can add further complexity
to property design and optimization.
The fundamental metallurgical concept behind RCCAs and
refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), a sub-class of RCCAs, is
to promote the entropic contribution to the total Gibbs energy pro-
vided by the complex composition and thereby improve the stabil-
ity of disordered solid solutions relative to intermetallic phases,
especially at high temperatures. The general consideration of
refractory elements in these alloys, typically elements from groups
IV-VI in the periodic table, is meant to design alloys with very high
melting temperatures and superior high-temperature resistance.
The microstructures of these alloys usually exhibit a body-
centered-cubic (BCC) crystal structure, although other phases such
as the B2 phase [4,5] or Laves phase [6,7] have been reported. Of
particular interest, RCCAs based on refractory elements such as
molybdenum (Mo), niobium (Nb), tantalum (Ta), and tungsten
(W) have been shown to retain high strength up to fairly high tem-
peratures (>1600 /C176C) [8]. Other alloys that include titanium (Ti),
zirconium (Zr), hafnium (Hf), or vanadium (V) as alternative com-
positional elements have also shown good mechanical strength at
high temperatures [9,10,7,11–15]. Speciﬁcally, Coury and cowork-
ers [7,11] showed that the near-equiatomic NbTaTi-based quater-
nary and quinary alloys exhibited good strength at high
temperatures. They also reported that alloys containing chromium
(Cr) in place of Nb or Mo had much higher strength and a greater
retention of strength at high temperatures. The complete origin
of strength in these alloys is still under investigation, but it is
believed to be dependent on a combination of an athermal (from
edge-dislocation glide) [14] and a thermally-activated (from
screw-dislocation glide) [16] component of the yield strength.
Generally, one can expect screw-dislocation mobility to dictate
yield strength in BCC alloys [17,18], however, it has been recently
shown that in MPEAs with high atomic-size mismatch (i.e., where
the radii of the elemental constituents differ signiﬁcantly), edge-
dislocation mobility can become greatly restricted, allowing it to
overtake screw-dislocation mobility as the limiting factor in the
yield strength. Because of this competition, it is expected that com-
positional changes in RMPEA components, especially for elements
with atomic sizes signiﬁcantly differing from the mean atomic vol-
ume in the alloy, could have substantial effects on the yield
strength behavior of the alloy.
The majority of the aforementioned studies have primarily
focused on mechanical properties of RHEAs with near-equiatomic
compositions. Considerably less is known about the variation in
these mechanical properties for non-equiatomic compositions, or
about the associated thermal properties such as the coefﬁcient of
thermal expansion or speciﬁc heat. Thermal properties are espe-
cially important when considering these classes of alloys for ther-
mal barrier applications. Currently, there is an increasing effort to
evaluate compositional dependencies of RCCA properties through
high-throughput experimental techniques such as laser-beam
directed energy deposition (LB-DED) additive manufacturing
(AM) [19–22] for instance. These methods can be costly and difﬁ-
cult to conduct, sometimes hampered by inhomogeneities in spec-
imen microstructure such as porosity [23,24] or uneven grain-size
distribution [25]. On the other hand, computational models such as
density functional theory (DFT) have proven to be predictive for
quantifying fundamental thermo-mechanical material properties
as a function of composition. For instance, Tong and coworkers
[26–28] used DFT simulations to investigate the effect of alloying
elements on the variation in the elastic properties and electronic
structures for various RHEAs with near-equiatomic compositions.
Other groups [29–33] have used DFT simulations to generate data
sets needed to ﬁt interatomic potentials for molecular-dynamics
simulations of RHEA systems. In both cases, the good agreement
between simulation results and experimental data provide cre-
dence to using DFT tools to predict properties of such complex
alloys.
In this work, we investigate the variations of both mechanical
and thermal properties across a broad compositional space for
the MoNbTaTi quaternary refractory alloy using DFT simulations
complemented with experimental validation. Many studies have
already been performed on similar alloys containing W instead of
Ti [34,35,26,32]. However, Ti, often considered a non-traditional
refractory metal [36], has unique thermophysical properties mak-
ing it an interesting alternative alloying element. Ti is a lightweight
metal and so replacing W, an extremely dense metal, with Ti is
intended to yield alloys with reduced weight and a lower density
as compared to MoNbTaW but with similar performance other-
wise. Even though other elements such as Al, Cr, or Si might
enhance corrosion resistance at elevated temperatures [37–39],
we did not considered those elements since each presented poten-
tial complications related to magnetic effects in our simulations or
difﬁcult fabrication factors during our AM process. Finally, previ-
ous CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagram) models have found
that the BCC phase in MoNbTaTi quaternary refractory alloys is
very stable relative to the formation of secondary or intermetallic
phases [7]. On the modeling side, we employed a systematic com-
positional search method where the composition of the alloy is
varied one element at a time through a range of compositions
around the equiatomic value. For this range of compositions, we
calculated changes in lattice constants, densities and mechanical
properties such as the elastic moduli and yield strength based on
a mechanistic, dislocation-based theory for BCC alloys. We then
calculated phonon dispersion behavior using perturbation theory
and employed the harmonic and quasi-harmonic approximations
to determine Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies, entropies, coefﬁ-
cients of thermal expansion, heat capacities, Grüneisen coefﬁcient,
and the isothermal and adiabatic bulk moduli as a function of tem-
perature. We complemented this computational study by experi-
mentally fabricating a MoNbTaTi alloy by the LB-DED technique
and measuring the same elastic and thermophysical properties
for direct experimental comparison and validation of our DFT pre-
dictions. While the elastic and thermophysical properties alone are
generally not sufﬁcient to evaluate the appropriateness of new
alloys, this work serves as a starting point for identifying key areas
of the composition space yielding emerging improved properties.
2. Methods
2.1. Density functional theory simulations
For the various MoNbTaTi alloys studied, we performed all of
our DFT calculations using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package
(VASP) [40–43]. In VASP, the periodic wave functions are approxi-
mated using plane-wave basis sets, and the description of the ion–
electron interaction is modeled according to the projector-
augmented wave (PAW) method [44,45]. The electron exchange–
correlation energy is approximated according to the generalized
gradient approximation under the Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof
(PBE) parametrization [46]. The PBE approximation has been found
to produce accurate and reasonable predictions almost universally

The plane-wave energy cutoff for the electronic wave functions
was set to 400 eV, ensuring that the total energy of the system con-
verged to a value less than 1 meV/atom. Gaussian smearing with a
smearing width of 0.01 eV was used. The minimization threshold
energy for the electronic self-consistent loop was set to 10 /C0 6 eV,
and the ionic minimization force criterion to 0.02 eV.Å /C0 1. During
the initial structural relaxation of each supercell, artiﬁcial mag-
netic moments were applied to the atoms to test for potential mag-
netic ordering effects. In every case, the applied moments were
found to diminish to zero indicating the alloys were non-
magnetic and spin-polarization was not necessary for subsequent
calculations.
2.2. Special quasi-random structures of MoNbTaTi alloys
We simulated the random ordering of the atomic species in the
solid-solution alloys via special quasi-random structures (SQS)
[47,48]. The concept of SQS is to build special periodic random
structures with a small number of atoms in the DFT supercell in
order to match as closely as possible the cluster (e.g., pair, triplet,
... ) correlation functions of the ﬁnite-sized supercell to that of
the true random structure. Because of the relatively small number
of atoms and in order to further improve the precision of our pre-
dictions, for each composition studied, we used four separate SQS
calculations. For the calculations of structural and mechanical
properties, all four SQS structures were modeled, and properties
are reported as the average of all four structures. For the calcula-
tions of thermodynamic properties, only the SQS structure which
performed most closely to the average was modeled.
The SQS supercells used in this work contained 72 atoms and
were constructed to represent a 4 /C2 3 /C2 3 replication of the base
BCC (space group: 229, Im/C223m) conventional, unit-cell structure. A
3 /C2 5 /C2 5 k-point mesh was found to sufﬁciently minimize the
total energy of these supercells to below 3 meV. An example of
the supercell structure depicting the equiatomic quaternary alloy
is shown in Fig. 1 . While this supercell may be considered some-
what small, we found that it adequately converged both the total
energy per atom and elastic constants and moduli for the equia-
tomic composition.
In this work, we aim to explore the compositional dependence of
material properties in RCCAs. As such, we employed a simple search
method to observe and quantitatively interpret the dependence of a
property on the composition of each individual principal element.
The material compositions modeled can be described using the
notation AxfBCDg1/C0 x, where the composition of one element (A) is
varied at a time while constraining the compositions of the three
remaining elements (B, C, D) to be equal to one another. This allows
for four separate element tracks, in which each of the Mo, Nb, Ta,
and Ti elements are slotted into the ‘A’ position. We varied the com-
position of the A element over a range that includes three composi-
tions below and above the equiatomic composition. We also
included a seventh composition above the equiatomic composition
where the A element is at 50 at.%. All four of these element tracks
converge to the equiatomic composition, where the composition
of each element in this case is 25 at.%. In total, for each element
track, 8 different unique compositions (i.e., x = 12.5, 16.667,
20.833, 25.0, 29.167, 33.333, 37.5, and 50.0 at.%) were modeled.
The properties for each composition, were then averaged over the
four SQS structures. In the remaining of this manuscript, all compo-
sitions are reported as atom-fraction (at.%) ratios.
In addition to these four element tracks, we also modeled two
separate, special compositions. These special compositions corre-
spond to atom-fraction ratios of the alloys fabricated for (i) exper-
imental testing of the thermophysical-properties in this work, with
a composition of Mo
18.1Nb23.6Ta23.6Ti34.7, and (ii) experimental
testing of yield strength in the work reported by Coury et al.
[7,11], with a composition of Mo 22.1Nb23.6Ta27.8Ti26.4.

2.4. Calculation of thermodynamic properties
We calculated the thermodynamic properties using density
functional perturbation theory (DFPT) and the open-source pack-
age Phonopy [53,54]. Within the harmonic approximation, lattice
vibrational (and thus thermodynamic) properties can be described
by the phonon spectrum, or more speciﬁcally the phonon density
of states of the material. DFPT allows for the calculation of the sec-
ond derivative of the total energy with respect to atomic displace-
ments which then yield the phonon vibrational modes of the
material. The quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA) was then
applied within Phonopy to determine volume-dependent thermo-
dynamic properties that depend on anharmonic effects, such as the
thermal expansion. The details of the Phonopy calculations have
already been described elsewhere [54] and so are not explicitly
given here. However, their detailed description is provided in the
Supplementary Information.

2.5. Experimental methods: Fabrication and testing of MoNbTaTi bulk
specimens
In addition to our DFT simulations, we utilized laser beam direc-
ted energy deposition to fabricate MoNbTaTi bulk specimens for
testing. The Mo and Ta powders were obtained in spherical form
from Tekna (Quebec, QC, Canada), while the Nb powder was
acquired from Edgetech Industries, LLC (Miramar, FL, USA), and
the Ti powder was acquired from LPW Technologies (now Carpen-
ter Technologies, PA, USA). Each powder came with a different
particle-size distribution. The Mo powder had a particle-size distri-

for the Ta powder, and 90 /C6 45 lm for the Ti powder. To fabricate
the MoNbTaTi RCCA specimens, the elemental powders were
blended within a single powder feeder, targeting an initially equia-
tomic composition. We used an open-architecture LB-DED AM sys-

moisture. The powder-feeding system employs a rotary-feed wheel
and carrier gas to deliver the ﬂuidized powder feedstock, which
independently controlled the powder ﬂow rate for each con-
stituent. During fabrication, laser power varied from 220 to

3. Results
3.1. Crystal structure
The ﬁrst set of calculations consisted of relaxing and optimizing
the BCC unit-cell structure of each of the four SQS cells modeled at
each composition. From these simulations, average lattice con-
stants and densities (taken at 0 K) were determined and are
reported in Fig. 3 for each of the four element tracks. We observe
signiﬁcant changes in both properties as a function of the compo-
sition. Our results show that there is a particularly strong contrac-
tion of the lattice spacing with high Mo concentrations (and thus
expansion at low Mo concentrations). In contrast, Ti has only a
minimal effect regardless of concentration, while Nb and Ta cause
moderate expansions with greater concentrations. The contraction
of the lattice constant with greater Mo concentration is no surprise,
since Mo atoms are smaller than the other three elements in this
alloy. Likewise, the lattice expansion seen for the Nb and Ta tracks
is likely due to the accompanying decrease in Mo concentration
rather than a size effect of the Ta and Nb atoms, both of which
lie close to the average atomic size.
The variation of the alloy density also meets expected behaviors
with Ta, by far the heaviest element, and Ti, the lightest, having the
most signiﬁcant effects. For a comparison with experimental val-
ues, we turn to the near-equiatomic alloy composition (Mo 18.1-
Nb23.6Ta23.6Ti34.7) fabricated for this work, which was found to
have a density of 9.549 g /C1 cm/C0 3 and an average lattice constant of
3.249 Å. These measurements compare favorably well with the
DFT calculated values of 9.507 g /C1 cm/C0 3 for the density and a 3.254 Å
lattice constant. While the DFT values do show a slight over-
prediction of bond lengths, the deviation in both lattice constant
and density are well within the realm of reasonable accuracy. We
also compared our lattice predictions with those measured by Coury
et al. [7] who used X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Mo 22.3Nb23.4Ta28.3-
Ti26.0 sample specimen. For this, we used the GSAS-II software [56]
on the experimental XRD data from Coury and coworkers and
obtained an estimated experimental lattice constant of 3.2406 Å as
compared to a 3.2499 Å predicted from our DFT simulations. Here
again our prediction compare favorably well as compared to the
experimental data.
Atomic misﬁt volumes are calculated using the average atom
volume from each SQS cell and are plotted in Fig. 4. Misﬁt volumes
are a way to both qualitatively and quantitatively measure the

commonly applied motifs within RCCAs and MPEAs studies, and
search for correlation among cohesive energy, the valence electron
correlation (VEC), and the calculated mechanical and thermal
properties [61,62]. We have calculated cohesive energies, Ecoh,
directly from the DFT total energies as the difference between
the alloy total energy and the energy of individual elements in
their ground-state structures, normalized for the number of atoms
in the simulation cell. The VECs are simply taken as the average
number of valence electrons for all atoms in the alloy, in this case
the simulation cell. We present in Fig. 9 the resulting correlations
for every supercell along all four element tracks and note a few
observations. Looking at the correlation between the cohesive
energy and VEC (left panel in Fig. 9), we observe a strong negative
correlation where higher VEC alloys equates to lower cohesive
energies. The same linear negative correlation is observed between
the bulk modulus at 0 K, B0 and the cohesive energy. The fact that
the cohesive energy appears to decrease ( i.e., becoming more
stable) as the elastic moduli become stiffer should be expected,
since the cohesive energy essentially describes the strength and
stiffness of the bonds formed between atoms, which is directly
captured in the formulation of the elastic constants. Additionally,
the strong correlation of the cohesive energy with VEC, and de facto
with the moduli, is reasonably understandable, when considering
that transition metals with more d-electrons can form more bonds
with neighboring atoms, strengthening the lattice structure. Ana-
lyzing the correlations according to the individual element tracks
also yields some interesting observations. Each track in the cohe-
sive energy correlations appears to behave slightly independently
from one another. While overall they generally exhibit similar
trends, there are slight differences between the element tracks.
The Ta track, while mostly tightly clustered due to relatively low
changes in Ecoh or modulus, deviates most signiﬁcantly from the
overall direction of the correlation. Mapping these observations
to composition differences, we ﬁnd that alloys with a high Mo con-
tent and low Ti content have the highest VECs and form the stron-
gest bonds within the alloy, while variations of the composition
within Nb or Ta tracks have only very minor effects on the overall
cohesion. Additional observations on the stability based on the cal-
culated Helmholtz free energy and entropy are provided in the
Supporting Information.

and internal energy are effectively the same thing, so changes in

As illustrated above, the cohesive energy and VEC serve as good
predictors of the alloys with greater mechanical strength, particu-
larly so in the case of high Mo concentrations. This is because the
cohesive energy is a measure of the strength of the bonds formed
between the atoms in the alloy, which plays a signiﬁcant role in
the underlying alloy strength before microstructural features such
as grain size and dislocation density can be considered. Here,
increasing the concentration of Mo results in stronger alloys,
because Mo can form the strongest bonds with other d-transition
metal atoms. This is generally true of the greater group-VIB transi-
tion metals (Cr, Mo, W), where the valence band in each metal pos-
sesses a half-ﬁlled d-shell (ﬁve electrons) after promotion of a
lower lying s electron. Because of this promotion, the effective
nuclear charge is enhanced, resulting in a stronger pull and tighter
physical contraction of the valence elections. This results in a smal-
ler atomic radius, giving to the substantial increase in atomic size
mismatch with greater Mo contents but also more importantly to
the localization of the valence d-electrons. The resulting bonds
formed with neighboring transition metal atoms thus take on a
more localized nature (i.e., covalent) which tend to be much stron-
ger than traditional metal bonds. In the case of pure Mo and W, the
d-band is dominated by this covalent bonding, while the half-ﬁlled
s-band takes on the more de-localized metallic bonding character,
thus as the composition of these group-VIB metals in an RCCA is
increased, one can expect that the average bond type follows this
behavior. Fig. 10 shows just that, where the total density of states
(DOS) is plotted for a low Mo (12.5 at.%) and a high Mo concentra-
tion (50 at.%) supercell. In the low-Mo alloy, the DOS crosses the
Fermi energy without much change in behavior between the occu-
pied and unoccupied sides. In the high-Mo alloy, the DOS drops
precipitously just at the Fermi energy before rising again several
eV later. This pseudo-band gap region, largely resembling the
DOS of pure Mo or W, is indicative of covalent bonding and repre-
sents a splitting between the low energy bonding states and high
energy anti-bonding states in the d-band. Thus, as more Mo is
added to the alloy, the average bond type between atomic neigh-
bors assumes a more covalent character, which, combined with
atomic size mismatch effects, results in stronger and stiffer bonds
that increase the elastic and mechanical strength of the alloy.
A useful takeaway from our DOS analysis is that Mo can serve as
a suitable replacement or substitute for W in some alloys. Often, W
is incorporated into RCCAs for its favorable high-temperature
properties, such as the increased retention of strength, high melt-
ing point, and low thermal expansion. However, these come at a
price since W is very dense and heavy, making any W-based alloys
not ideal for use in applications where weight requirements are a
signiﬁcant factor, such as in aerospace or aeronautical applications.
This work conﬁrms that Mo, which possesses many of the same
high-temperature properties as W, just at a reduced scale, can also
impart these qualities onto a RCCA without the subsequent
increase in density. The origin of the high-temperature strength
in Mo and W is essentially the same (i.e., the covalent bonding),
so it should be expected that properties imparted onto an alloy
should scale similarly from one to the other. Likewise, improved
high-temperature strength has also been observed in some Cr
(the other group-VIB member) containing alloys as well. Thus, for
the design of strong RCCAs that retain their properties at high tem-
peratures, one should try to maximize the level of covalent d-
bonding by including higher contents of group-VIB metals while
also attempting to maximize the degree of size mismatch between
atoms by including other refractory metals such as Nb and V. As
the group-VIIB metals (Mn, Tc, Re) also possess half-ﬁlled d-
bands, though not as localized as in the group VIB metals, they
may also potentially contribute some level of high-strength prop-
erty improvement to the RCCA. However, since Tc and Re are
HCP metals, they could also negatively impact some properties
and the phase stability of these alloys, as seen in this work with Ti.

Finally, we close this discussion with a comment regarding the
entropic effects on mechanical properties. Our results show that
the strength mechanisms studied in this work, namely the general
elastic stiffness and the yield strength, do not appear to have a
strong dependence on the total compositional/conﬁgurational
entropy. Conﬁgurational entropy should be at a maximum at the
equiatomic composition, however, the strongest alloys in this sys-
tem were found on the Mo-rich side of the composition space. So,
in this regard, conﬁgurational entropy may not be an important
factor in designing alloys within this particular system. This really
depends on what properties one seeks to optimize during the
design stage. The handful of thermophysical data points gathered
in this work are not necessarily enough to fully characterize ther-
modynamic stability in this alloy system. This is particularly true
when considering the formation of other principal or intermetallic
phases or the potential for short range order effects, which are not
easily captured in the SQS approach employed here. One can, how-
ever, consider other contributions to material strength including
an excess conﬁgurational term resulting from different-sized
atoms, and non-conﬁgurational terms stemming from vibrations
or electronic effects. For instance, an excess conﬁgurational term
born out of the lattice disorder that results from differently-sized
atoms in the alloy [63] likely plays a signiﬁcant role in the
strengthening mechanisms studied in this work. The misﬁt vol-
umes presented in Fig. 4 directly capture such effect and suggest
that excess Mo should increase this conﬁgurational term, which
directly coincides with the stiffer elastic constants and high yield
strength seen for those alloys. So, while the overall conﬁgurational
entropy appears to be weakly connected to strength, it is not clear
to what extent other contributions to the entropy will inﬂuence its
magnitude and how it impacts the design phase of high-strength
alloys in systems such as the one investigated in the present study.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we presented a high-throughput ab initio database
of material properties for the MoNbTaTi alloy, analyzing changes in
thermo-mechanical properties as a function of the composition by
varying the composition one element at a time. We elicit the direct
effect of each element on important material properties including
crystal structure properties such as the lattice constant, density,
and average atomic volume (i.e., through misﬁt volume analysis),
elastic and mechanical properties such as the elastic constants,
elastic moduli, and yield strength, and thermodynamic properties
as described by phonon dispersion such as thermal expansion, heat
capacity, Grüneisen coefﬁcient, free energy, and entropy. Major
take aways are the following:
/C15 The overall strength and alloy stiffness are promoted by
increasing the relative Mo concentration, or conversely by
reducing the relative Ti content. Changes in Ta and Nb content
had very little effect on calculated properties such as yield
strength or elastic moduli.
/C15 The reason Mo increases the yield strength is likely due to a
combination of high bulk and shear moduli, and for the rela-
tively small size of the Mo atom relative to the other atoms in
the alloy (accounted for in the misﬁt volume parameter). High
concentrations of Mo cause the average bond between atomic
neighbors to shift from metallic towards covalent, signiﬁcantly
increasing bond strength and contributing to the higher bulk,
shear and Young’s moduli. Taken along with the effect on yield
strength, increasing the Mo concentration in alloys such as
these should result in greater strength at both low and high
temperature ranges.
/C15 Regarding thermodynamics properties, the coefﬁcient of ther-
mal expansion is reduced for nearly every composition mod-
eled, relative to the equiatomic composition. We also
observed that the Ta and Ti element tracks have the strongest
on heat capacity, while the variation in the other element track
show minimal changes in this property. This analysis of the
thermodynamics properties shows that, regardless of the com-
position, this class of quaternary alloys has good stability and a
low sensitivity to temperature.
/C15 We found strong correlations between the alloy cohesive ener-
gies, VEC, elastic stiffness and mechanical strength, as well as
with thermodynamic properties such as the heat capacity.
/C15 Considerations on alloying elements and compositions that pro-
mote not only higher elastic moduli and larger atomic size mis-
match, but also a shift from metallic to covalent bonding should
be used as design features to pave the way for the design of
novel high-performance refractory complex concentrated alloys
beyond the equimolar composition found in ‘classical’ high-
entropy alloys.
