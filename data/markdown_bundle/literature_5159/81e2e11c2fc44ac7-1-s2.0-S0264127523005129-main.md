abstract
Improving fatigue properties of engineering alloys using conventional laser shock peening (LSP) with
protective coating frequently causes surface grain subdivision, which is undesirable for single crystal
(SX) superalloys. Herein, an alternative approach, i.e., LSP without coating (LSPwC), is proven to be fea-
sible in the surface strengthening of SX superalloys with preserving its original SX nature. The thermal
effect of LSPwC generates a layered heterogeneous near-surface microstructure, comprising a recast
layer ( /C24 0.8
lm thick) and a severely deformed layer. Upon rapid remelting process, the recast layer
converts to a c solid solution with its outer layer ( /C24 0.4 lm thick) decorated by densely dispersed
Al-rich oxide nanoparticles. Such a particulate-reinforced composite possesses a nanohardness 55%
higher than the base metal. The severely deformed layer with denser dislocations in the
c matrix than
in the c0 interior forms a 400 lm-deep work-hardened layer. Under cyclic loads, the near-surface struc-
ture still preserves the SX structure with increased stacking faults density in the c matrix. Despite
increased surface roughness, the synergistic action of compressive residual stress, work hardening,
and heterogeneous near-surface microstructure prolongs the fatigue life of SX superalloys by /C24 36.5%.

1. Introduction
Modern gas turbine engine blades are best made from single
crystal (SX) Ni-based superalloys because they function well under
severe thermo-mechanical stress [1]. The elimination of grain
boundaries and the presence of high-volume-fraction (>60 vol%)
L12 c0-Ni3(Al, Ti) precipitates [2] account for their exceptional
high-temperature strength. Because of the almost equal lattice
characteristics between c0 precipitates and c austenite matrix, a
coherent interphase boundary with a minimal lattice misﬁt is
formed. Though SX superalloys are remarkably capable in the
harsh environment of high-temperature gas and hot corrosion,
premature failure occurs following surface-initiated damage
mechanisms [3,4], signiﬁcantly lowering the service life of the tur-
bine blades. Particularly, the dovetail joint, connecting the turbine
blade and disc, is highly susceptible to fretting fatigue damage
[5,6], which occupies almost 1/6 of the fatigue failures of gas tur-
bine engines [7].
Mechanical surface treatment approaches like shot peening
and laser shock peening (LSP) can improve engineering compo-
nents’ fatigue performance. Through the impingement between
the target material surface and shots, shot peening imposes sev-
ere plastic deformation and compressive residual stress in the
near-surface region [8]. LSP has recently been considered a better
alternative to shot peening because it produces a deeper com-
pressive stress ﬁeld [9] and a better surface ﬁnish [10]. During
the LSP process, short-duration laser pulses ablate a protective
coating ( e.g., black tape or aluminum foil) over the target material
surface into a high-temperature plasma [11]. Then the rapidly
expanded plasma is conﬁned by a transparent conﬁning medium
like water, introducing high-pressure shock waves of the order of
GPa. The propagation of shock waves into the interior of the tar-
get material causes severe plastic deformation and work harden-
ing, greatly impeding the initiation and propagation of fatigue
cracks [12,13].
LSP has been demonstrated to be an efﬁcient and cost-effective
strategy to prolong the service life of SX Ni-based superalloys.
Through numerical simulation, Fang et al. [14] and Tang et al.
[15] reported that the compressive stress in the LSP-treated DD6
SX superalloys can reach up to 600 MPa, which is beneﬁcial for
slowing fatigue crack growth rate and extending the alloys’ fatigue
life [16]. In addition to mechanical properties, LSP can improve the
hot corrosion resistance [17] and wear resistance [18] of SX super-
alloys. The above performance improvement mainly arises from
the severe plastic deformation gradient associated with the high-
pressure plasma shock wave and the ultrahigh strain rate (>10
6
s/C0 1) [19]. More speciﬁcally, conventional LSP technique using a
millimeter-sized laser beam with a pulse duration in tens of
nanoseconds and a few joules pulse energy sometimes leads to sig-
niﬁcant surface grain reﬁnement in Ni-based alloys [20–22] and
other structural materials [23–25]. Grain reﬁnement in these alloys
is governed by forming various types of boundaries ( e.g., disloca-
tion walls, high-angle grain boundaries, and twin boundaries) that
subdivide the original coarse grains. Despite the Hall-Petch
strengthening mechanism, the introduction of grain boundaries
destroys the integrity of the SX turbine blade and seriously weak-
ens its thermo-mechanical strength and service lifetime [26,27].
Furthermore, the weak resistance to grain boundary migration
caused by the absence of grain boundary strengthening elements
(e.g., B and C) in SX superalloys [28] is unable to stop the nano-
grains from continuing to grow at the service temperature of tur-
bine blades, potentially leading to near-surface softening and
fatigue life reduction [29]. In this case, avoiding surface grain sub-
division is critical for strengthening SX superalloy components via
LSP technique.
In contrast to conventional LSP which employs a protective
coating over the target material surface, a more practical and
cost-effective technique, called LSP without (protection) coating
(LSPwC), has been developed. LSPwC prevents grain reﬁnement
[30] and contributes to improved performances comparable with
those attained from conventional LSP. Sano et al. [31] reported that
LSPwC can signiﬁcantly increase stainless steel’s rotating-bending
fatigue life and stress corrosion cracking resistance. Nie et al.
[32] also demonstrated the LSPwC’s potential to enhance the
high-cycle fatigue performance of DZ17G superalloys. After a com-
parison of c00 -c0 precipitation-hardened IN718 superalloys treated
by LSP with and without a protective coating, Gill et al. [33]
reported that LSPwC brings forth a rough surface consisting of a
non-uniform recast layer under the thermo-mechanical effect.
However, detailed knowledge is missing for the microstructure
and mechanical properties of LSPwC-treated c0 precipitation-
hardened SX superalloys. To this end, this study focuses on the
detailed characterization of the near-surface microstructure of SX
superalloys after LSPwC treatment to gain a deep understanding
of its impact on hardness and fatigue properties.
2. Experimental methods
2.1. Sample preparation
The 2nd generation SX superalloy DD6 with a nominal compo-
sition (wt%) of 5.6Al-9Co-4.3Cr-2Mo-8W-7.5Ta-2Re-0.5Nb-0.1Hf-
Ni (Bal.) [34] was used as the base metal in this study. Before
LSPwC treatment, the SX alloy ingots were subjected to standard
heat treatment reported in [17] to achieve a homogenized
microstructure with a low level of residual stress and dislocation
density and a unimodal size distribution of c0 precipitates. Then
the ingots were processed into rotating-bending fatigue bars with
the bars’ length along the [0 0 1] direction (Fig. 1a). Detailed geom-
etry and dimensions of the fatigue bars can be found elsewhere
[35].
After mechanically grinding with 2000 grit sandpaper, the
gauge area in the middle of the fatigue bars was treated by
coaxial-water feeding LSPwC ( Fig. 1a). During this process, the fati-
gue bars rotated with their cylindrical axes that are perpendicular
to the laser beam. The sample surface was peened once using a Q-
switched Nd: YAG laser system where the laser had a spot size of
/C24 0.4 mm and a wavelength of 532 nm. The repetition rate, pulse
duration, and pulse energy were 500 Hz, 8 ns, and 50 mJ, respec-
tively. To ensure the uniformity of surface hardening on a whole
sample, an overlapping rate of /C24 50% was used.

metal) was ﬁrst characterized. A slice cut from the ingot with its
surface normal along [0 0 1] direction was thinned in 8 vol% HClO 4
and 92 vol% ethanol using a twin-jet electro-polishing machine and

3. Results
3.1. Surface morphology
The rough recast surface after LSPwC treatment is shown in
Fig. 1c. A sub-millimeter-sized dent array, whose spacing depends
on the overlapping rate ( /C24 50%) or the laser beam scanning offset
(/C24 200 lm) [39], is visible as a result of the plastic deformation car-
ried by successive laser peening. The material gets piled up close to
the edges of the dents. A closer look ( Fig. 1 d) reveals that the sur-
face has a lunar-crater-like morphology and a non-uniformly dis-
tributed micro-voids, which are resulted from the re-melting and
re-solidiﬁcation of the top layer in response to laser pulses [33].
Fig. 2 compares the surface topographies obtained using CLSM
before and after LSPwC treatments. Different from the artiﬁcially
parallel-scratched feature in the untreated surface ( Fig. 2 a), the
LSPwC-treated surface is more undulating ( Fig. 2b), consistent with
the SEM observation ( Fig. 1c). Fig. 2c shows the line roughness pro-
ﬁles of those two samples. As expected, the LSPwC-treated surface
in an undulating fashion shows higher roughness than the
untreated one. From the line proﬁle, the average diameter of these
dents was measured to be /C24 150 lm, smaller than the laser spot
size ( /C24 400 lm). Both the average roughness (Sa) and root mean
square roughness (Sq) values of the LSPwC-treated surface are
almost three times higher than those of the untreated surface,
according to a quantitative roughness measurement ( Fig. 2d). Sim-
ilar ﬁndings have been also made in a LSPwC-treated IN718 super-
alloy [33].
3.2. Microstructural characterization of the as-peened sample
Fig. 3a depicts the morphologies of c0 precipitates at a depth of
up to 20 lm from the laser-peened surface. Unlike the continuous
and lengthy (>10 lm) slip bands that transmit multiple c0 precip-

the LSPwC-treated samples are mainly conﬁned in isolated c0 pre-

The above results indicate that the recast layer grows epitaxi-
ally on the SX substrate during the LSPwC process. A similar sce-
nario has been extensively reported in the laser remelting or
laser deposition of SX superalloys [42–44]. According to traditional
solidiﬁcation theory [45], the degree of constitutional supercooling
increases with the decrease in G/R, resulting in the formation of
planar, cellular, columnar dendritic, and equiaxed dendritic struc-
tures in sequence, where G is temperature gradient at the solid–
liquid interface and R is solidiﬁcation rate. In the case of LSPwC
process, the SX substrate acts as the heat sink, generating a very
steep temperature gradient and a large G/R at the solid–liquid
interface. Simultaneously, random nucleation (i.e., stray grain) is
greatly suppressed because of the substantially reduced constitu-
tional supercooling during the rapid solidiﬁcation process [46].
Consequently, the melt solidiﬁes in the planar growth mode since
no cellular or dendritic patterns are observed and grows epitaxially
relative to the beneath SX substrate.
A detailed characterization of the recast layer was performed.
Fig. 5 a, an enlarged view of the square-framed region in Fig. 4 a,
shows the microstructural and chemical heterogeneity in a layer-
wise manner. The superlattice peaks in the selected area diffraction
pattern (SADP, Fig. 5a2) demonstrate the
c/c0 dual-phase architec-
ture in the severely deformed layer. Typically, a partially dissolved
c0 precipitate (outlined by red triangles) beneath the fusion line,
resulting from the LSPwC-induced surface re-melting, is detected.
The c0 precipitate distribution in the severely deformed layer is
also conﬁrmed by the element partitioning, where Co and Cr are
enriched in the c channels but Al tends to partition into the c0 inte-
rior ( Fig. 5 b). In contrast, the recast layer is converted to a solid
solution (evidenced by the SADP in Fig. 5 a1) and can be further
divided into two parts: (I) the upper part, or dispersed oxide layer,
contains numerous Al-rich oxide particles, and (II) the lower part,
or precipitate-free layer, is chemically homogenous and devoid of
both c0 precipitates and oxide nanoparticles.
According to Fig. 5c, a lower dislocation density is presented in
the recast layer compared to that in the severely deformed layer,
which is consistent with the KAM proﬁle in Fig. 4d. In the dispersed
oxide layer, strong pinning sites provided by the oxide particles
can prevent dislocation motion in the solid solution matrix
(Fig. 5 d). Additionally, the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image
and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns
(Fig. 5 e) conﬁrm the amorphous nature of oxide particles.
The dislocation conﬁgurations in the severely deformed layer
were further investigated. As shown in Fig. 6 a and b, a slightly
higher dislocation density is found in the
c channel than that in
the c0 interior. Despite that the tangling of dislocations in the c

For SX alloys, measuring residual stress using diffraction
method is substantially more difﬁcult than their polycrystalline
counterparts [48]. Alternatively, various models for residual stress
calculation through depth-sensing indentation approaches have
been developed, among which Wang’s model [49] was reported
to be most suitable for evaluating the residual stress distribution
in a shot-peened DD6 SX superalloy [50]. From the viewpoint of
indentation work during the indentation process, Wang et al.
[49] proposed that the total work of residual stress can be analyzed
by the difference between the indentation load–displacement
curves with and without residual stress. Typically, as schemed in
the inset of Fig. 7 c, compressive residual stress ( rR < 0) can lead
to a leftward shift of the load–displacement curve relative to the
stress-free case ( rR = 0). Without considering the pile-up and
sink-in of residual indentation, the residual stress can be estimated
as follows [50]:

4. Discussion
4.1. Formation mechanism of LSPwC-induced microstructure
The ﬁndings in this study reveal that LSPwC imposes a thermo-
mechanical impact on the surface of SX superalloys, which, in turn,
leads to an inhomogeneous near-surface microstructure. The
microstructural inhomogeneity comprises a dispersed Al-rich
oxide layer (depth of 0–0.4
lm), a supersaturated precipitate-
free layer (depth of 0.4–0.8 lm), and a severely deformed base
metal layer ( c/c0 dual-phase architecture containing high-density
dislocations) along the laser-peening direction. The former two
layers (together referred to as the recast layer) are closely related
to the thermal effects (i.e., surface re-melting and subsequent re-
solidiﬁcation) introduced by laser pulses. Despite the mechanical
and thermal impacts, the near-surface microstructure retains its
SX character and adheres to the base metal epitaxially. A possible
formation mechanism of the LSPwC-induced microstructure is
interpreted in Fig. 11 and brieﬂy summarized as follows:
At the site of the incident laser beam interacting with the target
material surface, the high-energy-density laser pulse creates a
plasma (Fig. 11a), allowing thermal energy absorption and produc-
ing localized surface re-melting [59]. This is followed by the abla-
tion of molten metal, resulting in the formation of vaporized

and consequently droplets/vapor of molten metal react with water
and get oxidized [59]. Following that, the fast expansion of plasma

which drive the suspended liquid/vapor droplets to be embedded
in the re-melted metal. In the ﬁnal solidiﬁcation stage, the liquid/-

surface microstructure ( Fig. 11 c). Because of a stronger oxygen
afﬁnity than other elements (such as Cr, Co, and Ni) [54], Al atoms
prefers to combine with oxygen during the above process, so the
oxide particles are predominantly enriched in Al ( Fig. 5 b).
Particularly, a rapid solidiﬁcation/cooling rate and a unidirec-

expected under the combined actions of short-pulse duration and
water quenching. These solidiﬁcation conditions permit the epitax-

base metal ( Fig. 4 b), similar to the directional dendritic growth of
Ni-based superalloys produced through additive manufacturing
[60]. Additionally, a supersaturated c solid solution by suppressing
c0 precipitation is formed in the recast layer ( Fig. 5a). This suggests
that the LSPwC process has an extremely high cooling rate higher
than metal additive manufacturing (10 3–106 K/s) that always can-
not completely inhibit c0 precipitation during cooling process
[61,62]. Due to the sub-millimeter-sized laser pulses and lower
energy used in this study, the LSPwC-induced thermal effect is con-
ﬁned to only a small depth of /C24 0.8 lm from the surface, which is
much shallower than that reported in IN718 superalloys (5–
10 lm) treated by high-energy LSPwC [33].
The shock waves produced during the LSPwC process, on the
other hand, govern the plastic deformation behavior in the near-
surface region ( Fig. 11 d). Unlike dislocations primarily activated
by thermal stress in the recast layer ( Fig. 5 c), shock waves in the
severely deformed layer result in a higher density of dislocations.
Speciﬁcally, within the severely deformed layer, the soft c channels
yield more easily than c0 [63] in response to shock waves and carry
more plastic deformation ( Fig. 6a–c), while c0 precipitates undergo
less signiﬁcant plastic deformation ( Fig. 6 e and f) and their mor-
phology is somewhat unchanged ( Fig. 3 a–c).
It is noteworthy that the layered heterogeneous near-surface
microstructure observed in the present study is dependent on
the LSPwC parameters. As forementioned, a larger laser spot size
and a higher pulse energy may cause surface grain reﬁnement like
the conventional LSP process. Moreover, the formation of the nano-
sized Al-rich oxide particles in the recast layer might be greatly
suppressed if the conﬁning medium (i.e., water) is replaced by
transparent overlay glass or quartz. As such, proper selection of
the processing parameters is essential for the application of LSPwC
technique on the SX superalloy components.

4.2.2. Work-hardened surface layer
As aforementioned, there is no evidence of the change in c0
morphology and grain structure in the severely deformed layer
(below the recast layer). Consequently, the observed hardness
increase (Fig. 7a and 7b) cannot be accounted for by the hardening
effects of precipitate size, solid solutions, or grain reﬁnement, but
mainly correlates with work hardening contributed by the disloca-
tion density gradient along the laser-peening direction. As sug-
gested by Fig. 3 f, GND density, proportional to the local KAM
value, increases gradually with decreasing depths, which is mainly
relevant to the strain gradient induced by the shock waves during
LSPwC process. On the other hand, as the dislocations are predom-
inately stored in the narrow
c channels and blocked by high-
volume-fraction c0 precipitates ( Fig. 6 a and b), such a dislocation
conﬁguration has been demonstrated as a redundant character
[41], or statistically-stored dislocation (SSD) [69]. It is thus inferred
that SSDs are also prevalent within the work-hardened surface
layer. Although the quantitative dislocation density evaluation is
unavailable in this study due to the difﬁculty in measuring SSD
density, the dislocation density gradient and its contribution to
work hardening in LSP-treated Ni-based superalloys have been
veriﬁed [41].
The maximum hardness attained in the recast layer decorated
by densely dispersed nano-sized oxides can be elucidated by the
dispersoid hardening mechanism [70]. Indeed, the recast layer, like
a particulate-reinforced metal matrix composite, has the potential
to provide superior mechanical properties [71]. The amorphous
structure of the Al-rich oxides layer is likely to lower the large lat-
tice mismatch energy at the oxide/matrix interface and facilitate
the load transfer from the matrix to the oxide [72], thus preventing
strain localization and premature failure. The enhanced GND con-
tent originating from a signiﬁcant thermo-mechanical effect may
be responsible for the higher misorientation ( Fig. 4 d–e) in the
near-surface region.
As revealed in Fig. 8 , the presence of a work-hardened surface
layer plays a decisive role in the fatigue fracture behaviors of SX
Ni-based superalloys. After LSPwC treatment, the surface is
strengthened and the weak areas are minimized in the interior.
The combined action of work-hardened surface and compressive
residual stress help to prevent surface crack initiation and force
fatigue cracks to initiate from the ﬂaws ( e.g., casting pores,
Fig. 8 i) with highly localized stress in the sub-surface region.
Unlike the multiple crack initiation sites in the untreated sample,
the presence of a single crack origin in the LSPwC-treated sample
is associated with a failure with lower stress concentration [53].
4.2.3. Near-surface microstructure
Conventional high-energy LSP frequently introduces massive
dislocation and twinning activities, leading to grain subdivision
and even surface nanocrystallization [73,74]. Under the thermal
effect of the laser beam, high-density dislocations and high-level
residual stress in the near-surface region may promote dynamic
recrystallization [74]. However, grain subdivision and recrystal-
lization are always forbidden for SX superalloys. Grain boundary
strengthening elements (like carbon, boron, and hafnium) have
generally been excluded in SX superalloys to improve the incipient
melting temperatures [75], which, however, leads to rapid degra-
dation of thermal stability if grain boundary is introduced. Surface
recrystallization can seriously weaken both the stress rupture life
[76] and the fatigue life [29] of single-crystal superalloys. In the
present study, the sub-millimeter-sized low-energy laser beam
was employed to minimize the thermal effect, and thus simultane-
ously achieve surface strengthening and grain orientation preser-
vation. After fatigue failure, the SX grain structure near the
fracture surface is still preserved, regardless of surface oxidation.
In addition, the accumulation of dense SFs and L-C locks inside
the
c channels (Fig. 10c and d) can greatly enhance the strain hard-
ening and strengthen the c matrix, which is considered beneﬁcial
for the fatigue strength improvement for SX superalloys [77].A s
a result, the LSPwC-treated SX superalloys are expected to have
good structural stability in the surface region in response to
thermo-mechanical stress.
Overall, the protective coating used in the conventional LSP pro-
cess protects the alloy’s surface from being ablated by the laser
beam. Nevertheless, it is a time-consuming affair since the protec-
tive coating is damaged severely during the LSP process and
required to be removed after LSP process, making it slow and
expensive in industrial applications [78]. Some components with
complex geometry ( e.g., turbine blade root) are not always accessi-
ble for the application of protective overlay [33]. Particularly, the
excessive plastic deformation subjected by conventional LSP pro-
cess is likely to cause surface nanocrystallization or trigger recrys-
tallization at high temperatures [15,74], which is undesirable for
SX Ni-based superalloys. Therefore, the LSPwC technique with a
sub-millimeter-sized laser beam and a low-input pulse energy
(tens-of-mJ level) has attracted much attention recently. Due to
the local surface ablation and remelting, however, typical surface
craters with increased surface roughness and layered heterogenous
microstructures are obtained. Their formation mechanism and
effect on the surface integrity and fatigue properties of structural
materials require further exploration.
5. Conclusions
In summary, this work demonstrates that LSPwC treatment can
strengthen the c/c0 dual-phase SX superalloys without causing
grain subdivision and provides an in-depth understanding of the
formation mechanism of near-surface microstructure and its inﬂu-
ence on fatigue performance. Key conclusions are summarized as
follows:
(1) LSPwC-treated SX superalloys show a layered heterogeneous
microstructure associated with surface melting, vaporiza-
tion, and shock-assisted rapid solidiﬁcation processes. The
microstructural inhomogeneity comprises a recast layer
(/C24 0.8 lm thick) and a severely deformed layer.
(2) Upon rapid solidiﬁcation, the recast layer preserves its orig-
inal SX nature but converts to a SX c solid solution, embed-
ded by densely dispersed Al-rich oxide nanoparticles at the
outermost surface. This particulate-reinforced composite
layer possesses the maximum nanohardness (a 55% increase
compared to the base metal).
