1. Introduction
There is a continued drive for the development of high strength
light alloys due to the strong demand to improve the energy
efﬁciency in the transportation sector. Precipitation hardened alu-
minium alloy is one of the commercially available light alloys
for structural applications. The maximum tensile yield strength
is ∼800 MPa for the Al–Cu–Li based Weldalite alloy [1], which is
attributed to the uniform dispersion of T1 (Al2CuLi) and /H9254′(Al3Li)
precipitates. It is intriguing to explore the possibility of strength up
to 1 GPa in Al-based alloys. To open up the possibility, one promis-
ing technique is the reﬁnement of grain size down to a nanometer
scale.
Al–TM systems (TM: transition metals) have been studied for
a long time as potential heat resistant light alloys. However,
the limited solubility of TM in Al hinders the precipitation of
sufﬁcient amount of secondary phases by conventional process-
ing routes. The effort to extend the solubility of TM in an /H9251-Al
matrix was made by non-equilibrium processing such as rapid
solidiﬁcation, mechanical alloying and electron beam deposition.
These non-equilibrium processing routes also resulted in the suc-
cessful fabrication of “nanocrystalline” supersaturated alloys or

nanocomposites with ultra-high strength [2–6]. Recently, Sasaki
et al. [7] reported the successful fabrication of bulk nanocrystalline
Al–5 at%Fe alloy by the combination of mechanical alloying (MA)
and spark plasma sintering (SPS). This alloy was strengthened by
the nanocrystalline grains and high volume fraction of intermetallic
particles, and exhibited the ultra-high strength over 1 GPa at room
temperature and 500 MPa at 350 ◦C with large plastic strain in com-
pression [7]. However, none of these alloys showed tensile ductility
due to the high volume fraction of the intermetallic particles.
One way to enhance the ductility retaining the high strength
is to reduce the high volume fraction of the brittle Al6Fe and
Al13Fe4 phases while keeping the nanocrystalline grain structure.
The previous investigations suggested that Zr worked as an effec-
tive alloying element as a grain reﬁner in conventional Al alloys and
Al–Fe–Zr alloy processed by powder metallurgy process [1,2,8,9].
Therefore, the substitution of Fe with a small amount of Zr could
be a promising way to reduce the volume fraction of the brittle
intermetallics and retain nanocrystalline structure to achieve high
strength with a small amount of the alloying element (<1.5 at%). In
this work, we have investigated the effect of Zr additions on the
microstructures and mechanical properties of mechanically milled
and spark plasma sintered Al–Fe alloys.

4. Discussion
In
 this work, we have fabricated bulk nanocrystalline
Al–Fe–Zr alloys processed by MA and SPS, and investigated their
processing–structure–property relationship. The Al–1Fe–0.5Zr
alloy fabricated by the optimized processing condition exhibited
relatively high compressive yield strength of ∼854 MPa with 19%
plastic strain. Although the strength is lower than that of the
Al–5 at%Fe alloy exhibiting 1 GPa class strength [7], it is interesting
to note the high strength up to 850 MPa has been achieved from
the Al-based alloy containing only 1.5 at.% of solute elements.
The compressive yield strength for the Al–1Fe sample was sig-
niﬁcantly lower than the yield strength reported by Sasaki et al.
[12] for the Al–5Fe samples. The addition of Zr enhanced the com-
pressive yield strength but the optimized yield strength for the SPS
processing
 at 420 ◦C was somewhat lower than that observed for
the Al–5Fe alloy, 854 MPa. Niu et al. [13] reported the tensile yield
strength of ∼500 MPa for the Al–2.5Fe–1Zr alloy following mechan-
ical milling and hot extrusion processing, which is signiﬁcantly
lower than the compressive yield strengths observed in this inves-
tigation for all the Al–1Fe–(0-1)Zr alloys. The compressive yield
strengths reported by Sirinivasarao et al. [11] for Al–Zr alloys was
also lower than that reported here for signiﬁcantly higher concen-
trations of Zr.
The microstructure characterization results indicate that the
high strength obtained in the present sample is mainly due to
the ﬁne grained structure, and the dispersion of the intermetal-
lic phases such as Al6Fe and Al3Zr. Since the strength of the
sintered compact varies consistently with the grain size of the as-
mechanically alloyed powders, Fig. 2(a), the optimization of the

Such segregations of the alloying elements during SPS would sup-
press the grain growth together with the pinning effect by Al6Fe
and Al3Zr particles that are precipitated out during SPS [12,14,15].
The
 joule heating at the early stages of the SPS process would
result in localized melting of the powder surfaces, leading the for-
mation of coarse grained Al. The reduced fraction of the coarse
grained Al is considered to contribute to the higher compressive
yield strength of the Al–1Fe–0.5Zr alloy. The sample subjected to
SPS processing at 400 ◦C showed very small plastic strain for the
yield strength of 900 MPa. This may be because the volume fraction
of the coarse grained Al in the sample sintered at 400 ◦C is signiﬁ-
cantly lower than that that for the sample processed at 420 ◦C. The
previous work [7] reported a bimodal grain size distribution with
nano-scale grains and coarse grains were required to achieve high
plastic strains.

5. Conclusions
Bulk nano-crystalline Al–1Fe–0.5Zr alloys fabricated by
mechanical alloying and spark plasma sintering exhibited high
compressive yield strength of 854 MPa with a plastic strain of
19% at room temperature and 455 MPa and 16.7% at 250 ◦C. The
high strength and high plastic strains were attributed to the
presence of high density of nano-crystalline grains with grain size
of 65 nm and a small volume faction of coarse grains distributed
through the microstructure and a ﬁne distribution of Al6Fe and
Al3Zr precipitates forming in the inter granular regions. Control
of mechanical alloying process and SPS condition can be utilized
to tailor the mechanical properties of nano-crystalline Al alloys
through control of the microstructure.
