abstract
The effects of cryomilling on the microstructures and high temperature mechanical properties of oxide
dispersion-strengthened (ODS) steel were examined. Cryomilling was newly tried on this ODS steel to
control oxides, grains, and dislocation microstructures. Fe–14Cr–3W–0.4Ti (wt.%) alloy powder and
0.3 wt.%Y2O3 powder were mixed and were mechanically alloyed (MA) through ball milling at each of
room temperature (RT) and /C0150 /C176C and then hot isostatic pressing (HIP), hot rolling, and annealing pro-
cesses were implemented to manufacture two types of ODS ferritic steel, K1 (RT) and K4 ( /C0150 /C176C). Oxide
particles were shown to be ﬁner and more uniformly distributed in K4 (5–10 nm size distribution) than in
K1 (average size 30 nm). The two alloys were subjected to high temperature compression (RT /C24900 /C176C)
tests. K4 represented higher yield strength under all temperature conditions. However, K4 showed rapid
strength decreases at high temperatures exceeding 700 /C176C and showed similar levels of strengths to K1 at
900 /C176C. This is considered attributable to the fact that although cryomilling increased the number density
of oxide particles, it simultaneously reduced grain sizes too much, so that grain boundary weakening at
high temperatures could not be sufﬁciently prevented.

1. Introduction
The advanced oxide dispersion-strengthened (ODS) steel is a
material consisting of the Fe matrix in which several-nano to sev-
eral tens of nano-sized oxide particles are uniformly dispersed at
high number densities, which is attracting great attention as a
structural material for generation IV reactors and fusion reactors
because its resistance to high temperature creep and neutron irra-
diation damage is quite excellent [1–5]. In addition, because of its
excellent high-temperature mechanical properties and its charac-
teristics, such as excellent corrosion and oxidation resistance, it
is expected to be applied as a material for the heat-resistant
components of various gas turbines and engines for which the
enhancement of heat resistance and durability limits is required.
That is, currently, studies to develop advanced ODS steel are being
actively conducted because the possibility of demand for this
material is shown not only in the areas of future-type nuclear
power and nuclear fusion systems, but also in various other
industrial ﬁelds such as automobiles, aviation, power generation,
and munitions [6].
In general, ODS steel is manufactured by mixing raw material
powder with Y 2O3 powder, mechanically alloying (MA) the mix-
ture through ball milling, and consolidating it through hot extru-
sion or hot isostatic pressing (HIP) [1]. Here, the MA and
consolidating processes are the main processes that determine
the properties of ODS steel. Through the MA, Y
2O3 powder is disso-
ciated into the matrix and alloyed with the raw material powder.
Thereafter, in the consolidating process, it meets the alloy element
(Ti) in the matrix to form nano-oxide particles (Y 2Ti2O7,Y 2TiO5)
[7–9]. These oxide particles are thermally and mechanically very
stable to suppress the movements of dislocations and grain bound-
aries (to improve high-temperature strength and creep properties)
under high-temperature stress. In particular, these particles, smal-
ler than 10 nm in size, exist coherent with the matrix to reduce
misﬁt strain with matrix, thereby maintaining the alloy stably at
high temperatures and increasing resistance to void swelling and
helium embrittlement due to neutron irradiation [10–13].
However, dispersing Y2O3 uniformly in the matrix through mill-
ing is a quite complicated and difﬁcult process that very sensitively
reacts not only to the properties of raw material powder, but also
to milling conditions. If the MA process is not optimized, Y 2O3 will
not be uniformly distributed to show a rapid growth of grains at

for a long time, and great amounts of heat are generated in pro-

microstructures of ODS powder and the alloy and thus may consid-
erably affect high-temperature mechanical properties, which are
among the most important properties of ODS steel, no studies on
this matter have been reported thus far.
In particular, if MA is implemented under the cooling conditions
of cryogenic temperatures, high-impact energy will be delivered to
the powder so that grains and oxide particles can become ﬁner and
evener in size, and oxide particles can be distributed uniformly,
leading to more-excellent strengthening effects [16,17]. However,
due to increases in brittle fractures, it may become easier to have
inﬂows of impurities from the milling media, or the interfaces
between powder particles may become unstable. In addition, as
diffusion is suppressed, the solubility of alloy elements in matrix
may become lower, leading to unexpected effects on the high-tem-
perature mechanical properties of ODS steel.
Therefore, the present study was intended to examine the high-
temperature mechanical (compression) properties of ODS steels
(RT milling material: K1, /C0150 /C176C milling material: K4) manufac-
tured through MA under the cooling conditions of RT and
/C0150 /C176C, which are extremely contrasting temperatures, and to
analyze the effects of microstructure factors on the mechanical
properties to ﬁgure out the correlations between cryomilling and
high-temperature deformation/fracture behavior.
2. Experimental procedure
The raw material powder used in the present study was an alloy
powder with a chemical composition of Fe–14Cr–3 W–0.4Ti (wt.%)
manufactured through gas-atomization into globular shapes with
particle sizes in a range of 50–100 lm. This raw material powder
was mixed with 0.3 wt.%Y 2O3 powder with particle sizes in a range
of 30–50 nm, and the mixture was put into a milling chamber to
perform mechanical alloying (MA). The MA was implemented for
40 h in a highly pure Ar (99.999 wt.%) gas atmosphere at each of
RT and /C0150 /C176C temperature conditions (±5 /C176C temperature devia-
tion). At this time, ball milling was done in dry states, the ball-to-
powder weight ratio was 25, the milling container and the grinding
media used were made of AISI 52-100 hardened steel, and the rota-
tion speed was set to 100 rpm. Liquid nitrogen was used as a refrig-
erant for the /C0150 /C176C temperature condition, and the liquid
nitrogen was made to ﬂow outside the milling chamber so that it
would not come into direct contact with the powder. Temperatures
were monitored using a thermocouple connected to the inner wall
of the milling chamber. Thereafter, the mechanically alloyed pow-
der was put into a mild steel can made of SUS316, and 1 Pa level
vacuum state was created in the can at a temperature of 400 /C176C,
the can was sealed, and the powder was consolidated through
HIP at 1100 /C176C. Finally, the material was hot rolled at 1150 /C176C with
a thickness reduction ratio of 50% and annealed at 1100 /C176Ct o

HIP is applied under such conditions, reﬁned grains are produced
by the change of substructure (or recrystallization), and at the
same time the evenly distributed oxide particles generate a pin-

Fig. 3 shows the results of the XRD phase analysis of the two
alloys in which both the K1 and K4 show only a-ferrite single-
phase. At this time, Y 2O3 or other secondary phases were not
detected in XRD results. The reason for this has been reported to
be the fact that dissolution into the matrix, amorphization or for-
mation of nano-sized grains and residual microstrain due to lattice
distortion occurred due to severe deformation during milling, so
that the intensity of XRD peaks became lower and peak broadening
appeared [8,9]. Therefore, in actual microstructures, in addition to
nano-oxide particles, there may exist Y 2O3 that have not been com-
pletely dissolved or other secondary phases reported by many
other papers [19,20], such as metal carbide and Cr oxide.
TEM and EDS analyses were conducted to identify the existence
of secondary phases, and the results are shown in Fig. 4. As can be
seen in the TEM images of the K1 in Fig. 4 (a) and K4 in Fig. 4 (b),
dark phases (white arrows) are located on the grain boundaries
of both alloys, and the sizes are several hundred nm in the case
of the K1 and several tens of nm in the case of the K4. The dark
phases were analyzed using EDS, and according to the results,
the relevant dark phases were identiﬁed as W carbide in most
cases and Cr oxide in some cases. First, metal carbides such as W
carbide are known to be formed at temperatures just below the
temperature at which solidiﬁcation begins and cannot be regarded
to have been formed by combining the W solid-dissolved in the
matrix and C. Therefore, the W carbide is assumed to have come
in from grinding media. On the other hand, the Cr oxide is quite
likely to have existed in the initial raw material powder and seems
to have been distributed and additionally formed in the matrix
while undergoing MA, consolidation, and following processes.
The existence of secondary phases is evidence that ODS materials
are very sensitive to not only process conditions such as milling
temperatures, but also contamination. These secondary phases
may weaken grain boundaries (W carbide) or suppress the forma-
tion of nano-oxide particles (Cr oxide) to affect the RT and high-
temperature mechanical properties of the two materials to some
extent.
Jump-ratio imaging was conducted for each of the elements (Fe,
Y, Ti, O) using EF-TEM to identify the existence, sizes, and distribu-
tion of nano-oxide particles, and the results are shown in Fig. 5
[17]. Nano-oxide particles formed by Y, Ti, and O elements in the
form of clusters can be seen in the K1 in Fig. 5 (a) and K4 in
Fig. 5 (b). As for the sizes and distribution of oxide particles, in
the case of the K1, oxide particles of approximately 30 nm are spar-
sely distributed, and in the case of the K4, very small oxide parti-
cles not larger than 5 nm are distributed in grains, and those
larger than 10 nm are distributed on grain boundaries showing rel-
atively high number densities. Meanwhile, the SANS analysis, per-
formed to quantitatively measure the number density of oxide
particles, discovered that K1 had a number density of
8.6 ⁄ 1021 m/C03 and K4 of 8.8 ⁄ 1022 m/C03. The ﬁndings conﬁrmed
that K4 had more oxide particles in unit volume compared to K1.
In this case too, ﬁner Y 2O3 particles are deemed to have been
distributed more uniformly in the matrix in the case of /C0150 /C176C
milling than in the case of RT milling because of the differences
in impact energy that resulted from milling temperatures. How-
ever, since ﬁne Y–Ti–O oxide particles are not formed during MA
but during consolidation, and the formation mechanism has not
yet been clearly identiﬁed, just the dissolution and dispersion of
Y2O3 are not sufﬁcient to explain the sizes and distribution of
nano-oxide particles. Fu reported that vacancies in the lattice
would bind with interstitial oxygen atoms to form oxygen-vacancy
pairs, and these pairs would draw elements with oxygen afﬁnity
such as Y and Ti to cause the formation of O-, Y-, and Ti-enriched
nanoclusters [21]. Meanwhile, Xu, et al. reported that the diffusion
would be suppressed due to low temperatures so that the mobility
of vacancies would decrease, leading to decreases in the quantity
of vacancies that would be dissipated due to dislocation or grain
boundaries, resulting in increases in the concentration of excess
vacancies [22,23]. Based on these reports, the abovementioned
results ( Fig. 5 ) can be interpreted as follows: when MA was con-
ducted at the /C0150 /C176C milling temperature, the increase in the
quantity of oxygen-vacancy pairs due to increases in the density
of vacancies has caused the formation of more nano-oxide particles
during consolidation while increasing the entire binding energy to
prevent more coarsening from occurring during consolidation, hot
rolling, or annealing at high temperatures, consequently leading to
ﬁner and uniform distribution of nano-oxide particles in the K4.

reported to be Hall–Petch strengthening, Orowan strengthening,
dislocation forest strengthening, and solid solution strengthening

mechanisms. The K4 is judged to show higher strength than the

higher. However, this corresponds to only RT to 500 /C176C, at which
the strength of K4 is maintained in any event, and it is considered
that at higher temperatures where the K4 shows remarkable

mechanisms decrease or other microstructural factors affect the
high-temperature deformation and fracture behavior.

In general, grain boundaries have the largest effects on metal
materials’ high-temperature deformation and fracture behaviors.
Grain boundaries become thermally unstable at high tempera-
tures. If stress is imposed at high temperatures, grain boundary
sliding and intergranular fractures will be caused. In particular,
in the case of steel, the embrittlement of grain boundaries appears
quite remarkably at high temperatures because of the existence of
a segregation between the alloy and impurities or secondary
phases on the grain boundaries [27,28]. In the case of materials
manufactured through processes such as MA, trace elements such
as C and N form thin ﬁlms on grain boundaries that adversely affect
high-temperature mechanical properties [6]. Therefore, the unique
fracture surface of the K4 is thought to be a type of intergranular
fracture, and the modes of the intergranular fractures are judged
to vary according to temperature. Fig. 13 shows the observation
of the fracture surface of the K4 at a high magniﬁcation. In the case
of the fracture surface of the 500 /C176C compression specimen, dim-
ples with very sharp fracture tips are observed, and particles
(white arrow) assumed to be secondary phases can be seen to be
located inside of some dimples. The fracture surfaces of 700 /C176C
compression specimens and higher temperature compression
specimens show very big differences. Given the grain size

rather a cause of strength degradation at high temperatures. Since
these alloys are ferritic steel manufactured through MA, grain
boundaries are weaker at high temperatures, and the high fraction
of high angle boundaries causes grain boundary sliding more easily

4. Summary and conclusions
In the present study, the microstructures and high-temperature
mechanical (compression) properties of ODS steels manufactured
through MA under the cooling conditions of RT (K1) and /C0150 /C176C
(K4) were examined, and the following conclusions were obtained:
1. The grain sizes of K4 (300 nm) were smaller and more uniform
than those of K1 (0.5, 1.5 lm), and the misorientation distribu-
tion of K4 was shown to have higher fractions of 5 /C176or lower
low-angle boundaries and 55 /C176or higher high-angle boundaries
than K1. Whereas the pore sizes of the two alloys were shown
to be similar at approximately 2.25–2.3
lm, porosity showed
a very big difference with 0.102% in the K1 and 0.968% in the
K4. Nano-oxide particles existed in grains and on grain bound-
aries in the form of Y–Ti–O nanoclusters. The sizes of nano-
oxide particles were approximately 30 nm in the case of the
K1 and 5 nm or smaller in grains and at least 10 nm on grain
boundaries in the case of the K4. The K4 showed much higher
number densities and more uniform distribution than the K1.
In addition, W carbide and Cr oxide were located on some grain
boundaries of both alloys.
2. According to the results of high-temperature compression tests,
although the K4 showed higher yield strength than the K1
under all temperature conditions, the yield strength of the K4
rapidly decreased at 500 /C176C and higher temperatures and
became a little higher than that of the K1 from 700 /C176C. The K1
showed traces of the occurrence of uniform deformation with-
out any internal cracks, whereas the K4 showed the propaga-
tion of internal cracks along the pores, which are stress
concentration sites that formed macro surface cracks. Mean-
while, when fracture surfaces of macro surface cracks of the
K4 were observed, the form of intergranular fractures was
shown at all temperatures, and as temperatures increased, the
number of dimples decreased and intergranular fractures
appeared more clearly.
3. Therefore, the effects of cryomilling make K4 have ﬁner grains
and higher oxide particle number density, thereby having excel-
lent RT strength, but the hard matrix and high porosity
adversely affect ductility and toughness so that fracture trends
are present uniform at high temperatures. In addition, the
excessively small minimum grain sizes compared to oxide par-
ticles’ number density maximizes grain boundary areas to cause
strength degradation due to grain boundary weakening at high
temperatures (relatively low temperatures). Therefore, when
manufacturing ODS steel, selecting an MA cooling condition
(milling temperature) at appropriately low temperatures rather
than extremely low temperatures should make a balance
between grain sizes and oxide particle number density to
improve strength as well as having good effects on ductility
and toughness.
