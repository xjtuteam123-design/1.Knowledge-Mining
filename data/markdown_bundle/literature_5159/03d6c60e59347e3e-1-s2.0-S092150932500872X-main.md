1. Introduction
Multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) are a new type of alloy 
consisting of three or more metallic elements as principal constituents 
( > 5 %, at%) [ 1 – 3 ]. These alloys leverage high configurational entropy 
to inhibit intermetallic compound formation, promoting the develop -
ment of a uniform solid solution phase [ 4 – 6 ]. This attribute endows 
MPEAs with significant application potential. [ 7 , 8 ]. Currently, the 
design concepts for MPEAs are merging with traditional alloys [ 9 , 10 ], 
such as aluminum-based [ 11 , 12 ] and titanium-based MPEAs [ 13 – 15 ]. 
These MPEAs can simultaneously leverage the advantages of the matrix 
alloys and high-entropy alloys to achieve outstanding performance in 
specific areas [ 16 ].
High-temperature titanium-based alloys are defined as titanium al -
loys capable of sustained operation at temperatures exceeding 300 
◦
C. 
[ 17 – 20 ]. In the development history of high-temperature titanium al -
loys, near- α titanium alloys were the first to be put into use, capable of 
long-term operation at 300 – 450 
◦
C [ 21 – 26 ]. However, as the tempera -
ture requirements for high-temperature alloys in applications such as 
aircraft engine blades increased [ 27 , 28 ], researchers gradually devel -
oped high-temperature γ -TiAl alloys [ 29 ] and Ti
2
AlNb alloys [ 30 ]. The 
γ -TiAl alloy predominantly utilizes the hard and brittle tetragonal γ 
phase to bolster its high-temperature strength and is now widely used in 
engine blades [ 29 , 31 , 32 ]. However, a major drawback is its lack of 
plasticity at room temperature [ 33 , 34 ], resulting in extremely poor 
workability [ 35 , 36 ].

are included in the supplementary materials. After each hot rolling pass, 
the ingot was returned to the furnace and held at temperature for 10

Following the final hot rolling pass, the rolled sheet was reheated and

Section 3 presents the tests and analysis of the microstructural evo -

high-temperature deformation, the alloy primarily undergoes solid so -

strengthening, accompanied by solid-state phase transformations. 
Dislocation strengthening is one of the important strengthening 
mechanisms of the alloy. During plastic deformation of the Ti61Si0.4 
alloy, a large number of dislocations nucleate and multiply, and in -
teractions such as entanglement occur between dislocations ( Fig. 16 (a)), 
or dislocations are impeded by precipitates/crystal boundaries, which 
blocks their movement and enhances the strength of the alloy. However, 
at elevated temperatures, the accelerated diffusion rate of atoms fills 
many dislocations generated during plastic deformation, leading to a 
weakening of the dislocation strengthening effect at high temperatures. 
In addition, as a multi-principal element alloy, the BCC lattice of the 
Ti61Si0.4 alloy contains a large number of solute atoms. Differences in 
size and elastic modulus between the solute atoms and titanium atoms 
lead to local distortions in the lattice and the formation of stress fields, 
which require greater stress for dislocations to slip through the lattice 
during deformation, thereby achieving solid solution strengthening. 
Solid solution strengthening is one of the primary strengthening mech -
anisms of the Ti61Si0.4 alloy, but at high temperatures, the increased 
motion of atoms reduces the pinning effect of distorted lattices on dis -
locations, thus decreasing the effect of solid solution strengthening. 
Moreover, extensive precipitation of secondary phases further di -
minishes the impact of solid solution strengthening.
