Atomistic methods are used to anneal two body-centered cubic (BCC) chemically complex alloys (CCAs) 
in order to assess the effect of chemical short-range order on alloy strength. The two alloys, a model 
quaternary Co 16.67 Fe 36.67 Ni 16.67 Ti 30 and ternary Nb 33.33 Ti 33.33 Zr 33.33 , are represented using simple Zhou in- 
teratomic potentials. Chemically random cells are annealed at temperatures 65% of the average melting 
temperature of the individual elements using a Monte Carlo approach, and the critical stress required to 
move a/2[111] screw dislocations in the initial and annealed cells are estimated using molecular dynam- 
ics simulations. It is shown that annealing leads to a softening of the BCC quaternary alloy relative to the 
random state, especially for deformation at low temperatures. On the other hand, short-range order has 
minimal effect on solid solution strengthening in the ternary alloy. These results are modeled using an 
extension of the Suzuki model of substitutional solid solution strengthening developed for BCC chemi- 
cally complex alloys. Here the model is modiﬁed to account for the effects of chemical short-range order. 
Good agreement is shown between the model results and direct atomistic simulation data. The model 
presented should be useful in predicting BCC CCAs with adequate high temperature strength, which will 
accelerate development of high temperature, high strength BCC alloys for aerospace applications.

1. Introduction 
Recently there has been a renewed interest in refractory metal 
alloys for high temperature structural applications [ 1 , 2 ]. New alloy 
development strategies, emerging from concepts developed in the 
High Entropy Alloy community, are being applied to a broad range 
of single-phase solid solutions, as well as precipitation strength- 
ened alloys [1-4] . Early studies of several refractory complex con- 
centrated alloys (RCCA) produce excellent high temperature spe- 
ciﬁc strength as compared to commercial Ni-based alloys. Some of 
these alloys also have excellent room temperature (compressive) 
ductility which is a requirement for low cost ingot processing [1- 
5] . 
Designing such materials requires a better understanding of the 
relative contributions of different solution hardening mechanisms 
as a function of temperature. This is particularly important given 
the complexity and range of the multivariate composition space 
presented by RCCA’s [1] . Several groups have developed solution

ies. Such studies suggest that random distributions of solutes, at

istry and the changing local strain ﬁeld. Depending on the chem- 
istry and the volume sampled, the variations in the equilibrium 
core structure range from slight asymmetries, favoring a particu- 
lar (110) plane, to full kinks on different (110) planes. This behav- 
ior has been veriﬁed qualitatively using atomistics in other RCCA’s 
over a wide range of chemistries [6-11] . Recently, a ﬁrst princi- 
ples method (Density Functional Theory) was used to verify the 
presence of such partial kinks on screw dislocations in NbTiZr and 
Nb 17 Ti 33 Zr 50 [12] . In that study, deviations from straight compact 
cores were also shown to occur in response to chemical changes 
along dislocation line. Several groups have incorporated the effects 
on classical BCC deformation mechanisms, due to the variations in 
dislocation core structure, into analytical models of solution hard- 
ening [6-11] .

Atomistic studies of RCCAs to date have assumed that a random 
distribution of elements over a large cell would provide a good ap- 
proximation of the alloy ground state. While this is a reasonable 
starting point for the study of deformation in a chemically complex 
alloy it is likely to be a poor approximation of the chemical distri- 
bution for alloys slated for any high temperature application. First, 
engineering alloys are normally annealed at temperatures slightly 
above their expected use temperatures. The heat treatment is typi- 
cally designed to improve homogenization of the chemical and mi- 
crostructural distribution. Here homogenization implies reaching a 
reasonable approximation of chemical equilibrium such that the 
distribution that will be maintained for long periods while the ma- 
terial and component are in service. Thus realistic models of so- 
lution strengthening in a complex solid solution will need to in- 
clude some assessment of the role of chemical short range order 
(CSRO). Second, it is generally accepted that a random chemical 
distribution in a chemically complex alloy will include volumes of 
high enthalpy that would be unstable with respect to annealing. 
As the number of solute species increases the likelihood of produc- 
ing outliers in the distribution of solute-dislocation interactions in- 
creases, and in some cases the outliers would likely dominate any 
model for solution hardening. Atomic conﬁguration with high en- 
thalpy are likely to interact strongly with the dislocation because 
the shearing of the lattice can reduce (or increase) the local en- 
ergy. While not all complex concentrated alloys will produce CSRO 
the possibility should be considered before any realistic model of 
solution hardening can be assessed. 
Previous work in the face-centered-cubic complex concentrated 
alloys has shown that the overall effect of annealing on alloy 
strength is balance between the energy of the emerging diffuse 
anti-phase boundaries (ordering as well as segregation) and the 
decrease in the solid solution strengthening [13] . The ﬁrst pro- 
duces an athermal strengthening mechanism associated with the 
CSRO introduced at a speciﬁc annealing temperature. The second 
solution hardening mechanism is thermally activated and can be 
represented as the variance in the distribution of solute disloca- 
tion interaction energies throughout the sampled volume of mate- 
rial. Upon annealing this variance is expected to decrease, thereby 
lowering the solid solution strengthening. A similar balance be- 
tween the effects of CSRO and classic solution hardening are likely 
present in the RCCA’s, though the details of the mechanism may 
differ. For exam ple, using ab initio methods, Sheng and co-workers 
have shown that when MoNbTaW is annealed the variance in dis- 
location energies in the matrix decreases [14] . Further, the variance 
decreases with lower annealing temperatures. 
In the compositionally complex BCC metals recent work has fo- 
cused on solution hardening mechanisms due to solute-dislocation 
interactions increasing the stress required to move kinks, and the 
strengthening effects of jogs (dipoles) resulting from kink colli- 
sions along extended screw dislocations [7-10] . Here we focus on 
strengthening mechanisms associated with chemical short range 
order [13] in two BCC complex-concentrated alloys, Co 16.6 -Fe 36.6 - 
Ni 16.6 -Ti 30 and equiatomic NbTiZr [ 6 , 7 ]. Our goal is to extend cur- 
rent models of solution hardening in the RCCA’s to include the ef- 
fects of CSRO and to validate the approach using atomistic molec- 
ular dynamics over a range of temperatures. To that end simu- 
lation cells containing random chemical distributions of elements 
are numerically annealed in the temperature range of standard ho- 
mogenization processing using Hybrid Molecular-dynamics/Monte- 
Carlo simulations. Interatomic interactions are described by pair- 
wise (EAM) potentials due to Johnson and Zhou [ 15 , 16 ]. Annealing 
produces chemical short range order that increases with decreas- 
ing annealing temperature. Moreover, it is expected that anneal- 
ing homogenizes regions of high enthalpy as chemical species ar- 
range into more compact conﬁgurations, and atom pairs that prefer 
to be nearest neighbors form small clusters lowering the system

The Large-scale molecular dynamics massively parallel simula- 
tor (LAMMPS) [ 19 ] is used to perform the Monte Carlo and Molec- 
ular Dynamics calculations described in this work. The simula- 
tions employed Zhou and Johnson interatomic potentials devel- 
oped for the elements: Co, Fe, Nb, Ni, Ti, and Zr [ 15 , 16 ]. Simu- 
lation cells were created with an underlying BCC lattice using a 
dislocation coordinate system: X = [1 1 1], Y = [-1 -1 2], and Z = 
[1 -1 0] as shown in Fig. 1 . The simulation cell dimensions are

lattice constant a = 2.96 ˚A and a = 3.39 ˚A, respectively [ 5 , 6 ]. Peri- 
odic boundary conditions are enforced along the dislocation line 
direction < 111 > , while the cell boundaries in the Y and Z direction 
have free surface boundary conditions. Perfect screw dislocations 
are inserted in between two Z planes by displacing the atoms ac-

cal short-range order (CSRO) has minimal effect on the elastic con-

2.3. Applying stress and Identifying the critical stress 
The stress at a given temperature was found using the fol- 
lowing procedure [ 6 , 7 , 13 ]. The simulation cell with the disloca- 
tion is scaled by a thermal expansion factor according to the de- 
sired temperature. A pure shear stress is then applied ( τxz on 
the Z = (1-10) glide plane; see Fig. 1 ) by straining the atoms to a 
desired ﬁxed shear strain and then holding the boundary atoms 
along Y and Z (an outer layer of atoms with a thickness equal to 
2R cut , where R cut is the cut-off radius of the interatomic potentials) 
ﬁxed while the rest of the atoms are evolved using a stochastic 
(Langevin) thermostat for 5 ps with a thermalization timescale of 
1 ps [13] . This procedure allows for fast thermalization of the vi- 
brational modes in the strained stress while reducing the breath- 
ing effects from the open surfaces. The thermostat is subsequently 
switched to a Nose-Hoover thermostat using a chained thermostat- 
ing scheme (N = 5) to minimize oscillations at a target temperature. 
To ﬁnd the critical stress, this procedure was applied for different 
windows of shear stresses until the breakaway stress from a dis- 
tribution of pinning points along the entire dislocation line during 
a 100 ps simulation[13]. As described in the next section, a dis- 
location moving through the BCC lattice leaves debris trailing the 
dislocation motion. Such debris provides an unequivocal signal to 
determine if the dislocation has moved from its initial position or 
not. 
3. Analytic Model 
3.1. Suzuki Model of Substitutional Solid Solution Strengthening for 
BCC Random Alloys 
Suzuki developed a model for substitutional solid solution 
strengthening in BCC alloys considering well characterized mecha- 
nisms of plastic ﬂow in these materials. Such a model is described 
in detail elsewhere [20-24] . For concentrations and temperatures 
above a critical concentration and temperature, kink motion on 
screw dislocations rather than nucleation, can be considered to be 
a rate controlling mechanism. Such a model has been extended for 
the case of RCCAs in previous publications [ 7-9 , 25 ]. In this sce- 
nario, kinks overcoming solutes move between hard edge dipole 
obstacles formed on the screw dislocation. The hard dipole obsta- 
cles are formed on the screw dislocation during its motion. It is 
envisaged that kinks, which can spontaneously form due to solute 
interactions at the core of screw dislocations, gliding on different 
{110} planes collide and form vacancy or interstitial jogs. During 
motion at low temperatures the screw dislocation drags these jogs, 
leading to the formation of vacancy and interstitial dipoles. In or- 
der to introduce the effects of CSRO, the next section, we brieﬂy 
recapitulate this model.

the end of this section.

4.1. Molecular Dynamics Simulations 
Fig. 5 a shows the critical resolved shear stress required 
to move a/2[111] screw dislocations in the quaternary 
(Co 16.67 Fe 36.67 Ni 16.67 Ti 30 ) and ternary (Nb 33.33 Ti 33.33 Zr 33.33 ) alloys 
as a function of temperature obtained using atomistic molecular 
dynamics simulations using simple Zhou potentials. Potentials of 
this form use an analytic expression for the cross potential of any 
two chemical species, and because the cross potentials reference 
only the simple metal data they are best suited for modeling solid 
solution behavior. The critical resolved shear stresses are shown 
for both the random and annealed state. The shear stresses shown 
for the random state are close to results obtained in refs.[ 5 , 6 ] for 
the quaternary and ternary alloys. Short range order has minimal 
effect on the critical resolved shear stresses for moving screw 
dislocations in the ternary alloy. In the quaternary alloy, at low 
temperatures (5 and 100K), short range order tends to decrease 
the critical resolved shear stresses relative to the random state. 
Solid solution hardening in the BCC metals occurs in part due 
to kinks on different glide planes colliding and forming vacancy 
and interstitial dipoles. Atomistic simulations can provide some 
insights on the nature of this behavior in complex alloys. 
Once screw dislocations form edge dipoles from kink collisions 
these defects are expected to be dragged out, producing debris 
characteristic of edge dipoles. Atomistic simulations (current and 
previous work) indicate that width of these dipoles, h in Fig. 4 , 
are approximately one step in height. Identifying these volumes 
using conventional Burger’s circuit analysis, for example the Dis- 
location Extraction Algorithm [29] , is problematic because of the 
narrow height of the dipoles. However, the volumes may be iden- 
tiﬁed by the vacancies and interstitials left in the wake for the 
dragged out dipoles. An example of the formation and dragging of 
edge dipoles is shown in a time series of close up images in Fig. 6 
for Nb 33.33 Ti 33.33 Zr 33.33 , at 5K and an applied stress of 0.02 μ. The 
screw dislocation moves by passing kinks until pinning points form 
at around 2.5 ps, the dislocation bows around the defects (2.5- 
12.5ps) and then drags out vacancies (red) and interstitials (blue) 
(15-17.5 ps) eventually pinching off the dipoles and moving away. 
The distribution of vacancies and interstitials in deformed cells can 
provide some insights into changes in deformation behavior be- 
tween the random and annealed cells. The vacancy (red) and inter- 
stitial (blue) volumes were identiﬁed by constructing conventional 
Voronio cells and using the procedure described below. 
Fig. 7 show the Voronio volume distribution of atoms in sim-

4. Summary 
Atomistic methods were used to explore the effects of 
annealing on the solution hardening of two body-centered- 
cubic complex-concentrated alloys: a model quaternary 
Co 16.67 Fe 36.67 Ni 16.67 Ti 30 and ternary Nb 33.33 Ti 33.33 Zr 33.33. Large 
simulation cells with random distributions of these chemistries 
were annealed using a constant pressure Monte Carlo metropolis 
method at temperatures consistent with conventional metallur- 
gical practices. Atomistic molecular dynamics as a function of 
temperature (5-1200K) shows that annealing leads to a softening 
of both alloys relative to the random state with the BCC quater- 
nary alloy showing signiﬁcant softening below room temperature. 
Short-range order has minimal effect on solid solution strength- 
ening in the ternary alloy. Based on these ﬁndings one cannot 
make a general conclusion about how annealing will affect yield 
stress. However, annealing produces two changes in the chemical 
distribution that affect yield stress. First, annealing eliminates 
regions of high enthalpy that produce strong solute-dislocation 
interactions and removing these regions softens the material 
compared to a quenched (random) sample ( equation 10 ). Second, 
Chemical Short Range Order (CSRO)and segregation introduced by 
annealing produce athermal hardening as dislocations cut diffuse 
Anti Phase Boundaries ( equation 13 ). Experimentally the balance 
of these two effects is strongly dependent on the annealing 
parameters (temperature, duration and cooling rate) as well as 
the alloy composition. In order to make a predictive model for 
solid solution effects in compositionally complex alloys we have 
included the effects of CSRO into an analytic model of solution 
hardening. 
An extension of Suzuki’s model of substitutional solid solution 
strengthening, developed for BCC chemically complex alloys[7,8], 
was modiﬁed to include the effects of CSRO and used to model 
the solution hardening of these alloys. The good agreement be- 
tween the model results and direct atomistic simulation data pro- 
vides validation for the extension of the Suzuki approach for an- 
nealed systems containing chemical short range order. The new 
model shows that in the quarternary alloy, solution strengthen- 
ing decreases relative to the random state with short range or- 
der whereas part of the decrease is offset by the development of 
diffuse anti-phase boundary strengthening. The model also shows 
that solution strengthening can decrease or increase relative to the 
random state with short range order, the sign of the change de- 
pending on the interplay between Warren-Cowley coeﬃcients and 
solute-screw dislocation interaction energies. In general, if unlike 
atoms attract leading to classical short range order, there is a de- 
crease in var(E) leading to a decrease in classical solid solution 
strengthening. However, if unlike atoms repel leading to solute 
clustering or segregation, var (E) increases resulting in an increase 
in classical solid solution strengthening. The fact that solute clus- 
tering leads to an increase in strengthening in these types of al- 
loys is well known in the literature [37] . It is worth noting that 
diffuse anti-phase boundary strengthening is athermal and always 
strengthens the short range ordered state relative to the random 
state. The model presented should be useful in predicting refrac- 
tory complex concentrated alloys with adequate high temperature 
strength, which will accelerate development of high temperature, 
high strength BCC alloys for aerospace applications.
