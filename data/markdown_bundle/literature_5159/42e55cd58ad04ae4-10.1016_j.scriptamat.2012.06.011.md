Controlling reversible martensitic transformation in titanium alloys
with high strength and low elastic modulus

This paper reports on b-type titanium alloys with high b transus temperature and low martensite start temperature that display
high strength and low elastic modulus. The strategy is based on Zr and Sn additions to Ti–Nb-based alloys. The a00 martensite in Ti–
24Nb–4Zr–8Sn alloy is structurally much closer to the b phase than other alloys in terms of transformation strain and tetragonality,
allowing homogeneously nucleated reversible mechanisms to be triggered before the formation of nanoscaled equiaxed a00 martensite
by strain glass transition.

Keywords: Martensitic transformation; Titanium alloys; Elastic behavior; Strength; Lattice parameter
Reversible martensitic transformation (MT) be-
tween the b phase, which has a body-centered cubic
(bcc) structure, and the orthorhombic a00 martensite has
recently attracted great attention due to developments
of shape memory Ti alloys and multifunctional Ti alloys
with high strength and low elastic modulus [1–6]. Among
this last family of Ti alloys, Gum metals and Ti2448 alloy
(with respective chemical compositions (in wt.%) of Ti–
36Nb–2Ta–3Zr–0.3O [3] and Ti–24Nb–4Zr–8Sn [4])
have been shown to display peculiar nonlinear elasticity.
In classical cases, this behavior can usually be related to a
reversible stress-induced MT. However, as a softening
mechanism [7,8], the reversible MT fails to explain the
high ratio of strength to elastic modulus (approaching
/C24 2%) of these alloys [3,4], similar in value to bulk metal-
lic glasses with amorphous structure [9]. Besides the
above controversial issues, some interesting features con-
cerning the MT in these groups of alloys have been re-
ported recently and are summarized as follows.
Firstly, a reversible transformation leading to nano-
sized a
00 martensite with equiaxed morphology has been
observed by an in situ tensile transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) analysis of a modiﬁed Ti2448 alloy
with low Sn and O contents (Ti–24Nb–4Zr–7.5Sn–
0.08O) [10]. This martensite morphology is in sharp
contrast with that resulting from conventional MT (dis-
playing acicular morphology) but similar in shape to the
stress-induced strain glass transition observed in non-
martensitic NiTi alloys [11–13]. Additionally, the diﬀer-
ential scanning calorimetry measurements fail to detect
phase transformation peaks for both Ti2448 alloy and
non-martensitic NiTi alloys [11,14]. This may suggest
that the stress-induced strain glass mechanism could
be a possible deformation mechanism for multifunc-
tional Ti alloys, resulting in both high strength and
low elastic modulus.
Secondly, the signiﬁcant peak broadening of the
stress-induced a
00 martensite has been observed during
a number of in situ X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) experi-
ments on Ti2448 [15,16]. Synchrotron XRD measure-
ments showed that the a
00 martensite diﬀraction peaks
were strong and sharp in a modiﬁed Ti2448 alloy in
which the stress-induced MT has been proven to occur,
but very weak and broadened in Ti2448 alloy [15]. To-
gether with signiﬁcant peak shifting of both b phase
and a
00 phases, the peak separation of the two phases
is very diﬃcult to detect, resulting in large errors. Since
nanosized a00 martensite was observed during in situ
TEM analysis [10], this peak broadening may be ex-
plained by the size eﬀect. The calculation results sug-
gested that the a00 martensite displays an average size
of about /C24 2.5 nm [15]. Such a phenomenon was also

Lastly, the occurrence of the stress-induced MT is
strongly orientation dependent if measured on single
orientated crystals. Tensile tests on single-crystal Gum
metal showed that both h100 i and h111 i crystals exhibit
a classical linear elastic deformation behavior, whereas
the h110 i crystal clearly displays a pseudoelastic behav-
ior with reversible strain up to 2.5% [17]. Subsequent
analyses provided a reasonable explanation by demon-
strating that the critical stresses to induce the MT, when
loading along the h100 i and h111 i axes, were beyond
the tensile strength [18]. Tensile tests on Ti2448 alloy
showed similar deformation behavior, exhibiting a lower
elastic modulus combined with higher strength and lar-
ger reversible strain when compared to Gum metals [19].
The critical stresses to trigger the MT have been esti-
mated based on the assumption of pure shear stress
for both h100 i and h111 i loaded crystals in both tensile
and compression conditions and have been evaluated as
lower than the corresponding tensile and compressive
strength. However, no link could be established between
the pseudoelasticity and the reversible MT for Ti2448. A
possible mechanism of crystal distortion was then pro-
posed to explain this peculiar behavior [19].
The above overview suggests that the nature of the
MT in these b -type Ti alloys and its relation to the elas-
tic deformation behavior are far from clear. Since both
Gum metal and Ti2448 are Ti–Nb–Zr-based quaternary
alloys, it might be meaningful to investigate the binary
and ternary systems and to carry out a systematic anal-
ysis of the published data. In this work, it is shown that
Ti–Nb-based Ti alloys with single b phase having both
higher phase transformation ( T
b) temperature from the
b phase to the a phase and lower Ms temperature to
the a00 martensite generally display lower elastic modu-
lus. This means that the b phase should ideally have
low chemical stability with respect to a phase precipita-
tion but high chemical stability with respect to a00 mar-
tensite precipitation. This fact validates the combined
addition of neutral elements Zr and Sn as a way to max-
imize the DT (Tb–Ms) range. It will also be shown that
the a00 martensite in Ti2448 alloy is structurally much
closer to the b phase than other Ti alloys in terms of
tetragonality and transformation strain.

The data in Figure 1b suggest that a low elastic mod-

have Tb temperatures ranging from 850 to 1000 K,
retention of b phase at room temperature necessarily
creates a high level of metastability, and stress-induced
phase transformations such as a00 martensite and/or x

an applicative point of view, the key point is the critical
stresses required to induce these transformations and the
inﬂuence of the transformations on the macroscopic
deformation behavior and mechanical properties. Re-
cent studies have highlighted that the elastic softening
due to the reversible MT in b -type Ti alloys may cause
detrimental eﬀects on the stress- and strain-controlled
fatigue properties [22–24]. The suppression of the MT
or, at least, a delay in reaching the higher critical stress
is therefore crucial regarding the potential load-bearing
applications of these alloys.

To understand to what extent the stress-induced pre-
cipitation of a00 contributes to the elastic deformation of
these Ti alloys and how it actually aﬀects the strength-
to-modulus ratio, it is helpful to examine how alloying
elements modify the tetragonality of the a
00 martensite
(measured by its lattice parameter ratio, c/a) and the lat-
tice transformation strains associated with the MT (g1, g2
and g3, with reference to the well-known a00/b lattice cor-
respondence) [15,27]. The data for Ti–Nb, Ti–Nb–Zr, Ti–
Nb–Zr–Sn and Ti2448 alloys are collected in Figures 2
and 3 . In the studied composition ranges, the c/a ratios
of the ternary alloys are quite close to hexagonal close-
packed Ti (a0 martensite with hexagonal structure having
a ratio of 1.573) and decrease with the addition of alloying
elements Nb, Zr and/or Sn ( Fig. 2). Ti2448 alloys, on the
other hand, have ratios close to bcc Ti ( b phase with bcc
structure having a ratio of 1.414) and the ratios decrease
with the increase in O content from 0.15% to 0.20% and
0.40% (marked by solid stars in Fig. 2 ). The maximum
transformation strain decreases from 2.5% to 1.3% with
the increase in O content in the same range ( Fig. 3 a). It
is quite obvious that the maximum transformation strains
associated with the MT for Ti2448 alloys are much smal-
ler than for other alloys ( Fig. 3b).
The small transformation strains of the MT in Ti2448
alloys may explain some of diﬃculties encountered with
direct experimental measurements of the M
s tempera-
ture [14]. Kim et al. [27] reported a linear relationship
between Ms and the maximum transformation strain
for Ti–Nb binary alloys. If this relation holds for quater-
nary alloys, extrapolations to lower strains would yield
M
s of the Ti2448 alloys with 0.15% and 0.40% O as
/C24 150 K and /C24 80 K, respectively.
In addition, the Ms temperatures of Ti alloys can
also be decreased by grain reﬁnement [25] and sub-
grain formation [28]. These mechanisms of MT sup-
pression were shown to be eﬀective in modifying the
elastic deformation behavior of a modiﬁed Ti2448 al-
loy [29]. This alloy, with coarse-grained microstruc-
ture, displays a Ms of /C24 220 K [14] and exhibits a
“double yielding ” stress–strain curve characteristic of
stress-induced MT. After a grain-reﬁnement process,
however, the “double yielding ” disappeared, resulting
in nonlinear elasticity very similar to that of Ti2448
[28]. It is reasonable to suppose they would have iden-
tical M
s, i.e. the grain reﬁnement decreasing the Ms
/C24 70 K. Because grain reﬁnement seems to inhibit
strongly the mechanism of reversible MT upon load-
ing, some other reversible deformation mechanisms
must be taken into account to explain the signiﬁcant
occurrence of a nonlinear elastic behavior and an en-
larged recoverable strain due to grain reﬁnement of
the Ti2448–0.4O alloy (with an M
s of /C24 80 K as esti-
mated above) [30].
It is clear from the above discussion that the peculiar
elastic properties of Ti2448 alloy are associated with a
combination of high Tb (/C24 970 K) and low Ms (/C24 150 K).
In the particular case considered here, theb phase chemical
stability with respect to the a phase is low, but displays a
high level with respect to the a00 martensite, leading to
low elastic modulus and nonlinear elastic deformation
behavior, respectively. Because the diﬀusional phase trans-
formation from the b to the a phase is sluggish in Ti alloys
containing a suﬃcient amount of b stabilizers [31],t h e
complete retention of the high-temperatureb phase is easy
to achieve through alloy design strategies.
Ti2448 alloy also has a high ratio of strength to mod-
ulus, approaching that of amorphous metallic materials:
this is not compatible with the reversible MT, which is a
softening mechanism. A solution-strengthening mecha-
nism also fails to provide a satisfactory explanation be-
cause ternary alloys containing similar amounts of
solutes to Ti2448, such as Ti–32Nb–(8, 10, 12)Zr and
Ti–25.5Nb–10Sn alloys, exhibit strength in the range
of 400-450 MPa [2,20], which is only about half of the
Ti2448 strength [28]. The alloy has such a high strength

that grain reﬁnement to the nanoscale has only a slight
strengthening eﬀect [4,32]. If the MT induced at high
stress can be fully suppressed, an even higher strength
can be reached. These unexplained results point to the
possibility of new reversible deformation mechanisms
that can contribute signiﬁcantly to strengthening.
A mechanism termed “nanodisturbance” (planar
nanoscaled areas of local shear with tiny shear vectors)
was proposed to explain the plastic deformation of
Gum metal [33]. An in situ high-resolution TEM analy-
sis of a modiﬁed Ti2448 alloy found that nanodistur-
bances homogeneously nucleated reversibly during the
stress relaxation regime [10]. With further increase of
external applied stresses, they may evolve to homoge-
neously nucleated reversible dislocation loops and then
to a nanosized equiaxed a
00 martensite [10]. Because for-
mation of martensite with this peculiar morphology can
reasonably be explained by the stress-induced strain
glass transition ﬁrst found in non-martensitic NiTi al-
loys [11–13], these two reversible mechanisms before
the onset of a possible MT could provide a satisfactory
explanation for the combination of high strength and
nonlinear reversible elastic deformation seen in Ti2448.
As a consequence, heavy suppression of the MT (i.e.
making M
s as low as possible) is a ﬁngerprint of this
group of Ti alloys, and the remarkable properties of
Ti2448 may be actually achieved over a wide composi-
tional range of Ti–TM (transition metal)-based alloys
with the additions of Zr, Sn, Al, O and/or N.
Besides the above two-dimensional reversible mecha-
nisms, three-dimensional ones operating within nano-
scaled regions would also be possible. One such
mechanism is the incipient kink band [34], a disloca-
tion-based mechanism that explains the reversible defor-
mation of some layered high-strength ceramics. Another
is nanoscaled multiplane shear (NMS) [35], a defect-
based mechanism that explains the ultrahigh strength
of nanowires and nanocrystallites. The reversible NMS
is a generalization of the two-dimensional nanodistur-
bance, which has already been observed in a modiﬁed
Ti2448 alloy [10].
In summary, the b transus ( T
b) and the martensite
start temperature ( Ms) are shown to be key parameters
in determining the elastic deformation behavior and
mechanical properties of b -type Ti alloys. Eﬀective sup-
pression of the MT makes possible the occurrence of
alternative strengthening reversible deformation mecha-
nisms, such as nanodisturbance and dislocation loops.
The combined alloying addition of Zr and Sn is eﬀective
in depressing the MT and raising the b transus temper-
atures of Ti–Nb-based alloys, leading to the extraordi-
nary combination of high strength and low elastic
modulus visible in the Ti2448.
