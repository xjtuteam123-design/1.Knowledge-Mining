Refractory high-entropy alloys are promising high-temperature materials owing to their excellent soften- 
ing resistance. However, most RHEAs that can withstand softening at 1200 °C usually suffer from appar- 
ent brittleness at room temperature. Here, a high strength-ductile matching Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 eutectic 
high-entropy composite (HEC) consisting of body-centered-cubic and multi-component carbide phases 
was fabricated by arc melting. The HEC exhibits a high yield strength (1340 MPa), ultimate compres- 
sive strength (2347 MPa) and plasticity (8.90 %), which exceeds those of previously reported RHEAs and 
HECs. Quantitative calculations based on experimental observations suggest that semi-coherent phase in- 
terface plays an essential role for strength enhancement, and interfacial dependent strengthening dom- 
inates among the interface strengthening terms. Our study envisages a strategy to design strong and 
ductile HECs aiming at high-temperature applications.

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have been widely studied because 
they provide a large compositional space for designing materials 
of desired properties [1–6] . The HEAs with a face-centered-cubic 
(FCC) structure has a high ductility but low strength [ 7 , 8 ], whereas 
the HEAs with a body-centered-cubic (BCC) structure, composed of 
refractory metals such as Mo, Nb, Ta, W and V, have a remarkably 
high strength at high temperatures up to 1200 °C. However, these 
refractory-metal based HEAs (RHEAs) usually suffer from intrinsic 
brittleness upon deformation at room temperature, which poses a 
major challenge for their processing and applications [ 9 , 10 ]. Fur- 
thermore, these single-phase RHEAs also suffer from inferior casta- 
bility and severe compositional segregation during solidiﬁcation 
process [ 11 , 12 ]. 
Multiphase HEAs [ 8 , 13–19 ], including eutectic HEAs [20–24] , 
are capable of overcoming the strength-ductility trade-off owing 
to the strong interfacial strengthening and plasticity effects. Sim- 
ilarly, eutectic high-entropy composites (HECs) reinforced by sili- 
cide ceramics are developed by introducing Si to HEAs [25–27] . It 
has been widely recognized that HECs with the eutectic structure 
have favorable characteristics for high-temperature applications, 
i.e. , good casting ﬂuidity, thermal stability, and excellent high- 
temperature creep resistance [ 12 , 27 , 28 ]. In particular, we have pre-

viously developed a carbide-reinforced Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) x (x = 0.5) 
HEC with a room-temperature compressive yield strength of 1202 
MPa, ultimate strength of 2067 MPa and a retained plasticity of 
10 % [ 29 , 30 ]. However, the yield strength improvement is marginal 
compared to those of MoNbTaW (1058 MPa) and MoNbTaWV 
(1246 MPa), and a compositional optimization and mechanism of 
improving strength and ductility have not yet performed and re- 
vealed. 
This work reports that both yield strength and plasticity can be 
further improved to 1340 MPa and 8.90 % by increasing the TaC 
addition to x = 0.8 (molar ratio). The resultant Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 
HEC is compositionally and microstructurally more uniform than 
previously reported Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) x HECs, owing to that the 
composition is closer to the eutectic point with TaC addition. 
Firstly, a detailed microstructural investigation is performed to un- 
veil the room-temperature deformation mechanism. Secondly, the 
interface strengthening mechanisms in the HEC are investigated to 
clarify the mechanistic contribution of phase interface, which pro- 
vides a basis for designing HECs with desired mechanical proper- 
ties. 
High-purity Re, Mo, Nb, W and TaC powders (better than 
99.99%) were used to prepare a Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC with a 
molar ratio of 0.5: 1: 1: 1: 0.8 by arc melting. The powders were 
mixed using a low-energy mixing device for 24 h, and subse- 
quently compressed at 400 MPa for 5 min, and then melted us- 
ing an arc melting furnace under a high-purity argon atmosphere 
for ﬁve times to ensure chemical homogeneity. The resultant HEC

Fig. 1 (a) shows a compressive engineering stress-strain curve 
of the Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC. The HEC exhibits a 0.2 % off- 
set yield strength (YS) of 1340 ± 21 MPa, ultimate strength (US) 
of 2347 ± 40 MPa, and plastic strain (PS) of 8.90 ± 0.15 %, re- 
spectively. A comparison of room-temperature compressive proper- 
ties between the Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC and previously reported 
1200 °C-softening-resisted RHEAs and eutectic composites [ 9 , 30–
32 ] is displayed in Fig. 1 (b). Obviously, the Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 
HEC demonstrates a good combination of YS, US and PS compared 
with other reported RHEAs except for the TiMoNbTaWV, which 
shows a relatively higher YS of 1515 MPa but this RHEA suffers 
from softening above 1200 °C because of the addition of relatively 
a low melting-point element Ti [32] . Beside the room-temperature 
compressive properties, we have previously studied the high- 
temperature compressive properties of the Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.5 
HEC [33] , which possesses a high YS of ~9 0 1 MPa, US of 1186 
MPa at 1200 °C and exhibits a high thermal stability with an- 
nealing at 1300 °C for 168 h. Obviously, the presently studied 
HEC also possesses excellent high-temperature properties because 
of a similar chemical composition and microstructure with those 
of previously reported Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.5 HEC. Thus, the present 
Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC has demonstrated excellent compressive 
properties both at room temperature and high temperatures. Ow- 
ing to these excellent properties, this HEC has great potential to be 
used as materials in high-temperature applications, such as rocket 
engine nozzle, electron tube ﬁlament, melting electrode, etc. 
Fig. 2 (a) displays an XRD pattern of the bulk 
Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC. Two sets of diffraction peaks can be 
immediately identiﬁed by the BCC and FCC (prototype NaCl) struc-

Microstructure observations by SEM reveal the formation of eu- 
tectic structure with ﬁne lamellar spacings, as demonstrated in an 
SEM-BSE and corresponding enlarged images [ Fig. 2 (b, c)]. Chemi- 
cal spectrometric analysis by SEM-EDX further conﬁrms that the 
BCC phase is a solid solution phase with a chemical composition of 
W 33.6 Mo 21.2 Ta 16.2 Nb 13.3 Re 12.1 C 3.6 . Notably, the partitioning of car- 
bon in the BCC phase could be intrinsically introduced by addition

metric analysis. In contrast, the FCC phase is a carbide phase with 
a chemical composition of (Nb 29.5 Ta 22.8 Mo 5.4 W 4.0 Re 0.6 ) 62.3 C 37.7 ( see 
Table 1 ), and this phase is marked with multi-component carbide 
(MC) in Fig. 2 (c). The off-stoichiometric ratio of the carbide phase 
suggests that carbon atoms on the FCC lattice are deﬁcient, which 
could support the existence of carbon vacancies.

the BCC phase. The interface between the two phases after com-

To unveil the fracture mechanism of the as-cast HEC, the frac- 
tographs of the HEC were observed, as shown in Fig. 5 (a). The 
HEC shows a transgranular fracture. Locally, the MC phase reveals a

observed in the MC phase [ Fig. 5 (b)], indicating that the cracks ini- 
tiated within the hard and brittle MC carbide. Thus, the fracture 
mechanism in the HEC could be explained as follows:

Using Eq. (3) , the overall interface barrier strength τ int from 
BCC to MC is calculated to be ~7 . 9 -8 . 6 GPa, in which the interface 
dependent strengthening modes ( τ K + τ misﬁt + τ ch ) dominate the 
overall strengthening, and magnitude of the stacking faults mis- 
match strengthening ( τ ch ) is the largest one. Compared with the 
strengthening by BCC-FCC HEA interface (~ 3.8 - 4 GPa) and by 
the conventional BCC-FCC interfaces (~ 0.3 - 1.1 GPa) [15] , the τ int 
value in the present HEC is signiﬁcantly larger, indicating a good 
strengthening combination by BCC and carbide phases in the HEC. 
In addition, because the dislocations in the BCC phase are repelled 
by the interface (image force τ K > 0), the dislocation-based plastic- 
ity of the BCC phase is responsible for the ductility of the HEC. 
In this study, a strong and ductile Re 0.5 MoNbW(TaC) 0.8 HEC was 
fabricated by arc melting. The as-cast HEC exhibited a eutectic 
structure comprising of BCC and MC carbide phases with a semi- 
coherent interface. The as-cast HEC presented an ultra-high ulti- 
mate compressive strength (2347 ± 40 MPa), a retained plastic 
strain (8.90 ± 0.15%) and a high yield strength (1340 ± 21 MPa), 
obviously exceeds those of previously reported 1200 °C-softening- 
resisted RHEAs and HECs. The transgranular fracture behavior of 
the HEC supports a strong bonding at the semi-coherent interface. 
Quantitative calculations suggested the BCC-MC interface provided 
a signiﬁcant strengthening effect. Speciﬁcally, the interface depen- 
dent strengthening ( τ K + τ misﬁt + τ ch ) dominates the overall in- 
terface strengthening terms, and magnitude of the stacking faults 
mismatch strengthening ( τ ch ) is the largest one; whereby the plas- 
ticity of the HEC arose from dislocation gliding in the BCC phase as 
evidenced by the fact that BCC dislocations are repelled by the in- 
terface.
