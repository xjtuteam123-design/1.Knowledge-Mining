ABSTRACT
Physics-based models for predicting the mechanical behavior of Ni-based superalloys as a function of
microstructure features require the use of microstructure data for calibration and veri ﬁcation. Accurate
representation of the heterogeneity of microstructure features requires accurate selection of the representative
microstructure data size (i.e. image size). Thus, this work is carried out to address the in ﬂuence of
microstructure data size on the accuracy of a discrete dislocation dynamic model in predicting the critical
resolved share stress (CRSS) of IN100. Microstructure features from backscattered electron images were
extracted using image processing techniques. Single point statistics (e.g. area fraction, precipitate size, and
distance between γ` particles) and higher order statistics using two-point correlations were calculated from
segmented 2-D images. Modi ﬁed Bhattacharyya Coe ﬃcient analysis techniques were employed to calculate
three-dimensional particle size distributions. Results indicate a signiﬁ cant inﬂuence of the microstructure data
size on the calculated CRSS.
1. Introduction
Ni superalloys are critical materials for use in high temperature
propulsion systems where their unique creep resistance, strength, and
damage tolerance properties are optimized using thermomechanical
processes aimed at controlling microstructure [1– 3]. Generally, their
mechanical behavior is strongly in ﬂuenced by the formation of ordered
L12 precipitates ( γ’) within the FCC γ matrix. The in ﬂuence of
thermomechanical processing parameters on various metrics (i.e. size,
distribution, and volume fraction) of the γ’and their related impact on
mechanical properties have been studied extensively [e.g. 4– 8].
For example, studies have shown the importance of micro-twin-
ning, dislocation cutting, dislocation bypass looping, and other defor-
mation mechanisms associated with creep and high temperature
deformation, all of which are directly impacted by the size, chemistry
and distribution of γ’ precipitates [8,9]. For example, Kozar et al.
showed the antiphase boundary (APB) energies signi ﬁcantly impacted
the strength of multimodal Ni-based superalloys [9]. Stronger APB
values increased the overall strength of the material, identifying
requirements to account for dislocation behavior in property models.
Wlodek reported that in Rene 88DT, the low positive γ’/γ lattice
mismatch provided a ﬁner distribution of γ’. They also reported that
large sub-solvus γ’ phases led to increased crack nucleation in fatigue
tests and reduced the ability to form serrated γ grain boundaries [10].
Research by Gayda et al. involving the LSHR alloy linked γ’ sizes to
notch dwell fatigue behavior and found that creep capability decreased
as γ’ size increased [11]. Additional work by Parthasarathy et al., [12]
further established the important in ﬂuence of γ’ precipitates on the
mechanical performance, providing insight into the modeling of tensile
behavior using the volume fraction and size of γ’.
The work by Parthasarathy also identi ﬁed the importance of
coherency strains associated with the mis ﬁt between the precipitate
and matrix. Unfortunately, because the γ’ chemistry is heavily in ﬂu-
enced by the di ﬀusion of segregating elements in and out of the
precipitate, the cooling rate of the material impacts coherency strains.
For example, researchers studying the nickel-based alloy Udimet 720
found that decreasing cooling rates changed the morphology of the γ’
precipitates and increased the unconstrained mis ﬁt
due to changes in γ’
chemistry. Higher cooling rates resulted in γ’with lower levels of Ti and
Ta, producing smaller precipitates [13].
Diﬀerent models have used this information to predict coarsening
of Ni3Al structures, including the trans-di ﬀusion interface model which
relies on elemental di ﬀusion across a di ﬀuse γ’/γ interface and
modiﬁed LSW models utilizing speci ﬁc element bulk di ﬀusion data to

limit the coarsening rates [14,15]. Commercial software models have
attempted to predict the microstructure evolution of γ’ using thermo-
dynamic properties and mobility information [16,17]. The models rely
heavily on experimental data used to validate kinetic parameters.
Unfortunately, characterizing the di ﬀerent generations of γ’ that form
during thermal treatment is often di ﬃcult, depending on the size of the
precipitates and their morphological structure. Typically, γ’ forms as a
coherent spherical particle that grows and coarsens into a cuboidal
shape. Further coarsening often leads to morphological instabilities
and the precipitate takes on a more complex non-cuboidal shape.
Characterizing the complex shapes often requires 3-dimensional
analysis techniques to capture the microstructure modeling parameters
needed to predict material behavior. Unfortunately, imaging with
electron microscopy techniques is complicated by the need to generate
suﬃcient contrast between γ’ and γ phases that allow segmentation at
the required magni ﬁcations. This is often di ﬃcult due to the z-contrast
similarities between the two phases. Etching may help increase the
contrast, however, even lightly etching the materials can signi ﬁcantly
bias measurement results, especially for small γ’ precipitates ( <
50 nm).
Research shows the strengthening mechanisms are strongly depen-
dent on the size and volume fraction of γ’, chemical segregation, γ grain
size, and the channel width between the γ’precipitates. The movement
of dislocations is strongly in ﬂuenced by the APB energies that in ﬂuence
cutting and bowing of γ’, even slight changes in the APB energy can
result in 10% di ﬀerences in yield strength [9,18,19] at temperatures
below 800 C. Researchers also found a tertiary γ’ volume fraction
increase from 0.06 to 0.11 increased the yield strength by approxi-
mately 10% for temperatures below 800 C.
Additional work by Rao et al. [18,19] augmented the microstructure
to property relationships by modeling the strong in ﬂuence of tertiary γ’
volume fraction and particle spacing on the critical resolved shear
stresses required for slip. Their work found that decreasing γ’ size
increased the CRSS more strongly in cases of higher APB, resulting
from a decrease in channel width. The increase in γ’ volume fraction,
especially
tertiary γ’, further increased the CRSS. Fortunately, strength
models have successfully simulated the yield properties for nickel-
based superalloys, including IN100, based on microstructure charac-
terization of scanning electron microscope (SEM) images and electron
back scattered di ﬀraction (EBSD) data sets [20,21]. Changes in the γ’
volume fractions and γ grain size dramatically changed the overall yield
strength of the material, re-emphasizing the need to carefully measure
microstructure characteristics and chemistries.
Given the model results, it is critical to size characterize the
microstructure using image sets that accurately capture feature statis-
tics. Numerous studies have developed sampling methodologies for
representing three dimensional features in two dimension images
[22,23]. However, the collection of large data sets is expensive,
especially sets comprised of SEM images and EBSD information.
Fortunately researchers have developed processes for optimizing
collected image sizes in e ﬀorts to decrease instrument costs and
operation times [24,26], and developed di ﬀerent techniques for rapidly
assessing features from segmented images and data [27,28]. For
example, the use of 2 point correlation techniques can calculate the
regions of homogeneities (ROH) of heterogeneous microstructures and
can provide e ﬃcient process for determining γ’ and γ feature statistics
from large data sets. This includes determining minimal distances
between particles (channel width) and precipitate aspect ratios, both of
which are di ﬃcult to obtain by standard manual segmentation techni-
ques.
Thus, this article investigates the impact of SEM image size on the
calculated metrics of various microstructure features and determines
its in ﬂuence in models calculating the CRSS of IN100. In addition, an
eﬃcient method for determining the required size of image needed to
capture the relevant model parameters is presented using the ROH
concept.

2. Methods and procedures
The IN100 Ni-based superalloy alloy was used for this research as a
model material due to it is signi ﬁcant use in high temperature turbine
engine applications. The material has been widely studied by various
research groups. In particular, the strengthening mechanisms for
IN100 have been well documented and characterized, with results
used to develop fast acting models to predict material performance
[19,20].
An XL30 SEM operating at 20Kv, equipped with a high speed
Hitachi camera and EDAX EBSD collection system was used to
generate (secondary electron images) SEI and EBSD data that was
used in situ to identify large grains with the (111) perpendicular to
beam direction. Once the grains were identi ﬁed, samples were rotated
manually to align the [011] direction with the reference direction of the
scan.
The 12 µm x 12 µm sized SEI images of lightly etched samples were
imaged using a Sirion SEM operating at 5Kv. The images were
converted to binary images to highlight the segmented γ’. Standard
threshold techniques were employed to provide the segmentation. Post
analysis of images were conducted by dividing each image into 9
images of 6 µm x 6 µm each, and also divided into 49 images of 3 µm x
3 µm size. The layout of the images is provided in Fig. 1 . Many of the
images overlap each other to minimize the bias created by eliminating
γ’ from the segmented images that contact the image boundaries.
Photoshop was used to list the number of pixels associated with
each of the γ’ precipitates. This information was calibrated for
magniﬁcation before the 2D measurements for each feature were
converted to 3D equivalent sphere diameters using a stereological
correction factor of 4/π [28]. The total number of pixels for all γ’
precipitates were also used to provide the area fraction measurements.
2-point correlation techniques were also used to calculate region of
homogeneity in the microstructure based according to [26] which also
estimated the area fraction of γ`. To account for the anisotropy in the
morphology of γ` during the calculation of the single point metrics in
each ROH, the Chord length distribution (CLD) technique was used
[26]. One of the many bene ﬁts of using the 2-point and the CLD
techniques is identifying the direction of statistically minimum spacing
which was used to determine the average minimum spacing between γ`
particles.
The CRSS values were calculated for each of the image sets using a
dislocation dynamics model. The reader is referred to the original work
for full details [18,19]. The method is described here brie ﬂy. The three
image sets were evaluated corresponding to the three locations within
the γ grain. As detailed by Rao and colleagues (18, 19), the model
predicts the critical resolved shear stress by simulating the required
stress to drive dislocations through the γ– γ’microstructure. In general,
since the glide system in both γ and γ’is (110){111}, the microstructure
is modeled using a set of Cartesian axes where x=[110], y=[112], and
z=[111] directions. At the start of the dislocation glide simulation, a
pair of (a/2) (110) dislocations (i.e. a super-dislocation when in γ’)i s
placed
along one edge of the glide plane, parallel to x. Each dislocation
is formed from a large number of straight segments, joined at nodes.
The force at each node has multiple components including far- ﬁeld
segment forces, local segment forces, and forces from APBs. At each
iteration of the simulation, the node is moved using a kinetics model
that ascribes a mass and damping constant to its motion, related to the
resultant forces acting on it. The resulting new shape of the dislocation
is determined based upon the nodal positions. The process is repeated
iteratively until all dislocation segments come to a halt. At this stage,
the applied stress is incremented until the dislocations glide through
the entire simulated glide plane. The smallest applied stress at which
this occurs is taken to be the critical resolved shear stress of the
material.

previously been used by researchers for comparing two and three-
dimensional feature size distributions [24,25]. The MBC is bounded
between 0 and 1, where a value of 0 implies that two models are
identically distributed, and higher values imply greater variance
between distributions. The MBC is applied to discrete PDFs as shown

4. Discussion
4.1. CRSS model analysis
As expected, the volume fraction calculations by Ni-Seg ™ (2 pt
statistics) and Photoshop ™ are similar. However, the pronounced
diﬀerence in other size metrics results from accounting for the
anisotropic morphology of the particles in Ni-Seg ™ in addition to
possible inclusion of data at the image border edge during the 2-point
image correlation analysis and the over tracing of grain boundaries
during the Photoshop technique.
The Photoshop process data was also scrubbed to remove small γ’
particles from the segmented values prior to determining area fractions
and equivalent diameters. This was not done in the automated Ni-
Seg™ (2-point correlation and CLD based method).

overall higher CRSS for the entire region. Since the CRSS model

simulation, small regions of strongly di ﬀerent particle spacing and sizes
are important to capture.
Given the inﬂuence of the minimum spacing between γ’precipitates
(as captured by Ni-Seg ™) on the simulated yield strength, the use of
the 2-point correlation techniques provided critical information on the
microstructure that was not readily apparent using standard Photoshop
segmentation techniques. Due to the anisotropic morphology of γ’
particles it is not feasible to utilize standard single point metrics
directly from Photoshop (i.e. the number of γ’ precipitates and the
average γ’ equivalent diameter) to calculate an equivalent minimum
inter-particle distance which has a signi ﬁcant impact on the CRSS
calculations. The implementation of higher order statistics (2-pt
correlation technique) in Ni-Seg provides an automated fashion to
accurately estimate the min particle spacing. In addition, it also enables
the implementation of this technique on a location speci ﬁc fashion that
provides the quanti ﬁcation of min-particle spacing in Ni-based super-
alloys with gradient microstructures (location speci ﬁc variability) and
hence the prediction of location speci ﬁc CRSS in such alloys.

4.2. Modi ﬁed Bhattacharyya coe ﬃcient analysis
Similarity analysis of particle distributions was carried out dis-
cretely using the Modiﬁed Bhattacharyya Coe ﬃcient (MBC), which has
previously seen application comparing three-dimensional particle size
distributions [24,25]. Among the possible statistics that could be used
for geometric similarity analysis (see Ref [26]), the MBC was selected
for the particle size distributions because it was able to detect (with
relatively high sensitivity) subtle variations in the PDFs between 12
μmx12 μm images and 6 µm x 6 µm image subsets.
Similarity analysis were performed for a) the smallest 25% of
particles using a 10-bin scheme, b) the largest 25% of particles using a
10-bin scheme, and c) the full distributions of particles between each
image using a 30-bin scheme. For this analysis, particles less than 25
contiguous pixels were removed, and the corresponding results are
shown in Table 3 below. Note that the binning scheme used for
comparison in this case allowed for inclusion of the largest and smallest
grains in all data sets.
Assuming that the MBC threshold used in previous cases [24,25] as
a starting point for trend comparison (i.e., MBC ≤0.13 provides a
relatively accurate distribution), the full distribution of particles in each
image were relatively close to one another. It can also be observed that
the largest particles had, on average, more di ﬀerences than were
observed with the smaller particles, or with full distributions.

5. Conclusions
Results show that the simulated yield strength increases as the
average γ’particle spacing decreases. The largest γ’particles appear to
have a more signi ﬁcant e ﬀect on CRSS than the smallest particles,
given microstructures with similar volume fraction of γ’. In addition,
the CRSS is observed to be location-dependent, both within a single
image and more generally throughout the IN100 sample. The size of
the captured image strongly impacts the measured γ’sizes and volume
fractions which altered the simulated CRSS values. This conclusion
strengthens the need to sample regions within the grains that fully
capture limiting γ’particle spacing and size parameters. Also, the use of
higher order statistical methods including the 2-pt correlation techni-
ques and the chord length distributions provided an e ﬃcient method
for determining location-speci ﬁc microstructure metrics of various
including particle spacing features that were not readily available using
more traditional techniques based on single point metrics. The results
indicate the largest image sample size paired with the 2-pt correlation
techniques (i.e. 2-pt correlation, chord length distribution, etc.) for
particle segmentation and quanti ﬁcation provided the most accurate
particle identi ﬁcation/quantiﬁcation and simulated CRSS results.
