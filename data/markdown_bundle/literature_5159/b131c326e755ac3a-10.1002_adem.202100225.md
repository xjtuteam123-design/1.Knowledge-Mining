1. Introduction
High-temperature structural materials are used for critical com-
ponents in many industries, including power generation and gas
turbines. Among conventional structural materials, Ni-based
superalloys exhibit an excellent combination of properties at ele-
vated temperatures. However, the average working temperature
of such alloys is less than /C25 1050 /C14 C as the melting point of the
principal content, Ni, is 1455 /C14 C, which represents an absolute
limit for the temperature capability of Ni-
based superalloys. [1] Since 2004, high-
entropy alloys (HEAs) or multicomponent
alloys have attracted much attention due
to their unique properties. [2,3] In particular,
refractory HEAs (RHEAs) based on refrac-
tory elements with high melting points
have shown excellent high-temperature
performance.[4,5] For example, the classic
MoWTaNb and MoWTaNbV exhibit
yield strengths of 548 and 842 MPa, which
are, respectively, more than twice and
four times higher than that of Inconel
718 at 1000 /C14 C.[6] Thus, RHEAs show
signiﬁcant potential for high-temperature
applications.
However, the high density and brittle-
ness at room temperature (RT) limit the
practical application of these RHEAs. [6] Subsequently, the
Hf ZrTaTiNb RHEA was developed, which had a yield strength
of 929 MPa and excellent malleability ( ε > 50%) at RT, but its
yield strength reduced to 92 MPa at 1200 /C14 C.[7] Elemental addi-
tions or substitutions are effective methods to balance the den-
sity, malleability, and strength of RHEAs and obtain the
required property pro ﬁle. It was found that alloying with sub-
group IV or V transition metals can transform the intrinsically
brittle Mo and W to intrinsically ductile materials. This can be
attributed to changes in the electronic structure induced by the
Jahn–Teller distortion and the transition of the elastic instability
mode from tensile to shear failure. [8] This strategy is expected to
be applied in RHEAs consisting of brittle Mo and W elements.
Furthermore, most reported RHEAs possess disordered body-
centered cubic (BCC) structures, which can obviously
simplify alloy design and heat treatment processes compared
to multistructure alloys. [4] Mu et al. found that the overall
performance of RHEAs with the disordered BCC structure
was enhanced with increase in entropy, evidenced by better
thermodynamic and mechanical stability, increased stiffness,
and so on. [9] A previous study shows that equimolar
Hf ZrVTaMoWTiNb RHEA has a large mixing entropy of
17.29 J·mol/C0 1 K/C0 1, but the brittle Laves phase was observed
in the as-cast microstructure. [10] Unfortunately, crack nucle-
ation and propagation usually occur along with the Laves phase,
causing a visible loss in RT malleability.
In this study, subgroup IV (Ti) and V (Nb) elements are added
to Hf ZrVTaMoWTi xNby (x ¼ 1, 2 and y ¼ 1, 2) RHEAs

Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) with excellent high-temperature perfor-
mance are attractive for high-temperature structural applications. However,
many RHEAs suffer from high density and poor room temperature (RT) mal-
leability. Herein, the effects of Ti and Nb contents on the phase equilibrium and
mechanical properties of Hf ZrVTaMoWTi xNby RHEAs are studied to achieve
unique balanced properties in a wide temperature range. With the increase of Ti
or Nb content, the Laves phase precipitation is suppressed and RT malleability is
improved. Particularly, the Hf ZrVTaMoWTi 2Nb2 RHEA without Laves precipi-
tates exhibits a high yield strength of 1.7 GPa and a compressive strain of /C24 30%
at RT. Moreover, it retains a high systemic yield strength of 31 MPa cm 3 g/C0 1 at
1200 /C14 C, which is nearly three times that of Hf ZrTaTiNb and nearly equal to that
of MoWTaNb, suggesting its potential applicability at elevated temperatures.

The previous calculation of phase diagram modeling has

2.3. Mechanical Properties
The compressive engineering stress–strain (σ–ε) curves of the as-
cast four RHEAs at RT are plotted in Figure 4 a. The TiNb alloy
had a 0.2% offset yield strength ( σ0.2) of 1750 MPa and failed
without too much prior plastic deformation because of the mas-
sive C15 precipitates in the interdendritic regions. The Ti2Nb
had a yield strength of 1770 MPa and a moderate malleability
of 13.5%. Similarly, the yield strength of TiNb2 is 1720 MPa with
a malleability of 14%. The Ti2Nb2 retains the high yield strength
of 1700 MPa with a total elongation to failure of /C24 30%. With the
increase in Ti or/and Nb contents, the change of yield strength is
not obvious, though the VF of the C15 Laves phase decreases
signiﬁcantly. Recent studies showed that the intrinsic malleabil-

3. Conclusion
In summary, the effect of Ti and Nb elements on the microstruc-
tural evolution, phase formation, and mechanical properties of
Hf ZrVTaMoWTixNby RHEAs were systematically studied. The
following conclusions were drawn. 1) Compared to the TiNb
alloy, the VF of C15 Laves phase in the Ti2Nb and TiNb2 alloys
decreases signi ﬁcantly, and the C15 Laves phase disappears
completely in the Ti2Nb2 alloy. The results indicate that the
increase in Ti or Nb content inhibits the Laves-phase precipita-
tion in the Hf ZrVTaMoWTixNby system. 2) At RT, the Ti2Nb has
a yield strength of 1770 MPa and a moderate malleability of
13.5%, while the yield strength of TiNb2 is 1720 MPa with a mal-
leability of 14%. Particularly, the Ti2Nb2 retains the high yield
strength of 1700 MPa with a total compressive strain to failure
of /C24 30%. The improvement of malleability is mainly attributed
to the reduction in the VF of C15 precipitation. In addition,
enrichment by group IV and V elements also plays an important
role. 3) The Ti2Nb2 alloy has a high SYS of 175 MPa cm 3 g/C0 1,
which is /C25 1.9, 1.7, and 2.3 times, respectively, that of the classic
Hf ZrTaTiNb, MoWTaNb, and MoWTaNbV RHEAs at
RT. Moreover, it possesses strong high-temperature softening
resistance and reaches a high speci ﬁc yield strength of
31 MPa cm3 g/C0 1 at 1200 /C14 C.
