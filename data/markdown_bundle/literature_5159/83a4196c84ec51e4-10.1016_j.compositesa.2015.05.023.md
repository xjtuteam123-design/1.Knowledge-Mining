Abstract 
A new type of composites containing eutectic oxide fibres and a high entropy alloy (HEA) with a 
melting point of about 1450oC as the matrix is obtained via liquid infiltration route. A low pressure 
of argon gas necessary to infiltrate a fibre bundle with the molten matrix alloy is an evidence of 
good wetting in this system that provides a sufficiently high strength to the fibre/matrix interface. 
Composite specimens were tested to measure the strength at 20 - 1300oC. It was found that the 
composite strength does not decrease to a temperature of 1200oC. The analysis of the microstructure 
and strength data suggests an expectation of a high creep resistance of the composites under 
development. 
Keywords: A. Metal-matrix composites (MMCs), B. Strength, E. Liquid metal infiltration 
1. Introduction 
Enhancing the use temperature of structural materials is an important aim of modern materials 
science. One way to reach this goal is to develop metal matrix composites (MMCs). Single 
crystalline and eutectic oxide fibres crystallised from the melt by the internal crystallisation method 
(ICM) are appropriate reinforcements for composites to be used at temperatures up to about 1600oC 
[1]. Up to now, a choice of matrix materials has been limited to nickel-based alloys [2,3] and 
molybdenum alloys [4]. 
The usage of molybdenum as the matrix is an easiest way to produce oxide-fibre/metal-matrix 
composites [1,5] but Mo-containing composites require a special attention to prevent oxidation [4]. 
A nickel-based matrix limits the use temperature of composites by a temperature of about 1200oC 
and this is an inherent limitation because of a relatively low solidus temperature of nickel alloys. 
Another problem arising whith using a nickel alloy as the matrix is poor wetting of oxides with

An invention of high entropy alloys (HEAs), which are normally composed of five or more

compositions of HEAs could make the solution of the wetting problem easier. However, 
publications on the development of oxide-fibre/HEA-matrix composites are unknown to the present 
authors. Hence, the present short communication is, perhaps, the first in this field. 
2. Fabrication of an oxide-fibre/HEA-matrix composite 
Fibres of Al2O3-Al5Y3O12 and Al2O3-Al5Y3O12-ZrO2 eutectics were used as reinforcements. The 
microstructure and properties of the fibres obtained by ICM are described in details in Ref. [11]. A 
high entropy alloy containing Fe, Co, Ni, Cr and W with a melting point of about 1450oC was 
chosen as the matrix. The alloy is composed of two phases, namely, a base BCC phase and 
inclusions of a µ-phase of the Fe7W6-structure containing all the elements of the alloy, but with 
their ratios differing from that of the base phase. Such a microstructure determines high strength 
and ductility of the alloy [12]. 
Specimens 60 mm long and a diameter of 4 mm were pr oduced by pressure liquid infiltration. A 
casting mould differs from that made of quartz, whi ch was used in earlier experiments to make 
oxide-fibre/nickel-based-matrix composites [2]. In the present experiments, a molybdenum foil 25 
microns thick covers the inside surface of the qua rtz mould to prevent the interaction between a 
highly reactive HEA melt and quartz. The mould is s upported from outside by a graphite tube. At 
the bottom of the mould, the debries of sapphire fibres are laid down. 
The casting mould filled with the fibres is placed in a chamber for pressure casting, which is 
pumped out to a pressure of about 10-1 mm Hg. A piece of the matrix material located in the 
crucible at the top of the mould is then heated to the temperature shown in Table 1 and the chamber 
is filled with argon gas. The gas pressure and infiltration time are shown in Table 1. The necessary 
gas pressure is much lower than that in the case of infiltrating oxide fibres with superalloys, which

3. Microstructure of the composites 
A typical microstructure of the composite is presented in Fig. 1. Note that ICM-fibres have non-
circular shapes of the cross sections. The micrographs show that the matrix microstructure is 
composed of two phases. The results of X-ray microanalysis of the matrix are presented in Table 2. 
It can be seen that the base phase (called grey in the Table) contains all the elements in nearly equi-
atomic ratios, tungsten being an exception. Molybdenum occurs as a result of partial dissolution of 
the molybdenum foil used to prevent an interaction of the molten matrix alloy and silica in the 
mold. The composition of an inclusion (white phase in the Table) is close to Fe1-(x+y+z)CrxCoyNizW6. 
A deviation of the measured composition from the exact composition of the µ-phase is partially a 
result of capturing a part of the base phase by the ionization pear caused by the electron beam. 
In these experiments, all specimens have relatively large fibre volume fractions, between 40 and 
50%. 
4. Composites strength 
Composite specimens were tested in the 3-point bending to measure the strength at 20 - 1300oC. 
Temperature dependence of the strength is presented in Fig. 2. A load/displacement curve of a 
specimen tested at room temperature is shown in Fig. 3. Note two interesting observations. 
First, the room temperature strength values are relatively low and this is to be the case since the 
specimens tested were with large fibre volume fractions. Indeed, the dependence of strength of a 
brittle-fibre/ductile-matrix composite on fibre volume fraction Vf has a maximum. An inherent 
reason for the composite strength going down at Vf  Vf

number of adjacent fibres. The crack size arising because of fracture of a fibre cluster can be too 
large for the matrix to dissipate the elastic energy and arrest the crack. The probability of fibre 
cluster formation increases with the fibre volume fraction increases. A corresponding model of the 
composites failure behaviour was proposed by Mileiko [13] and supplemented by many 
experimental data. Recent experimental results revealing such a behaviour of oxide-fibre/nickel-
based matrix composites were presented by Novokhatskaya et al. [3]. A particular value of Vf

homogeneity of fibre packing. Usually, for composites produced by infiltration of a fibre bundle

Composites of the type under consideration are to b e developed for high temperature use. Hence, 
creep properties of the composites are of importanc e. Although at the present stage of the work no 
creep tests have been performed yet, the results of strength tests together with a previous experience 
of the authors in studying creep of fibrous composi tes allow formulating some expectations. The 
theory of creep behaviour of MMCs [6] and a large bulk of the experimental data [2,3,16] show that 
the most important structural parameter of composit es with oxide fibres produced via liquid 
infiltration route is the fibre/matrix interface st rength determined by a degree of wetting in the 
system. If a matrix melt wets a fibre then the matr ix heals the surface defects that yields an increas e 
in the effective fibre strength by a factor as high as 5 in the case of a fibre obtained by the intern al 
crystallisation method [1,15]. The effective fibre strength determines creep resistance of composites 
at temperatures, at which fibre creep is negligible . The oxide fibres used in the present work do not 
creep at a temperature of 1200oC. Also it is important to note that good wetting and, consequently, a 
strong interface make the basis of high creep resis tance of the composites with large fibre volume 
fractions. Therefore, the composites described in t his short communication can be expected to have 
a high creep resistance at temperatures to 1200oC.

5. Conclusions 
• A new type of metal matrix composites containing oxide fibres and a high entropy alloy as 
the matrix is obtained via liquid infiltration route. 
• A low pressure of argon gas necessary to infiltrate a fibre bundle with the molten matrix 
alloy is an evidence of good wetting of the oxide fibre with high entropy alloys, which is to 
provide a sufficiently high strength to the fibre/matrix interface. This determines high values 
of the composite strength. 
• Strength of the composite specimens tested does not decrease to a temperature of 1200oC. 
• The analysis of the microstructure and strength data suggests an expectation of a high creep 
resistance of the composites under development.
