1. Introduction
The refractory Nb-based alloys are widely used in aerospace
industry as high temperature materials, due to their high melting
points, low vapor pressure, qualiﬁed high temperature strength and
creep resistance with pronounced stiffness [1–5]. Especially, the
Nb-Si based in situ composites have great promise for advanced
turbine engine applications, and a good deal of attentions have
been paid on the improvement of the mechanical and environmen-
tal properties of Nb–Si based composites [3–6]. The manufacture
of complex components from Nb-based alloy tends to take arduous
efforts using traditional casting and forging techniques, being more
time-consuming and high-cost.
The powder-based additive manufacturing (AM) technologies,
including selective laser melting (SLM) [7,8], electron beam melt-
ing (EBM) [9,10] and laser melting deposition (LMD) [11], have
gained considerable recognition since introduced. In addition to
fewer time-consuming machining procedures, the microstructure
and the performance of metals fabricated by AM may also be differ-
ent with those by traditional casting or forging techniques, owing
to the unique additive manufacturing manner as well as the rapid
cooling process [11–13]. Sander et al. [12] argued that the SLM-

a very important role in the printing process of AM [15–19]. Spher-

to their favorable ﬂowability with smaller contact area among pow-

water atomization, centrifugal atomization, plasma rotating elec-
trode processing (PREP) and plasma atomization are frequently
employed to prepare spherical metal powders [21–25]. One unpar-
alleled advantage of PREP is the removal of contamination by
erosion of the ceramic crucible, compared with other atomization
techniques. During the PREP process, the rapidly rotating electrodes
are melted by plasma arc, and the alloy melt were then formed
and rapidly solidiﬁed during free fall. Less chemical composition
loss tends to be caused by PREP, due to the rapid solidiﬁcation pro-
cess. Besides, the additively manufactured parts using gas atomized
powders had a denser structure than that using water atomized
powders, owing to the lower oxygen content and higher packing

2. Experimental
With a nominal composition of Nb-37Ti-13Cr-2Al-1Si (at.%), the
Nb-based pre-alloyed powders were prepared by PREP in Ar atmo-
sphere. The Nb-based alloy disks, produced via vacuum induction
melting (VIM), were used as electrodes for PREP. According to
the difference on diameter, the pre-alloyed powders were clas-
siﬁed into four kinds, i.e., N1 (45–75 /H9262m), N2 (75–180 /H9262m), N3
(180–250 /H9262m) and N4 (250–380 /H9262m). The chemical composition
of powders was measured using the inductively coupled plasma
atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES, Agilent 725E) method. In
order to examine the microstructure evolution of Nb-based alloy
powders at high temperature, heat treatment (HT) at 1450 ◦C for
3 h was performed on the N2 powders and the VIM alloy (for com-
parison) in a vacuum furnace under high purity argon atmosphere.
Ahead of HT, the N2 powders were sealed in a silica tube to avoid
any oxidation.
The phases of Nb-based alloy as well as the pre-alloyed powders
were identiﬁed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, D/max-2500, Cu K/H9251),
which was performed at a 2/H9258 scanning rate of 6◦/min. Surface mor-
phologies were examined using a scanning electron microscopy
(SEM, JSM7500). The accelerated voltage was 5.0 kV in secondary
electron imaging mode and 15.0 kV in backscattered electron imag-
ing mode, respectively. The operating current was 10 /H9262A in both
modes. The oxides formed on the powder surfaces were deter-
mined by X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS, PHI Quantera SXM).
A monochromatic AlK/H9251 was used as the X-ray radiation source
under vacuum higher than 1.3 × 10−5Pa. High resolution XPS nar-
row scans were acquired in the ﬁxed analyzer transmission mode,
and the selected region spectra were recorded covering the Nb3d,
Si2p, Ti2p, Cr2p, Al2p, and C1s photoelectron peaks. All the binding
energies were calibrated by the C1s peak (284.8 eV) to compensate
for surface charges effects. The amounts of the elements at different
chemical states were estimated from intensities of XPS signals, and
the area under the deconvoluted peak was taken for calculation.
Analysis of the N2 powder surface was carried out on an Auger
election spectroscopy (AES, PHI-700). The single powder for AES
analysis was 112.65 /H9262m in diameter, and the etched area on pow-
der surface was 2 /H9262m × 2 /H9262m in size. For the depth proﬁling, the Ar
ion gun was used and the etch rate was 3 nm/min, using thermal
oxidized SiO2/Si as a reference.
Microstructure analysis was undertaken on an election probe
microanalyzer (EPMA, JXA-8230). Elemental distribution images
were obtained by wavelength dispersive X-ray spectrometers
(WDS) attached to EPMA. Prior to observing the cross-sectional
microstructures, the pre-alloyed powers were mounted with epoxy
resin, grounded up to 1200 # using metallographic abrasive papers
and ﬁnally mechanically polished on Fe2O3/CrO3 impregnated
cloths. To determine the correlation of microstructure and mechan-
ical property, the secondary dendrite arm spacing (SDAS) of the
pre-alloyed powders and the VIM alloy was obtained by measuring
more than twenty dendrites using the Adobe Photoshop CS6 pro-
gram. Microstructure of the N1 powders was further examined by
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100). For TEM exam-
ination, N1 powders were ﬁrstly mounted in an epoxy compound.
Then they were sectioned into thin slices, dimpled and ion-milled
eventually.
The Vickers hardness of the pre-alloyed powders and the VIM
alloy were measured using a microhardness tester (FM-800) with a
100 g load and a dwell time of 10 s. At least six measurements were
taken from different powders. Some errors (<5%) were inevitable
during microhardness tests, resulting from factors such as surface
quality and the ambiguity of the edges of indentations.
3. Results
The composition of Nb-based alloy powders measured by ICP-
AES is 46.51Nb-36.01Ti-13.66Cr-2.39Al-1.43Si (at.%). The result is
close to their nominal composition, though the experimental errors
are inevitable. It suggests no chemical loss is caused by the PREP
process. As shown in Fig. 1(a), spherical Nb-based alloy powders
with smooth surfaces are obtained by PREP. Resulting from the
high energy plasma beam, the rotating Nb-based alloy is melted
and the melt drops are formed. The drops are then rapidly solidiﬁed
during free fall in a containerless state. The phenomenon that pow-
ders are bonded together is restrained and powders are solidiﬁed
to form single spherical powders. Spherical powders are supposed
to enhance the ﬂowability of powders, guaranteeing the stability of
powder laying in the layer-based AM process. The typical dendritic
structures, observed on the surface of a powder with 123.82 /H9262m
in diameter, are displayed in Fig. 1(b). It is evident that surfaces of
the Nb-based alloy powders are featured by dendritic structures.
Besides, few oxide particles are observed on the powder surfaces.
As shown in Fig. 1(c), the Nb solid solution (Nbss) phase is detected
by XRD in both the Nb-based alloy via VIM and the pre-alloyed
powders. Other nonequilibrium phases are not detected by XRD
patterns.
In order to control the purity as well as purity-related proper-
ties of the AM-processed components, it is necessary to understand
the surface oxidation of the Nb-based alloy powders. Fig. 2 shows
the chemical states of the alloying elements in Nb-based alloys
obtained by the XPS narrow scans. The oxides of Nb2O5, SiO2, TiO2,
Cr2O3 and Al2O3 are determined from their binding energies as
well as their peak positions. The percentages of Moxi/(M0 + Moxi)
for Nb, Ti and Cr are 84.5 at.%, 52.2 at.% and 71.9 at.%, respectively,
where Moxi is the amount of the alloying element at oxidation state
and M0 is its residual amount at metallic state. The surface com-

detected by AES, is shown in Fig. 3. The elements of Nb, Cr, Ti, C, O are
detected on the powder surface. Al and Si are not detected on the

the Nb-based alloy (2 at.% Al and 1 at.% Si). Normalized atomic con-

content of Nb, Ti or Al is determined as (Nb, Ti or Al)/(Nb + Ti + Al),
while the content of oxygen is determined as O/(O + Nb + Ti + Al).

melt crystallizes. The nucleation and growth of the Nb-based alloy
droplets, introduced by the melting of rotating Nb-based alloy elec-
trode, are intensively determined by the thermal convection and
radiation. The solidiﬁcation process of powders fabricated via PREP
is intrinsically in accordance with that via containerless processing

Analogous to the Ostwald ripening of precipitates, the typical
mechanism, responsible for the coarsening of dendrite arms, is the
fragment and dissolution of thinner arms and the increase in diam-
eter of thicker branches [37,39]. Fragmentation of the dendrite
arms tends to take place, when suffering from a sudden reduc-
tion of growth front, or an increase of local temperature of the
system, or a change in ﬂow condition, etc. The process is driven
by the Gibbs–Thomson effect, namely the variation in the mean
solid–liquid interfacial curvature [37,39,40]. It is achieved through
the variation in the solid-liquid interfacial curvature and surface
energy in the system is minimized eventually. A similar phe-
nomenon has been observed that the dendritic structure evolves
towards solid cylinders with longer coarsening time in Pb–Sn sys-
tem by three-dimensional reconstructions [37]. Besides, dendrites
observed in VIM alloy are evidently coarser than that in PREP pow-
ders (Fig. 4). It could be inferred that, the dendrite growth of as-cast
Nb-based alloy with low undercooling is dominantly controlled by
solute diffusion, while that of the pre-alloyed powders via PREP
is controlled by thermal diffusion, with much larger undercooling
[33]. As a thermally activated process, the growth of Nbss dendrites
is promoted by the high-temperature HT, leading to the formation
of large Nbss grains. The dendrite boundary migration is driven by
the energy provided by HT.
Furthermore, a coarser dendrite trunk exhibits a brighter con-
trast, indicating less remaining alloying elements (Fig. 4(b)). The
supersaturation inside the thinner dendrite trunk is supposed to
be caused by solute trapping during growth [38]. During the den-
drite growth in smaller powders with larger undercooling, solute
trapping sets in and lead to the decrease of concentration gradi-
ent, and the increase of solute distribution coefﬁcient [38,39,41]. It
may provide an additional driving force for the breakup of dendrites
through diffusion in the solid, by extending the kinetic undercool-
ing degree. A gradient in composition gives rise to a solute ﬂux from
the larger arm to the smaller one [42]. Consequently, smaller arms
remelt, while the larger ones grow, leading to the difference on
composition (image contrast) between the coarser and the thinner
dendrites.
Hardness is a measure of the resistance to localized plastic defor-
mation, and correlates well with the microstructure ﬁneness and
the precipitation of strengthened phases. For each plastic indenta-
tion, the diamond indenter having pyramidal geometry is forced
into the surface of the sample. On reaching the yield stress, dislo-
cations pile up behind the dendrite boundaries, acting as obstacles.
The free path of dislocations is thus limited, and strain harden-
ing is increased resultantly. The sample with lower SADS typically
contains more dendrite boundaries, resulting in a higher hardness,
which is in accordance with other literature [43–45]. Moreover, fol-
lowing the Hall-Petch relationship, the microhardness of HV tends
to linearly increase with the inverse square root of SADS [43,44,46].
It is worthy of note that the hardness of the VIM alloy is higher
than that of the N4 powders, which seems to be in conﬂict with
Hall–Petch relationship. The “abnormal” increase of hardness of the
VIM alloy is probably caused by the precipitation of ﬁbrous Laves
Cr2Nb particles (Fig. 3), which are large enough to impeding dis-
location motion, known as second-phase hardening [47]. Namely,
due to the precipitation of Laves Cr2Nb particles, the microstruc-
ture reﬁnement hardening effect is masked by the second-phase
hardening effect, resulting in the increase of the hardness of the
VIM alloy.

pre-alloyed powder surface. The oxygen concentration (wt.%)
decreased exponentially with the distance from powder sur-

ders was featured by ﬁne dendrite structures. The elements of
Si, Ti, Cr concentrated on the interdendritic areas. The average
SDAS values of the pre-alloyed powders ranged from 0.85 /H9262m
to 5.14 /H9262m, while that of the VIM alloy was 76.57 /H9262m. With the
increase of powder size, the value of SDAS increased and the
microhardness decreased.
(3) After HT at 1450 ◦C for 3 h, the microstructure of Nb-based
pre-alloyed powders evolved into large grains from dendrite
structures. The Cr2Nb phases were not only precipitated at the
grain boundaries, but also formed inside the grains.
