1 Introduction
Ti-based alloys are light in weight, have extraordinary
corrosion resistance, the ability to withstand extreme
temperatures, and outstanding mechanical properties
(tensile strengths of 900 to 1500 MPa with ductility of
10 to 15% elongation). This combination of proper-
ties makes them widely useful for industrial and med-
ical applications [1]. Ti-based alloys could potentially
be strengthened further by grain reﬁnement; ultraﬁne
grained (ufg) and nanocrystalline (nc) alloys could pro-
vide much greater strength than their coarse-grained
counterparts, as predicted by the Hall-Petch Law that
predicts a continuous rise of strength with decreasing
grain size. However, the engineering applications of ufg
and nc materials are limited by their low ductility [2].
Three major limitations to ductility for nc materials have
been identiﬁed. These are: (1) artifacts from process-
ing, (2) force instability in tension, and (3) crack nucle-
ation or propagation instability [3]. These are key issues
that must be addressed if one hopes to achieve both
high-strength and high ductility for nc and ufg mate-
rials in general. Various approaches have been used to
improve the ductility of nanostructured metals and al-
loys including bimodal (or multi-modal) grain size distri-
butions, growth twins, dispersions of nano-precipitates,
and transformation-induced plasticity [2, 4]. For exam-
ple, by mixing up the length scales, specially by creating
a bimodal grain size distribution with micrometre-sized
grains embedded inside a matrix of nanocrystalline and
ultraﬁne grains ( <300 nm), a fairly large uniform elon-
gation of 65% and simultaneously good yield strength of
400 MPa was achieved in rolled Cu [5]. It has been shown
that a Ti 60Cu14Ni12Sn2Nb10 alloy consisting of micron-
sized β-Ti dendrites and a nanoscale matrix microstruc-
ture exhibits a remarkably high compressive strength of
2.4 GPa and 15% plastic strain to failure at room tem-
perature [6]. Recently, Louzguine et al. [7–12] reported
that eutectic and hypereutectic Ti-Fe alloys consisting of
A2 β-Ti and B2 TiFe phases show a strength of /223c2G P a
and reasonable ductility of 4–7% in compression. The
improved mechanical properties of eutectic and hyper-
eutectic Ti-Fe alloys have been reported by the additions
of third elements such as Sn, Ga, Co, T a, and Nb [13–18].
The addition of 3.85 at.% Sn to eutectic Ti-Fe alloys re-
sults in the increased strength and ductility [13]. The for-
mation of a bimodal eutectic structure in (Ti70.5Fe29.5)Sn9
alloy has a strong inﬂuence to enhance the macroscopic
ductility of 16% while preserving an ultimate strength

the strength and the plasticity of ufg and nc materi-

The as-developed Sn and Nb combined modiﬁed Ti-
Fe hypereutectic alloys having bimodal structure ex-

2.7 GPa but also a compressive plastic strain of 15%. The
microstructure-properties correlation and the deforma-
tion behavior of the alloys have been investigated in de-
tail. Such a study is expected to be helpful in developing
high-performance nc and ufg materials via manipulation
of the microstructure.
2 Experimental
Master alloys with a nominal composition (at.%) of
Ti65Fe35 and (Ti 65Fe35)96.15-xSn3.85Nbx (x = 0, 3, 5 and
7 at.%) were made by cold crucible levitation melt-
ing Ti, Fe, Sn, and Nb metals in an Ar atmosphere
of 99.99% purity . The ingots were turned over and
re-melted four times to ensure compositional homo-
geneity . The phase constitutions of the alloys were char-
acterized by a D/MAX-3C X-ray diffractometer (XRD)
using Cu K α1 radiation ( λ = 0.1541 nm) in the range
2θ = 20–100° with a step size of 0.02 ° and a count-
ing time of 1 s per step. The compression specimens
(3 mm in diameter and 6 mm long) were machined by
electrical-discharge machining (EDM). The compression
tests were conducted in a CMT5305 electronic universal
testing machine at room temperature at a strain rate of
1 × 10−3 s−1. Micrographs and fracture surface were ex-
amined with a JSM-7001 scanning electron microscope
(SEM) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX)
spectroscopy .
Discs of /223c0.2 mm in thickness were cut by EDM
for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) specimens.
The discs were mechanically ground to a thickness of
/223c80 μm. Electron-transparent foils were prepared by
jet electropolishing using an electrolyte consisting of

In the present study, the Ti 65Fe35 and
(T i65Fe35)96.15-xSn3.85Nbx (x = 0, 3, 5 and 7 at.%) alloys
exhibit bimodal structure with the micrometer-scale
primary TiFe dendrites distributed within an ultraﬁne
eutectic matrix as shown in ﬁgures 2a–e. The reduced
lamellar spacings of the eutectics of the β-Ti and
TiFe phases in (Ti 65Fe35)96.15-xSn3.85Nbx (x = 0, 5 and
7 at.%) alloys were caused by a decrease of interface
energy [25–27] due to the additions of Sn and Nb.
Park et al. [28] reported that Fe-Nb eutectics having a
reduced lamellar spacing with Al additions. 3.85 at.%
Sn and 3–7 at.% Nb additions do not change the phase
constitutions of the Ti 65Fe35 alloys, while a structural
transition from lamellar to rod-like occurs in eutectics
revealed by microscopic observation (Figure 2c) in
(T i65Fe35)93.15Sn3.85Nb3 alloy . The volume fractions of the
primary B2 TiFe phases measured by XRD were about
30% for Ti 65Fe35, 25% for (Ti 65Fe35)96.15Sn3.85, and 24%
for (Ti 65Fe35)93.15Sn3.85Nb3 alloy, respectively . Interfacial
energy considerations suggest that a rod-like morphol-
ogy should grow in preference to a lamellar morphology
in a system containing a low volume fraction of one of
the phases [29, 30]. The effect of decreasing the volume
fraction of one of the phases in promoting a lamellar
to rod-like structural transition has been observed in
off-eutectic Pb-Sn and Al-Cu alloys [30–32]. With in-
creasing Nb content, alloy composition will move closer
to the eutectic composition since Nb is a β-Ti stabilizer,
which results in decreased volume fractions of the
primary TiFe dendrites. The formation of the lamellar
eutectics again in the (Ti 65Fe35)91.15Sn3.85Nb5 (Figure 2d)
and (Ti 65Fe35)89.15Sn3.85Nb7 (Figure 2e) alloys leads to
the decreased mechanical properties. The improved
mechanical properties of the Sn and Nb modiﬁed Ti-Fe
alloys are attributed to their bimodal structure and
the morphology of the eutectics. Sn and Nb additions
increase the lattice parameter differences between β-Ti
and TiFe phases. The larger lattice mismatches will
introduce coherent strain at the interface of the β-Ti and
TiFe phases, which may favour the absorption of dislo-
cations emitted from the β-Ti(Fe, Sn, Nb) phase during
deformation and provide better slip transfer at the A2/B2
interface [13, 33], thereby improving the ductility of the
Sn and Nb modiﬁed hypereutectic Ti 65Fe35 alloys. The
rod-like morphology of the B2 TiFe phase in eutectics
is believed to be another important factor increasing
