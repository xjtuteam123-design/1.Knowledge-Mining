The present work studied the effect of Ti, Nb, or Ta addition on the microstructural stability of coherent 
γ/γ′ phases in the designed [Al‑Ni12](Al1(Al/Ti/Nb/Ta)0.5(Cr0.5Mo0.5W0.5)) superalloys. The coherent 
microstructure with cuboidal γ′ nanoparticles could be formed during aging at 1173 K in these designed 
superalloys due to the moderate lattice misfit. The addition of Ti, Nb, or Ta element can not only reduce 
the size of γ′ nanoparticles, but also improve the microstructural stability during aging, as evidenced by 
smaller coarsening rate constant in Ti/Nb/Ta‑added alloys than that in Al‑added alloy. Moreover, Ti/Nb/
Ta‑added alloys exhibit prominent mechanical properties, which are ascribed to the γ′ particle size since 
the volume fraction values of γ′ in designed alloys are comparable and it keeps nearly constant during 
aging. Besides, the addition of Ti, Nb, or Ta could also enhance the precipitation of W/Mo‑enriched μ 
phase on grain boundaries.
Introduction
Ni-base superalloys possess excellent high-temperature resist-
ance to creep and fatigue strength because the spherical or 
cuboidal L12 γ′-Ni3Al nanoparticles coherently precipitate into 
the face-centered cubic (FCC)-γ solid solution matrix [1 ]. In 
order to obtain an optimal γ/γ′ coherent microstructure for 
prominent comprehensive properties, more than ten alloying 
elements were usually added to the Ni-base superalloys [2 –5]. 
And all these alloying elements would like to be classified into 
Al-like Al series (Al, Ti, Nb, Ta, being γ′ -forming elements), 
Cr-like Cr series (Cr, Mo, W), and Ni-like Ni series (Ni, Co, 
Re, Ru, Ir), respectively, according to the interaction between 
the alloying element with the base Ni [6 –8]. In particular, the 
matching among them can affect the lattice misfit (δ ) of γ/γ′ 
on a large extent and then decide the morphology of γ′ nano-
precipitates [2, 9–14]. When δ < 0.2%, γ′ nanoparticles exhibit 
a spherical shape, while they tend to form a cubic shape when 
δ is between 0.2 and 0.6%, where the δ is more sensitive to the 
variation of Al series of elements [ 8]. Moreover, it has demon-
strated that the thermal stability of γ/γ′ coherent microstructure, 
tensile strength, and creep properties at elevated temperatures of 
superalloys are closely dependent on the δ [15, 16]. For example, 
the rupture lifetimes of TMS-238 (Ni–6.5Co–5.9Al–7.6Ta–4.6
Cr–0.1Hf–1.1Mo–4W–6.4Re–5Ru wt %) and TMS-196 (Ni–5.
6Co–5.6Al–5.6Ta–4.6Cr–0.1Hf–2.4Mo–5W–6.4Re–5Ru wt %) 
single-crystalline superalloys are t r = 1930 h and tr = 1001 h at 
1373 K/137 MPa, respectively. The different creep rupture life -
times are attributed to the fact that the former has a relatively 
larger lattice misfit (δ = − 0.44%) than the latter (δ = − 0.39%) 
[17, 18]. A larger magnitude of lattice misfit can result in a 
finer spacing of misfit dislocations and a stronger γ/γ′ interface, 
which would in turn act as a stronger barrier to the moving 
dislocations, resulting in denser γ/γ ′ dislocation networks, so 
TMS-238 alloy has a longer creep rupture lifetime [19, 20].
However, an excessive addition of refractory elements 
(Nb, Ta, Mo, W , Re, etc.) would destabilize the γ/γ′ coherent 
microstructure and induce the precipitation of brittle topo -
logical close-packed (TCP) phases, such as σ-(Cr,Mo)(Ni,Co) 
 (D8b-CrFe type), μ-(Ni,Co,Cr) 7(Mo,W ,Ti,Nb,Ta)6 (D85-Fe7W6 
type), η-Ni 3Ti (D024-Ni3Ti type), and so on, which worsens 
the high-temperature creep properties of Ni-base superal-
loys [21–24]. So, the added amount of each element should be

Results
Thermodynamic prediction
The phase diagrams of experimental alloys were analyzed by 
Pandat software, as shown in Fig. 1. It is found that the melting 
point and the solvus temperature of γ ′ of the S1-Al alloy with -
out Ti/Nb/Ta addition are approximately 1624 K and 1561 K, 
respectively. Moreover, the γ′ phase precipitates coherently from 
the γ matrix at 1561 K and the amount of precipitation gradu -
ally increases with decreasing temperature. The separate addi-
tion of Ti, Nb, or Ta element could increase both the melting 
temperature and solvus temperature of γ′ in S2-S4 alloys, being 
about 1660 K and 1600 K, respectively [Fig. 1(b)–(d)]. Moreover, 
several TCP phases with different molar fractions also appear 
in these alloys when the temperature decreases below 1150 K, 
as exampled by the orthorhombic Cr/Mo-rich P-Cr 18Mo42Ni40 
phase with a molar fraction of f < 0.9% within the range of 
914–1142 K and the Cr/Mo/W-rich μ-Ni 7(Cr, Mo, W)6 phase 
(f < 1.3%) below 914 K in S2-AlTi alloy [Fig.  1(b)]. In addition, 
the δ-Ni3Nb phase with an orthorhombic structure could coexist 
with the μ phase in S3-AlNb alloy below 886 K and the molar 
fraction could reach up to about 7% [Fig. 1(c)].

Microstructural evolution of γ/γ′ during aging process
Figure  5 shows the SEM image of the current alloys aged for 
100 h, 200 h, and 500 h, respectively, at 1173 K, which will be 
used to study the microstructural stability of γ/γ′. With the aging 
time increases, the particle size of γ′ in S1-Al alloy will increase 
from the r = 234 ± 45 nm in 100 h-aged state to r = 274 ± 73 nm 
in 200 h-aged state, and then to r = 331 ± 78 nm in 500 h-aged 
state [Fig. 5(a-1)–(c-1)]. While in the S2-AlTi alloy with addi-
tion of Ti element, the particle size of γ′ will be decreased to 
r = 238 ± 62 nm in 200 h-aged state and r = 269 ± 67 nm in 
500 h-aged state [Fig. 5(b-2, c-2)]. By contrast, the particle size 
of γ′ in S3-AlNb and S4-AlTa alloys with Nb or Ta addition are 
smallest after 500 h aging, being about r ~ 210 nm [Fig. 5(c-3, 
c-4)]. The variations of the particle size of γ′ precipitates in dif-
ferent states are summarized in Fig. 6(a), which shows a gradu-
ally increasing tendency.
Figure 6(b) summarizes the volume fraction (f ) of γ′ pre-
cipitates in different aged state for the current alloys, which 
can be seen that the volume fraction of γ′ precipitates in each 
alloy keeps nearly constant after aging for 50 h with the val-
ues of 78% in S1-Al alloy and f = 74–75% in S2-S4 alloy with 
Ti, Nb, or Ta addition after 500 h aging. The fact that the 
volume fraction remains constant indicates that the particles 
are beginning to coarsen, which can be explained by Ostwald 
ripening theory [38, 39]. Furthermore, these experimentally 
measured f values in 500 h-aged alloys were also compared 
with the values calculated by the thermodynamic calculation 
software (Fig. S2). It indicated that the experimental value is 
slightly lower than the calculated one, but both show a similar 
variation tendency with Ti, Nb, and Ta addition.
In order to further characterize the elemental distribu-
tion in the γ/γ′ coherent microstructure, the S4-AlTa alloy 
after 500 h aging was analyzed with the EPMA (Fig. S3). It 
can be seen that Al and Ta elements are mainly segregated in 
the L12-γ′ nanoprecipitates, while Cr, Mo and W elements are 
enriched in the FCC-γ matrix.

Mechanical properties
Figure 7(a) shows the microhardness (HV) of the current alloys 
at different aged state. In the solid-solutionized state, all alloys 
exhibit a relatively high microhardness 370–450 HV due to the 
nanoprecipitates precipitated in the FCC matrix. With the aging 
time increasing, the HV could slightly decrease. The microhard-
ness of S1-Al alloy in different aged states are 371 ± 5 HV (solid-
solutionized state), 356 ± 4 HV (50 h-aged state), and 319 ± 5 HV 
(500 h-aged state), respectively, which indicates that the particle 
size of γ′ particles will coarsen gradually (r = 123–331 nm and 
f ~ 78%, Fig. 6). In addition, adding Ti, Nb, or Ta element will 
enhance the microhardness values of alloys, such as the values of 
364 ± 4 HV in 500 h-aged S2-AlTi alloy (r ~ 270 nm and f ~ 75%), 
412 ± 6 HV in 500  h-aged S3-AlNb alloy (r ~ 220  nm and 
f ~ 74%), and 420 ± 9 HV in 500 h-aged S4-AlTa alloy (r ~ 200 nm 
and f ~ 75%), which shows that the increase of microhardness 
is related to the size of γ′ precipitates. It is clear that the main 
reason for the higher microhardness of S3-AlNb and S4-AlTa 
alloys is the smaller particle size of γ′ precipitates.

Discussion
Influences of the Ti/Nb/Ta addition 
on the microstructural stability of γ/γ′
In order to obtain the lattice constants of γ and γ′ phases, 
we analyzed the XRD results of the aged alloys from 50 to 
500 h (Fig. S4). Figure  8(a) shows the change of δ with the 
aging time in these experimental alloys. The lattice misfit 
of γ/γ′ in 50 h-aged S1-Al alloy is δ = − 0.41%, while the 
Ti, Nb, or Ta addition increase the lattice misfit of γ/γ′ up 
to δ = − 0.42% (S2-AlTi), − 0.43% (S3-AlNb), and − 0.48% 
(S4-AlTa), respectively, which is mainly contributed to that 
the Ti, Nb, or Ta addition widens the gap between the lattice 
constants of FCC-γ and L12-γ′. With the aging time increas-
ing, the absolute values of γ/γ′ lattice misfit will be reduced 
slightly. For instance, the δ of γ/γ′ in S2-AlTi alloy decreases 
from − 0.42% in 50 h-aged state to − 0.32% in 500 h-aged 
state. Actually, after aging for 500 h, the δ values of these 
alloys are comparable, being about − 0.30 to − 0.37%. This 
change is associated with the elemental repartition due to 
atomic diffusion, which reduces the compositional differ -
ence between γ/γ ′ and then the lattice misfit [41, 42]. In 
500 h-aged state, the regular cuboidal γ′ precipitates exist in 
each alloy, which is ascribed to the moderate lattice misfit 
between γ/γ ′ [11].

 Ni45.08 Al4.24 Ta2.31 Cr7.00 Mo27.12 W14.25 (at.%). Since the ratio 
of (Ni, Cr) to (Mo, W , Ta) is 52:44, the bright precipitates 
are identified as μ-(Ni,Cr) 7(Mo,W ,Ta)6 phase. While the con-
tinuous γ ′ phases on grain boundaries are enriched by the Al 
and Ta elements, as identified by the chemical composition 
of Ni75.79 Al15.97 Ta4.56Cr1.23Mo0.80W1.65 (at.%). Relevant stud-
ies have shown that the rapid diffusion of the elements on 
the grain boundaries is the main reason for coarsening of γ′ 
nanoprecipitates. Furthermore, the coarsening of γ′ nanopar-
ticles decreases the volume fraction of the adjoining γ phase, 
which corresponds to an increase in the concentration of TCP 
phase forming elements in γ and then inevitably affects the 
formation of μ phase [51, 52]. In fact, trace addition of B or 
Zr element would contribute to purify the grain boundaries 
and prevent the brittle phase precipitation or the coarsening 
of γ′ nanoparticles [53 –55].
Conclusions
The effect of Ti, Nb, or Ta addition on the thermal stability and 
microstructural evolution of coherent γ/γ′ in the [Al-(Ni 12)]
(Al1(Al/Ti/Nb/Ta)0.5(Cr,Mo,W)1.5) superalloys was investigated 
systematically. The following conclusions could be drawn.
(1) All these superalloys possess a similar coherent micro -
structure with cuboidal γ′ nanoparticles precipitated 
into the FCC-γ matrix, which could be more regular 
during aging at 1173 K due to a moderate lattice misfit 
(δ = − 0.30 to Al 0.48%) between γ/γ′. Particularly, 
the addition of Ti, Nb, or Ta element can improve the 
microstructural stability of γ/γ′ obviously, as demon-
strated by the fact that the coarsening rate constant 
of γ′ nanoparticles (K = 3.70–7.70 nm3 s−1 at 1173 K) 
in Ti/Nb/Ta-containing superalloys is lower than that 
(K = 18.33 nm3 s−1) of the alloy containing Al alone. 
Moreover, the particle size of γ′ nanoparticles in Nb/
Ta-added superalloys after 500 h aging is r ~ 210 nm, 
much smaller than those of Al/Ti-added alloys 
(r = 331 ± 78 nm for S1-Al and (r = 269 ± 67 nm for 
S2-AlTi in the same state). The volume fraction of γ′

Experimental
Button ingots of each experimental alloy, ~ 30 g in mass, 
were prepared using high purity Ni (99.9 wt%), Cr (99.9 
wt%), Mo (99.5 wt%), W (99.5 wt%), Al (99.9 wt%) Ti (99.9 
wt%), Nb (99.5 wt%), and Ta (99.9 wt%) in a vacuum arc-
melting unit equipped with water cooled copper hearth 
and a tungsten electrode. Each ingot was melted six times 
with flipping after each cycle for homogenous melting 
throughout the ingot under a partial Ar atmosphere. Ingots 
of ~ 100 g were also arc-melted and cast in the form of rods 
(diameter, ϕ-10 mm, length, L-70 mm) using a split water-
coper mold in a vacuum suction casting unit for compres-
sion tests. All alloys were vacuum-encapsulated in quartz 
tube for aging heat-treatment. Alloy samples were solution-
treated at 1573 K for 15 h and subsequently aged at 1173 K 
for 50, 100, 200, and 500 h, respectively. All alloy samples 
were water-quenched following solution-treated and aging 
treatment.
The X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8, Bruker) analysis were 
carried out employing a Cu source X-ray diffractometer for 
phase identification and also used to estimate the room-
temperature γ and γ′ lattice constants and lattice misfit for 
each heated-treated sample. The microstructures of samples 
were characterized by optical microscopy (OM, OLYMPUS 
GX53), scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Supra 55) 
and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM2100FFEG). 
For OM analysis, the heated-treated samples were mechani-
cally ground, polished, chemically etched using a corrosive 
solution containing 5 g FeCl3⋅6H2O, 25 ml HCl and 25 ml 
 C2H5OH for 30–50 s. For SEM analysis, the samples electro -
lytically etched in a corrosive solution with 42% H3PO4, 34% 
 H2SO4 and 24% H2O at 5 V for 10–15 s. For TEM analysis, the 
samples were prepared by thin disks with a diameter of 3 mm 
to the thickness of 50 μm and subsequently double-jet elec -
tropolished using a solution of 5% HClO4 and 95% CH3OH 
at a temperature of 243 K and a voltage of 16 V to get the 
transparent regions for TEM. The composition of each phase 
and elemental distributions were measured by field-emission 
electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JXA-8530F PLUS), the 
accelerating voltage and probe current being 15 kV and 1.0 × 
 10–8 A, respectively. With the ImageJ software, a large num -
ber of precipitates for each heated-treated condition, over 6 
SEM micrographs, were hand-traced and their area A was 
used to calculate the mean circular equivalent (r = √A/π ). 
Also, the volume fraction f of γ′ precipitates was given by the 
area fraction Af with ImageJ software [56]. Phase equilibria 
were investigated by Pandat software with the latest PanNickel 
database [ 57, 58]. The hardness of the alloys was measured 
using a hardness testing machine (HVS-1000) with an applied 
load of 0.5 kg and a 15 s dwell time. Each hardness value is the 
average of 15 measurements performed over multiple grains. 
The compression tests for the aged (1173 K, 200 h) samples 
with 3 mm × 6 mm (diameter × length) were carried out on a 
Material Test System (UTM5504) at 1123 K. A constant rate 
of 1 × 10–3 S−1 was used in this study.
