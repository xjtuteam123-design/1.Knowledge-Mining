Flow stress anomalies in c/c0 two-phase Co–Al–W-base alloys
Akane Suzuki, Garret C. DeNolf and Tresa M. Pollock *
Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Michigan, 2300 Hayward Street, Ann Arbor, MI 48109, USA
Received 28 September 2006; revised 27 October 2006; accepted 31 October 2006
Available online 1 December 2006
The high-temperature strength of new cobalt-base alloys reinforced with a high volume fraction of a Co 3(Al, W) ternary L12
intermetallic compound has been examined. The ﬂow stress exhibits a strong, positive dependence on temperature above 873 K
and the strength of a Co–Al–W–Ta quaternary alloy is comparable to that of nickel-base superalloys at 1173 K. The ﬂow anomaly
is attributed to the activation of multiple slip modes within the precipitates. Above the peak ﬂow temperature, h112 i dislocation
shearing of precipitates is the dominant deformation mode.

Keywords: Cobalt alloys; Intermetallic compounds; High temperature deformation; Dislocations; Stacking faults
The recent discovery of the stable ternary
Co3(Al, W) intermetallic compound with a geometrically
close-packed L12 structure [1] provides a pathway for
development of a new class of load-bearing, cobalt-base
high-temperature alloys. The thermodynamic coexis-
tence of the c-Co solid-solution phase with face-centered
cubic (fcc) structure and the c
0-Co3(Al, W) phase and the
similarity of their lattice parameters permit establish-
ment of a two-phase structure with a high degree of
coherency. This structure consists of submicron regularly
aligned cuboidal c
0 precipitates embedded in a continu-
ous c matrix phase. This structure is morphologically
identical to the microstructure of Ni-base superalloys,
which consist of the c-Ni solid-solution matrix phase
(fcc) and the c0-Ni3(Al, Ti) compound ( L12).
Ni-base superalloys are commercially important
materials due to their application in numerous high-
temperature components of aircraft engines and power-
generation systems, including turbine blades, where they
are utilized in single crystal form. While they possess
many attractive properties, the usage of conventional
Co-based alloys, where strengthening arises from car-
bides and elements in solid solution, has been limited
because of their inferior high-temperature strength
compared to the Ni-base superalloys [2]. However, Co-
based alloys potentially possess superior hot corrosion,
oxidation and wear resistance compared to Ni-base
alloys [2,3]. Therefore, development of novel Co-based
superalloys reinforced with high volume fractions of
the L1
2 compound oﬀers new possibilities for application
in severe environments, such as in industrial combined-
cycle power-generation systems that utilize gasiﬁed coal
rather than natural gas.
In this study, the high-temperature strength and
strengthening mechanisms of the c/c0 two-phase Co–
Al–W-based alloys were examined in order to explore
their potential for high-temperature applications.
Alloys with compositions of Co–9Al–9W and Co–
9Al–10W–2Ta (at.%) were melted as 65 g button ingots
using a Crystalox induction levitation melting system
under an argon back-ﬁlled atmosphere after evacuation.
These alloys are hereafter referred to as 9Al–9W alloy
and 2Ta alloy, respectively. Pure 99.9% Co and
99.99% Al pellets and a combination of compressed
high-purity elemental powders of 99.5% Co, 99.95% W
and 99.9% Ta were utilized. The 9Al–9W and 2Ta alloys
were homogenized at 1623 K for 43.2 ks, and then aged
at 1173 K for 540 ks and 1273 K for 360 ks, respectively,
to achieve a c/c
0 two-phase microstructure. For these
heat treatments, the alloys were sealed in quartz cap-
sules, which were argon back-ﬁlled after evacuation.
After holding in the furnace, the samples were quenched
into water quickly by breaking the capsules.
Diﬀerential thermal analysis (DTA) was conducted
using a Seteram Setsys 18 system, calibrated with high-
purity Ni and Ag using a Pt reference prior to the exper-
iment [4]. The cylindrical samples (3.5 mm in diameter
and 3–4 mm in height) with mass ranging from 250 to
300 mg were prepared, and transformation temperatures
were determined during heating at 5 K min

of the specimens were polished to remove surface resid-

compression ﬁxture in a 5582 screw-driven Instron with
a digital data acquisition system. The displacements
between the platens were measured by linear variable
diﬀerential transducers. A clamshell type resistance
furnace was used for high-temperature tests. During
the test, the temperature variation was maintained with-

Microstructure was examined by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) using a Philips XL30-FEG. A Philips
CM12 TEM was used for dislocation analysis. From the
specimens after compression tests, discs 0.12 mm in
thickness and 3 mm in diameter were cut, mechanically
polished, and then subjected to twin-jet electropolishing
in a solution of methanol with 5 vol.% perchloric acid at
243 K and 18–20 V.

the 9Al–9W ( Fig. 1 a) and 2Ta ( Fig. 1 b) alloys aged at

The heat treatment temperatures were chosen in consid-
eration of the transformation temperatures, determined
by DTA. The liquidus, solidus and c
0 solvus tempera-
tures were 1763, 1731 and 1273 K, respectively, for the
9Al–9W alloy, and 1724, 1680 and 1352 K, respectively,
for the 2Ta alloy. Thus, the addition of Ta to the Co–
Al–W ternary alloy strongly stabilizes the c
0 phase.
The morphology of the c0 phase in the 9Al–9W (a)
and 2Ta (b) alloys is cuboidal with 100 and 300 nm cube
edge lengths, respectively. The volume fraction of the c
0
phase ranges from 60% to 70%, similar to the advanced
Ni-base single crystal alloys.
Compression testing was conducted to examine the
temperature dependence of the strength of these alloys.
The 0.2% ﬂow stress is plotted as a function of temper-
ature in Figure 2 . For both alloys, the temperature
dependence of the 0.2% ﬂow stress exhibits three stages
of variation. The ﬁrst stage is the normal decay of ﬂow
stress with temperature over the temperature range from
the room temperature to 873 K. In the second stage,
there is an anomalous positive temperature variation
where the ﬂow stress substantially increases with tem-
perature. The third stage again displays a negative tem-
perature variation, which is typical for materials above
50% of their melting temperatures. The 0.2% ﬂow stress
of the 9Al–9W alloy decreases from about 520 MPa at
room temperature to 420 MPa in the ﬁrst stage, and
then increases to 460 MPa at 973 K, followed by rapid
decrease with temperature. In the case of the 2Ta alloy,
the 0.2% ﬂow stress decreases from 630 MPa at room
temperature to 455 MPa in the ﬁrst stage, and then
increases to 575 MPa at 1073 K, followed by slight
decrease with temperature. It is apparent that the
addition of Ta signiﬁcantly improves the high-tempera-
ture strength by suppressing the onset of the third stage
where the strength decays. Compared to the 0.2% yield
strength of the conventional Co-base alloys [5], also
plotted together in Figure 2 , the 0.2% ﬂow stresses of
the new two-phase alloys are comparable at room
temperature. However, the strength of the Ta-contain-
ing alloy exceeds the conventional Co-base alloys at
high-temperatures above 973 K. Interestingly, the ﬂow
stress even exceeds that of a Ni-base superalloy [5] at
the highest temperature.
The inverse dependence of the ﬂow stress on temper-
ature and the appearance of a peak in the ﬂow stress at
elevated temperatures is observed in a number of inter-
metallic based systems [6–14]. The Ni-base superalloys
that contain a large volume fraction of L1
2 precipitates
are also known to show the ﬂow stress anomalies [5,15–
17]. However, the anomalous increase in the 0.2% yield
stress is slight in two-phase Ni-base superalloys, as
shown in Figure 2 . The Co-base alloys examined here
exhibit larger ﬂow stress anomalies, with a 50 and
120 MPa increase in the 9Al–9W and the 2Ta alloys,
respectively, compared to Ni-base alloys, in spite of
similar volume fractions of precipitates. This result
indicates that this Co-based alloy system has a great
potential for high-temperature structural applications
above 1173 K. Further improvements in high-tempera-
ture strength may be achieved through stabilization of
the c
0 phase via higher-order alloying additions.

tions from the octahedral {1 1 1} plane to the cube
{0 0 1} plane becomes frequent. Because pairs of
1/2h110 i dislocations are coupled by APBs, cross-slip
of one dislocation of the pair extends the APB from
the octahedral to the cube plane. This type of disloca-
tion conﬁguration makes further glide diﬃcult and
results in the anomalous hardening, though detailed
models for this process are still under debate. In the
c/c
0 two-phase alloys that contain a disordered c phase,
dislocation movement ﬁrst occurs in the disordered
phase and then, dislocations enter the c
0 phase with
progress of deformation. Once dislocations enter the
precipitates, they may behave in a manner similar to
the single-phase intermetallics [21]. Therefore, observa-
tion of h110 i dislocations on both octahedral and cube
planes within the precipitate phase suggests operation
of similar dislocation processes in the Co-based and
Ni-based L1
2 phases.
The existence of the disordered phase and the pres-
ence of the interfaces also inﬂuence the deformation
modes. Stacking faults in the c
0 phase are also com-
monly observed defects in two-phase Ni-base superal-
loys deformed in the low-temperature regime around
1033 K [15,22–25], but not in L1
2 single-phase alloys.
Shearing of c0 precipitates by 1/3 ½11 /C222/C138 dislocations can
take place when either ½11 /C222/C138 (1 1 1) or ½10 /C221/C138 (1 1 1) slip
system is activated. In the former case, a 1/2 ½10 /C221/C138 dislo-
cation in the matrix channel reacts with another
1/2½01 /C221/C138 dislocation or dissociates at the c/c0 interface,
and a 1/3 ½11 /C222/C138 dislocation enters the c0 precipitate, leav-
ing a 1/6 h112 i dislocation at the interface [15,22–24].
In the latter case, the dissociation or reaction of ½10 /C221/C138
dislocations forming 1/3 ½11 /C222/C138 dislocations takes place
in the matrix channel, and the 1/3 ½11 /C222/C138 dislocations
shear the c0 precipitates [15]. The characterization of
the partial dislocations will be reported elsewhere, but
these modes of shearing might be expected to occur
more easily in Co-base alloys, considering their lower
stacking fault energy compared to Ni base alloys
[2,26]. The Shockley partial dislocations within the pre-
cipitates exhibit unusual periodic constrictions at
1073 K (Fig. 3b), that may imply that a new type of peri-
odic pinning occurs during slip. The higher density of
faults within the precipitates in this Co-base system
compared to the Ni-base systems may also be the reason
for the greater rise in ﬂow stress as the anomalous peak
is reached.
Although the stacking faults are common defects at
lower temperatures in c/c
0 Ni-base superalloys [15,22–
25], it is obvious that the shearing of the c0 precipitates
accompanied by stacking fault formation is the domi-
nant deformation mode at higher temperatures in the
c/c0 Co-base alloys. The shearing of the c0 precipitates
by partial dislocations, which includes the reaction of
dislocations at the c/c0 interfaces to form partial disloca-
tions and gliding of the partial dislocations across the c0
precipitates, is apparently a thermally activated phe-
nomenon in this alloy system. Therefore, the ﬂow stress
anomalies are attributed to the activation of multiple
slip modes within the c0 precipitates, which include the
slip of h110 i dislocations below the peak and thermally
activated slip of h112 i dislocations above the peak.
In summary, Co–Al–W based c/c0 two-phase alloys
exhibit the ﬂow stress anomalies above 873 K. The
Ta-containing alloy has a strength comparable to a
conventional Ni-base superalloy at 1173 K, due to its
strong ﬂow stress anomaly. The ﬂow stress anomalies
are attributed to the activation of multiple slip modes
within the c
0 precipitates, which include the slip of
h110 i dislocations below the peak and thermally acti-
vated slip of h112 i dislocations above the peak.
