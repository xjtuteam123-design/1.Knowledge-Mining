High temperature tensile properties of W-Re-ThO 2 alloys 
Anhua Luo, Kwang S. Shin and Dean L. Jacobson 
Department of Chemical, Bio and Materials Engineering, Arizona State Universi~, Tempe, AZ 85287-6006 (U.S.A.) 
(Received May 23, 1991) 
Abstract 
The tensile properties of W-Re-lwt.%ThO2 alloys with rhenium concentrations of 0-26wt.% were 
examined in a temperature range 1600-2600 K. The effects of rhenium concentration, testing tempera- 
ture and strain rate on the strength properties and deformation behavior of these alloys were investi- 
gated. It was found that rhenium had a moderate strengthening effect of the thoriated tungsten at 
temperatures up to about 2000 K. In the temperature range 2000-2600 K, rhenium concentrations 
above l I wt.% in the thoriated tungsten resulted in a strength degradation instead of strengthening 
owing to rhenium-introduced structural instability. The addition of rhenium increased both the tem- 
perature sensitivity and the strain rate sensitivity of the thoriated tungsten at high temperatures. The 
post-test specimens were characterized by scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron 
microscopy. The dominant deformation mechanism was the cross-slip of screw dislocations when 
deformation occurred in the temperature range 1600-2000 K and was grain boundary sliding when 
deformation occurred at temperatures above 2000 K. An examination on the fracture surfaces of the 
tensile-fractured specimens showed that the fracture mode of the W-Re-lwt.%ThO 2 alloys experi- 
enced a transition from transgranular to intergranular with either increasing temperature or increasing 
rhenium concentration. 
1. Introduction 
The need for materials with useful strength 
above 1600 K has stimulated the interest in 
refractory alloys. Because of its extremely high 
elastic modulus and melting temperature, tung- 
sten is one of the most attractive metals for high 
temperature applications. The major deterrents 
to the use of tungsten are its poor fabricability at 
room temperature and the reduction of strength 
retention at high temperatures. In order to 
improve the properties, and hence to utilize the 
metal, there was a significant effort in Lewis 
Research Center, National Aeronautics and Space 
Administration, during the Apollo era to develop 
tungsten-base alloys with improved room tem- 
perature fabricability and high temperature 
strength [1-4]. It was found that the addition of 
rhenium to tungsten could significantly improve 
the room temperature ductility [2-7]. On the 
contrary, thoria appeared to be an effective 
strengthener in tungsten at high temperatures 
[8-10]. The effect of rhenium on the high tem- 
perature mechanical properties of thoriated tung- 
sten has not been well documented. 
This paper presents the data which were 
obtained as a part of a continuing program 
designed to document the mechanical properties 
of tungsten-base alloys over a wide temperature 
range. The purpose of the present study was to 
examine the tensile properties of the 
W-Re-IThO2 (where the thoria content is given 
in weight per cent) alloys in a temperature range 
1600-2600 K, i.e. (0.45-0.70)T m (where Tm is 
melting temperature of tungsten in kelvins). The 
fracture mode of these alloys in the same tem- 
perature range was also examined. The present 
investigation was focused on the effects of 
rhenium concentration, testing temperature and 
strain rate on the strength properties and the 
deformation behavior of the W-Re- 1ThO 2 alloys 
at high temperatures.

3. Results 
Figure 1 shows the 0.2% offset yield strengths 
of the W-1ThO2 and the W-Re-IThO 2 alloys as 
a function of testing temperature. The yield 
strength of the alloys decreased monotonically 
with increasing temperature over the entire tem- 
perature range. It was found that rhenium had a 
moderate strengthening effect on the thoriated 
tungsten up to about 2000 K. The yield strength 
of W-11Re-IThO2 at 2000 K was 185 MPa, 
about 75 MPa greater than the yield strength of 
W-1ThO 2 at the same temperature. One interest- 
ing phenomenon observed in the present study 
was the reduction in strength due to the high con- 
centration of rhenium added to the thoriated 
tungsten in the temperature range 2000-2600 K. 
The yield strength of W-26Re-lThO2 dropped 
rapidly, eventually became smaller than the yield 
strength of W-11Re-IThO2, then W-3.6Re- 
1ThO2 and finally W-1ThO2. This indicated 
that in this temperature range the high rhenium 
concentration would weaken tungsten instead 
of strengthening it. The same phenomenon was 
observed in W-Re-HfC alloys [1, 11]. The high 
temperature strength of a W-3.6Re-0.26HfC 
alloy was greater than that of a W-23.4Re- 
0.27HfC. 
Figure 2 presents the ultimate tensile strengths 
of W-lThO2 and W-Re-IThO 2 alloys. The ten- 
sile strength of W-26Re-lThO2 was lower than 
that of W-11Re-lThO2 at temperatures above 
2000 K, was lower than that of W-3.6Re-lThO2 
at temperatures above 2200 K and was even 
lower than that of W-IThO2 at temperatures 
above 2400 K. Therefore it was concluded that 
the rhenium concentrations above 11 wt.% in the 
thoriated tungsten would cause a strength 
degradation in the temperature range 2000- 
2600 K.

4. Discussion 
In the present study, the rhenium concentra- 
tion of all the W-Re-lThO2 alloys is within the 
range of solid solution. It is well known that the 
solid solution strengthening results from the 
interaction between solute atoms and disloca- 
tions. According to the six mechanisms [9] by 
which the solute atoms could interact with dislo- 
cations, there are only two mechanisms able to 
act at high temperatures. One is the elastic inter- 
action which arises from the mutual interaction of 
elastic stress field that surrounds the misfit solute 
atom and the edge dislocation core. Another is 
the modulus interaction which occurs when the 
presence of the solute atom locally alters the 
modulus of the matrix. Both of the above 
strengthening mechanisms assume that the 
strengthening effect is proportional to the solute

## Page 7

concentration. We might ask why a high rhenium 
concentration would reduce the strength of the 
thoriated tungsten at high temperatures instead of 
strengthening it. The possible explanations for 
this phenomenon must come from the unusual 
manner of interaction between the rhenium 
atoms and the tungsten matrix. 
Zener [13] was the first to propose that the 
shear modulus G could be related to the struc- 
tural stability of b.c.c, crystals since the G value is 
in proportion to the bonding energy. A metal 
possessing a large G value exhibits a high struc- 
tural stability. Therefore the magnitude of the 
shear modulus is considered as an indicator of the 
structural stability of a b.c.c, crystal. Using this 
assumption, Ayres [14] found that the addition of 
rhenium would result in tungsten's structural 
instability because tungsten's shear modulus 
decreased sharply when alloyed with rhenium. It 
was determined that every 1 wt.% addition of 
rhenium to tungsten would result in a 0.7% 
reduction in the shear modulus of tungsten. The 
more rhenium that was added, the lower the shear 
modulus of tungsten, i.e. the more unstable the 
crystal structure. A similar phenomenon was 
observed in Mo-Re alloy [15] where every 1 wt.% 
Re added to molybdenum resulted in a 0.5% 
reduction in the shear modulus of molybdenum. 
According to Fuchs [16], the resistance to 
shear in metals results from two types of inter- 
action: (a) the Coulombic interaction between 
positive ions and negative conduction electrons 
and (b) the exchange interaction between ions, 
thought to be significant only between nearest 
and second-nearest neighbors. Fisher and Dever 
[17] have demonstrated that the shear modulus of 
metals from groups IVA and VIA are directly 
related to their d electron concentration. Zener 
[18] and Isenberg [19] have proposed that the 
high structural stability of the transition b.c.c. 
metals are mainly due to the repulsive exchange 
force between the second nearest neighbor ions, 
i.e. the directional nature of the d electron wave- 
functions. Since such a short-range interaction 
depends in a sensitive way on the electron con- 
figuration in the d shell, the alloying element 
which affects the electron configuration in the d 
shell would also affect the interatomic bonding 
strength. On the basis of the experimental obser- 
vation that tungsten alloyed with rhenium shows 
a decreased shear modulus, Ayres [14] concluded 
that rhenium added to tungsten would strongly 
change the directional nature of the d electron 
225 
wavefunctions and hence cause a decreased inter- 
atomic bonding strength and thus the structural 
stability. This is confirmed by the W-Re phase 
diagram [20]. The melting temperature, a 
measurement of the interatomic bonding energy, 
of W-Re alloy drops rapidly with increasing 
rhenium concentration. The melting temperature 
of a W-26Re alloy drops rapidly with increasing 
rhenium concentration. The melting temperature 
of a W-26Re alloy, for example, is approximately 
400 K lower than that of pure tungsten. This 
implies that the high rhenium alloy possesses a 
smaller bonding energy than the low rhenium 
alloy at a given temperature. This difference in 
melting point does not have a significant influ- 
ence on the mechanical properties at low or inter- 
mediate temperatures but will play a key role 
when metal is deformed at high temperatures. For 
example, deformation at a temperature of 2600 K 
corresponds to the deformation at a normalized 
temperature of 0.70 T m for W-1ThO2, 0.71 T m for 
W-3.6Re-IThO2 or 0.74Tin for W-11Re- 
1ThO 2. For the W-26Re-lThO 2 alloy, it corre- 
sponds to deformation at a normalized tempera- 
ture of 0.79T m. Considering an extreme case, a 
temperature of 3300 K corresponds to a temper- 
ature of 0.89 T m for W-1ThO2, but it corresponds 
to a temperature of 1.0T m for W-26Re-1ThO 2 
which has no strength at all at this temperature. 
On the contrary, the rhenium added to 
the thoriated tungsten causes solid solution 
strengthening due to the elastic interaction and 
the modulus interaction as mentioned earlier. 
Thus the mechanical properties of a thoriated 
W-Re alloy depend upon whether the solid solu- 
tion strengthening of rhenium or the rhenium- 
introduced structural instability is dominant at a 
given temperature. In general, the solution 
strengthening is very sensitive to temperature but 
the bonding energy is primarily related to the 
solute concentration. At temperatures below 
0.5Tin, the solution-strengthening effect is very 
predominate so that, the more rhenium added, 
the higher is the strength. At temperatures above 
0.5 T m the solid solution strengthening of rhenium 
becomes relatively small because the thermal 
activation and solute diffusion significantly 
reduce the interaction forces between the solute 
atoms and the dislocations. Once the temperature 
is high enough that the solution-strengthening 
effect of rhenium is no longer able to compensate 
for the rhenium-introduced structural instability, 
the weakening effect becomes dominant. At such

a temperature, the more rhenium added to tung- 
sten, the lower is its strength. If we extrapolate the 
strength of the W-Re-IThO2 alloys to the higher 
temperatures, we find that the strength of all 
W-Re-lThO2 alloys will eventually become 
smaller than that of W-lThO2. 
Therefore the percentage of rhenium needed 
to produce the maximum strength in the thoriated 
tungsten is a function of temperature. At tem- 
peratures below 2000 K, W-26Re-lThO 2 was 
found to possess the highest strength among all 
the alloys examined in the present study. In the 
temperature range 2000-2600 K, an addition of 
11 wt.% Re to the thoriated tungsten resulted in 
the largest strengthening effect. Naturally, it is 
expected that the strength of any W-Re-IThO2 
alloy would be lower than that of W-1ThO2 at a 
temperature above 2600 K. Therefore the work- 
ing temperature should be considered during 
alloy selection when using tungsten. 
From the results shown in Figs. 5 and 6, the 
high rhenium alloy is more sensitive to the change 
in strain rate than the low rhenium alloy. A strain 
rate sensitivity of about 0.14 at 2000 K in a 
W-1ThO 2 alloy was determined by King [11]; the 
comparison between his data and the present 
results suggests that there is an approximated 
linear relationship between the strain rate sensi- 
tivity and the rhenium concentration for W-Re- 
1ThO 2 alloys. 
Generally, a material with high strain rate sen- 
sitivity shows a significant temperature sensitivity. 
According to the relationship between the activa- 
tion energy and the rhenium concentration of the 
W-Re-IThO 2 alloys given in eqn. (3), the present 
study confirms that the addition of rhenium does 
increase the temperature sensitivity of the 
thoriated tungsten. This phenomenon is shared 
by many common solid solution alloys. Disloca- 
tion dipoles were observed in all the W-Re- 
1ThO 2 specimens which were deformed at high 
temperatures. Figure 11 is a typical TEM micro- 
graph showing such a substructure. No disloca- 
tion dipole was found in the W-1ThO2. A 
dislocation dipole consists of two parallel disloca- 
tions of opposite signs that are separated from 
each other by a short distance. The dislocation 
dipole is formed during the motion of a jogged 
screw dislocation. Because the only way that a 
screw dislocation can slip and take the jog with it 
is by a non-conservative process, the movement 
of a jogged screw dislocation should be strongly 
temperature dependent. 
In order to find the high temperature deforma- 
tion mechanism of the W-Re-IThO2 alloys, side 
surfaces of deformation zones were examined 
using SEM secondary-electron imaging. Disloca- 
tion slip was found to be the main deformation 
mechanism in the W-Re-IThO 2 alloys that were 
deformed at temperatures below 2000 K. Figure 
12 shows the side surfaces of specimens 
deformed at 1800 K. Numerous slip bands, 
appearing near 45 ° to the tensile loading axis, 
were observed on the surfaces of specimen tested 
at this temperature. The number of slip bands 
increased with increasing temperature up to 
about 2000 K and then decreased with further 
increases in the test temperature. From the wavy 
slip lines that appeared on the surface of the post- 
test specimen, it was concluded that the cross-slip 
of screw dislocations was the dominant deforma- 
tion mechanism for the W-Re-1ThO2 alloys that 
were deformed at temperatures below 2000 K. 
Because the cross-slip planes of screw dislocation 
were less defined, the screw dislocation would 
wander from one plane to another, producing the 
wavy slip lines that were observed on the speci- 
men surface. 
Grain boundary sliding, however, was found to 
be the dominant deformation mechanism in the 
W-Re-1ThO2 alloys when they were deformed at 
temperatures above 2000 K. Figure 13 shows the 
side surfaces of specimens that were deformed at 
2400 K. The slip bands can hardly be seen. 
Instead, deformation was concentrated at the 
grain boundaries where significant grain bound- 
ary sliding occurred. The grain boundary slid- 
ing became more and more predominate with 
increasing rhenium concentration and test 
temperature, resulting in the final rupture of the 
specimens. It appeared that the rhenium added to 
the thoriated tungsten promoted the grain bound- 
ary sliding; possibly by a mechanism whereby 
segregated rhenium atoms formed a rhenium-rich 
region with a low melting temperature locally at a 
grain boundary so reducing the intergranular 
strength. The segregation of rhenium at grain 
boundaries in Co-Re alloys was observed by 
Booth [21]. These segregated rhenium atoms 
could promote the propagation of microcracks 
along grain boundaries and hence lower the grain 
boundary strength. As shown in Fig. 10, the 
frcture mode of the W-Re-1ThO2 alloys changes 
from transgranular to intergranular with increas- 
ing rhenium concentration at a given temperture.

5. Conclusions 
The following conclusions were drawn from 
this study of the high temperature tensile proper- 
ties of W-Re- 1ThO 2 alloys. 
(1) Rhenium had a moderate strengthening 
effect on thoriated tungsten up to about 2000 K. 
(2) Because of the rhenium-introduced struc- 
tural instability, rhenium concentrations above 
11 wt.% in thoriated tungsten would result in a 
strength degradation in the temperature range 
2000-2600 K. 
(3) The addition of rhenium increased the 
temperature sensitivity of the thoriated tungsten. 
The activation energy of plastic deformation 
of the W-Re-IThO 2 alloys was approximately 
linearly proportional to the rhenium concentra- 
tion. 
(4) The addition of rhenium made the 
thoriated tungsten more sensitive to the strain 
rate. At 2000 K, the W-3.6Re-IThO 2 alloy had a
