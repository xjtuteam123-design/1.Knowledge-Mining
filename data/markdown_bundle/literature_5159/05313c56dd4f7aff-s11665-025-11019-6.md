ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Fabrication of High-Strength
and High-Thermal-Conductivity Mg-Zn-Gd-Y Alloy Rolled
Sheets via Synergistic Modulation of Dislocation
and Recrystallization
Xin Li, Jiancheng Zhao, Zeyu Zhao, Hailong Shi, Xuejian Li, Chao Xu, Xiaoshi Hu, and Xiaojun Wang
Submitted: 4 September 2024 / Revised: 13 November 2024 / Accepted: 13 December 2024 / Published online: 26 March 2025
Magnesium alloy sheets with high strength and high thermal conductivity hold signiﬁcant promise for
aerospace, automotive, and electronics applications. However, the challenge of balancing mechanical
properties with thermal conductivity, along with poor rolling formability, has hindered their widespread
practical use. In this study, we developed a Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 alloy that achieves high strength and high
thermal conductivity by optimizing pass thickness reduction during conventional hot rolling. The as-rolled
alloy exhibited a tensile yield strength of 295 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 325 MPa, an elongation of
11.2%, and a thermal conductivity of 120.5 W/(m ÆK). Through the combined effects of high-density dis-
locations and W phase particles, low-temperature recrystallization of the as-rolled alloy occurred at 200 /C176C,
further enhancing thermal conductivity while maintaining a balance between strength and plasticity.
Subsequent heat treatment at 200 /C176C for 1 hour resulted in a tensile yield strength of 275 MPa, an ultimate
tensile strength of 316 MPa, an elongation of 16.7%, and a thermal conductivity of 128.6 W/(m ÆK). By
incorporating mechanical strengthening mechanisms into magnesium alloys with high-thermal-conductivity
potential and ﬁne-tuning these mechanisms, we achieved a harmonious combination of strength, plasticity,
and thermal conductivity. This study provides a value reference for the composition design and process
optimization of high-strength and high-thermal-conductivity magnesium alloy rolled sheets.
Keywords mechanical properties, Mg-Zn-Gd-Y alloy,
recrystallization, rolling, thermal conductivity
1. Introduction
As the lightweight ﬁeld develops toward multi-function-
ality and high efﬁciency, there is a growing demand for
highly integrated electronic components and high-efﬁciency
heat exchanger devices. It is crucial to efﬁciently dissipate
the heat generated by these devices during operation to
maintain equipment stability (Ref 1-3). As a commonly used
frame structure and bearing part of such devices, the sheet
has requirements for its light weight, high strength, and high
thermal conductivity. Magnesium, being the lightest metal
structural material, exhibits good thermal conductivity, thus
demonstrating excellent potential for development (Ref 4-6).
The poor mechanical properties of pure magnesium make it
difﬁcult to meet the demands of practical applications, and
alloying and thermal deformation are common methods to
improve the mechanical properties. Unfortunately, there is a
contradiction between the strength and thermal conductivity
of magnesium alloys. The main reason is that many
mechanical strengthening mechanisms, such as solid solution
strengthening, dislocation strengthening, ﬁne grain strength-
ening, and second phase strengthening, produce lattice
distortions that disrupt the periodic arrangement of atoms in
pure magnesium, increase the probability of electron scatter-
ing, and ultimately lead to a decrease in thermal conductivity
(Ref 7-10). When alloying elements exist in the form of solid
solution atoms, severe lattice distortion occurs, leading to a
much greater reduction in thermal conductivity than that in
the form of second phase (Ref 11). In order to ensure that
magnesium alloys exhibit good thermal conductivity, the
content of solid solution atoms in the matrix should be
minimized and alloying elements close to the size of Mg
atoms with the same valence should be selected (Ref 12).
Many studies have demonstrated that among the commonly
used alloying elements, the addition of Zn has the slightest
reduction in the thermal conductivity of pure magnesium (Ref
7, 13). This addition also contributes to precipitation strength-
ening, making it a promising primary alloying element for
high-strength and high-thermal-conductivity magnesium alloys.

Rolling is a widely used method to enhance the mechanical
properties of magnesium alloy sheets. Nevertheless, the
limited plasticity of magnesium and the intricate stress
conditions during rolling make magnesium alloy susceptible
to edge cracking and crushing (Ref 19, 20). These issues
signiﬁcantly decrease material yield and can potentially
diminish mechanical properties (Ref 21-23). Previous studies
on magnesium alloys with high strength and high thermal
conductivity were often prepared by extrusion. Magnesium
alloys with low alloy element content were extruded at lower
temperatures with large extrusion ratios (Ref 24-28). How-
ever, the application of this idea in the rolling process will be
seriously limited by the formability of the sheet. High rolling
temperatures and small thickness reductions per pass can
ensure the formability of the magnesium alloy sheet, but they
cannot effectively reﬁne the grains or provide enough work
hardening. This ultimately resu lts in insufﬁcient strength and
greatly reduces production efﬁciency (Ref 29). Extrusion
before rolling can reﬁne the microstructure, enhanced the
formability of the sheet during rolling, and lead to improved
mechanical properties (Ref 30). However, this method limits
the size of the sheet. Therefore, the conventional multi-pass
hot rolling method still has an irreplaceable role in the
preparation of wide and thick plates. The optimization of the
rolling process is crucial to achieving high-performance
magnesium alloy rolled sheets.
Due to the contradiction between strength and thermal
conductivity and the poor rolling formability of magnesium
alloy sheets, the preparation of high-strength and high-thermal-
conductivity magnesium alloy rolled sheets, especially wide
and thick sheets, is facing great challenges. The preparation of
magnesium alloys with high strength and high thermal
conductivity, especially through the conventional rolling pro-
cess, is lacking in the ﬁeld of magnesium alloys (Ref 4, 5, 31).
In this study, a Mg
97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 (at.%) alloy primarily
composed of W phase was designed. The microstructure and
properties of the material were regulated by rolling process
optimization and heat treatment. The rolling process is carried
out by the conventional multi-pass hot rolling method on a
standard rolling mill without heating the mill rolls, so as to
ensure the universal applicability of the rolling process. The
results of this study are expected to provide a reference for the
preparation of high-strength and high-thermal-conductivity
magnesium alloy rolled sheets in terms of composition design
and process optimization.
2. Experimental Procedure
2.1 Material Preparation
The as-cast Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 alloy was prepared by anti-
gravity casting in CO 2/SF6 protective atmosphere. Pure Mg
(99.9%), pure Zn (99.9%), Mg-30Gd (wt.%), and Mg-30Y
(wt.%) were added sequentially to the crucible in a resistance
furnace at 760 /C176C. After the melting, stirring, and standing, the
melt was poured into a steel mold (preheated to 300 /C176C). The
starting rolled samples with the dimension of
45 9 30 9 8 mm were sliced from the ingot by wire-electrode
cutting. The samples were homogenized at 500 /C176C for 12 h,
followed by water quenching.
Due to the poor rolling formability of magnesium alloys,
edge cracks were easy to occur. In this study, a rolling method
with universal applicability was designed. The conventional
rolling method was used, and mill rolls were not heated. The
mill roll diameter was 100 mm, and a constant rolling speed of
20 m/min was adopted during the rolling process. The samples
were heated to 450 /C176C in a resistance furnace for multi-pass
rolling. The hot-rolled sheets after each pass were reheated to
450 /C176C and held for 10 min to ensure the rolling temperature
consistency, and the sheets cooled in the water after the ﬁnal
rolling pass. Before rolling, the sheets were preheated at 450 /C176C
for 30 min. In the pre-rolling stage, the low-strain rolling was
introduced with thickness reductions of approximately 10%.
The sheets were rolled from 8 to 4 mm in thickness with the
total reduction of 50% by six passes. Then, the medium-strain
rolling was applied in the second stage. The sheets were rolled
to 2 mm after one pass 20% and 37.5% thickness reductions.
Finally, the 2-mm-thick sheets were rolled with a large ﬁnal
rolling reduction of 50% which resulted in a total reduction of
87.5% with sheets thickness of 1 mm. The thickness variation
of sheets during rolling is shown in Table 1. The as-rolled
sheets exhibit excellent rolling formability with almost no edge
cracks can be observed as shown in Fig. 1. Heat treatment was
carried out with as-rolled samples at 200 /C176C, followed by water
quenching.

3.1 Microstructural Characterization of As-cast
and As-Homogenized Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 Samples
The XRD pattern of the as-cast and as-homogenized
Mg97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 samples is shown in Fig. 3. Both samples
are mainly comprised of a-Mg and W phase (Mg 3Zn3RE2).
Figure 4 presents the morphology of the second phase in the as-
cast and as-homogenized samples. A large number of network
eutectic phases can be observed in the as-cast sample, while
having all transformed into spherical shapes after homogeniza-
tion treatment. The energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and
element distribution of as-cast and as-homogenized samples are
shown in Figs. S1 and S2. Zn, Gd, and Y elements are enriched
in the second phases, suggesting the existence of Mg-Zn-RE
phase. The results of EDS point scanning show that the ratio of
Zn/(Gd + Y) is close to the ideal ratio of W phase in points A

the samples is W phase. The network W phase at the grain

mechanical properties. Nevertheless, by adjusting the size and
distribution of the W phase, it can also serve as a strengthening
phase (Ref 32, 33). The spheroidization of the W phase is a
spontaneous process that occurs during homogenization treat-
ment. Due to the diffusion of Zn and RE atoms, the network W
phase has undergone two processes of fracture and spheroidiza-
tion, and ﬁnally to the coarsening stage (Ref 34, 35). The
process can be described by the Thomson–Freundlich equation:

3.2 Microstructural Characterization of As-rolled
and As-rolled + heat-treated Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 Samples
Magnesium alloys often demonstrate limited formability
during rolling and are susceptible to edge cracks and crack
propagation. The previous work of this study investigated the
effects of various rolling methods on the formability and
mechanical properties of magnesium alloy samples, with results
detailed in the Supplementary Information section. This earlier
research indicates that achieving a constant thickness reduction
per pass presents challenges in balancing formability with the
mechanical properties of the sheets on a standard rolling mill,
as illustrated in Figs. S4 and S5. Building on these ﬁndings, this
study regulated the deformation for each pass during the rolling
process.
In this study, the thickness reduction per pass was gradually
increased in three stages. From the ﬁrst pass to the sixth pass, a
thickness reduction of 10% was employed to maintain the
formability of the sheets in the early stages, prevent edge
cracks, and slightly enhance the strength of the samples. Given
that no edge cracks developed in the sheets, the seventh and
eighth passes involved an increased thickness reduction. No
crack propagation occurred, which facilitated further strength
enhancement. The plastic deformation capability of the mag-
nesium alloy at elevated temperatures was improved. The heat
retention between rolling passes allowed the sheets to return to
a temperature conducive to deformation, while also partially
alleviating the distortion stored within the samples, thereby
enhancing formability. However, heat retention can also
diminish the strength of the sample, making the ﬁnal rolling
reduction critical for the strength of as-rolled sheets. Since the
sheets exhibited no signiﬁcant edge cracks by the end of the
eighth pass, a substantial ﬁnal rolling reduction of 50% was
implemented to improve the strength. Figure S6 illustrates the
changes in hardness at different stages. By incrementally
increasing the thickness reduction across three stages, the
hardness of the samples was signiﬁcantly enhanced, indicating
a continued increase in strength. This study successfully
optimized the mechanical properties of the samples while
preserving their formability.
Following plastic deformation, magnesium samples are
frequently subjected to heat treatment to enhance mechanical
properties. Annealing and aging are common methods used to
improve plasticity and strength in magnesium samples, respec-
tively. Both treatments are beneﬁcial for enhancing thermal
conductivity by decreasing internal stresses and facilitating the
precipitation of the second phase, respectively. Based on the
aging temperature commonly used for many Mg-Zn-RE
samples, 200 /C176C was selected as the heat treatment temperature
for this study (Ref 37, 38).
The EBSD results were utilized to obtain information on the
orientation, recrystallization, and dislocations of the samples. In
this study, the W phase could not be resolved and the raw
EBSD data were only qualitatively analyzed as the as-rolled
samples underwent a large plastic deformation resulting in a

deformed grains and recrystallized grains in wrought samples.
The GOS value of recrystallized grains is usually less than 1-2 /C176,
while the grains with GOS value greater than 5 /C176are considered
to be deformed grains (Ref 41, 42). In this study, grains with
GOS values less than 2 /C176were individually selected and
combined with the distribution of large-sized W phases to
study the recrystallization region, as shown in Fig. 6(a), (d), and
(g). It should be noted that Fig. 6(a), (d), and (g) is obtained by
superimposing the original SEM-SE images of large-sized W
phases and EBSD results of magnesium matrix, and ensures the
accurate distribution of the second phase and recrystallized
grains. The results indicate that the percentage of recrystallized
grains in the as-rolled sample is 12.5%. After heat treatment for
1 h, the percentage increases to 16.7%, and further increases to
36.4% after 2 h of heat treatment. The formation of recrystal-
lized grains in the as-rolled sample is attributed to the dynamic
recrystallization (DRX). The lower deformation temperature
and larger strain rate are often not conducive to the occurrence
of DRX. The less proportion of recrystallized grains in as-rolled
samples is mainly related to the lower temperature and faster
rolling speed after the sample contacts the mill rolls. Followed
by static recrystallization (SRX) during the heat treatment at
200 /C176C, a gradual increase in the proportion of recrystallized
grains occurs. The recrystallized grains in the as-rolled sample
have more distribution near the large-sized W phases, showing
a random orientation, and the average grain size is 1.57 lm.
After 1 h heat treatment, the recrystallized grains show a more

and the maximum texture intensity is 4.12, showing a slight
increase compared to the heat treatment for 1 h.

4.1 Effect of Dislocation and W Phase on Recrystallization
In Mg-Zn-RE alloys, the conventional aging temperature of
200 /C176C typically does not induce recrystallization. Surprisingly,
this study observes substantial recrystallization in the as-rolled
sample following heat treatment at 200 /C176C, as depicted in Fig. 5
and 6. The large total thickness reduction and ﬁnal rolling
reduction led to a signiﬁcant accumulation of dislocations in the
as-rolled sample as shown in Fig. 10. Meanwhile, the large
grain size in as-rolled sample is able to store more dislocations,
providing enough stored energy that can drive the nucleation of
recrystallization during the subsequent annealing process (Ref
50). The high dislocation density is rearranged to form sub-

In addition, a large amount of energy is stored around the

After heat treatment at 200 /C176C for 1 h, there is an increase in the

During the rolling process, energy tends to accumulate around
the large-sized W phases due to deformation incoherence. If
these particles are abundant and do not agglomerate together,
this high-energy region can spread throughout the entire
sample, increasing its overall stored energy. Consequently, in
some cases, if the dislocation density is not high enough or the
number of second phases is insufﬁcient, the overall state of the
sample may not surpass the recrystallization threshold at low
temperatures. When the heat treatment time reaches 2 h, there
is a signiﬁcant increase in both the number and size of
recrystallized grains. The distribution of recrystallized grains is
no longer limited to the vicinity of the W phases, but expands
throughout the area, indicating that at this point SRX is
approaching completion and most grains have entered the stage
of grain growth. As shown in Fig. 5, 10, and 11, a signiﬁcant
reduction in dislocation density is observed when the heat
treatment time reaches 1 and 2 h, respectively. The hardness of
the sample decreases with prolonged heat treatment, while the
rate of decrease diminishes, suggesting a decrease in the
dislocations stored within the sample. Consequently, the SRX

Annealing is frequently employed following various
advanced preparation processes to regulate the microstructure
and properties of metal materials (Ref 55, 56). This process
reduces residual stress and alters grain boundary migration,
facilitating the regulation of dislocation and recrystallization.
Additionally, it may modify the type and proportion of texture,
ultimately achieving the desired microstructure and properties.
The methodology of this study is grounded in this perspective.
Large deformation rolling not only enhances the strength of the
samples but also creates opportunities for further regulating
recrystallization during subsequent annealing. Recrystallization
results in grains with more random orientations, thereby
diminishing the intensity of the basal texture present in the
initial as-rolled samples. The primary objective of this study is
to reconcile the conﬂicting relationship between strength,
plasticity, and thermal conductivity in wrought magnesium
alloys. All microstructural regulation efforts are directed toward
this goal. Through the regulation of recrystallization, the
plasticity and thermal conductivity can be signiﬁcantly
improved at the expense of a small amount of strength.
4.2 Strengthening and toughening mechanisms
of Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 samples
The microstructure of as-rolled Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 sample
consists of a major portion of deformed grains and a small
number of recrystallized grains. The deformed grains have
high-density dislocations and play a major role in strengthening
the sample. The ﬁne recrystallized grains mainly enhance the
plasticity of the sample by coordinating the deformation (Ref
57). Ultimately, as-rolled sample obtains a high TYS of
295 MPa while maintaining an EL of 11.2%. Subsequent heat
treatment at 200 /C176C consumes dislocations through SRX and
forms more ﬁne recrystallized grains. The strength of as-
rolled + heat-treated samples is lower than that of as-rolled
sample, but higher EL is obtained. When the heat treatment is
1 h, small recrystallized grain sizes are observed with a more
random orientation. The microstructure still maintains a
heterogeneous structure composed of large-sized deformed
grains and small-sized recrystallized grains. The distribution
pattern shows that deformed grains are predominant, with ﬁne
recrystallized grains forming a continuous chain near the
deformed grains. (0001) < 11
20 > base slip is the easiest
deformation mode of magnesium alloy at room temperature.
Figure 14 shows the (0001) < 11 20 > basal slip diagram of
as-rolled and as-rolled + heat-treated alloys. The small-sized
recrystallized grains have a higher Schmidt factor and are easier
to basal slip during deformation. After heat treatment for 1 h,
the continuous small-sized recrystallized grains will be able to
coordinate the large-sized deformed grains around which it is
difﬁcult to perform basal slip, as shown in Fig. 14 (b). This
structure maximizes the strengthening effect of deformed grains
and the coordinated deformation ability of recrystallized grains,
leading to a balance of strength and plasticity. Compared to the
as-rolled sample, a great increase in plasticity is obtained while
sacriﬁcing a small amount of strength. Extending the heat
treatment time to 2 h leads to a notable increase in the degree of
recrystallization. This results in a decrease in the density of
dislocations, accompanied by an increase in the size and
maximum texture intensity of recrystallized grains. The ability
of the samples to resist crack initiation is enhanced, but the
ability to inhibit crack propagation is weakened, which
ultimately leads to a decrease in fracture elongation. Continue
to extend the heat treatment time, the hardness gradually
decreases. It is foreseeable that the strength of the sample will
decrease with the increase in heat treatment time. The sample
after heat treatment for more than 2 h no longer meets the
design requirements of high-strength magnesium alloy samples.

4.3 Effect of Dislocation and W Phase on Thermal
Conductivity of As-rolled Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 Samples
The inﬂuence of alloying element content on the properties
of magnesium alloys is never monotonous, and this change has
been conﬁrmed in mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties
(Ref 60, 61). In this study, in order to meet the performance
requirements of high thermal conductivity, the type, content,
and proportion of elements, as well as the primary types of
second phases were considered. The composition of the W
phase (Mg
3Zn3RE2) ensures that it is able to absorb a large
number of sampling elements, especially the RE elements, thus
reducing the amount of solid solution atoms in the matrix. The
microstructure also reveals the presence of ﬁne precipitates,
which further reduces the content of sampling elements in the
matrix. The results in (Ref 62) and the control group (as shown
in Fig. S8 and Table S2) further indicate that the composition of
Mg
97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 has great potential as a high-thermal-con-
ductivity magnesium alloy. Under this composition ratio, the
co-addition of Zn and RE elements promotes the formation of
W phase. Finally, Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 sample maintains good
thermal conductivity. The elemental content in the matrix of as-
rolled and as-rolled + heat-treated samples was analyzed by
EPMA-EDS. Ten points in the matrix of each sample were
randomly selected to calculate the average content of Zn, Gd,
and Y , the results are shown in Table 4. The distribution and
content of the elements before and after heat treatment do not
change signiﬁcantly, with the total amount of RE elements in
the matrix being less than 0.5 at.% and the amount of Zn being
close to 1.0 at.%. Combined with the results of KAM and TEM,
it is shown that the increase in thermal conductivity of the
sample after heat treatment is due to the reduction of dislocation
density and lattice distortion during the SRX, rather than the
generation of new precipitated phases. Figure 16 shows the
schematic diagram of electron heat conduction, where electron
scattering due to solid solution atoms and local deformation can
reduce the thermal conductivity of magnesium. Among them,
solid solution atoms can cause serious lattice distortion, which
has the most serious inﬂuence on the thermal conductivity of
magnesium alloys. Reducing solid solution atoms and local
deformation is an effective way to improve the thermal
conductivity of magnesium alloys.

5. Conclusions
In this study, a rolling process with wide applicability was
developed, Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 alloy sheets achieved both high
strength and high thermal conductivity. The conclusions are
drawn as follows:
1) Excellent mechanical properties and sheet formability
were achieved by gradually increasing the thickness
reduction of per pass in three stages at 450 /C176C. The as-
rolled Mg 97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 sample exhibits a yield strength
of 295 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 325 MPa,
and an elongation of 11.2%.
2) The combined effect of the W phase and high-density
dislocations facilitated recrystallization of the as-rolled
Mg97Zn2Gd0.5Y0.5 sample during heat treatment at
200 /C176C. After 1 hour of heat treatment, the sample
achieved a better balance between strength and plasticity,
with a tensile yield strength of 275 MPa, an ultimate ten-
sile strength of 316 MPa, and an elongation of 16.7%.
3) The atomic ratio of Zn / (Gd + Y) = 2.0 (at.%) produces a
large number of W phases, which decreases the amount of
solid solution elements within the matrix and provides
excellent thermal conductivity. Heat treatment further im-
proves the thermal conductivity by reducing the dislocation
density. High thermal conductivities of 120.5 W/(mÆK) and
128.6 W/(m ÆK) are obtained for as-rolled and as-
rolled + heat-treated (200 C/1 h) samples, respectively.
