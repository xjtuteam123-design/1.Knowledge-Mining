REVIEW
Investment casting of turbine components –
practical implications of emerging concepts
and ideas for solidification of Ni based
superalloys
N. D’Souza*
This paper presents applications of materials characeterisation methods and computational
models in addressing the origins of defects encountered in investment casting of turbine
components using Ni based superalloys. Some typical case studies are illustrated. In single
crystal seeding, the role of thermal transients arising after mould soak and initial withdrawal has
been assessed and shown to account for the nucleation propensity for stray grains and their
subsequent competitive growth. In defects pertaining to latter stages of solidification; in the case
of hot tears the role of minor alloy chemistry in altering the local freezing kinetics was identified,
while the occurrence of non-equilibrium phases in high refractory containing newer generation
alloys was studied by understanding the solidification sequence. Applications in the foundry
arising from these studies in relation to operating process windows and physically based criteria
for assessing alloy chemistry from the view of castability have been briefly discussed.
Keywords: Ni alloys, Solidification sequence, Differential thermal analysis, Undercooling, Competitive growth, Freezing kinetics
Dedicated to the memory of Professor Malcolm McLean
Introduction
Superalloys are a class of materials speciﬁcally devel-
oped for high temperature applications. The enhanced
high temperature strength is principally derived from
the ordered c9 precipitates. Improved efﬁciency and
increased turbine entry temperatures (TET) have pro-
vided the impetus for constant developments in turbine
design and in the development of stronger and micro-
structurally stable alloys for prolonged periods at
increasingly elevated service temperatures.
Most turbine components are manufactured using the
state of the art investment casting (‘lost wax’) method.
The as cast microstructure therefore plays a crucial role
in the life and performance of the component.
Consequently, it is imperative to understand the origin
of defects arising during casting and thereby establish a
robust operation process window such that these defects
are minimised. The common defects that incur the
greatest loss in yield and therefore cost are secondary
grains, such as freckles in single crystal components,
porosity and hot tears in components having equiaxed
morphology and residual eutectic that requires an
expensive heat treatment operation post-cast to elim-
inate it.
In this article, we discuss some typical casting defects
and speciﬁcally describe how we employ both existing
as well as emerging concepts in solidiﬁcation science
in understanding their root cause of occurrence.
Importantly, we also suggest ‘ideas’ or possible applica-
tions of the results of our studies in overcoming these
defects. We present the following case studies:
(i) secondary grain nucleation and growth – impli-
cations to single crystal seeding
(ii) implications of alloy composition:
(a) relevance to hot tearing in equiaxed
morphologies
(b) formation of non-equilibrium interdend-
ritic constituent with relevance to heat
treatment.

Stray grain defects in single crystal seeding
Nucleation of stray grains
The origin and growth of stray grains beginning from
the seed melt back up to 30 mm ahead of the growth
front was done using optical and electron microscopy in
conjunction with EBSD. A profuse nucleation of
equiaxed stray grains around the perimeter of the seed
was observed within a distance of 2 mm from the melt

stray grains could be a transient process caused by the
thermal ﬂuctuations during melt back and initial with-
drawal, i.e. when the solidiﬁcation front curvature
changes from convex upwards (melt back) to concave
downwards (initial withdrawal) because of the nature of
heat ﬂow.

Growth of stray grains
The next consideration is the growth of these nucleated
grains. The survival of stray grains after nucleation is
determined by their growth, which is dominated by
competitive growth with the single crystal dendrites.
This is observed in Fig. 1 b, which shows that only stray
grains A, B and C have survived well beyond the melt
back interface. Their respective axial orientations are
given in the inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) in Fig. 1 c. Given
the off-axial single crystal dendrites, it is important to
represent the spatial location of these stray grains in
relation to the inclination of the single crystal dendrites.
This is described in Fig. 4 a and b. It is observed that the
surviving grains are restricted to the portion of the seed
cross-section, where the off-axial primary dendrites form
a diverging disposition with the surface.
Therefore, there are two distinct cases that deal with
competitive grain growth based on the orientation of the
single crystal dendrites:
(i) converging – during the thermal transient stage,
the existence of high undercooling provides a
favourable condition for nucleation. However
during subsequent growth, the evolving primary/
secondary dendrite arm network of the columnar
dendrites causes a reduced local undercooling in
the volume of liquid available for the growth of
the stray grain. The space available for the
growth of the stray grains also diminishes as the

Applications
Nucleation
Iterative process models using a commercial database,
ProCAST to calculate the local heat ﬂux and local front
curvatures have been run to model the thermal transient
in the foundry. The parameters considered were:
(i) range of initial withdrawal rates
(ii) modifications to the mould assembly in the
vicinity of the seed location at the chill plate.
Consequently a process window has been established,
within which the occurrence of stray grain nucleation at
the seed melt back has been signiﬁcantly suppressed.
Growth
Additionally, using the results derived from the axial
orientation dependence on competitive grain growth, a
patent application
8 has been submitted. This deals with
the positioning of the blade cavity in relation to the
single crystal seed surface, i.e. referring to Fig. 4 a, the
base of the spiral is not at the centre of the seed cross-
section. The emphasis here is that in the case of a turbine
blade having an off-axial ,001. orientation, we can
eliminate the need of a spiral helix that is otherwise used
as a precautionary measure. The location where the
blade cavity blends on to the seed surface is restricted to
the seed cross-section where the dendrites form a
converging disposition with the mould wall and the
exact co-ordinates are dependent on the extent of melt
back during soak and the seed perimeter.

Implications
Using this approach, it was possible therefore to identify
the role of minor alloying elements on the local freezing
kinetics that plays a prominent role in the hot tearing
propensity of an alloy. We have shown that the retarded
freezing rate of terminal interdendritic liquid in the
chemistry containing higher levels of Zr arises from
increased microsegregation and results in the precipita-
tion of a highly segregated [Zr–Nb] rich non-equilibrium
phase. This same chemistry variant also shows the
greatest propensity to hot tears compared with chem-
istry II. Therefore, using a DTA based approach we can
deﬁne a local freezing rate parameter, which can be used
to assess the propensity for tears in chemistry variants
within the master heat of the alloy.

Implications
Having identiﬁed the solidiﬁcation sequence that per-
tains to last stage solidiﬁcation, the next step is to
determine the extent of microsegregation across the
dendrite cross-section from the dendrite core to the
periphery of the secondary dendrite lobe, i.e. when
the peritectic reaction initiates. Additionally, the weight
fraction of interdendritic constituent has to be calcu-
lated. These aspects form the subject of an ongoing
investigation. Also implications of this on the heat
treatment temperature are being studied.
Conclusions
1. Illustrative case studies have been presented, which
highlights the approach adopted in the analysis of
different problems encountered within the foundry
pertaining to directional and equiaxed solidiﬁcation of
Ni based superalloys.
2. In single crystal seeding, detailed experiments and
microstructure models highlight the role of thermal
transients and therefore undercooling in the nucleation
and growth of stray grains formed at/above the seed
melt back.
3. In the case of last stage solidiﬁcation, the role of
alloy chemistry has been elucidated:
(i) in the case of hot tearing studies, the local
freezing kinetics calculated from the measured
DTA data and microstructural characterisation is
shown to be an important parameter is assessing
the proneness to hot tears
(ii) in the case of newer generation alloys, using DTA
and microstructure examination it is possible to
identify the solidification sequence of the inter-
dendritic constituent, which form the non-
equilibrium phases.
Applications in the foundry arising from these studies
have been brieﬂy discussed.
