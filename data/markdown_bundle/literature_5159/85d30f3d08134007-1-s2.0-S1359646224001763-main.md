ABSTRACT 
This study demonstrates the crucial trade-off between strength and ductility in titanium alloys. High strength- 
ductility synergy is achieved through severe plastic deformation (SPD) at a relatively lower temperature, 
resulting in ultrafine grains and well-dispersed (Ti, Zr)
6
Si
3 
nano-precipitates. The samples SPD-processed at 800 
◦
C demonstrate excellent room-temperature properties with a tensile strength of 1558.9 MPa and 7.2 % ductility. 
The findings highlight the impact of SPD temperature and alloying elements on silicide precipitation, revealing 
two different precipitation mechanisms depending on the SPD processing temperature: single diffusion- 
controlled nucleation at a higher SPD temperature while a coupled diffusion and dislocation-assisted nucle -
ation at a lower SPD temperature. Lower-temperature SPD is critical to achieving superior strength-ductility 
synergy. 
Strength and ductility are crucial mechanical characteristics, often 
mutually exclusive in materials. While improving strength, ultra-fine 
grain (UFG) materials face challenges due to inadequate ductility [1] . 
Addressing the “ strength-ductility ” trade-off in UFG materials has been a 
topic of extensive research in the past years [ 2 , 3 ]. Several methods, 
including nanoscale precipitates, have been developed to improve the 
strength-ductility balance [4 – 6] . Notably, equal channel angular press -
ing (ECAP) applied to different Al alloys, combined with subsequent 
treatment, achieves high strength and ductility [7 – 9] . These outcomes 
emphasize the effectiveness of introducing nanoscale precipitates to 
enhance the ductility of UFG materials. The key to successful pre -
cipitates is ensuring their size is small and evenly distributed. 
Near- α titanium alloys based on Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Si are important 
structural materials in the aerospace, marine, and biomedical industries 
due to their lightweight, high strength, corrosion resistance, and high- 
temperature resistance [10 – 14] . Thermomechanical processing is an 
important method to regulate microstructure and improve mechanical 
properties of titanium alloys. In previous studies, high room tempera -
ture strength and ductility were typically achieved through thermo -
mechanical deformation in the upper part of the α + β phase field (30 – 50 
◦
C below the β -transus temperature) [15 – 19] . However, due to the high 
stacking faults of titanium alloys, conventional high-temperature 
deformation (such as hot extrusion and hot rolling) is difficult to 
introduce sufficient strain into the matrix to obtain UFG and fine pre -
cipitates [20] . It is worth noting that severe plastic deformation (SPD) 
can provide sufficient strain to obtain UFG materials with exceptional 
mechanical properties [21 – 23] . During the SPD process, the 
Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Si titanium alloy is usually accompanied by the precip -
itation of silicides. It is generally believed that silicides are usually 
precipitated at the interface of α and β phases, α grain boundaries, and 
dislocations [ 18 , 24 ]. During thermal exposure and long-term aging 
processes, these unevenly and discontinuously distributed silicides will 
grow and coarsen, leading to further deterioration of the ductility of 
titanium alloys [ 25 , 26 ]. However, the previous study has proven that 
deformation processing at lower temperatures could enhance nucleation 
inside grain interiors since high-density dislocations provide heteroge -
neous nucleation sites for precipitates, which is conducive to obtaining 
dispersed precipitates [27] . In addition, our previous research has 
shown that multiple forgings can promote the precipitation of fine sili -
cides at dislocations and within grains, which is beneficial for obtaining

Based on the above results, we elucidate the silicide precipitation 
mechanism. Dislocations and possibly other crystal defects act as het -
erogeneous nucleation sites, aiding the diffusion kinetics for precipita -
tion precipitate nucleation and subsequent growth [50 – 55] . Due to 
limited elemental diffusion at low temperatures, Zr and Si elements, 
crucial for silicides, primarily diffuse through dislocations [ 19 , 49 ]. 
Silicide precipitation is mainly influenced by Si. Smaller-sized Si atoms 
segregate to dislocations (the Cottrell atmospheres) [49] , nucleating 
silicides when Si element content at the dislocation reaches solid 
solubility. 
The above analysis indicates a simultaneous occurrence of DRX and 
silicide precipitation. Matrix DRX consumes the dislocations generated 
by severe plastic deformation, while nanoscale precipitation relies on 
the dislocations providing channels for Zr and Si elements [ 52 , 53 ]. At 
higher temperatures (900 
◦
C), silicides mainly distribute at α / β phase 
and α grain boundaries, inhibiting grain growth, but not significantly 
promoting DRX. Conversely, abundant silicides precipitate inside grains 
at lower forging temperatures (800 
◦
C), promoting matrix DRX by 
pinning dislocations and hindering silicide precipitation. Additionally, 
there is a competing relationship between the nucleation and growth of 
silicides. As more silicides nucleate, they consume Zr and Si elements, 
limiting silicide growth. Consequently, forging at lower temperatures 
leads to titanium alloys with UFG matrices and dispersed nano-silicides. 
Fig. 5 shows the dynamic precipitation of silicide and matrix DRX 
during SPD. At 900 
◦
C, silicides nucleate from the α / β phase boundaries 
through discontinuous nucleation, relying on Si and Zr diffusion along 
grain boundaries. Under this condition (high temperature), dislocations 
are quickly annihilated; Si and Zr enrichment in the residual β phase at 
grain boundaries leads to heterogeneous precipitation and larger sili -
cides [ 25 , 44 ]. At lower temperatures (800 
◦
C), reduced grain size and 
high dislocation density increase nucleation sites, enhancing Si and Zr 
diffusivity and leading to dispersed smaller silicides. 
During the forging process, silicides act as dislocations pinning 
agents, accumulating dislocations and forming numerous sub-grains 
within grains while inhibiting grain growth at boundaries. The dy -
namic precipitation of silicides aligns with matrix DRX, where DRX 
consumption of dislocations is not conducive to the precipitation of 
silicides. Nevertheless, the β -to- α transformation enhances the chemical 
driving force for silicide precipitation by reducing Si solubility [49] . 
Comparing the effects of SPD at different temperatures indicates that 
lower-temperature SPD is crucial for obtaining ultrafine grains and 
nanoscale silicides simultaneously, thereby leading to excellent 
strength-ductility synergy in titanium alloys. 
In summary, we achieved superior strength-ductility synergy in a 
near- α titanium alloy through SPD at relatively lower temperatures. 
Notably, at 800 
◦
C, the alloy exhibits a tensile strength of 1558.9 MPa 
and ductility of 7.2 %. Silicide precipitation mechanisms involve 
diffusion-controlled precipitation at grain boundaries and dislocation- 
assisted nucleation within grains, primarily influenced by forging tem -
perature. Diffusion-controlled precipitation dominates in high- 
temperature forging, while coupled diffusion-controlled and 
dislocation-assisted nucleation dominates in low-temperature forging. 
Lower-temperature SPD is crucial for obtaining ultrafine grains and 
dispersed silicides, simultaneously resulting in excellent strength and 
high ductility.
