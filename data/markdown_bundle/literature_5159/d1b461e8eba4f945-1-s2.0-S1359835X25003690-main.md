ABSTRACT
CNTs were first employed in Nb-Si based alloys fabricated by laser melting deposition (LMD) to enhance me -
chanical properties, particularly fracture toughness. The results showed that CNTs underwent complete 
decomposition in alloys fabricated at LP = 2100 W, and the breakdown of CNTs provides carbon atoms to the 
molten pool, facilitating the microstructure transformation from a hypoeutectic structure consisting of Nbss and 
Nb
3
Si to a near-eutectic structure consisting of Nbss and γ -Nb
5
Si
3
, accompanied by a refinement of the micro -
structure. The fracture toughness of 2100 W-3CNTs alloy reached 15.2 MPa ⋅ m
1/2
, 1.62 times higher than the 
alloy without CNTs addition. By optimizing process parameters, CNTs-reinforced Nb-Si based alloys were suc -
cessfully fabricated at LP = 1800 W, achieving simultaneous improvement in fracture toughness and compressive 
strength. The fracture toughness of 1800 W-3CNTs alloy increased to 16.7 MPa ⋅ m
1/2
, which was attributed to the 
content and size of ductile Nbss increased, as well as the debonding and pull-out of residual CNTs during the 
fracture process. These factors lead to crack deflection, bridging, and branching, thereby increasing the energy 
required for crack propagation. The Orowan strengthening caused by the array-like distribution of CNTs in Nbss 
significantly enhanced the compressive strength of the 1800 W-3CNTs alloy, making its compressive strength 
superior to other alloys, reaching the maximum value of 2190 MPa among all LMDed alloys.
1. Introduction
With the continuous advancement of high-performance aerospace 
vehicles, the operating temperature of hot-section components is 
constantly increasing [ 1 ]. The blade materials of the new generation of 
aero-engines are facing high-temperature service environments of 
1200 
◦
C~1400 
◦
C, exceeding the extreme service temperature of nickel- 
based superalloys (approximately 1150 
◦
C) [ 2 – 4 ]. Nb-Si based alloys 
show great potential to replace nickel-based superalloys due to their 
high melting point (~1800 
◦
C) and excellent mechanical strength at 
elevated temperatures [ 5 ]. However, low fracture toughness of Nb-Si 
based alloys at room temperature is an essential obstacle to their 
application [ 6 – 8 ]. Alloying is an effective strategy to improve the frac -
ture toughness of Nb-Si based alloys [ 9 – 15 ]. Nevertheless, the 
commonly used alloying elements for Nb-Si based alloys are typically 
low melting point elements, leading to a significant reduction in high- 
temperature mechanical properties [ 16 ]. Moreover, the introduction 
of high-density elements such as Ta (16.6 g/cm
3
) and Hf (13.31 g/cm
3
) 
increases the density of the alloy, which in turn leads to increased 
centrifugal stress on the rotor during service, ultimately reducing the 
thrust-to-weight ratio of the engine [ 17 , 18 ].
Wang and Zhao et al. introduced a small amount of carbon into the 
Nb-16Si-24Ti alloy during the arc melting process and observed that it 
inhibited the formation of the Ti
5
Si
3 
phase while promoting the gener -
ation of the Nb
5
Si
3 
phase. The microstructure of the alloy changed from 
a hypoeutectic structure (Nbss + (Nb, Ti)
3
Si) to a near-eutectic structure 
(Nbss + γ -(Nb, Ti)
5
Si
3
), accompanied by a significant improvement in 
fracture toughness [ 16 , 19 ]. Furthermore, Wang et al. found carbon 
could effectively enhance the high-temperature compressive strength of 
the Nb-24Ti-16Si-2Al-3V-xFe-yC alloy (x = 4, 6; y = 1, 3, 5) in high- 
temperature compression experiments at 1300 
◦
C [ 6 ]. Therefore, car -
bon (an element in the same group as silicon) was reported to improve 
the mechanical properties of Nb-Si based alloys at both room and high 
temperatures, thereby offsetting the decline of high-temperature

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and graphene are one-dimensional and 
two-dimensional nanomaterials composed of carbon atoms with high 
tensile strength, high Young ’ s modulus, and low density, usually used to 
reinforce and even toughen composite materials [ 22 – 24 ]. Xiong et al. 
successfully fabricated graphene-reinforced Nb
5
Si
3
/Nb composites with 
excellent room-temperature fracture toughness and compressive 
strength through Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS). The high fracture 
toughness of the composite was attributed to the pull-out of graphene 
during the fracture process, as well as the crack deflection and bridging 
caused by graphene [ 25 , 26 ]. Furthermore, Long et al. investigated the 
toughening mechanism of CNTs in Nb-Si based alloys through the finite 
element analysis, and the results indicated that both the length and 
interfacial bonding strength of CNTs affected the pull-out force [ 27 , 28 ]. 
Additionally, adding 3 wt% CNTs in Nb-20Si composites through SPS 
increased the fracture toughness from 2.52 MPa ⋅ m
1/2 
to 4.21 MPa ⋅ m
1/2 
[ 29 ]. However, common fabrication methods such as arc melting and 
directional solidification, which involve long periods of high- 
temperature melting, make it difficult to maintain CNTs in Nb-Si 
based alloys. Although CNTs-reinforced Nb-Si based composites have 
been successfully fabricated by SPS technology, their fracture toughness 
is unsatisfactory, and this technology has certain limitations in prepar -
ing complex structural samples. Therefore, there is an urgent need to 
explore a new method to effectively fabricate CNTs-reinforced Nb-Si 
based alloys.
Laser melting deposition (LMD) technology, with its high energy 
density, rapid cooling rates, and small molten pool characteristics, 
provides the prerequisites for the retention of CNTs. Despite this, there 
are currently no literature reports on the preparation of CNTs-reinforced 
Nb-Si based alloys using this technology. Current research on CNTs- 
reinforced Nb-Si based alloys mainly focuses on theoretical calcula -
tions and simulations, with relatively few experimental fabrication and 
characterization studies. Therefore, this study attempts to fabricate 
CNTs-reinforced Nb-16Si-20Ti alloy using LMD technology and to 
explore the effects of CNTs addition and distribution on the micro -
structure and properties of Nb-16Si-20Ti alloy.
2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Samples preparation
2.1.1. Raw materials pretreatment
The raw materials used in this study include elemental powders of 
Nb (75~100 μ m), Si (75~100 μ m), Ti (75~100 μ m), and nickel-coated 
carbon nanotubes (CNTs) with a diameter of 30~50 nm and a length of 
10~20 μ m (Some of the original CNTs powder had diameters exceeding 
50 nm, but the overall average diameter was within the range of 30~50 
nm.). All these powders with purities of over 99.5 wt% and the 
morphological characteristics are shown in Fig. 1 (a) – (d). Before mixing 
the powders, the CNTs powder was calcinated in air at 500 
◦
C for 4 h to 
remove amorphous carbon and carbon nanoparticles. Subsequently, the 
CNTs were non-covalent modified via Cetyltrimethylammonium Bro -
mide (CTAB) and ultrasonicated to prevent agglomeration. After 
completing these steps, the CNTs solution was mixed with Nb, Si, and Ti 
powders, using ethanol as the solvent, and ball-milled at 100 rpm for 8 h 
(ball-to-powder ratio = 3:1). Finally, the mixture was dried in a vacuum 
oven at 100 
◦
C for 3 h to prepare the powder mixture for this experi -
ment. According to the amounts of CNTs addition, the powder mixtures 
with designed compositions are named as 0CNTs, 1CNTs, 2CNTs, 
3CNTs, and 4CNTs, respectively. The morphology of 0CNTs and 3CNTs 
powder mixtures are shown in Fig. 2 a.
2.1.2. Laser melting deposition
Bulk samples with the nominal composition of Nb-16Si-20Ti-xCNTs 
(x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 at.%) were fabricated using LMD equipment (LMD8060, 
provided by Shenyang Zhongke Yuchen Technology Co., Ltd., with a 
laser spot size of 4.0 mm) in an Argon atmosphere, and the specific 
process parameters for LMD are detailed in Fig. 2 b. The pure Ti plate 
with a dimension of 100 mm × 100 mm × 15 mm served as the sub -
strate, and the macroscopic morphology of the fabricated alloy sample is 
shown in Fig. S1. The fabrication alloys, with designed compositions and 
laser power, were named 2100 W-0CNTs, 2100 W-1CNTs, 2100 W- 
2CNTs, 2100 W-3CNTs, 2100 W-4CNTs, 1800 W-3CNTs, and 1800 W- 
4CNTs respectively, corresponding to their respective quantities of CNTs 
additions.

2.1.3. COMSOL Multiphysics finite element simulation
This study employed a method combining experimental design with 
COMSOL Multiphysics finite element simulation to ensure the retention 
of CNTs (conducted based on previous work [ 21 ]). The key to the 
experiment lies in controlling the cyclic temperature, especially the peak 
temperature, by reducing the Laser Power (LP) during sample fabrica -
tion. First, the temperature change at a certain point on the substrate 
was measured using a K-type thermocouple and a paperless recorder 
(HIOKI-LR8431, with a recording interval of 10 ms). Subsequently, the 
finite element model was calibrated against experimental measurements 
to enhance its precision.
Two distinct process strategies were devised, taking into account the 
melting points of CNTs (3550 
◦
C) and Nb (2477 
◦
C), and the thermal 
cycle outcomes of the LMD process at different LP were depicted in 
Fig. 2 c. Scheme 1: Bulk samples with compositions ranging from 0CNTs 
to 4CNTs were fabricated at LP = 2100 W. The highest peak temperature 
(2872.3 
◦
C) achieved in this scheme was approximately centred between 
the melting point of Nb and CNTs. Scheme 2: Bulk samples with com -
positions from 3CNTs and 4CNTs alloys were fabricated at LP = 1800 W. 
The highest peak temperature of this method (2572.5 
◦
C) was slightly 
above the melting point of Nb and far from the melting point of CNTs. 
Consequently, the method described above fine-tuned the peak tem -
perature of the LMD process to attain precise temperature control within 
the melting point range of Nb and CNTs by decreasing the LP from 2100 
W to 1800 W.
2.2. Microstructure characterization
5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm samples were cut from each alloy by an 
electrode discharge machine (EDM) for microstructure observation and 
phase analysis. X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, Smartlab-9000x) was 
applied for the phase identification, operated with the assistance of Cu 
K
α 
radiation ( λ = 1.5406Å), under conditions of 40 kV and 200 mA at a 
scanning speed of 5
◦
/min. The microstructure morphology was 
observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Quanta250FEG) 
equipped with backscattered electron (SEM-BSE) mode and the chemi -
cal compositions of the constituent phases in various alloys were pre -
cisely determined using a field emission electron probe (EPMA, JXA- 
8530F). 5 mm × 5 mm × 2.5 mm samples were cut by EDM, and the 
cross-section of each sample was sandpaper polishing, mechanical pol -
ishing, and argon ion polishing for EBSD analysis. The distribution, 
volume fraction and size of each constituent phase were analyzed by 
EBSD data collected from a multifunctional field emission scanning 
electron microscope (ZEISS GeminiSEM 460). The TEM samples were 
mechanical ground to a thickness of less than 50 μ m, and then final 
thinning was performed using a dimple grinder and a precision ion 
thinning instrument (Gatan695). The TEM samples (LP = 2100 W and 
1800 W) were detailed characterization by field emission transmission 
electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100F), operating at 200 KV, with 
particular attention to the morphological and distribution characteris -
tics of CNTs in the prepared Nb-Si based alloys
