1 Introduction
CoCrNi alloy, as a prototypical FCC-structured medium-
entropy alloy (MEA), has emerged as a promising candidate 
for advanced structural applications owing to its exceptional 
damage tolerance and tunable mechanical properties 
[1–6]. Notably, Gludovatz et al. [5 ] first demonstrated its 
remarkable fracture toughness (> 200 MPa·m^1/2) through 
arc-melting synthesis. At low temperatures, the tensile 
strength of CoCrNi medium-entropy alloy increased to 
1.3 GPa, the fracture elongation improved to 90%, and the 
fracture toughness rose to 275 MPa·m 1/2. Yoshida et al. 
[7] treated CoCrNi at liquid nitrogen temperatures and 
measured a tensile strength of 1.3 GPa and an elongation 
of 80%. However, during the smelting process, issues 
such as segregation and uneven microstructure hinder its 
performance. In 2018, Deng et al. [8 ] prepared CoCrNi 
medium-entropy alloy using equal-channel angular pressing. 
Their research showed that grain refinement, dislocation 
generation, three-dimensional twinning structure, and L-C 
lock significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of 
CoCrNi medium-entropy alloy. Annealing after deformation 
further improved the material’s mechanical properties. After 
annealing at 500 °C, the yield strength reached 1.5 GPa, 
and the strain was 4.1%. By adjusting annealing twins, 
the yield strength of CoCrNi alloy reached 1 GPa with a 
plasticity of 45%. Additionally, CoCrNi medium-entropy 
alloy also exhibits good corrosion resistance. Research by

Shang et al. [9 ] indicated that CoCrNi medium-entropy 
alloy shows better corrosion resistance and passivation 
performance in HCl solution compared to other FCC 
high-entropy alloys. Factors affecting the performance of 
CoCrNi medium-entropy alloy have been partially studied 
both domestically and internationally. Moravcik et al. [10] 
found that a higher Cr content (~ 33 at.%) makes CoCrNi 
medium-entropy alloy more likely to form a highly stable 
and dense Cr2O3 oxide film on its surface, significantly 
improving corrosion resistance. Hachet et al. [11] cold-
rolled and recrystallized the Al0.32CoCrFeMnNi high-
entropy alloy and found that grain refinement increased 
its yield strength from 300 to 620 MPa, while elongation 
decreased from 56 to 20%. Zhang et al. [12] found that after 
45% cold rolling and annealing at 1000 °C for 2 h, the yield 
strength of Fe35Co21Ni6Cr18Mn20 high-entropy alloy 
increased from 516 to 725 MPa due to grain refinement 
and numerous annealing twins, with a slight increase in 
elongation. Furthermore, Parakh et al. [13] discovered that as 
the grain size increased, the proportion of grain boundaries 
decreased, leading to fewer corrosion initiation points and 
thus improved corrosion resistance of AlCoCrFeNi high-
entropy alloy. Grain size also significantly affects friction 
and wear properties. Zhu et al. [ 14] added TiC particles 
to FeCoNiCuAl high-entropy alloy, which inhibited 
grain growth, increased hardness, reduced the friction 
coefficient, and enhanced wear resistance. In terms of 
deformation mechanisms, research by Laplanche et al. [15] 
indicated that in the initial deformation stage of CoCrNi, 
1/2 <110> dislocations decompose into 1/6 <112> Shockley 
partial dislocations on (111) planes; as strain increases, 
nanoscale twinning appears as an additional deformation 
mechanism, hindering dislocation movement and leading 
to enhanced work hardening, thereby delaying necking and 
improving strength and ductility [5 , 15–21]. It is obvious 
that cold rolling and annealing are crucial processes to 
address these issues. Cold rolling can significantly increase 
the material’s hardness and strength, but it may also lead 
to a decrease in plasticity and an increase in internal stress. 
Annealing, on the other hand, can alleviate these problems 
by promoting recrystallization, reducing internal stress, and 
improving plasticity. Although extensive research has been 
conducted on the effects of cold rolling and annealing on 
the properties of medium-entropy alloys [8–14], systematic 
studies on the influence of annealing process parameters on 
CoCrNi are still insufficient.
Extensive research has been conducted on the preparation 
and performance enhancement of CoCrNi medium-entropy 
alloy, and good results have been achieved in understanding 
its excellent performance compared to traditional alloys and 
some other high-entropy alloys, as well as the principles and 
mechanisms behind these properties. However, research on 
the effect of annealing processes on medium-entropy alloy 
performance is still insufficient, and more experiments are 
needed to further explore the impact of annealing on the 
microstructure and properties of medium-entropy alloys to 
find the optimal annealing process for cold-rolled CoCrNi 
medium-entropy alloy.
2 Experiment
The medium-entropy alloy used in this study was prepared 
by vacuum melting high-purity (≥ 99.9 wt.%) Co, Cr, and 
Ni metals. The original billet had a diameter of 3 inches and 
a thickness of 4 mm. Samples were processed using a cold-
rolling mill and electrical discharge wire cutting machine. 
During the cold-rolling process, each pass reduces the thick-
ness by 0.2 mm. Figure 1a shows samples with different roll-
ing deformation amounts, and Figure  1b shows the dimen-
sions of tensile samples.
The samples were cold-rolled at room temperature, with 
each pass controlled to a reduction of no more than 5%, 
achieving final rolling deformation amounts of 0%, 25%, 
50%, and 75%. The samples were cut into rectangular prisms 
of size 10 mm × 5 mm using an electrical discharge wire

adhesive was mixed in a 2:1 ratio, poured into molds 
containing the samples, and allowed to cure before 
subsequent polishing and other treatments. To avoid the 
effects of heating on unannealed samples, room-temperature 
curing was chosen (with room-temperature curing for 
24 h). The diameter of the cold-mounted sample base was 
30 mm, and the height was approximately 17 mm. Before 
observation with a metallographic microscope, the samples 
were polished, ground, and etched. The etching solution was 
a mixture of 5 g CuSO4, 20 ml HCl, and 20 ml H2O, and the 
metallographic microscope used was the LEICA DMI5000 
M optical microscope. The TM3000 scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) was used for microstructure analysis 
and fracture surface characterization of tensile samples. 
Hardness measurements were taken using the XD-1000 
TMC micro-Vickers hardness tester with a load of 100 g and 
a dwell time of 10 s. Each sample was measured at 10 points, 
and the average value was used as the measurement result. 
Tensile experiments were performed using the CMT6203 
electronic universal testing machine according to GB/T 
228.1–2010 standards, with a strain rate of 0.5 mm/min. 
The gauge length of the tensile samples was 10 mm, and the 
width of the gauge area was 2 mm.
3 Results and Discussion
Figure 2 presents a comparative analysis of X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) patterns for the CoCrNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) 
subjected to varying degrees of cold-rolled deformation and 
subsequent annealing treatments. The patterns reveal four 
distinct diffraction peaks located at 2θ angles of 44.4°, 47.3°, 
51.5°, and 75.8°, respectively. These peaks were systemati-
cally cross-referenced with Powder Diffraction File (PDF) 
database entries to elucidate their crystallographic origins. 
It is evident from the figure that the three prominent dif-
fraction peaks at 2θ = 44.4°, 51.5°, and 75.8° exhibit excel-
lent agreement with the face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal 
structures. These peaks correspond to the (111), (200), and 
(220) crystallographic planes of the FCC phase, respec-
tively. The sharp and intense nature of these peaks sug-
gests a well-ordered FCC lattice, consistent with the high-
entropy alloy’s inherent preference for minimizing Gibbs 
free energy through maximal atomic packing efficiency [1]. 
This confirms that the alloy predominantly retains the FCC 
structure across all deformation and annealing conditions. 
However, a critical observation reveals that the diffraction 
peak at 2θ = 47.3° aligns with the hexagonal close-packed 
(HCP) structure of cobalt, specifically the ( 1011 ) crystallo-
graphic plane. The presence of this low-intensity HCP peak 
indicates a minor secondary phase, likely originating from 
residual non-equilibrium solidification processes during 
alloy synthesis or localized atomic rearrangements induced 
by deformation. This dual-phase behavior underscores the 
complexity of phase stability in HEAs under mechanical 
and thermal perturbations. Notably, with increasing cold-
rolled deformation, the XRD patterns remain qualitatively 
unchanged in terms of peak positions and phase compo-
sition. No additional diffraction peaks emerge, implying 
that cold rolling does not trigger phase transformations or 
precipitate formation. They remain in the FCC structure, 
and no new phases emerge. The intensity of the HCP peaks 
decreases with the increase in cold-rolling deformation, sug-
gesting that cold rolling aids in eliminating the HCP phase 
from the samples. When comparing the 2θ values of the 
diffraction peaks in the figure with the PDF card data for 
the FCC structure crystals of Ni and Co, it is observed that 
the experimentally measured 2θ values exhibit slight shifts. 
These angular deviations (Δ2θ ≈ ± 0.1–0.2°) reflect lattice 
parameter variations due to severe lattice distortion caused 
by atomic-scale chemical disorder and residual stresses from 
cold rolling. This is attributed to the lattice distortion effect 
in the medium-entropy alloy. Similarly, it can be observed 
that as the annealing temperature increases, no new diffrac-
tion peaks appear in the XRD patterns, indicating that the 
crystal structure of the samples remains unchanged after 
annealing, still maintaining the FCC structure, with no new 
phases emerging. The decrease in XRD diffraction peak 
intensities for specific crystallographic planes with increas-
ing annealing temperature suggests that recrystallization 
may reduce preferential grain orientations, potentially sup-
pressing texture formation.

The SEM and EDS spectra of samples annealed at RT, 
500 °C, 700 °C, and 900 °C without rolling are shown in 
Figures S1 to S4. It can be observed that the material exhib-
its severe elemental segregation. Specifically, the contrast in 
elemental distribution becomes progressively pronounced 
with increasing annealing temperature, indicating a ther -
mally activated diffusion process. The Ni element is enriched 
in the coarse grain regions, while the Cr and Co elements 
are enriched in the fine-grain regions. The degree of seg-
regation of the Cr element is slightly less than that of the 
Co element (which may correlate with the lower activation 
energy for Co diffusion in this alloy system or differences 
in their respective solid solution hardening effects). This 
segregation pattern suggests that during solid-state diffu-
sion, Ni atoms exhibit higher mobility in grain boundary 
networks compared to Cr and Co, possibly due to differ -
ences in crystal structure compatibility and solution energy 
within the host matrix [1 –3]. Figure 7 shows the SEM and 
EDS spectra of the CCN-900-75 sample. It can be seen that 
for the unrolled samples, annealing can partially alleviate 
element segregation. The observed homogenization can be 
attributed to enhanced bulk diffusion at elevated tempera -
tures, where atomic rearrangement across grain boundaries 
and through lattice defects facilitates the redistribution of 
solute elements toward thermodynamic equilibrium. How -
ever, even under combined high-temperature annealing 
(900 °C) and severe plastic deformation (75% reduction), 
significant elemental partitioning persists, as evidenced by 
the persistent Ni-rich and Cr/Co-depleted regions in Fig.  7. 
It can be inferred that under the combined effect of rolling 
and annealing treatment, the process that alleviates element 
segregation is still annealing, with rolling having little to no

Figure 8 illustrates the variation of microhardness with 
annealing temperature and cold-rolling deformation amount 
for the CoCrNi high-entropy alloy. The immense stress from 
cold rolling severely affects the alloy’s microstructure, elon-
gating, flattening, or even crushing the grains, causing lattice 
distortion and increased dislocations, resulting in increased 
hardness and work hardening. This includes grain elongation 
along the rolling direction, development of lamellar sub-
structures, and high-angle grain boundary fragmentation—
processes collectively termed "grain refinement by deforma-
tion. As demonstrated in Fig. 9, a clear positive correlation 
exists between cold-rolling deformation amount and micro-
hardness at equivalent annealing temperatures. This behav-
ior aligns with classical strain hardening principles, where 
increasing deformation imposes higher densities of immo-
bile dislocation tangles and stacking faults that contribute 
to flow stress elevation. The increase in microhardness from 
0 to 50% deformation is more significant compared to the 
increase from 50 to 75% deformation, indicating that after 
a deformation amount greater than 50%, the ability of cold 
rolling to improve microhardness by altering the material’s 
structure gradually weakens and may stabilize with further

It can be seen that for the same cold-rolling deformation 
amount, microhardness varies in three regions with 
annealing temperature. In the RT to 500  °C range, 
annealing softening occurs, and microhardness decreases

characterized by annealing-induced softening, where 
microhardness decreases with rising annealing temperature. 
The dominant mechanism here involves the annihilation of 
vacancies and the rearrangement of dislocations introduced 
during cold-rolling, leading to a reduction in lattice 
strain energy without significant structural changes. In 
the 500–900 °C range, annealing hardening occurs, with 
microhardness first increasing and then decreasing with 
increasing annealing temperature, and the highest point 
of the curve is affected by the cold-rolling deformation 
amount. A unique annealing–hardening phenomenon 
occurs, where microhardness initially increases to a peak 
before declining. This non-monotonic behavior arises from 
competing processes: (a) precipitation hardening due to 
the formation of fine secondary phases (e.g., carbides or 
intermetallics) and (b) recrystallization-induced softening 
as deformed grains are replaced by equiaxed, low-energy 
structures. In the 900–1100  °C range, microhardness 
rapidly decreases with increasing annealing temperature.

temperature, primarily attributed to excessive grain growth 
and potential overaging of precipitates. At these elevated 
temperatures, thermodynamic driving forces promote

following calculations were made: At 700 °C, the hardness

the 75% deformation sample is 423.8 HV, a 2.9% increase

4 Conclusions
This study explores the effects of cold-rolling deformation 
amount and annealing temperature on the microstructure and 
performance of CoCrNi high-entropy alloys, identifying the 
optimal processing parameters. The main conclusions are 
as follows:
(1) At the same annealing temperature, microhardness 
increases with the cold-rolling deformation amount. 
The increase in microhardness from 0 to 50% 
deformation is more significant compared to the 
increase from 50 to 75%. At the same deformation 
amount, microhardness decreases with increasing 
annealing temperature below 500 °C and increases 
then decreases above 500 °C. The highest hardness 
is observed in the 700–900 °C range, with the peak
