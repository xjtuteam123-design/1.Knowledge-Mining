abstract
Background: Given the lack of studies of acetabular defect reconstruction in primary total hip arthro-
plasty (THA) using tantalum augments, this study aims to evaluate clinical and radiographic results for
treatment with tantalum augments to reconstruct acetabular defects in primary THA.
Methods: We retrospectively reviewed 19 patients (19 hips) with acetabular defects who underwent
primary THA using tantalum augments, with a minimum follow-up of 2 years. Clinical, radiographic, and
surgical data were retrospectively evaluated.
Results: Mean follow-up was 5.1 years (range 2.5-7.6). Harris Hip Score improved from 35.8 (range 19-
56) preoperatively to 85.3 (63-98) at last follow-up ( P < .01). Oxford Hip Score, University of California
Los Angeles activity scale, and Short Form-12 score also improved signi ﬁcantly from presurgery to last
follow-up. Mean operation time and blood loss were 124.7 minutes and 530 mL, respectively. Mean hip
center position was 2.97 cm (range 2.35-3.58) horizontally and 2.06 cm (1.29-2.92) vertically, and mean
acetabular inclination was 38.9
/C14 (range 27 /C14 -47/C14 ) at last follow-up. These parameters were not signi ﬁ-
cantly different from those recorded immediately postoperatively ( P > .05). There was no aseptic loos-
ening, cup and augment migration, screw breakage, or presence of hip infection at last follow-up. All hips
were radiographically stable.
Conclusion: Porous tantalum augments combined with titanium shells lead to satisfactory clinical and
radiographic outcomes for the reconstruction of acetabular defect in primary THA at a mean 5.1 years of
follow-up. This approach confers anatomical cup placement, simple operation, and a high rate of stable
ﬁxation.

Acetabular bone defects are most commonly found in revision
total hip arthroplasty (THA) and are caused by aseptic loosening,
osteolysis, infection, and periprosthetic fracture [1,2]. However,
surgeons can also encounter the situation of acetabular bone defect
in primary THA. Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is one of
the common reasons for deformed acetabulum, such as supero-
lateral bony wall defects, in primary THA. Previous history of hip
tuberculosis or infection often has advanced arthritis with severe
deformities, which include bone defects, dislocation, and limb
length discrepancy [3,4]. THA has been often used for the treatment
of these secondary arthritides following infection [5,6]. Post-
traumatic osteoarthritis is a usual complication after open reduc-
tion and internal ﬁxation of acetabular fractures [7] and is often
accompanied by acetabular bone de ﬁciencies, which need THA to
recover the acetabular integrity [8]. In addition, extensive acetab-
ular bone defects can also exist in severe rheumatoid arthritis,
osteoarthritis, and avascular necrosis as well as rapidly destructive
coxarthrosis [9,10]. Therefore, acetabular de ﬁciencies encountered
in primary THA seemingly involve more multiform pathogenesis
and manifestation than those in revision surgery.

acetabular defect in primary THA are lacking. The majority focus on

Tantalum metal augments possess a higher porosity and coef ﬁcient
of friction, and have propensity for bony ingrowth and biological
ﬁxation [1,11,14,17,18]. Previous studies have con ﬁrmed favorable
results for tantalum cup with augments in revision THA associated
with acetabular bone defects [17,19,20]. However, the use of
tantalum augments combined with a porous titanium-coated cup
for the reconstruction of acetabular defect in primary THA has been
rarely reported. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the clinical
and radiographic results of such a combination for the recon-
struction of acetabular defect in primary THA.
Materials and Methods
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hos-
pital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.
According to the records of hip arthroplasty in our institution, a
total of 89 patients were diagnosed acetabular defect in primary
THA. Among them, 24 patients (24 hips) received acetabular
tantalum augments (Continuum; Zimmer, Warsaw, IN) for the
treatment of acetabular defect from March 2007 to December
2014. Of these patients, 1 was lost to follow-up and 1 died of
causes unrelated to THA within 2 years of the surgery, 2 had
insufﬁcient follow-up time (less than the minimum 2 years) at the
ﬁnal follow-up, and 1 patient lacked intact preoperative and
postoperative radiographic data. These 5 patients were therefore
excluded, leaving 19 patients with acetabular defect treated with
primary THA who were eligible for the study. The clinical, radio-
graphic, and surgical data of these 19 patients were retrospectively
evaluated.
There were 6 men and 13 women of average age 51.6 years
(range 19-65) at the time of surgery. The diagnoses were DDH in 9
patients, post-traumatic osteoarthritis in 2, previous history of hip
tuberculosis or infection in 5, severe osteoarthritis secondary to
slipped capital femoral epiphysis in 2, and severe hip osteoarthritis
secondary to dysplasia epiphysialis multiplex in 1. The degree of
dislocation of DDH according to Crowe type were group II in 4 hips,
group III in 3, and group IV in 2. The patients with previous history
of hip tuberculosis or infection showed extended destruction of the
femoral head and acetabulum, 4 of whom presented with high
dislocation. The indications for arthroplasty for all patients were
severe hip pain resulting in stiffness, limping, and dif ﬁculty with
walking, and performing daily activities.
A posterolateral approach was used in all patients. After opening
the hip articular capsule, the femoral head was resected and the
osteophytes and ﬁbrous tissue were removed to expose the true
acetabulum and acetabular defect. We sequentially used hemi-
spheric reamers at the native hip center until the medial wall of the
true acetabulum with punctate bleeding cancellous bone was
reached. A trial acetabular component was inserted with the

sized tantalum augment with good contact with the host bone. The
half-moon tantalum augment (Zimmer) was secured to the bone
with multiple screws to provide the complete cover for the un-

acetabular cup edge, the overhang need to be cut to keep matching.
The portion of the tantalum augment to contact the acetabular
component was coated with polymethylmethacrylate. Morselized
bone graft was placed into any remaining cavities and spaces. The
porous-coated acetabular component (Pinnacle; DePuy, Warsaw,

augment, and ﬁxed with dome screws in all hips. Transverse sub-
trochanteric osteotomy was performed in 9 patients with high hip
dislocation to equalize the leg lengths and avoid excessively
stretching the sciatic nerve.
Postoperatively, all patients were placed in an abduction brace
and encouraged to take early exercise for hip ﬂexion, extension,
and abduction while on their bed on the second postoperative day,
along with multimodal analgesia. Partial weight-bearing walk was
performed within the ﬁrst 3 weeks. Gradual full weight bearing
with crutches was allowed after 4-6 weeks, and the use of
crutches was abandoned at between 8 and 12 weeks after the
operation.
Clinical and radiographic evaluation was performed preopera-
tively and at 3 and 6 months after surgery, then annually until the
last follow-up. Clinical outcomes were assessed by health-related
quality-of-life questionnaires. All patients received annual ques-
tionnaires by mail, telephone, or outpatient department visits. They
were required to answer the Oxford and Harris Hip Score [21,22],
Short Form-12 [23] (physical and mental), University of California
Los Angeles (UCLA) activity scale [24], and a patient satisfaction
score [25] (including function, recreation, and pain). In those
patients with DDH or healed infected hip presented with high
dislocation, the Trendelenburg test was used to evaluate restora-
tion of hip abduction strength. Intraoperative and postoperative
complications were also recorded.
Radiographic evaluation consisted of standard anteroposterior
radiographs of the pelvis and anteroposterior and lateral radio-
graphs of the hip, which were obtained preoperatively and at all
follow-up intervals ( Fig. 1 ). The radiographic data of all patients
were reviewed sequentially by 2 independent orthopedists.
Radiolucent lines adjacent to the acetabular component and
augment were identi ﬁed as described by DeLee and Charnley [26]
and recorded when they involved 50% of the affected acetabular
zone [1]. The height of the femoral head center of the hip was
measured perpendicular to the interteardrop line; the horizontal
hip center position was measured between the femoral head center
and the perpendicular drawn from the interteardrop line at the
teardrop; and the cup inclination angle between the horizontal line
drawn through the interteardrop line and the cup wire marker was
calculated [13]. Cup loosening was recorded when there was more
than 6 mm of migration in the vertical or horizontal directions or
more than a 10
/C14 change in inclination or progressive radiolucent
lines of >2 mm around the cup and augment [26,27]. Radiological
signs of osseointegration between host bone and the acetabular
components were assessed using the criteria of Moore et al [28].
Signs included the absence of radiolucent lines and the presence of
superolateral buttress, medial stress shielding, radial trabeculae,
and inferomedial buttress. If 3 or more signs were present, bone
ingrowth was considered.
Measurement data are presented as mean values and standard
deviation. The differences between clinical and radiological out-
comes before and after surgery were compared using a paired
Student's t-test. Differences at P < .05 were considered to be sta-
tistically signiﬁcant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS
software (Version 19; SPSS, Chicago, IL).
Results
The average operation time was 124.7 minutes and the mean
blood loss 530 mL. The mean hospital stay was 15.7 days. A poly-
ethylene liner was used in 7 patients and a ceramic liner was used
in the remaining 12. The size of the acetabular cup ranged from 44
to 52 mm. The most commonly used augment size was 50/10, in 12
patients; size 50/15 was used in 5 patients and 50/20 in 2. The mean
number of screws used to ﬁxing augments was 2.3 (range 2-4).
At a mean follow-up of 5.1 years (range 2.5-7.6), the Harris Hip
Score improved from 35.8 (range 23-56) preoperatively to 85.3
(72-98) at last follow-up ( P < .01); the Oxford Hip Score decreased
from 49.1 (36-56) preoperatively to 21.3 (12-42) at last follow-up
(P < .01); and the University of California Los Angeles activity
scale increased from 2.9 (2-6) preoperatively to 6.9 (6-10) at last
follow-up ( P < .01). The Short Form-12 score at last follow-up
improved signi ﬁcantly compared with preoperative scores
(Table 1 ). The evaluation of satisfaction for surgery and Trende-
lenburg's sign is also shown in Table 1.
On last follow-up pelvis radiographs, the mean hip center
position was 2.79 cm (range 2.35-3.58) horizontally and 2.06 cm

Discussion
Tantalum is used popularly in the management of bone loss in
acetabular reconstruction. Several mechanical properties make
porous tantalum an excellent material for acetabular reconstruction.
First, tantalum has greater porosity, approximately 2-3 times that of
cobalt chromium and titanium, and creates more bone and ﬁbrous
ingrowth potential [29,30]. Moreover, the elastic modulus of porous
tantalum (also known as trabecular metal) is similar to that of sub-
chondral bone. Therefore, porous tantalum enables physiological
transfer of load from implant to host bone, minimization of stress
shielding, and preservation of limited bone stock [31]. Finally,
biomechanical testing has demonstrated that porous tantalum has a
high coefﬁcient of friction, which provides superior implant stability
compared with that provided by traditional cementless implants
[32]. Successful long-term results depend heavily on achieving
intraoperative stability of the acetabular components [20].
Porous tantalum used in the reconstruction of acetabular defects
mainly includes porous tantalum shells with and without augments
[33]. Augments can be used instead of structural allografts to ﬁll

superior bone defects [1,14,20,27]. Porous tantalum shells without
augments have been used to treat hip dysplasia in primary THA with
limited bone loss [1 1]. Even high dislocation of hip dysplasia with
intact bone bridge between the true and false acetabula can be
treated successfully by acetabular shells alone ( Fig. 2) [34]. Limited
acetabular defects in this situation can be ﬁlled by autogenous bone.
Porous tantalum shells alone deliver very good follow-up results at
almost 10 years after surgery [1 1,34,35]. Although porous tantalum
shells have shown excellent results for the management of
acetabular defects of DDH in primary THA, we have not used such
shells in this study for several reasons. First, the range of acetabular
defects in this study was relatively limited. Teardrop and medial
wall were seen clearly on radiographs of the pelvis in all patients.
Defects were all located in the weight-bearing region of the
superolateral acetabulum and 58% located in the posterosupero-
lateral acetabulum. The medial wall and the anterior and posterior
column of the true acetabula were intact. Superolateral and post-
erosuperolateral acetabular defects result in high hip dislocation,
which in this study was reduced to true acetabula with bleeding
cancellous bone and bone-cup contact area. Therefore, a conven-
tional uncemented cup with screw ﬁxation can also possess pref-
erable bone and ﬁbrous ingrowth potential. Second, porous
tantalum shells are more expensive than conventional porous ti-
tanium coating cups. Using tantalum shells in the primary THA is
not cost-effective [14]. Finally, if these patients suffer from hip
infection, aseptic cup loosening and migration, or other complica-
tions, revision after primary THA will be required. Porous tantalum
shells provide a better bone ingrowth material at this stage.
Although the patients in this study had bleeding cancellous
bone and bone-cup contact area for true acetabula, we were unable
to provide adequate initial stability for the cup alone because of the
acetabular defects in the superior acetabular rim. Therefore, the
crucial step lies in reconstructing the defect using a structural
buttress to provide a weight-bearing region for the cup [27].
Impaction bone grafting with morselized grafts leads to favorable
results in the reconstruction of acetabular defects in patients with
hip dysplasia [1 1,13]. However, the absorption of bone grafts was
observed [1 1], which may affect the long-term stability of the cup.
In addition, Iwase et al [13] use metal mesh to ﬁx the morselized
bone graft and a cemented cup to achieve adequate stability.
However, results of long-term survival of cemented acetabular
components have been relatively poor, re ﬂected by a high rate of
aseptic loosening, especially in younger patients [36]. Furthermore,
a high aseptic loosening rate after long-term follow-up after
reconstruction of large defects with impaction grafting was also
reported in the literature [16,37]. Bulk femoral head autografting
with both cemented and uncemented implants is another method
used to restore acetabular defects [38,39]. The union and stability of
the autograft have been identi ﬁed as critical factors in achieving a
successful outcome with this technique [40]. However, femoral
head autografts are known to have slow and incomplete incorpo-
ration, limiting their ability to respond to stresses under cyclic
loading [41,42]. Moreover, collapse and resorption of the autografts
is a concern [43]. Because of poor bone quality and the small size of
femoral heads in the patients with DDH or severe secondary
osteoarthritis with high hip dislocation, the source of autografts is
limited in these patients so that allografting is necessary [13]
.
However, allografting carries a risk of disease transmission, and the
process of impaction bone grafting and preparation of a suitably
shaped bulk autograft are time-consuming techniques [35]. In this
study, tantalum augments were used to reconstruct acetabular
defects in primary THA for the following reasons. First, the
acetabular defects greater than 30% are a major indication for using
tantalum augments. The shape of defects is cambered when the
defects are greater than 30%. Therefore, structural buttress need to
be designed as arc to cover the defects completely. It is dif ﬁcult to
trim a bulk bone graft into suitable arc and hard to cover the defects
completely using a bone graft. However, the half-moon tantalum
augment can provide cambered buttress to cover the circular
acetabular defects completely. If the superolateral acetabular
defects were less than 30%, bulk bone graft may be used ( Fig. 3 ).
Second, tantalum augments possess the property of better bone
and ﬁbrous ingrowth potential and a microstructure similar to that
of trabecula, which contributes to the integration of augment and
host bone [29]. Third, tantalum augments have a variety of shapes
and sizes to cope with different manifestations of acetabular
defects in primary THA [33]. Surgeons can choose augments of
suitable diameter and thickness to ﬁll defects directly and shorten
the operation time. Fourth, the augments and titanium shell can be
connected with bone cement to create a massive structure with
better initial stability. Finally, tantalum augments eliminate the
complications of the collapse and resorption of bone graft and
disease transmission by an allograft.
We report herein that the combination of tantalum augments
and conventional shells confers favorable clinical and radiographic
results. This approach allows anatomical cup placement, good
initial stability, and a simple surgical procedure. However, there are
several limitations. The number of subjects in this retrospective
study was limited. A prospective control study with a larger sample
size is needed to further evaluate the results of the combined use of
tantalum augments and conventional shells in comparison with
other methods in reconstructing acetabular defects in primary THA.
Moreover, the longer term follow-up results for such reconstruc-
tion methods need further assessment. Finally, in this study, the
extent of acetabular defect was restricted to the superior acetabular
rim. The teardrop and medial wall of the acetabulum were intact in
all patients. Therefore, the results of such combined acetabular
components for the treatment of extensive acetabular defects need
further evaluation.
In conclusion, porous tantalum augments combined with tita-
nium shells show satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes
for the reconstruction of acetabular defects in primary THA at a
mean 5.1 years of follow-up. This approach confers anatomical cup
placement, simple operation, and a high rate of stable ﬁxation.
