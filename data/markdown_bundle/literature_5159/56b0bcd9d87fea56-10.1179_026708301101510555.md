Mechanical and chemical properties of Ni3Si
and Ni3(Si,Ti) alloys multiphased by
chromium addition
T. Takasugi, H. Kawai, and Y. Kaneno
The alloying behaviour and microstructure of Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys were ﬁrst
characterised by optical microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and scanning electron microscopy with electron probe
analysis. The microstructures of the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys consisted of large dispersed Ni
5Si2 phase and ﬁnely
precipitated Ni 3Si phase in nickel solid solution, while the Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys consisted of ﬁnely
precipitated Ni 3(Si,Ti) phase and nickel solid solution. Then, the high temperature mechanical properties, bend
strength, and oxidation and corrosion properties of the alloys were investigated. The Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
showed signiﬁcant strengthening over a wide range of temperatures, and also large compressive plastic deformation
at high temperatures. The strength and fracture toughness at ambient temperatures were correlated with the volume
fraction of Ni
5Si2 phase. The Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys did not show increased yield strength, but exhibited
improved tensile ductility and plasticity over a wide range of temperatures. Both Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si –
Ti – Cr quaternary alloys showed substantially improved oxidation resistance in air at 1173 K, compared with Ni3Si
and Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys. Also, the Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys showed corrosion
resistance comparable to that of the Ni 3Si and Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys. MST/4707

Introduction
Alloys based on Ni 3Si are considered to be candidates for
high temperature structural materials and chemically
resistant parts, as Ni
3Si displays increasing strength with
increasing temperature1 and also shows excellent oxidation
and corrosion resistance over a wide range of temperatures.
Furthermore, titanium addition to Ni 3Si leads to: (a)
increased strength as a result of solid solution hardening; 2
(b) enhanced peak temperature in the strength versus
temperature curve 2,3 via modiﬁcation of the speciﬁc
dislocation structure; 3,4 and (c) high tensile ductility at
ambient temperatures by a reduction in the propensity to
intergranular fracture. 2,5 Thus, Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys have
demonstrated a number of useful mechanical properties.2–5
Subsequent work has investigated improvement of the
mechanical and related properties of Ni
3(Si,Ti) alloys. It has
been found that doping with interstitials such as boron,
carbon, and beryllium enhances further the ambient
temperature ductility via prevention of intergranular
fracture.
5–7 Alloying with some substitutional (transition)
metals has also been carried out, mostly within their
solubility limits, and shown to result in favourable
mechanical properties.
8 In addition, a recent study has
demonstrated that niobium containing secondary phase
dispersions (such as D0a type Ni3Nb) in the Ni3(Si,Ti) phase
matrix lead to further strengthening over a wide range of
temperatures, and also improvements with regard to
moisture induced embrittlement as well as lack of high
temperature tensile elongation,
9–1 1 known to be problems
with the use of Ni 3Si and Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys.
In the present study, chromium expected to improve
particularly chemical properties such as oxidation and
corrosion resistance, has been added to Ni3Si and Ni3(Si,Ti)
alloys. The chromium addition in both cases was beyond
the solubility limit in the L12 phases of the alloys. The
microstructures of the chromium added Ni 3Si and
Ni3(Si,Ti) alloys were therefore expected to consist of a
multiphase. The alloying behaviour, microstructure,
mechanical properties, and chemical properties were
investigated by means of optical microscopy, X-ray
diffraction, scanning electron microscopy with electron
probe analysis, tensile testing, three point bend testing, and
oxidation and corrosion measurement. It has been demon-
strated that the Ni
3Si and Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys, with a
multiphase microstructure consisting of Ni 3Si (L12) phase,
Ni5Si2 phase, and nickel solid solution, have excellent
mechanical and chemical properties.
Experimental procedure
Alloy compositions used in the present study were classiﬁed
into two groups, as indicated in Table 1. The ﬁrst group
included Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys, denoted by TA, TB, TC,
and TD. These alloy compositions have been plotted on a
Ni – Si – Cr ternary phase diagram at 1323 K (Fig. 1a), and
are suggested to lie in the three phase region consisting of
Ni
3Si ( L12), Ni 5Si2 (hexagonal), and nickel solid solution
(fcc). The second group included Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary
alloys, denoted by QA and QB (Table 1). The two
quaternary alloy compositions have been plotted on a
Ni
3Si – Ni3Ti – Ni3Cr pseudoternary phase diagram at
1323 K (Fig. 1 b). In this case, chromium was added to
Ni80.3Si11.1Ti8.6 (at.-%) such that it substituted for titanium
sites, keeping the concentrations of the other constituents,
nickel and silicon, constant. All Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
used in the present study were doped with 0.005 wt-% boron
to improve the low temperature ductility. Also, for
comparison, Ni
3Si binary and Ni 3(Si,Ti) ternary alloys
were prepared using the same procedure as that used for the
above two groups of alloys.
Starting raw materials were 99 .9%Ni, 99 .999%Si,
99.9%Ti, 99 .99%Cr, and 99 .9%B (wt-%). Alloy button
ingots with dimensions 50 625615 mm were prepared by
non-consumable arc melting in argon gas on a copper

bend strength s
f was calculated from a load versus
displacement curve using the relationship sf~1.5 Pl/wt,
where P is the load, l is the span (i.e. 10 mm), and w and t
are the width and the thickness of the specimen,

Oxidation properties were determined using specimens of
dimension 1061061 mm. Before testing, all major surfaces
were abraded with 1500 grit SiC paper, then ultrasonically
cleaned with ethanol. Isothermal oxidation tests based on
the cyclic test were performed in static laboratory air at
1173 K. The specimens placed on an alumina boat were
inserted into and removed from the hot zone of a furnace.
Mass gain of the specimens exposed for 1, 5, 10, 30, 70, 140,
and 200 h was measured at room temperature, together
with the alumina boat. Therefore, the measured mass gain
in the present study includes the spalled oxide scales. The
oxidised surfaces were observed visually and by SEM.
Corrosion properties were determined in sulphuric acid
at 363 K using specimens of dimension 10 61061 mm.
Testing was carried out using solutions of 40, 60, 80, and
98% sulphuric acid, and mass loss was measured after
immersion for 24 h.

ALLOYING BEHAVIOUR AND
MICROSTRUCTURE
Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
Optical microstructures were observed on a longitudinal
section of the button ingot along which the alloys had
solidiﬁed. Microstructures of the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
are shown in Fig. 2. The TA alloy showed large dendritic
Ni
5Si2 phase, and two phase regions consisting of Ni 3Si
(L12) phase and nickel solid solution. The Ni 3Si phase and
nickel solid solution were resolved by SEM as shown in
Fig. 3, where the dotlike constituents in the two phase
regions are the Ni
3Si precipitates (in TD alloy in this case).
The microstructures of the other three ternary alloys were
similar to one another, but differed somewhat from that of
the TA alloy. Isolated, rounded regions of Ni
5Si2 phase
were surrounded by two phase regions of Ni 3Si (L12) phase
and nickel solid solution. The volume fraction of the Ni 5Si2
phase depended on the alloy (Table 1); the TA alloy had the
largest volume fraction of Ni5Si2 phase (73%), while the TC
alloy had the smallest (29%). Based on this observation, it is
suggested that the alloys solidify as (TA alloy) primary
allotriomorphs of Ni
5Si2, followed by a eutectic solidiﬁca-
tion of nickel solid solution and Ni 5Si2 (on rapid
solidiﬁcation in the arc furnace, the equilibrium Ni 3Si
phase may not form). On heat treatment, the eutectic would
coarsen to some extent, to give the large rounded areas of
Ni5Si2 seen in Fig. 2 b, c, and d. On cooling from the heat
treatment temperature, a ﬁne dispersion of Ni 3Si would be
precipitated from the nickel solid solution by solid state
transformation. The alloys containing lesser amounts of
silicon (TB and TD alloys) do not contain primary Ni 5Si2
(Figs. 2b and d) but only coarsened eutectic. The alloy with
lowest silicon content (TC alloy) consisted of nickel solid
solution and some coarsened eutectic, with precipitated
Ni3Si in the nickel solid solution phase (Fig. 2 c).

MECHANICAL PROPERTIES
Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
Figure 7 plots compressive yield stress (deﬁned at 0 .2%
offset strain) of the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys as a function
of test temperature. Data for the Ni
3Si binary alloy 16 are
also plotted for reference. It can be seen that, as the
temperature increased, the yield stress of the Ni 3Si binary
alloy increased, and then showed a peak, followed by a
rapid decrease. The Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys did not
display a positive temperature dependence of the yield
stress, in contrast to the Ni
3Si binary alloy. 1,16 As the test
temperature increased, the yield stress of the TA alloy
decreased rapidly from room temperature, those of the TB
and TD alloys from 473 K, and that of the TC alloy from
873 K. Nevertheless, the yield stress level of the Ni – Si – Cr
ternary alloys was substantially high, particularly at low
temperatures, in comparison with that of the Ni
3Si binary
alloy. It is suggested that the yield stress at low temperatures
is closely related to the volume fraction of Ni
5Si2 phase. It is
evident from Table 1 and Fig. 7 that the TA alloy, among
the studied Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys, shows the highest
strengthening at room temperature and the highest volume
fraction of Ni
5Si2 phase.

Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys
Figure 10 plots tensile properties of the Ni – Si – Ti – Cr
quaternary alloys as a function of test temperature. Both
QA and QB alloys maintained a nearly constant yield stress
up to 873 K, which then began to decrease rapidly.
However, the QA alloy showed consistently higher yield
stress than that of the QB alloy over a wide range of test
temperatures. It can also be noted that the yield stress of the
QA alloy was higher at room temperature and lower at
temperatures beyond 473 K than that of the Ni
3(Si,Ti)
alloy. The QA alloy exhibited a higher ultimate tensile stress
(UTS) than that of the Ni3(Si,Ti) alloy over the entire range
of test temperatures. In addition, the QA alloy showed a
substantially higher value of tensile elongation than that of
not only the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy, but also the QB alloy, as can
be seen in Fig. 10 c. It has been reported that tensile
elongation of the Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloy rapidly decreases with
increasing temperature and becomes zero at temperatures
beyond 700 K.5 In the present case, the tensile elongation of
the QA alloy showed a minimum at 873 K, accompanied by
an increase at the highest test temperature of 1073 K.

Figure 12 shows the effect of test temperature on the

Discussion

various types of microstructures can be prepared: a three
phase (consisting of Ni3Si, Ni5Si2, and nickel solid solution)
microstructure and two types of two phase (Ni 3Si plus
Ni5Si2, and Ni3Si and nickel solid solution) microstructures.
In the present study, the three phase microstructure was
investigated. In all Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys observed, the
Ni

enhancing the ﬂow strength. More attempts are needed to
improve further the ﬂow strength at low temperatures. The
second strengthener is the nickel solid solution, but this is
less effective in enhancing the ﬂow strength. The introduc-
tion of the Ni
5Si2 phase to the Ni 3Si binary alloy reduced
the ductility at low temperatures. It is likely that the Ni 5Si2
phase dispersions act as a stress enhancer, resulting in
premature fracture by facilitating transgranular or inter-
facial cracking. Therefore, ﬁnely distributed Ni 5Si2 phase
dispersions with coherency with the Ni3Si matrix may again
be required to improve the low temperature ductility, as well
as the low temperature strength.
At low to intermediate temperatures, the multiphase
microstructures of the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys were
shown to be still largely effective in enhancing the yield
strength. Nevertheless, a positive temperature dependence
of the yield strength was not exhibited by the Ni – Si – Cr
ternary alloys. On the other hand, it is assumed that the
Ni

understood from Fig. 7. Alternatively, (compressive) plastic

alloys at high temperatures, as shown in Fig. 8. It is likely
that such plastic deformation is guaranteed by activation of
the glide and/or climb motions of the dislocations in the
Ni
5Si2 phase.

Among the studied Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys, the TC
alloy with the largest chromium concentration showed the
poorest oxidation resistance. This result indicates that
microstructural factors as well as (overall) chemical
composition play an important role in the oxidation
behaviour. The volume fraction of Ni
5Si2 phase was
smallest in the TC alloy, among the Ni – Si – Cr ternary
alloys. Compared with the Ni
3Si and nickel solid solution,
the Ni5Si2 phase appears to be highly resistant to oxidation
because of its high silicon concentration, which is possibly
why the TC alloy shows the poorest oxidation resistance.
It was demonstrated in the present study that chromium
addition to the Ni
3Si binary and Ni 3(Si,Ti) alloys sig-
niﬁcantly reduced the oxidation thickness at 1173 K under
isothermal conditions. Since the structure, chemistry, and
microstructure of the oxidised layers have not been analysed
in detail, it is not possible to present an actual mechanism
for the effect of chromium addition on the oxidation of the
studied alloys. An extended investigation is currently being
undertaken and will be published elsewhere.
Finally, an overall evaluation of the properties of the Ni –
Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys of the
present study is given as follows. If strength over a wide
range of temperatures is taken into consideration, the Ni –
Si – Cr ternary alloys are preferable. If ductility and
plasticity (or deformability) are taken into consideration,
the Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys are preferable. For
oxidation resistance, the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys are
preferable to the Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys. For
corrosion resistance, the Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys
are preferable to the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys.
Conclusions
The alloying behaviour, microstructure, high temperature
mechanical properties, and oxidation and corrosion proper-
ties of Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary
alloys consisting of a multiphase were investigated by
optical microscopy, X-ray diffraction, scanning electron
microscopy with electron probe analysis, mechanical
testing, three point bend testing, and oxidation and
corrosion measurement. The following results were
obtained.
1. The microstructures of the Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys
consisted of large dispersed Ni
5Si2 phase and ﬁnely
precipitated Ni3Si phase in nickel solid solution, while the
Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys consisted of ﬁnely pre-
cipitated Ni3(Si,Ti) phase and nickel solid solution.
2. The Ni – Si – Cr ternary alloys showed signiﬁcant
strengthening over a wide range of temperatures, and also
large compressive plastic deformation (deformability) at
temperatures above 900 K. The strength and fracture
toughness at ambient temperatures were correlated with
the volume fraction of Ni
5Si2 phase.
3. The Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys did not show
enhanced yield strength, but demonstrated improved tensile
ductility and plasticity (or deformability) over a wide range
of temperatures.
4. Both Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni – Si – Ti – Cr quater-
nary alloys showed substantially improved oxidation
resistance in air at 1173 K, compared with Ni
3Si binary
and Ni3(Si,Ti) alloys. Also, the Ni – Si – Cr ternary and Ni –
Si – Ti – Cr quaternary alloys showed corrosion resistance in
sulphuric acid comparable to that of the Ni
3Si binary and
Ni3(Si,Ti) alloys.
