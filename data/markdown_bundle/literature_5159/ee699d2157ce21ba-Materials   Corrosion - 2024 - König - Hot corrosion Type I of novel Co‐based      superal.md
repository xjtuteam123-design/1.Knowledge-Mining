1 | INTRODUCTION
The efficiency of turbine engines increases by using higher
gas temperatures, which results in a striving for materials
with higher temperature capabilities. In 2006,
[1] the
γ′‐phase in the Co –Al–W system was (re ‐)d i s c o v e r e d ,
which allowed the development of Co alloys with better
mechanical properties at high temperatures. The γ/γ′
Co‐based superalloys have since then been a candidate to
replace the Ni ‐based superalloys since Co possesses a
higher melting temperature.
[1–4] Since superalloys are
tailored to achieve the best mechanical properties, they
often lack corrosion resistance. Therefore, bond coats
are applied to increase their chemical stability in the
combustion atmospheres at high temperatures.
[5] These
coatings prevent the delamination of the outermost
thermal barrier coatings and also provide the needed
oxidation and corrosion resistance. [6,7] They can be
applied via a pack cementation process, which enriches
the substrate surface in the coating element and results in,
for example, Al ‐ or Cr‐diffusion coatings. The Al ‐coatings
are used to provide oxidation resistance up to the highest
temperatures, while Cr coatings are advantageous at lower
temperatures and against hot corrosion, which is a
corrosion mechanism by molten salt deposits.
[8,9]
During service, sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) can accumulate
in a molten state at the surfaces in the hot sections of
turbines.
[10] The Na 2SO4 can dissolve the protective oxide
scales on alloys and coatings, [11,12] which reprecipitate as
porous and non‐protective corrosion products. Depending on
temperature, gas, and alloy composition, different mecha-

2 | EXPERIMENTAL
2.1 | Material
The substrate materials studied were the two γ/γ′ alloys
ERBOCo‐1 and ERBOCo ‐1X as well as derivates of them
with a decreased W content but the same Co/Ni ratio.
The compositions are given in Table 1. The alloys were
cast via Bridgman investment casting by the Institute of
Metals Science and Technology (WTM, University of
Erlangen‐Nürnberg (FAU), Germany). The received
12‐mm‐diameter rods had an intended (001) orientation
along the rod axis and were heat treated by the procedure
proposed by Volz et al., [2] which is provided in Table 2.
The homogenization was done before cutting the
samples of 3 mm thickness. Grinding to a P1200 finish
was done for coating samples after homogenization and
for the uncoated samples after the second aging step.
The pack cementation process was applied during the
first aging step to avoid a change in microstructure. The
coating process along with the microstructure have been
previously described in ‐depth
[18] and will be briefly
mentioned here (see Figure 1). The Al coatings consisted
of an outer β‐(Co,Ni)Al‐layer as well as an inner diffusion
zone, and were between 48 and 70 µm thick depending
on the alloy. The Cr coating consisted of an outward and
inward grown σ‐phase and an interdiffusion zone with a
thickness of about 14 µm. In both cases, the coating was
slightly oxidized during the second aging step, which was
performed in an Ar + 5% H
2 atmosphere, forming either
an Al 2O3 or MnCr 2O4 spinel scale.

2.3 | Microscopic examination and
evaluation
After the exposure to the Type I hot corrosion, the
specimens were weighed and X ‐ray diffraction (XRD;
Bruker D8 advance) patterns were taken (when possible).
The specimens were then embedded in epoxy resin. Cross‐
sections of the hot corrosion specimen were obtained by
grinding and polishing water‐free using dried petroleum as
lubricant and dried acetone for cleaning. The specimens of
the exposure for 300 h could not be taken out of the
crucibles without damaging them due to the thick and solid
corrosion product. They were embedded in epoxy while
remaining in their crucible. XRD measurements of the 300 h
specimens were not possible and cross ‐sections were
obtained by prolonged grinding using the previously
described water ‐free process. The cross ‐sections were
examined using light microscopy, scanning electron micros-
copy (SEM; Hitachi Flex SE M 1000, Hitachi SU5000, both
equipped with energy‐dispersive spectroscopy) and electron
probe microanalysis (EPMA; Jeol JXA‐8100).
Hot corrosion often shows an inhomogeneous attack,
and the formation of pits or locally increased material
losses are frequently observed. To measure and depict this
localized attack, the material loss was measured on
stitched light microscopic images at a regular distance,
following the procedure described by Nicholls et al.
[19] By
measuring the whole surface of the specimen (except close
to edges) and sorting the data, cumulative probability plots
of the material loss were derived. To obtain these plots,
light microscopic overlapping images with a ×100
magnification were taken along the entire surface and
subsequently stitched together. In these images the initial
middle line was estimated and drawn. From here the
residual metal was measured every 100 µm. By subtracting
the residual metal from the initial thickness (measured
with a micrometer screw gauge) the material loss was
obtained. With an overall number of material loss
measurements p,t h e nth value of the sorted metal loss
has the cumulative probability of

4.2 | Incubation stage and coating
performance
The microstructure of the coated specimen showed the
initial formation of the respective, protective oxides with
an α‐Al2O3 or a layered (Ni,Co)Cr 2O4/Cr2O3 scale being
observed for the Al ‐ or Cr ‐coating, respectively. The
subsurface zone of the Cr ‐coating was severely affected
by nitridation and sulfidation, while for the Al ‐coating
only pore formation and sulfidation were observed. Only
scarcely an increased Al depletion is observed for the
β‐NiAl‐phase of the Al ‐coatings. For one sample, a
transition to the breakaway stage occurred by a lateral
spread of the attack along the interface between inter-
diffusion and substrate and began from the edges (see
Figure 6). This growth along the interface was the result of
the decreased resistance of the interdiffusion zone or
substrate in comparison to the high resistance of the
β‐NiAl of the coating. The interdiffusion zone not only
showed an increased amount of Al, but also of Cr, Ti, W
and Ta that are well known to form tcp‐phase precipitates.
To a minor extent, such refractory metal ‐rich precipitates
were observed throughout the Al ‐coatings. At the edges
(see Figure 5) the number of precipitates of the heavier
elements increased and almost formed a pathway through
the coating. Regarding the elements in the precipitates, it
has been reported that Cr and Ta have a positive influence
on the hot corrosion resistance,
[23–25] while a selective
attack of W ‐rich precipitates has been observed. [26,27] It is
not possible to say at this point if the preferred attack from
the edges was mainly an edge effect or if the precipitates
were significantly contributing to a faster breakaway. The
transition to the breakaway corrosion by a lateral spread is
not observed for the Cr ‐coating. Local failure of the
coatings occurred frequently, when the subsurface zone
was severely degraded by internal corrosion. An incuba-
tion stage was not observed for the uncoated samples, but
the outer corrosion products on these samples consisted of
a thick layer of Ni‐ and Co‐oxides with mixed oxides of Co,
Ni, Ti, Cr, and Al below (see Figure 4). This micro-
structure could be the result of either the initial oxidation
of the alloy or the outward fluxing of Co and Ni via the hot
corrosion mechanism.
During the exposure for 100 h, the Al ‐coating proved
to be more resistant in comparison to the Cr ‐coating, as it
prevented any degradation of the substrate (except
ERBOCo‐1X‐W alloy; see Figure 2). The Al ‐coating was
more uniform and incorporation of Al
2O3 particles from
the filler of the pack cementation process or precipitation
of oxides in the coatings were hardly observed (see
Figure 1). Such precipitates or particles were flaws in the
coating and could increase its recession. The second
reason was the higher thickness of the Al ‐coating (see
Section 2.1), which provided a larger reservoir of the
oxide former. This can also be seen in Figure 6c, where
despite the failure of the oxide scale the Al ‐coating
remained protective. With the thinner Cr ‐coating, after
100 h a preferred attack occurred at the edges of the
specimens (see Figure 7), which was only observed on
one of the Al ‐coated specimens after the exposure for
100 h (see Figure 6a). In addition, the growth rate of
protective α‐Al
2O3 scales is expected to be slower than
of Cr 2O3 scales, which results in a slower recession
rate by pure oxidation. [28] Furthermore, spallation
(observed for the Cr coatings; Figure 8), sulfidation
(observed for both; Figures 6b, 7b,a n d 9), internal
oxidation and nitridation (observed for the Cr ‐coatings;
Figures 7b and 9) have to be taken into account. All
these factors finally resulted in a protective Al ‐coating
on three of the four alloys for the 100 h exposure, while
specimens with the Cr ‐coating were all at least locally
attacked.

corrosion rapidly consumes the whole specimen after
the previously described lateral spread below the coating

The hot corrosion mechanism is known to result in
sulfides and oxides via a reaction including the salt
deposit, while the same products can be the result of an
only gas ‐based sulfidation and oxidation. On hot corro-
sion the beneficial behavior of Cr has been extensively
discussed by Rapp and Otsuka
[23] and was explained by
the dissolution reactions in Equations ( 2) and ( 3). Al 2O3
is thermodynamically stable in more basic conditions
than Cr2O3, when Cr 2O3 dissolves to form both chromite
and chromate. On the one hand this has the effect, that
the basicity of the salt is buffered by the chromite ‐
chromate equilibrium and on the other hand, that the
solubility of Cr
2O3 as chromate depends on pO 2. The first
effect is of importance since the continuous oxidation
below the corrosion product decreases the pO 2.A
decrease in pO 2 results in a decrease in pSO 3 and
therefore also in an increased basicity. The chromite ‐
chromate equilibrium buffers this drop in pO 2 and the
increase in basicity is therefore less severe. The lower and
more uniform basicity in the salt results not only in a
lower basic solubility of the protective oxides, but also in
smaller solubility gradients in the salt, which are of
importance for a continuous hot corrosion attack (self ‐
sustained fluxing as proposed by Rapp and Goto
[29]). The
second effect, the dependence of the solubility of Cr 2O3
on pO2, means that the solubility is decreased in areas of
low pO2. Otsuka et al. [30] measured the pO 2 and pSO 3 in
Na2SO4 in contact with Ni and observed initially a rapid
drop in pO2. A similar drop in pO 2 is expected when local
failures in the oxide scale can occur and the salt gets in
contact with the coating or alloy. The locally decreased
pO2 increases the basicity (which is buffered by the first
effect), but also decreases the solubility of chromate,
which results in the precipitation of Cr 2O3 from the salt
on flaws in the Cr 2O3 scale. Thereby, the scale is
repaired, and a further attack is hindered. The solubility
of Al 2O3 does not have a direct dependence on the pO 2
and no buffer of basicity exists. On oxidation and
sulfidation, a similar positive influence of Cr or Cr 2O3
scales is not known and aluminides, especially Fe to
avoid low melting eutectics, were proposed as candidate
materials for sulfidizing and oxidizing conditions. [31,32] A
positive influence of Al additions to FeCr alloys was
shown, but in low pO 2 conditions, in which the higher
thermodynamical stability of Al 2O3 is of importance. [33]
Even though comparisons between alumina and chromia
former for oxidation and sulfidation are rare in literature,
the general trend shows no favorable behavior of Cr 2O3
scales or chromia formers, especially at the given
temperature. The observed superior behavior of the
Cr‐coatings can therefore only be explained by the hot
corrosion mechanism, which implies that this mecha-
nism dominates during the incubation stage and early
propagation stage.
4.3 | Propagation stage
The attack within the propagation stage manifests in
similar ways for all surface treatments. While the overall
corrosion products consisted of the oxides of the alloying
elements and were arranged in a layered structure for the
advanced degradation, the microstructure at the corro-
sion front differed. Three different features were identi-
fied for this state of the degradation: “preceding sulfides,”
“Ni sulfide, ” and “W at the corrosion front. ”
“Preceding sulfides ” shows a morphology, which is
described in the literature
[26,34,35] as typical for the hot
corrosion Type I of Ni ‐based alloys. The accepted
formation mechanism has been described in the intro-
duction and consists of basic dissolution of a potentially
protective oxide scale along with continuous oxidation
and (internal) sulfidation that rendered the salt more
basic.
[26] The attack progressed by oxidation and sulfida-
tion.[35] Since the sulfur of the oxidized sulfides was
pushed into the substrate, internal sulfidation was
promoted, which depleted the substrate of the major
elements forming protective oxides. In the present case
the degradation appeared by this oxidation and sulfida-
tion mechanism, while the relevance of the basic
dissolution in the advanced propagation stage can be
questioned since Na
2SO4 is only scarcely observed. The
internal sulfides agglomerated and their oxidation
resulted in the observed network. The surrounding,
remaining metal consisted mainly of Ni, Co and to a
minor degree of W. Ni sulfides were found in the inner
part of the remaining alloy and within the oxide network.
The finely dispersed Ni sulfides indicate the formation of
a molten eutectic at test temperature. This micro-
structure of an outer elemental Ni and inner Ni sulfide

5 | CONCLUSION
In the present study, the resistance to hot corrosion Type
I was investigated for four different alloys of the
Co–Ni–Al–W system with and without Al and Cr
coatings. To quantify the degradation and evaluate the
resistance to hot corrosion, the material loss was
measured. Both coatings were protective for 100 h, but
at longer times the Cr coatings outlasted the thicker Al
coatings, indicating a fluxing mechanism during the
incubation stage. Independent of the coating, the
microstructure observed for high material losses con-
sisted of three distinct morphologies, which were
preceding sulfides associated with oxidation and sulfida-
tion, the Ni sulfide, and W at the corrosion front. In line
with such sulfidation based mechanisms, a higher W
content and a higher Co/Ni ratio were identified to
increase the resistance of the investigated alloys.
