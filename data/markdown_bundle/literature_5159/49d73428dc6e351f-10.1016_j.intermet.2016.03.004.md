abstract
Microstructures, mechanical properties and oxidation behaviour of single-phase Fe 3Al (D0 3) and two-
phase a-Fe,Al (A2) þ Fe3Al (D0 3)F e eAleV alloys have been studied. Alloying with vanadium substan-
tially increases the strength of the single-phase alloys through solid solution hardening. In addition,
vanadium markedly increases the D0
3/B2 transition temperature. The change from B2 to D0 3 at a given
temperature yields an additional increase in strength. The two-phase alloys have a ﬁne-scaled coherent
microstructure with D0 3 precipitates ranging between 10 and 30 nm in the as-cast state. However,
marked coarsening of D0 3 is observed after annealing at 700 /C14 C for 1000 h. Up to this temperature the
alloys show an appreciable ﬂow stress and creep strength, comparable to those of other iron aluminide
based alloys with coherent microstructures. Oxidation tests at 700 e900 /C14 C revealed formation of thick
and porous Fe 2O3 þ Al2O3 scales above 700 /C14 C. Therefore, above 700 /C14 C the oxidation behaviour of these
alloys is inferior compared to that of other iron aluminide alloys with about the same Al content. This
poor oxidation behaviour does not improve when 4 e8 at.% Cr are added.

1. Introduction
Currently there is an increased interest in Fe eAl based alloys
[1,2], stimulated not only by their advantageous properties, i.e.
outstanding corrosion resistance, high wear resistance, and
favourable density compared to stainless steels, but also because
they are inexpensive as they contain no or only minor amounts of
strategic elements. Developments of Fe eAl based alloys have been
centred on Fe eAleX systems containing X ¼ Ti, Nb, Zr, Cr, Ta, Mo
and Ni [3e7], often with the addition of minor amounts of carbon to
precipitate carbides, while the Fe eAleV system has gained less
attention up to now. One purpose of these efforts is to increase the
strength at high temperatures, which is a major issue in the
development of Fe eAl based alloys. In this respect, the Fe eAleV
phase diagram offers two interesting features for alloy de-
velopments, namely stabilising the intermetallic phase Fe
3Al to
higher temperatures or generating two-phase coherent a-Fe,Al
(A2) þ Fe3Al (D0 3) microstructures.
Fe3Al with D0 3 structure has a higher strength at a given tem-
perature than a-Fe,Al (A2) or FeAl (B2). Unfortunately, Fe 3Al is only
stable up to 545 /C14 C in the binary Fe eAl system [8] but can be sta-
bilised to higher temperatures by alloying with a number of ele-
ments [9e12]. However, in most Fe eAleX systems the solid
solubility for X is quite small and therefore the achievable increase
in the D0
3/B2 transition temperature is often limited. In this
respect, the Fe eAleV system is of interest as the miscibility of V in
Fe3Al is at least 40 at.% [13]. Vanadium substitutes preferentially for
iron on the 8c sublattice [14] whereby the D0 3 structure is stabi-
lised to higher temperatures, i.e. the D0 3/B2 transition temperature
increases with increasing V content [10e12,15,16]. By substituting
25 at.% Fe by V, i.e. at the stoichiometric composition Fe 2VAl (the
L21 Heusler phase, which is the ternary equivalent of the D0 3
structure1), the D0 3/B2 transition temperature can be raised to
about 1050 /C14 C [12], corresponding to an increase of about 500 /C14 C
compared to the binary system.

Another effective way to increase the strength of Fe eAl based
alloys is the generation of coherent precipitates. Within a number
of Fe eAleX systems miscibility gaps between the disordered A2,
ordered B2 and D0 3(L21) structures exist, where coherent micro-
structures form on cooling [18e21]. Such a miscibility gap also
exists in the Fe eAleV system, though at comparably low Al con-
tents [22,23]. In the temperature range 500 e750 /C14 C a miscibility
gap between A2 and D0 3 exists roughly in the composition range
5e25 at.% Al and 0 e25 at.% V [22,23]. According to the measure-
ments by Maebashi et al. the miscibility gap shrinks markedly
when the temperature increases from 650 /C14 C to 750 /C14 C and an
additional two-phase equilibrium B2 þ D03 exists at 750 /C14 C [23].
However, a calculated isothermal section for 1227 /C14 C still shows the
presence of the miscibility gap at that temperature [24].
Though magnetic and electrical properties of compositions
Fe2þxV1-xAl, i.e. the Heusler phase, have been extensively studied
[16], up to now few investigations of mechanical properties and the
oxidation behaviour of Fe eAleV alloys have been performed, the
former mainly focussing on determining the hardness. In a sum-
marising report of the extensive work on Fe eAl based alloys at the
Ford Motor Co., Morgan & Zackay claim that vanadium is “less
satisfactory as hot strengthener for iron aluminium alloys ”, though
no detailed evidence is provided [25]. In their investigation of a
large number of different Fe eAl alloys, Savitskii et al. found that
already 0.5 at.% of V markedly increased the hardness of Fe eAl
alloys with Al contents between 28 and 31 at.%, which were
annealed 700 /C14 C/20 h [26]. Mints et al. also reported a marked in-
crease of the hardness of Fe 3Al by alloying with V [27]. The hard-
ness of (Fe1-xVx)3Al for x ¼ 0e0.3 between RT and 1 100/C14 C has been
investigated by Nishino et al. [13]. They found that above about
500 /C14 C the hardness substantially increased by alloying with V and
related this to the increase of the D0 3/B2 transformation temper-
ature. Age hardening of Fe 1/C0 2xAlxVx (x ¼ 0.08e0.15) has been
investigated by Ferreiros et al. [28] by measurement of the hard-
ness. After quenching from 1 100/C14 C they observed that the hardness
increased with increasing V content. By subsequent ageing at
650 /C14 C for 2 h the hardness increased markedly, but already after
ageing at 700 /C14 C for 2 h some alloys were over aged. Judkins & Rao
reported a tensile elongation at room temperature (RT) of Fe 3Al
containing 1 at.% V of 4%, matching that of binary Fe 3Al [1]. For B2-
ordered Fe e35 at.% Al with 2 at.% V Mendiratta et al. found sub-
stantial solid solution hardening. They measured an elastic bend
strength of 469 MPa and 345 MPa at 600 /C14 C and 700 /C14 C, respec-
tively, compared to 296 MPa at 600 /C14 C and 124 MPa at 700 /C14 C for
binary Fee35 at.% Al [5]. Recently, Kratochvil et al. reported that the
compressive yield strength of Fe e27 at.% Al at 800 /C14 C can be
increased four times by alloying with 4 at.% V [29]. It has also been
stated that addition of V improves the creep resistance but again no
evidence has been presented [30].
Regarding the oxidation of Fe eAleV alloys, the information is
scarce and contradictory. While Savitskii et al. [26] reported that
the oxidation resistance is good up to 1200 /C14 C [26], Culbertson &
Kortovich found that V degrades the oxidation resistance of Fe 3Al
already at 816 /C14 C [31].
Microstructures, mechanical properties and oxidation behav-
iour of single-phase alloys with increased D0 3/B2 transformation
temperature and two-phase A2 þ D03 alloys have been established.
In order to elaborate the potential for application of FeeAleV alloys,
the data are compared to those of other iron aluminide alloys with
comparable microstructures.
2. Experiments
Four FeeAleV alloys were produced under argon atmosphere by
vacuum induction melting and two Fe eAleVeCr alloys were made
under argon atmosphere by crucible-free levitation melting. For the
production of the alloys, pure elements (Fe 99.9% purity, Al 99.9%, V
99.99%, Cr 99.9%) have been used. All alloys were cast into a cold
copper mould of 20 mm diameter. Wet chemical analysis revealed
that intended and analysed compositions ﬁt quite well except that
the Al content of all six alloys is slightly higher than the intended
one ( Table 1). Samples were cut by electrical discharge machining
(EDM). Microstructures were observed by light optical microscopy
and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Jeol JSM e6490). Trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) was carried out on a Jeol
2000FX at 200 kV. Samples for TEM were prepared by electrolytic
etching with 20% HNO3 þ CH3OH at /C0 30 /C14 C. Chemical compositions
of individual phases were established by energy-dispersive spec-
trometry (EDS; EDAX) on the SEM.
For differential thermal analysis (DTA) a Setaram SETSYS e18
DTA was employed. Experiments were carried out in alumina
crucibles under argon atmosphere at heating rates of 5 and 10 K/
min. The temperature was calibrated by the melting points of
appropriate pure metals. Thereby an accuracy of ±1 K is achieved.
The reproducibility of the results was checked by repeated mea-
surements of the same samples during heating and cooling.
For mechanical testing samples of the size 5 /C2 5 /C2 10 mm3 were
cut by EDM. Compressive yield stresses at a deformation rate of
_ε ¼ 1 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 were determined in air using a Schenck eTrebel
universal spindle-driven testing machine. The creep behaviour in
air between 650 and 750 /C14 C was evaluated in compression by
stepwise increasing the load every time stationary creep had been
achieved. Besides testing the alloys in the as-cast state, one part of
each alloy has been tested after an equilibration heat treatment
700 /C14 C/1000 h in argon atmosphere with subsequent cooling in the
furnace to RT.
For the investigation of the oxidation behaviour by thermogra-
vimetric analysis (TGA), samples with dimensions of
10 /C2 10 /C2 1m m3 were ground to 1200 grit surface ﬁnish. TGA was
carried out using a Setaram SETSYS 16/18 thermobalance with
continuous recording of the mass gains. The oxidation behaviour
was established in synthetic air (20.5% O 2, 79.5% N 2) for up to
10 0 0 h at aﬂow rate of 1.54 /C2 10/C0 6 m3/s. Scales were characterised
by grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GI eXRD) with an incidence
angle of 2.0 /C14 using Co eKa1 radiation ( l ¼ 0.17890 nm) and scan-
ning the 2 Q range 22 e130/C14 on a Seifert Type ID 3003 diffractom-
eter. For inspection of cross sections, the TGA samples were
electroplated with Ni prior to cutting by EDM in order to preserve
the oxide scales.

3.2. Mechanical properties
The ﬂow stress of the single-phase alloys Fe e26Ale2V and
Fee26Ale4V ( Fig. 3 ) had been presented before [18,36]. These al-
loys have much higher strength than binary alloys with the same Al
content [37]. Moreover, the solid solution hardening effect of va-
nadium is rather strong when compared to other ternary alloying
elements. E.g. at 800 /C14 C the yield stress of Fe e26Ale2V and
Fee26Ale4V surpasses that of Fe e26Al alloyed with the same
amounts of Ti or Mo [18].
The compressive ﬂow stress of the two-phase alloys has been
established in dependence of temperature ( Fig. 3). The alloys have
been investigated in the as-cast state and after equilibration heat
treatment 700 /C14 C/1000 h. Fe e18Ale10V has a markedly higher
strength in the as-cast condition than Fe e17Ale5V. There is no
straightforward explanation for this difference. As the strength of
coherent microstructures crucially depends on the size, distance
and coherency stresses of the precipitates and as at least size and
distance of the precipitates differ in both alloys (Fig. 1), this could be
a possible explanation for the differences in their strength. After the
equilibration heat treatment, the strength of Fe e18Ale10V has
decreased so that the two alloys show about the same strength,

coarsen in this system up to 800 /C14 C [41].
Compressive creep tests with step-wise load increases have
been performed for the two-phase alloys between 650 and 750 /C14 C,
i.e. in the range of possible application temperatures, on as-cast
samples and on samples after an equilibration heat treatment
700 /C14 C/1000 h ( Figs. 5 and 6 ). The creep tests performed at 700 /C14 C
show a slightly lower strength for both alloys in the equilibrated
condition compared to the as-cast state. This difference can be
attributed to the coarsening of the precipitates through Ostwald
ripening during the preceding equilibration heat treatment. How-
ever, a much more pronounced loss in creep strength is observed
for alloy Fe e17Ale5V when tested at 650 /C14 C in the heat-treated
condition compared to the as-cast alloy ( Fig. 5 ). Post mortem ex-
amination of the microstructures of the crept samples reveals that
only slight coarsening of the D03 precipitates has taken place but no
raftening is observed ( Fig. 7 ), i.e. the coherent microstructures are
stable under creep conditions within the investigated temperature
range.
Compared to the creep strength of other as-cast Fe eAleX alloys
with coherent microstructures, the Fe eAleV alloys show a some-
what lower creep resistance ( Fig. 8 ). At 650 /C14 C they do have a
markedly lower creep resistance than Fe e25Ale2Ta [40] and
Fee23Ale8.5Ti [41]. At 750 /C14 C the creep resistance of Fee18Ale10V
is slightly better than that of Fe e25Ale2Ta [40], but signi ﬁcantly
lower than that of the Fe eAleTi [41] alloys (Fig. 8).
From the slopes of the creep curves in Figs. 5 and 6 stress ex-
ponents n have been determined according to Norton's power law.
Both FeeAleV alloys show a marked change in the stress exponent
n at higher strain rates ( Figs. 5 and 6 ). Up to a strain rate of about
10/C0 7 s/C0 1 the stress exponents n are >10, which are typically
observed when the dislocation movement is hindered by ﬁne
particles and deformation is only possible by cutting these particles
[43]. At higher strain rates, stress exponents of about 5 are
observed, which are typical if deformation occurs through climb
and glide of dislocations and values of n ¼ 4e5 have been
frequently observed in case of solid solution strengthened Fe eAl
based alloys [44e46]. The change in n should point to a change in
the deformation mechanism, in that at higher strain rates the ﬁne
particles are no longer effective obstacles for the motion of dislo-
cations but that they are sheared by them. In agreement with this,
Figs. 5 and 6 show that the change in the deformation mechanism
shifts to lower strain rates with increasing stress. Such changes in
the stress exponent have been observed before in case of Fe eAleTi
alloys with coherent A2 þ D03 microstructures (Fig. 8 ) [41]. How-
ever, the changes were less pronounced, i.e. from n ¼ 7e8 at low
strain rates to n ¼ 5e6 at higher strain rates ( Fig. 8). Also, in case of
the Fe eAleTi alloys, changes in the stress exponent n were only
observed when tested at 750 /C14 C or higher temperatures. No
changes of the stress exponent were observed in case of Fe eAleTa
alloys with coherent A2 þ D03 microstructures (Fig. 8) [40]. In case
of the FeeAleTi alloys the slight change in the stress exponent was
related to coarsening of the coherent precipitates in the as-cast
alloys during prolonged creep testing at 750 /C14 C, which was sup-
ported by microstructural observations of the crept samples [41].
Because the change in the stress exponent is much more pro-
nounced in case of the present alloys and as it is observed for alloys
tested in the as-cast condition and after an initial long term heat
treatment, it is believed that the reason for the change of the stress
exponent is different from that in the Fe eAleTi alloys in case of the
present alloys as detailed above.

(2.2 /C2 10/C0 15 g2cm/C0 4 s/C0 1) [48]. However, the value for Fe e26Ale4V
(1.4 /C2 10/C0 14 g2cm/C0 4 s/C0 1) is about one order of magnitude higher.
At 800 and 900 /C14 C, hyperbolic mass gains are observed, indi-
cating rapid oxidation of the samples ( Fig. 10). Cross sections of the
samples oxidized at 900 /C14 C revealed that the samples were fully
oxidized, not even leaving a metallic core. In case of Fe e26Ale4V
this was already the case after oxidation for less than 100 h.
GIeXRD ( Fig. 1 1) and SEM eEDS ( Fig. 12) revealed that only Al 2O3
and Fe 2O3 are observed. Analysis of the cross sections by EDS
revealed that both alloys oxidized at 900 /C14 C show some structuring
of the oxide (Fig. 12b, c). An outer layer of Fe2O3 lies above a layer of
Al2O3, while the core of the samples consists out of a mixture of
both oxides. The fact that no vanadium oxide was detected ( Figs. 1 1
and 12 ) hints to the formation of a volatile vanadium oxide. This
oxide is presumably V 2O5, which melts already at 675 /C14 C. Thus, the
present investigation shows also for the Fe e26AlexV alloys that
due to the formation of V 2O5 “vanadium cannot be used much
above 650 /C14 C in an oxidizing atmosphere ” [49].
As already the Fe e26AlexV alloys showed a poor oxidation
behaviour, no oxidation tests were performed on the two alloys
with even lower Al content for the following reason. It is well
known that about 17 at.% Al are necessary to form protective dense
and adherent Al
2O3 scales on Fe-Al [50]. The overall compositions
of the two-phase alloys are already at this limit. According to the
established phase diagram, the A2 phase in these alloys has an Al
content below about 15 at.% Al. Therefore, no oxidation resistance is
expected for these alloys. However, for binary Fe eAl alloys of such
low Al contents it is known that addition of Cr promotes formation
of protecting Al 2O3 scales, though the exact mechanism still needs
to be clari ﬁed [51,52].
Therefore, two alloys with 18 at.% Al, 4 at.% V and 4 and 8 at.% Cr,
respectively, were prepared. However, already in a preliminary test,
where the alloys were oxidised at 900 /C14 C in air in a furnace, they
showed no marked oxidation resistance. Examination of the scales
by SEM and EDS revealed that they are thick and porous and consist
of mixed oxides based on Fe 2O3 and Al 2O3, comparable to those
detected for Fe e26AlexV after oxidation at the same temperature.
As apparently additions of 4 e8 at.% Cr did not lead to any marked
oxidation resistance of Fe e18Ale4V, no further tests were
conducted.

Because knowledge on the mechanical and oxidation behaviour
of Fe eAleV alloys is quite limited, single- and two-phase alloys
have been investigated. Earlier reports, which show a strong in-
crease of the D0 3/B2 and B2/A2 transition temperature of Fe eAl by

addition of 4 at.% V increases the D0 3/B2 transition temperature by
about 155 /C14 C and the B2/A2 transition temperature by about 175 /C14 C.
Besides solid solution hardening, the increase in the D0 3/B2 tran-

for other Fe eAleX alloys with coherent microstructures. However,
at 700 /C14 C the microstructure coarsens quite rapidly, levelling the
strength with that of the single-phase alloys. At 700 /C14 C the single-

that of other iron aluminides, which form dense Al2O3 scales. Above
700 /C14 C thick and porous Fe 2O3 þ Al2O3 scales form, which are non-
protective and therefore rapid oxidation takes place. This unfor-
tunate oxidation behaviour does not improve by additional alloying
with 4e8 at.% Cr.
