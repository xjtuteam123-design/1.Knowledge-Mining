ABSTRACT
High Entropy Alloys (HEAs) have drawn significant attention due to their outstanding properties and potential
for industrial applications. The present study investigates the fabrication and evaluation of CuFeNiMnTi HEA,
synthesized by means of mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering (SPS). The sample that was
optimally prepared at 920

microhardness, fracture toughness, shear strength, and wear resistance. The addition of carbon from the process
control agent (PCA) and the interaction between titanium and carbon resulted in the formation of titanium
carbides, while oxide compounds served as lubricants, thereby reducing friction. The sintered CuFeNiMnTi alloy

1. Introduction
Conventional alloys frequently contain a principal element as well as
other elements, such as iron (Fe), aluminum (Al), and magnesium,
which serve to improve the microstructures and properties [1 – 4] . The
increasing number of alloying elements can result in the development of
intermetallic compounds and complicated microstructures, which may
affect their mechanical properties. The beginning of HEAs in 2004 [5]
has revolutionized conventional alloy concepts of design [1] . A HEA is
an alloy composed of a minimum of five primary elements in a nearly
equal ratio. These alloys are known for their high mixing entropy, which
may lead to the lattice distortion and slow cooperative diffusion. As a
result, rather than intermetallic compounds, HEAs typically form simple
solid solutions or amorphous structures [6,7] .
HEAs have exceptional mechanical properties including hardness,
resistance to softening during annealing, high strength up to 800
◦
C,
superior plasticity, and outstanding wear resistance. These distinctive
characteristics make their crystal structures extremely interesting for
further investigation [5,8 – 13] .
HEAs can be manufactured in several forms, which include bulk, cast
ingots, thin films, slabs, and fine powders. Two primary techniques for
producing bulk HEAs include vacuum arc melting (VAR) in conjunction
with suction casting, MA followed by sintering [14 – 18] . The selection of
processing techniques has a significant influence on the creation of HEA

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials and methods
A planetary ball mill was used for mechanical alloying of elemental
powders. The mill had a chamber made of hardened chromium steel and
hardened high-carbon steel balls in three sizes: 20 mm, 15 mm, and
7 mm. The use of various ball sizes in the MA process optimizes the
milling conditions and improves the efficiency of powder processing
[33] . Different ball sizes affect energy transfer during milling, influ-
encing particle size reduction, alloying levels, and powder uniformity.
This approach results in an enhanced distribution of reinforcing phases
and improved mechanical properties in the composite materials,
allowing precise control of milling parameters to meet specific material
standards and processing goals [33] . Salemi et al. [34] employed a
similar strategy to increase energy transfer during ball milling,
enhancing conditions for HEA formation. Argon gas was used to prevent
oxidation, and a rubber gasket sealed the chamber. The ball-to-powder
weight ratio was 10:1, and the milling speed was 350 rpm. Additionally,
1.5 wt% stearic acid was added as a PCA to prevent particle agglomer-
ation. The HEA powder of CuFeNiMnTi was produced using the SPS
method, and a graphite mold with a graphite sheet was used. The con-
ditions for the SPS operation are provided in Table 1 .
In order to investigate the phase evaluation and study the crystal
structure of the alloy, X-ray diffractometry (XRD), was used with a PMD
Philips XPERT system, with Cu K α radiation ( λ = 0.1542 nm) and a time
per step of 0.01 s at 2 θ = 10 – 100. The crystallite size and lattice strain of
the HEAs were determined using the Williamson-Hall technique [35] .
The microstructural examination of sintered alloys was performed using
a Philips XL30 SEM equipped with EDS.
2.2. Investigation of mechanical properties
In order to assess the mechanical parameters of the consolidated
samples, Vickers microhardness and shear strength tests were per-
formed. Vickers microhardness tests, carried out on polished samples,
were measured using a Koopa MH3 microhardness tester with a load of
300 g and dwell time of 10 seconds. The microhardness value was
calculated by using an average of at least five measurements. To mea-
sure the yield and ultimate tensile strength, a shear punch test at a
crosshead speed of 0.2 mm/min and room temperature was performed.
The diameter of the punch and the die were 1.5 and 1.54 mm, respec-
tively. The thickness of the sample has been discussed, with the di-
mensions being 10 × 10 mm. The sample was firmly secured on the
washer by the holders of the device, ensuring no movement during the
test. After completion of the shear punch test, the sample was cut along
its thickness. The thickness range for shear punch tests is reported to be
between 400 and 600 micrometers [36,37] . The sample thickness is 460
± 20 micrometers. The experiments were performed in triplicate; the
obtained average values were then taken as the shear stress and
strength, with the tolerance calculated from the difference between this
average and the maximum and minimum values. A schematic of the
shear punch test is presented in Fig. 1 [37] .

3. Results and discussions
3.1. Synthesis by mechanical alloying
3.1.1. X-ray diffraction analysis
Fig. 2 illustrates XRD results of CuFeNiMnTi powder after different
milling times of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 80 h.
The XRD analysis of the as-mixed initial powders is presented in
Fig. 2 , showing the peaks for each element. When MA is conducted, a
solid solution of Cu and Ni can be produced after 10 hours of mechanical
milling. It seems that the FCC phase has become more stabilized with the
presence of Cu [48] , Ni [49] and Mn [49,50] . In addition to FCC phase,
peaks associated with Fe and Ti are still visible after 10 hours of MA.
FCC and BCC phases, which appear to be Fe-based BCC, can be observed
at 20 hours. From 20 – 30 hours of MA, the peaks of FCC and BCC appear
to be merged, and by 80 hours, only peaks associated with FCC phase are
visible. It appears a solid solution composed of multiple elements in FCC
phase is produced. Initially, this FCC phase was produced by the com-
bination of Cu, Ni, and Mn; however, other elements were subsequently
integrated. Fe probably can easily dissolve in Ni according to Fe-Ni bi-
nary phase diagram, and Ti also dissolves in the initially formed solid
solution due to the high entropy of the alloy and the non-equilibrium
nature of MA method [48] . The objective of extending MA to a
maximum of 80 hours was to determine the stability of the solid solu-
tion, which was confirmed to be stable and did not demonstrate the
formation of an amorphous phase. Based on various studies,
FCC-structured HEAs are the most widely used alloys due to their
exceptional fracture toughness and strong plasticity at cryogenic tem-
peratures [48,51] . The FCC phase structure exhibits enhanced plasticity
along with low strength due to its increased number of slip systems. On
the contrary, BCC/HCP/Sigma/Laves phase exhibits limited plasticity
and higher strength due to its reduced number of slip systems [52] . BCC
phases have the ability to coexist with other phases, such as carbides,
which can also help to boost the level of strength and hardness [53] . The
rightward shift of peaks, corresponding to larger 2 θ angles, indicates a
reduction in lattice parameters, likely due to strain within the crystal
lattice and the formation of solid solutions as elements dissolve into each
other. The peaks broadening suggests a simultaneous decrease in crys-
tallite size and an increase in lattice strain, which are typical outcomes
of high-energy MA [32,54,55] . These observations show the refinement
of the crystalline structure and the transition from a mixture of
elemental powders to a more homogeneous alloyed phase, typical of MA
process.
The high configurational entropy in HEAs reduces lattice strain and
improves stability by preventing the formation of intermetallic com-
pounds or secondary phases. Having high entropy of mixing could
enhance the random diffusion of elements, extend the solid solubility
and lead to supersaturation [21] . During MA, as expected, elements with
lower melting points dissolve faster, while brittle alloy components need
longer times [21,56,57] . However, in this study, the XRD peaks showed
FCC solid solution primarily linked to Ni, likely because it has the lowest
melting point (1728 K) among the powders [56] . This suggests that
factors other than melting point, such as crystalline structure, also play a
role in the dissolution of elements in copper. This has also been reported
in other studies [21,56 – 58] . Table 2 provides information on the crys-
talline characteristics of the selected metals. However, the addition of
larger atoms to the FCC structure, such as Ti and Mn, may distort the
lattice and increase the lattice parameter.

and wear resistance. MA, a non-equilibrium process, stores high energy

phases or segregation [58,63] . Alloys can store energy effectively due to
their fine-grained crystalline structure. On the other hand, this stored
enthalpy can increase the solubility limit of elements in each other and
lead to the formation of a supersaturated solid solution. After perform-

stored energy which is mentioned above. SPS can produce metastable
phases, with the stored enthalpy serving as a driving force for the for-
mation of new phases [21] .
The decomposition of solid solution FCC phases into multiple FCC
phases upon the addition of Cu may be attributed to the positive
enthalpy of mixing between Cu and various common elements [48,64] .
Segregation occurs after SPS, likely due to the positive enthalpy of
mixing, indicating that copper tends to form separate phases with other
elements. The high temperature and pressure conditions during sinter-
ing promote the diffusion of atoms, resulting in the observed segregation
phenomenon [48,65] . The presence of carbon from PCA, along with Ti
and C affinity, leads to the formation of titanium carbide [63,66] .

4.2. Evaluation of shear punch test
Although no standard technique exists for determining the yield
point, the Shear Punch Test (SPT) is a method that is employed to
determine the mechanical properties of materials, with a particular
emphasis on the shear yield strength (SYS) and ultimate shear strength
(USS) [36] . The measured USS of our optimized sample was 439
± 19 MPa, while for CoCuFeMnNi HEA [39], it was 320 ± 18 MPa. The
strength of our sample was higher due to the distribution of titanium
carbide particles inside the matrix and the increasing of the lattice
distortion compared to CoCuFeMnNi HEA. Moreover, the UTS and the
tensile yield stress were calculated to be 790 ± 34 and 594 ± 25 MPa,
respectively. Lattice distortion in HEAs, because of differences in atomic
radius, crystal structure, and bonding energy, enhances strength by
hindering dislocation motion, improving solid solution strengthening,
and influencing microstructural features like precipitates and grain
boundaries. This may result in increasing the strength and hardness, and
can affect the nucleation and growth, resulting in the formation of fine
and dispersed phases that further strengthen the alloy [70] .

oxidative and adhesive wear at low temperatures to abrasive wear and
delamination at higher temperatures. At lower temperatures, a thin
protective oxide layer forms, limiting metal-to-metal contact and lead-
ing to mild oxidative wear combined with adhesive wear. However, at
elevated temperatures, the formation of a thicker, denser oxide layer

wear and delamination as the dominant mechanisms.
5. Conclusions
This study presents a successful method for synthesis and charac-
terization of a HEA composed of CuFeNiMnTi. The alloy was created
through MA and SPS. XRD test confirmed the transition from mixed BCC
and FCC phases to a single FCC phase with increased milling time. SEM
and EDS analyses showed efficient alloying, particle size reduction, and
uniform element distribution. The important findings are as follows:
• Phase Development: After SPS at 920
◦
C, the alloy exhibited two
distinct FCC phases and the emergence of titanium carbide.
• Mechanical Properties: Mechanical assessments revealed significant
improvements, including a hardness of 546 ± 14 HVN, microhard-
ness of 563 ± 22 HVN, ultimate tensile strength of 790 ± 34 MPa,
and tensile yield strength of 594 ± 25 MPa.
• Wear Performance: The formation of protective oxide layer at
elevated temperatures contributed to the stabilization of the friction
coefficient, demonstrating a better wear behavior compared with the
low temperature one.
These findings highlight the potential of CuFeNiMnTi HEA for
advanced engineering applications, thanks to its structural stability,
excellent mechanical properties, and favorable wear resistance. In
addition, the effectiveness of MA and SPS methods in producing high-
performance HEAs has been proven.
