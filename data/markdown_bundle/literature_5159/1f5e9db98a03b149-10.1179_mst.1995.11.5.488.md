Solid state gaseous nitrogenising was used to produce a wider range of nitrogen alloyed materials in a 10%Cr martensitic alloy.
It was possible to achieve maximum nitrogen levels of 0·45 wt-%. A high solution treatment temperature was required to obtain a
single phase martensitic structure. The effect of nitrogen content and heat treatment on microstructure in a variety of conditions
was studied using transmission electron microscopy and X-ray techniques. CrN, VN, and Cr2N phases occurred during
tempering ofmartensitic alloys in the range 400-750°C. High strength material is produced by introducing nitrogen; strength
and hardness increased linearly with increasing nitrogen content and superior properties were observed on tempering. The
presence of precipitation caused pronounced secondary hardening, and the increase and subsequent sharp decrease in strength
with increasing temperature were assumed to be the effect of coherent precipitation and loss of coherency, respectively. The
nitrogen alloyed martensitic structure isfiner and more uniform than carbon alloyed material, giving a very fine precipitate
distribution. Further increasing the nitrogen content of these martensitic alloys will be limited by the effects of brittleness and
retained austenite.· MST /2071

Introduction
Nitrogen as an interstitial alloying element is well known
for its beneficiai effects in steels. Phase stability, a high
strengthening effect, and corrosion resistance have made
nitrogen an attractive candidate for high temperature and
corrosion resistant applications.
Nitrogen alloyed austenitic stainless steels have been
extensively studied 1,2.and improved mechanical and cor-
rosion properties have been reported. Nitrogen martensite
has been studied in. nitrided alloys,3-5 and recently several
authors have investigated nitrogen alloyed martensitic
stainless steels.6-8 Whereas carbon forms many precipitate
carbides and carbonitrides, the addition of nitrogen is
reported to delay carbide precipitation, and in high nitrogen
steels results in the disappearance Qf carbide phases. In
austenitic strainless steels very high strengths can be
obtained by additional cold work;9,10when nitrogen is in
solid solution, however, such an enhancement in martensitic
steels is restricted by low ductility, which is due to
selftrapping of dislocations. This behaviour is the result of
tetragonality in nitrogen alloyed martensitic steels and
must be overcome before engineering application. Carbon,
in creating similar structures, has additional disadvan-
tages, being very unstable on tempering and forming
large particles. However, in martensitic· stainless steels
nitrogen addition results in a finer and more uniform
structure causing retention of properties on tempering,
and maintaining the advantages discussed above.

Solution treatment
Microhardness measurements on specimens cooled from a
nitrogenising temperature of 1000°C showed that there
was a gradient from case to core, indicating that nitrogen
was not uniformly distributed. However, this gradient was
absent in hardness results taken from specimens solution
treated at 1200°C. These results show a uniform nitrogen
distribution and this uniformity was confirmed independ-
ently using X-ray diffraction measurement. Figure 12 shows
the results of hardness v. nitrogen content for alloys
solution treated at 1200 and 10S0°C. The hardness values
are higher for material solution treated at 1200°C than that
treated at 10S0°C, as the concentration of nitrogen in solid
solution is greater at the higher temperature. The results
of the tensile tests carried out at room temperature on
martensitic alloys are shown in Fig. 13, and reveal a linear
dependence on nitrogen content.
As the addition of nitrogen prevented grain growth and
originated a finer structure, some dependence of strength
on grain size is expected. In fact, a Hall-Petch type plot
has been found for several medium alloy steels of different
austenitic grain size tested in the martensitic condition.35
However, when the fine structure of martensite is examined,
other possible grain sizes much finer than the austenite
grain size can be considered to contribute to the strength.
These are the packet size, the lath substructure, and also
microtwins which form in high nitrogen steels. Although
many of these boundaries are actually low angle sub-
boundaries, they do present obstacles to dislocation
movement. A model is presented based on the Hall-Petch
equation for the present nitrogen alloys.36Linear regression

Discussion
Experiment shows that it is possible to obtain a variety of
microstructures in nitrogen alloyed martensite, including
newly formed martensite, strengthened martensite aged at
500oe, and martensite overaged at 750oe. The heat
treatment and microstructural effects on the tensile and
fatigue strength of these materials are demonstrated above
and show the beneficial effect of optimised heat treatment
processes. Straining martensitic materials above the yield
strength resulted in a stress bearing capacity proportional
to the nitrogen level of the material. It is also shown above
that both as quenched and secondary hardened martensites
possess complex microstructures, and the microstructural
parameters controlling the mechanical properties of these
alloys are discussed. A simplified model of the nitrogen
atom/dislocation interaction is discussed further below.
It is of interest that for alloys tempered at 5000e better
fatigue resistance than for the original untempered marten-
site is observed. Material tempered at 5000e contains high
strain energy in the lattice, as a combined effect of
precipitation and nitrogen in solution, and givesa secondary
hardening effect. It was expected that the occurrence of
secondary hardening would result in a brittle material and
poor fatigue resistance. From TEM observations, the
difference between the two microstructures appears to be
insignificant. The high strain in as quenched material arises
froni y -+ a transformation which includes two stages:
atomic displacement and lattice displacement, in other
words the internal stress in untempered martensite arises
from atomic misarrangement (point defects) and dislocation
misarrangement (line defects). Strain arising from solid
solution effects should be added to these. In tempered
martensite the situation is almost entirely changed. In the
Nitrogen alloyed martensitic steel is amenable to a wide
range of heat treatment cycles.These alloys make it possible
to process the required microstructure easily by control
of alloy chemistry, being fully austenitic above about
8500e (encouraged by nitrogen alloying) and having a
suitable martensite start temperature, at about 200-280oe(depending on nitrogen concentration). Vanadium, com-
pared to strong ferrite formers, results in very fine nitride
precipitates that have a high resistance to overaging,
resulting in strong secondary hardening.
When all the nitrogen is in solid solution, the lattice
parameter increases linearly with increasing nitrogen con-
tent. Tempering this material at temperatures where alloying
elements can diffuse substantially results in nitrogen leaving
the lattice and fine precipitate formation. At temperatures
up to 5000e it is possible to harness the secondary
hardening effect, which is brought about by solid solution
strengthening and coherent precipitation. At higher temper-
atures the coherency between lattice and second pha~e is
lost and dispersed particles are formed.
The strengthening mechanism is shown to be controlled
by microstructural parameters. In as quenched material
nitrogen in solid solution results in tetragonality and high
strength. For martensite tempered at 7500e the strength is
controlled by the interaction of dispersed particles and
moving dislocations. In the secondary hardened instance
(at 5000e), although the lattice expansion is reduced,
preprecipitation effects and/or coherent precipitation
together with nitrogen in solution contributes to high
strength and good fatigue resistance.
It is important to note that nitrogen in solid solution is
the main factor in tempering enhancement, whereas removal
of nitrogen from the lattice and redistribution as precipitates
(or by inadequate solution treatment temperature) will
exclude this improvement.
