ABSTRACT
With the development of aerospace technologies, there is an urgent demand for materials with better high- 
temperature performance. Despite the bottleneck of combining high-temperature strength and room- 
temperature deformability, refractory high-entropy alloys are potential candidates. Here, we report a novel 
alloy, TiNbTaWC
0.7
, with a good strength-ductility synergy by introducing the eutectic carbide phase. The alloy 
is composed of alternated BCC matrix phase and FCC carbide phase, forming a fully eutectic microstructure. With 
the introduction of the eutectic carbides, the alloy is robust at 1200 
◦
C. The faceted phase interface together with 
the compositional complex carbides provides the alloy with good deformation ability at room temperature. The 
former promotes dislocation nucleation and transmission, and the latter generates stacking faults by partial 
dislocation movement. This strategy provides a new route to design promising high-temperature structural 
materials by combining the advantages of eutectic alloy, carbide strengthening, and refractory alloys utilizing the 
high entropy concept.
1. Introduction
The aerospace industry demands new high-temperature structural 
materials with better performance [ 1 – 3 ], which should meet the re -
quirements of have higher melting temperatures, stronger performance 
at high temperatures, better resistance to softening with increasing 
temperatures, and good malleability [ 4 – 6 ]. The appearance of re -
fractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) provides a potential choice. They 
may exhibit exceptionally good mechanical properties under the effect 
of high entropy, sluggish diffusion, cocktail, and lattice distortion 
[ 7 – 13 ]. Senkov et al. [ 12 ] reported the well-known NbMoTaW and 
NbMoTaWV alloy, whose yield strengths are beyond 400 MPa at 
1600 
◦
C. Wan et al. [ 13 ] designed the WReTaMo alloy which has a 
compressive strength of over 200 MPa at 1600 
◦
C. However, those 
RHEAs with good mechanical properties at high temperatures usually 
possess insufficient deformation ability and malleability at room tem -
perature [ 12 , 13 ], which severely restricts their application. Besides, 
high density is also one of the downsides of them.
The ultra-high mechanical properties of RHEAs at high temperatures 
arise from severer lattice distortion and stronger solid-solution 
strengthening than conventional alloys which quickly softens at higher 
temperatures [ 14 , 15 ]. Though owning better soften resistance, RHEAs 
composed of a single solid solution encounter with strength-ductility 
trade-off easily, making them hard to work under practical conditions 
[ 12 , 16 , 17 ]. Thus, there is a need to combine RHEAs with other 
strengthening mechanisms to obtain high-temperature strength, 
room-temperature deformation ability and low density at the same time 
[ 18 – 22 ]. The laser-aided preparation technique is a promising way due 
to the rapid cooling rate and high energy input, which will improve the 
mechanical performance of the RHEAs under grain refining effect. Zhao 
et al. [ 23 ] prepared WTaNbMo alloy by laser cladding under ultrasonic 
vibration and found that the microhardness of the alloy increased from

0.5

alloy fabricated by laser cladding. The average microhardness of the 
alloys increased with the increase in Al content. However, those RHEAs

3. Results
3.1. Alloy design and phase identification
To obtain a fully eutectic microstructure in which each phase plays 
an important role, the components of the alloy should be carefully 
considered. Firstly, the eutectic alloys own several advantages compared 
with alloys with single phase or non-eutectic strengthening phases, such 
as a high volume fraction of strengthening phase, strong interface 
bonding, a large number of interfaces at different scales which are able 
to tolerate the moving dislocations and thus improve the deformation 
ability; secondly, the carbides which are hard and strong even at high 
temperatures were chosen to be the strengthening phase in this study as 
many of the refractory elements are strong carbide-forming elements, 
such as Ti(as shown in Table 1 ); thirdly, the matrix of the eutectic alloys 
is supposed to provide sufficient deformation ability, thus the Nb and Ta 
elements were chosen as they are reported to be helpful for room- 
temperature ductility; also, the strengths of the carbide phase and the 
matrix phase should be comparable and W was chosen which is reported 
to be helpful to improve the high-temperature strengths of the RHEAs.

eutectic point roughly and Fig. 1 shows the results. Fig. 1 (b) and (d) 
were calculated under equilibrium conditions while Fig. 1 (c) is under

calculated according to the eutectic point in Fig. 1 (b). The calculated

and HfC shrinks after expanding the deficiency inside gets more, while 
that of NbC and TaC shrinks monotonically [ 38 ]. In this study, the 
composition of the carbides is complex, which will be discussed later, 
thus leading to a complicated trend.

4. Discussion
4.1. Phase formation
The as-cast TiNbTaWC
0.7 
alloy has a eutectic microstructure 
featuring alternating lamellae of BCC and FCC carbide phases inside. 
The BCC phase is enriched with Nb, Ta and W elements while Ti is the 
dominant element in the carbide phase. Among them, W and Ti have the 
most distribution discrepancy, arising from the least and most negative 
mixing enthalpy with C. The metal elements in the matrix that have the 
same BCC structure form a solid solution and solidify as a descending 
melting point sequence. W with the highest melting points becomes the 
most in the matrix. According to Biesuz et al. [ 38 ], when the carbon 
content is low, carbon bonds to elements with the most negative Gibbs 
free energy. When the carbon content is high enough that no metal 
phase would be formed in the system, carbon bonds to elements to form 
carbides with the highest melting points. In this study, the carbon con -
tent is relatively low and thus carbides with the most negative Gibbs free 
energy would come out of the melt first, which is TiC among the carbides 
of Ti, Nb, Ta and W. With more and more TiC solidified, the melt be -
comes rich in Nb, Ta and W and carbon deficient. Then Nb and Ta with 
secondary affinity bond with carbon. TiC, NbC, and TaC are all of Fm 3 m 
structure, thus the carbide phase in this alloy is also a compositional 
complex solid solution. The solidification sequence of the eutectic alloy 
is followed by: L → L + (BCC + FCC)

4.3. Strengthening and deformation mechanisms
By forming a eutectic microstructure composed of alternating 
lamellar BCC solid solution phase and FCC carbide phase, the as-cast 
TiNbTaWC
0.7 
alloy shows a good combination of strength and defor -
mation ability, possessing a relatively low density at the same time. 
From the results in the Supplementary Material, the W element 
contributed the most to the solid solution strengthening of the matrix. 
However, the eutectic alloy shows a surprising combination of strength 
and deformation ability, which must be dominated by other mechanisms 
together. To better figure out the origination, the post-deformation 
microstructure of the matrix, the carbides, and the phase interface be -
tween them need further analysis. The EBSD results of the TiNbTaWC
0.7 
alloy after compression at room temperature shows that the phase 
interface contains higher dislocation density than the interior of the BCC 
phase and FCC carbide phase. This result is in accordance with Xiong 
et al. [ 56 ]. When the load is applied to the eutectic alloy, stress con -
centration would happen at the phase interface, and a high density of 
dislocations nucleate at this position. The hindering of dislocation offers 
help in strengthening the eutectic alloy in this study. The TEM result in 
Fig. 11 (b) and (c) further confirmed it. The high density of dislocations 
and dislocation pile-up could be clearly observed at the phase interface 
between the BCC matrix phase and FCC carbide phase, indicating a high 
stress level at the phase interface. As the stress increases, dislocations 
propagate preferentially in the BCC phase which is “ softer ” and easier to 
deform than the carbide phase. Then the plastic deformation of the BCC 
phase starts. Recent studies [ 57 , 58 ] reveal that the edge dislocations 
dominate the high-temperature plastic deformation process in some 
RHEAs. Their experimental results indicated that as the temperature 
increased, the slip pattern changed from screw dislocation to edge 
dislocation. At 800 
◦
C, it is hard to find screw dislocations under TEM 
observation. They successfully used the edge dislocation model to pre -
dict the strengths at different temperatures. However, in many RHEAs, 
the deformation and strengthening are reported to be controlled by the 
screw dislocations [ 18 , 59 , 60 ]. Because of the complex core structure 
which results in an intrinsic high Peirls stress (lattice friction), the mo -
tion of screw dislocations is severely restricted compared to the edge 
ones, which is evidenced by the jogged dislocations and the presence of 
debris in the form of small loops and dipoles. Thus, whether screw of 
edge dislocations dominates the strengthening in RHEAs still remains to 
be explored. In this study, multiplanar dislocation slip is identified in 
Fig. 11 (a) where the {110} and {112} slip planes are activated. What ’ s 
more, dislocations with wavy structure could also be seen which in -
dicates a cross-slip behavior owing to the non-planar core structure 
which is mentioned above.
The improved room-temperature deformability is also related to the 
special faceted phase interface and the carbide itself. As stated in 4.2, the 
faceted phase interface is proved hard to shear and glide during the 
deformation process, which delays the failure of the alloys. In addition, 
the well-bonded phase interface is beneficial for the dislocation trans -
mission to both sides, further avoiding the possible stress concentration.

5. Conclusions
In this study, we have successfully developed a novel refractory high- 
entropy alloy reinforced with eutectic carbides. The alloy is composed of 
an alternated BCC matrix phase and FCC carbide phase, forming a fine 
eutectic microstructure. The apparent flat phase interface is faceted on 
an atomistic scale, which is beneficial to dislocation nucleation and slip 
transmission. The alloy is strong at 1200 
◦
C yet owns good room- 
temperature deformation ability. The high strength of the alloy is 
mainly related to the introduction of the carbides. At the same time, the 
alloy shows a certain deformation ability at room temperature, attrib -
uting to the faceted stable phase interface and firstly reported defor -
mation stacking faults in the carbides. In addition, the alloy also has 
advantages on density, containing Ti and C. Our study provides a new 
route to design promising high-temperature structural materials by
