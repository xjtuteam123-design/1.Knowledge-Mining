Analysis of oxidation behavior of Ni-base superalloys by laser-induced 
breakdown spectroscopy 
Tae-Hyeong Kim,a Dong-Hyoung Lee,a Donghoon Kim,a Changheui Jang,a and Jong-Il Yun*a 
Received (in XXX, XXX) Xth XXXXXXXXX 20XX, Accepted Xth XXXXXXXXX 20XX 
DOI: 10.1039/b000000x 5 
The application of laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) to elemental depth profile analysis of 
high temperature oxidation behavior of Ni-based superalloys is presented. Oxidation test of two Ni-base 
superalloys, Alloy 617 and Haynes 230, to be considered as the most promising candidates for very high 
temperature reactor (VHTR) was carried out in dry air at 900 oC. Duplex external oxide layer of MnCr2O4 
and Cr2O3 with internal Al2O3 oxides was mainly formed in both alloys. In addition, in Alloy 617, Ni and 10 
Ti enriched oxides were observed at the surface unlike Haynes 230, and Alloy 617 was more susceptible 
to intergranular oxidation. Generally, the oxidation of Alloy 617 was more extensive than that of Haynes 
230, i.e. Alloy 617 formed a thicker oxide layer of ~8 m compared to Haynes 230 with ~5 m oxide 
layer in thickness after 1000 h. Depth profiles obtained by LIBS are found to be in broad agreement with 
those obtained by established techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy 15 
coupled to energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (SEM-EDS), and secondary ion mass spectrometry 
(SIMS). 
Introduction 
The next generation nuclear energy systems known as Generation 
IV (Gen IV) reactor systems are intended to achieve advances in 20 
sustainability, economics, safety, and proliferation resistance. 1 
Very high temperature reactor (VHTR), one of the Gen IV 
candidates, is a graphite -moderated, helium -cooled reactor with 
thermal neutron spectrum. The outl et temperature of ≥900 oC 
enables highly efficient electric power generation and hydrogen 25 
production by high temperature electrolysis or thermochemical 
iodine-sulfur process. In addition, s uch a high core outlet 
temperature can also offer a broad range of process heat 
applications.1 A coolant helium gas is chemically inert and 
remains single phase under all conditions, so that it will generally 30 
not react with materials. In addition, exposing helium to neutron 
radiation does not make it radioactive, unlike m ost other possible 
coolants.2 However, the coolant helium gas contains very small 
amounts of reactive gases such as CO, CO 2, H 2, CH 4, and H 2O 
that can interact with metallic materials at high temperatures.3-6 35 
Two Ni-based superalloys, Alloy 617 and Haynes 230, are now 
promising candidate materials for hot gas ducts and intermediate 
heat exchangers in VHTR due to its high creep strength and 
oxidation resistance. Both of them are solid -solution and carbide-
strengthened alloys, based on Ni -Cr-Co-Mo and Ni -Cr-W, 40 
respectively.7 Therefore, t he oxidation behavior of Ni -based 
superalloys including Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 has been 
intensively investigated in air and helium environments.8-14 
As a part of the attempt to understand the oxidation behavior, 
laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) was applied to 45 
investigate the elemental depth profile of these alloys. LIBS is 
one of the effective techniques allowing for the performance of 
elemental depth profiling .15-19 Unlike other conventional surface 
analysis techni ques which need the cumbersome sample 
preparation, LIBS requires no or little sample preparation. In 50 
addition, LIBS measurements can be performed under all ambient 
conditions, high vacuum or argon atmosphe re. The most 
attractive appeal of LIBS lies in the ability to carry out in-situ 
analysis through fiber optic delivery even under extreme 
conditions such as high pressures, temperatures, and radioactive 55 
environments. In this sense, LIBS has been introduced under 
various harsh conditions.20-23 
In the present work, LIBS was conducted to investigate the 
feasibility for in-situ analysis of mechanistic high temperature 
oxidation of Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 in air at 900 oC. Other 60 
surface analytical methods such as X -ray diffraction (XRD), 
scanning electron microscopy coupled to energy dispersive X -ray 
spectrometry (SEM-EDS), and secondary ion mass spectrometry 
(SIMS) were employed to verify the results from LIBS 
measurements. Finally, the contribution of minor alloying 65 
elements on the oxidation resistan ce of Alloy 617 and Haynes 
230 has been examined in detail.

Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy 
The nanosecond laser pulse was produced by a Q -switched 10 
Nd:YAG laser (Brilli ant Co., Brio, wavelength 532 nm, pulse 
width 6 ns, repetition rate 10 Hz). The laser pulse energy used for 
the measurement was 10 mJ/pulse. The laser pulse was focused 
onto the sample surface using a fused silica plano -convex lens 
with 76.6 mm focal length. Since the depth resolution of LIBS 15 
depth profiling is dependent on the irradiance of laser pulse24,25, 
the lens-to-sample distance was optimized to 5 mm shorter than 
the focal length of the lens to achieve the highest depth resolution 
in depth profiling analysis . The definition of depth resolution of 
LIBS depth profiling and details are described in details in the 20 
previous study.25 The plasma emission was collimated and 
focused by the plano -convex lens with 50 mm foca l length onto 
the entrance of fiber optic at the angle of 45 o relative to the laser 
beam direction. The emission was delivered through a 
customized optic fiber to a Czerny -Turner spectrometer (Andor 25 
Technology, Shamrock SR -303i) and dispersed by a grating 
(1200 grooves/mm, 500 nm blaze) onto an intensified charge -
coupled device (Andor Technology, iStar). The entrance slit 
width of the spectrometer was set at the minimum width of 10 m. 
The delay time and the gate width were optimized for a signal -to-30 
background ratio to 1.4 s and 3 s, respectively. A set of five 
replicate measurements was carried out for each sample. A linear 
translation stage was used to move the specimen to ensure that a 
fresh surface is always available. Fig. 1 shows the representative 
LIBS spectra of oxidized Alloy 617 in the wavelength range of 35 
interest. The variation in the intensity of atomic emission lines as 
a function of laser pulse illustrates a qualitative trend of the 
elemental concentration which remains nearly constant in the 
bulk matrix region. 
Thin film X-ray diffraction 40 
The thin film X -ray diffraction was performed by D/MAX -RC. 
The glancing angle of 2 o was applied to reduce the penetration 
depth less than a few micrometers. T he diffraction angles (2 ) 
varied in the range of 20 -80o. Tube current and voltage applied 
were 30 kV and 60 mA, respectively. MDI JADE 7 software 45 
program was used as crystallographic database to obtain 
information about the pattern of specimen. 
Scanning electron microscopy coupled to energy dispersive X-
ray spectrometry 
The SEM-EDS measurement was conducted to analyse the oxide 50 
scale by Magellan 400. Imaging was recorded in the back -
scattered electron (BSE) image mode at accelerating voltage of 
20 kV . Since BSE image contrast is proportional to the atomic 
number, BSE image can provide the distribution of different 
element in the sample. F or all EDS measurement s, high 55 
accelerating voltage of 20 kV was applied to ensure peaks to be 
recorded and the interaction volume is around 1 m3. 
Secondary ion mass spectrometry 
The SIMS depth profiling was made on a CMECA IMS 7f for the 
purpose of comparing it with the LIBS result. O- ion was used as 60 
the primary ion. For the elimination of crater edge effect, the 
primary ion beam was focused to several beam diameters to 
produce a flat-bottomed crater. The analysis area was 30×30 m. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS 
The thin film XRD analysis was performed to identify the 65 
molecular structure of oxide scales grown on the specimen. Fig. 2 
shows the XRD pattern of Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 oxidized in 
air at 900 oC for 1000 h. Two main groups of peaks were 
exclusively detected for both alloys. One group of peaks was 
clearly identified as Cr2O3. The other group of peaks was a mixed 70 
oxide phase of Ni, Cr, and Mn. V arious possible combinations 
are fitted well, but it is presumably concluded to be a MnCr 2O4 
phase because the peak ratio of MnCr 2O4 to Cr2O3 for Alloy 617 
was relatively smaller than that for Haynes 230 (see Table 1). The 
small peaks corresponding to the metallic matrix were detected 75 
only in Haynes 230, indicating that the oxide layer thickness of 
Haynes 230 is thinner than that of Alloy 617. 
Cross-sectional SEM -EDS observations of two Ni -base 
superalloys oxidized in air at 900 oC for 1000 h are shown in Fig. 
3 and Fig. 4. As can be seen, the oxide layer thickness of Alloy 80 
617 and Haynes 230 was around 8 m and 5 m, respectively. As 
indicated by the XRD measurement, the oxide layer of Alloy 617 
was thicker than that of Haynes 230. The major element in the 
continuous external oxide layer was Cr in both alloys. As 
confirmed by XRD measurements, the Cr -rich external oxide 85 
layer was identified as Cr 2O3. At the oxide/metal interface, semi -
continuous internal Al oxides were confirmed in both alloys. In 
Alloy 617, Al internal oxides grew internally deep into the metal 
matrix along the grain boundary, resulting in the intergranular 
oxidation. Al-rich internal oxides were thought to be Al2O3.12 The 90 
lowest negative value of Gibbs free energy26 for the formation of 
oxides among alloying elements and the low concentration of 
aluminum enable the preferential internal oxidation of Al. The 
elemental distribution obtained by the EDS measurement 
indicates that major alloying elements such as Mo, Co, and W 95 
hardly contribute to the oxidation in spite of their high 
concentrations. Christ reported no significant effect of Mo and Co 
on the oxidation as well.10 
Neglecting minor elements of Mo, Co, and W, Alloy 617 and 
Haynes 230 may be then simplified as a simple ternary system of 100 
Ni-Cr-Al alloy. Three oxidation mechanisms of Ni -Cr-Al alloys 
at temperatures between 1000 oC and 1200 oC have been 
studied.27 Both Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 can be classified as 
Group II alloy in terms of i ts Cr and Al contents. Group II alloys 
contain sufficiently high content of Cr to form a continuous 105 
external oxide layer of Cr 2O3. Contrarily, Al tends to oxidize 
internally forming Al2O3 due to its low content. According to the 
phase diagram,28 the low Al contents of 1.1 wt.% and 0.38 wt.% 
in Alloy 617 and Haynes 230, respectively, are not sufficient to 
form an external oxide layer. Meanwhile, minor alloying 110 
elements Mn and Ti in Alloy 617 were not well detected by EDS 
due to their low concentrations. A ve ry weak enrichment of Mn

The comparisons between SIMS and LIBS depth profiles for 5 
Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 oxidized in air at 900 oC were 
presented in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6. Mn I 403.31 nm, Cr I 540.98 nm, 
Ni I 547.69 nm, and Al I 396 .15 nm were employed for the 
purpose of the LIBS analysis. T he SIMS measurement was 
carried out in order to confirm a soundness of the LIBS analysis 10 
in combination with the XRD and SEM -EDS. SIMS is the most 
sensitive surface analysis technique, being able to detect elements 
present in parts per billion ranges. Because of the difficulty in 
obtaining suitable standards for the quantitative sample analysis, 
a non-oxidized alloy was taken as a reference for converting the 15 
characteristic spectral line intensitie s of specific element to its 
elemental concentration. F or one measurement of LIBS, it took 
only 20 seconds with a pulse repetition rate of 10 Hz whereas 
around 12,000 seconds were needed for the SIMS measurement. 
Both SIMS and LIBS profiles are in good agr eement in terms of 20 
elemental trend and pattern with increasing internal depth from 
the surface. Local variation in the thickness and the composition 
of alloys leads to the non -sharp profiles but there is a clear 
stratification of the oxide layer. The enric hment of Mn was 
clearly observed in the outermost part of the oxide layer by both 25 
methods even in Alloy 617 in contradiction to the SEM -EDS 
measurement. A pronounced shoulder corresponding to the Cr -
rich sub -layer located at the oxide/metal interface was a lso 
observed by both methods in Alloy 617. Depth profiles of Ni 
showed the countertrend against Cr depth profiles. The Ni profile 30 
of Alloy 617 obtained by LIBS exhibited the enrichment of Ni 
oxide near the surface. A detailed description of the enrichment 
of Ni will be addressed in the later part of this paper. The peak 
position of Al was mainly located at the oxide/metal interface. In 
the LIBS depth profiles, the peak position of each Al 35 
compositional profiles from Alloy 617 was different from each 
other, whereas that obtained from Haynes 230 represented a fairly 
consistent tendency. 
For the understanding of the temporal oxidation behavior of 
Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 at high temperatures, the LIBS 40 
analysis of Cr, Al, Mn and Ti (only in Alloy 617) was conduc ted 
with varying oxidation time (0, 25, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 h), as 
shown in Fig. 7 and Fig. 8. Additionally, the emission line of Ti 
at 551.4 5 nm was observed in LIBS depth profiling. The LIBS 
measurements successfully provided the elemental distribution of 45 
samples according to the oxidation time. By LIBS, in particular, 
the clear change in the temporal behavior of Mn and Ti was 
observed. The outermost part of the oxide layer was rich in Mn 
and Ti in Alloy 617. The growth of the Mn -rich oxide layer was 
limited in Alloy 617 after 500 h oxidation, while it grew up 50 
continually in Haynes 230, depending upon low Mn content of 
0.05 wt.% in Alloy 617 and relatively high Mn content of 0.45 
wt.% in Haynes 230. At th e point, the concentration of Ti in 
Cr2O3 layer increases with oxidation time unlike Mn. The same 
characteristic variation between Mn and Ti profiles was found in 55 
other studies as well.29-31 
In the case of the Cr -Mn-Ti ternary system, 32 a part of Cr can be 
replaced by Mn 3+ at high oxygen partial pressures and by Ti 3+ at 
low pressures. The formation of continuous external oxide layer 
reduces the oxygen partial pressure inwardly from surface to 60 
interior. This, in turn, enables the Ti dissolution in the Cr oxi de 
layer. In this manner, the selective oxidation of Al can also take 
place at the oxide/metal interface. 
Considering both Cr and Al depth profiles, Cr oxides cover the 
existing Al oxides with increasing oxidation time. The 65 
mechanism of the outward growth of chromium oxide layer on 
the surface is the outward diffusion of Cr ions from the alloy to 
the surface and the inward diffusion of oxygen ions as anionic 
vacancies at a much lower rate. This transportation of chromium 
and oxygen can lead to the outward growth of Cr 2O3 layer, 70 
resulting in the back movement of maximum Al peak position in 
the profile. The rise of Al concentration after the peak position 
has been observed especially after 500 h oxidation time in Alloy 
617, indicative of the d evelopment of intergranular oxidation 
along the grain boundary. On the contrary to that, this feature 75 
could not be found in Haynes 230. 
The LIBS measurement gives the temporal trend to manifest 
which elements play a central role, especially for the minor 
alloying element like Mn and Ti. The development of the Al 
intergranular oxidation and the dissolution of Ti in the Cr oxide 80 
layer have been verified by LIBS. These features appeared after 
stopping the growth of the Mn -rich oxide layer in Alloy 617. In 
this respect, Mn is suggested as a key element which controls the 
oxidation behavior of the alloys although its concentration is 
much lower than other elements. 85 
The apparently more rapid diffusion coefficient of Mn as strong 
spinel former in Cr2O3 layer can lead to the preferential formation 
of MnCr 2O4 on the top of Cr 2O3. According to a study on the 
diffusivity of cations such as Ni, Cr, Fe, and Mn in Cr 2O3 layer,33 
the diffusion coefficient of Mn is two orders of magnitude higher 90 
than that of other cations, w hereas the diffusivity of Ni, Cr, and 
Fe appear in the same order of magnitude. 
The beneficial effect of the formation of MnCr 2O4 on the 
oxidation resistance is related to the reduction of Cr evaporation. 
The maximum evaporation rate of Cr from MnCr 2O4 is two 95 
orders of magnitude lower than that from Cr 2O3.34 Therefore, the 
formation of MnCr 2O4 layer on the top of the Cr 2O3 layer can 
reduce the Cr evaporation, which in turn leads to a lower 
oxidation rate. Due to the limited Mn content, Alloy 617 was not 
fully covered with the protective MnCr 2O4 layer whereas the 100 
MnCr2O4 layer was homogeneously distributed over the surface 
in Haynes 230. A lack of formation of the protective MnCr 2O4 
layer in Alloy 617 may give rise to the intensified oxidation in 
different parts of the surface. This, in turn, makes a difference in 
the oxidation resistance of the alloys. 105 
Fig. 9 illustrates the SEM-EDS surface analysis of Alloy 617 and 
Haynes 230. Differences in the surface morphology in Alloy 617 
were observed as expected. The predominant outer oxide layer 
was composed of Cr and Mn over the surface but some localized 
areas with large crystallites were highly enriched with Ni. On the 110 
other hand, Cr and Mn oxides were homogeneously covered over 
the surface in Haynes 230. As sh own in Fig. 9 (a), the EDS 
mapping analysis reveals that the nodule oxide observed in Alloy 
617 was enriched with Ti. These Ti -rich nodule oxides were 
already reported in the previous study.35 Furthermore, small 115 
spallation region was observed near the Ti r ich nodule oxide.

Based on the SEM observation and overlapping XRD peak 
positions of the formable oxides (MnCr 2O4, NiCr 2O4, and TiO 2), 
the XRD intensity of MnCr 2O4 can be identified as possible 
congruent formation of MnCr 2O4 and small fraction of NiCr 2O4 
and TiO2. 5 
The difference in the surface morphology is closely associated 
with the internal oxidation of aluminum. The well -developed 
MnCr2O4 with the homogeneous morphology is believed to 
prevent the diffusion of oxygen, whereas the formation of mixed 
oxides with the irregular morphology is thought to be less 10 
protective and the active diffusion process lead s to the internal 
oxidation.12 There is a rough relation between ionic radius and its 
diffusivity. Small Mn ions diffuse through the lattice of Cr 2O3 
and then form MnCr 2O4 on the top of Cr 2O3 over the surface. 
Instead of that, large Ti ions prefer to find short -circuit paths 15 
along the grain boundar y. Ti ions diffuse through the grain 
boundary and finally form Ti -rich nodule oxides. Within the Ti -
rich nodule oxide, the spallation region is developed. More rapid 
diffusion of oxygen through this spalled region induces the 
development of intergranular oxides, creating vicious cycle. The 20 
detrimental effect of Ti on the intergranular attack is in good 
agreement with the result of Incoloy 825 which is the chromia 
forming alloy as well.36,37 
Conclusions 
In the present work, the oxidation characteristics of Ni -base 25 
superalloys, Alloy 617 and Haynes 230 which are oxidized in air 
at 900 oC were investigated by LIBS. The established surface 
techniques such as XRD, SEM -EDS, and SIMS were also 
performed to analyze the formed oxide surface layers for the 
purposes of comparison. Although the concentration of Mn and 30 
Ti is one order magnitude lowe r than other alloying elements, 
these reactive cations provide great effects on the oxidation 
resistance. The enrichment of Mn and Ti in alloys was 
successfully identified by LIBS, but it was not detected by SEM -
EDS due to the low concentration for its sen sitivity. On temporal 35 
behavior of Mn revealed by LIBS, Mn was closely related to the 
oxidation behavior of those Ni -based alloys and served as a 
controlling element of oxidation. The present comparative study 
demonstrates that LIBS is a promising and power ful analytical 
tool for studying the oxidation mechanism of metallic materials at 40 
high temperatures.
