Effect of temperature and cooling regime on mechanical properties
of high-strength low-alloy steel
Esam M. Aziz 1,2 and Venkatesh K. Kodur 1,*,†
1Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, Michigan State University, East Lansing, MI, USA

SUMMARY
This paper presents results from experimental studies on the effect of temperature on mechanical properties
of high-strength low-alloy ASTM A572 steel commonly used in structural members in bridges. A set of
high-temperature tensile strength tests and post-temperature exposed residual strength tests is carried out
on ASTM A572 steel coupons in 20 –1000 °C temperature range. The residual strength tests on high-
temperature exposed steel coupons are carried out after subjecting the coupons to two methods of cooling,
namely, air cooling and water quenching. Results from these tests indicate that temperature-dependent
strength and stiffness degradation in A572 steel follow the same trend as that of carbon steel but with some
variations. A572 steel recovers almost 100% of its room temperature yield strength when heated to temper-
ature up to 600 °C, regardless of the method of cooling, while the extent of strength degradation in coupons
subjected to heating beyond 600 °C is dependent on heated temperature and method of cooling. Data gen-
erated in these tests are utilized to generate high-temperature stress –strain and residual stress –strain response
of A572 steel. These results are also utilized to propose temperature-dependent strength, elastic modules,
and residual strength reduction factors of A572 steel, which can be used in evaluating residual response
of ﬁre-exposed steel structures. Copyright © 2015 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.

1. INTRODUCTION
Steel used in construction applications are classi ﬁed into different categories based on chemical
composition, tensile properties, and fabrication process as carbon steel, high-strength low-alloy
steels (HSLA), heat-treated carbon steel, and heat-treated constructional alloy steel [1]. HSLA steel
provide better performance in terms of strength, weldability, and corrosion/weather resistance. One
commonly available HSLA is ASTM A572 Gr. 50 steel, and this steel, produced with different
chemical composition than conventional steels, is widely used in North America for bridge and
building applications. Similarly, in Europe, HSLA S355 steel is often used in building and bridge
applications [2]. HSLA S355 steel is equivalent to ASTM A572 Gr. 50 steel. The mechanical
properties of steel vary based on the chemical composition and grade of steel.
Steel structures exhibit lower ﬁre resistance because of higher thermal conductivity and faster
degradation of mechanical properties with temperature. Therefore, steel structural members in

resistance ratings. However, no speci ﬁc ﬁre resistance provisions are required for structural members
in bridges as per current American Association of State Highway and Transportation Of ﬁcials and
other bridge design standards. In recent years, there have been numerous ﬁre incidents in bridges
due to accidents involving fuel tankers in the vicinity of a bridge, which lead to burning of gasoline
fuel underneath a bridge. Some of these ﬁres lead to collapse or damage to steel girders in bridges

these cases, structural members in bridges, upon cooling, might retain much of their load-carrying
capacity after exposure to a ﬁre. Therefore, residual capacity assessment of ﬁre-exposed structural
members is necessary prior to routing traf ﬁc on the bridges.
Undertaking residual capacity evaluation of stee l structural members involves three stages of
analysis, namely, at ambient c onditions, during exposure to ﬁre, and then after cooldown to
room temperature [6]. Relevant high-temperatur e properties of steel are crucial for evaluating
realistic residual capacity of structural members exposed to ﬁre. For stage 2 of the analysis,
high-temperature mechanical propertie s (strength, elastic modulus, stress –strain relations, and
creep response) of steel are required to evaluate the response of structural members during
exposure to ﬁre. Similarly, for stage 3 analysis, residua l strength properties (strength, elastic
modulus, and residual stress –strain relation) of steel are re quired to evaluate the remaining
capacity of structural members after ﬁre exposure [7, 8].
A review of literature indicates that high-temperature property and residual strength property tests
have been carried out on properties of structural steel. However, most of the tests reported in
literature are carried out on conventional carbon steels (such as A36 and A992 or their equivalent
types) commonly used in structural member of buildings [9 –11], and there is no mechanical
property data speci ﬁc to ASTM A572 steel.
To ﬁll this knowledge gap, a comprehensive experimental study was undertaken and reported in this
paper on A572 Gr. 50 steel with the objective of generating high-temperature and residual strength data
for use in ﬁre resistance and residual capacity analysis of structural members. The experimental
investigation included tensile strength (stress –strain response during heating) and residual strength
(residual stress –strain response after cooling) evaluation at various temperatures. Results from these
tests are utilized to propose relations for high-temperature and residual strength properties of A572
steel.

For high-temperature mechanical property tests, namely, tensile and residual strength, rectangular steel

The chemical composition of A572 steel is slightly different from that of conventional A36 steel.

3.2. Test equipment
Two different test setups were speci ﬁcally designed to carry out high-temperature tensile and residual
strength tests on ASTM A572 Gr. 50 steel.
3.2.1. High-temperature tensile strength tests. The test setup for high-temperature tensile strength
tests was composed of a steel frame that is capabl e of applying tensile or c ompressive loading, an
electric furnace, and a data acquisition syste m. The universal testing machine has a loading
capacity of 200 kN, and the axial deformations in the test coupon subjected to tension is measured
by a displacement transducer [±38 mm linear variable differential transformer (LVDT)], with
0.0254 mm sensitivity, that is placed outside the f urnace. The programmable furnace that surrounds
the steel coupon can generate any target temperature up to 1000 °C at any given rate of temperature
rise and can keep the target temperature in steel coupon constant (inside the furnace) for the test
duration. Three thermocouples are mounted on the furnace wall to measure temperatures at the
upper, middle, and lower zones. The average values of these three thermocouples are taken to be
the furnace temperature. The furnace has an inner diameter of 203 mm and a height of 305 mm.
Therefore, the heating length of the specimen is to be 305 mm. The loading machine and the
furnace are connected to a data acquisition system and computer wherein temperature, load, and
displacement throughout the test are recorded. The test setup for high-temperature tensile test is
illustrated in Figure 2.

4. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Data from mechanical property tests on A572 Gr. 50 steel are utilized to generate high-temperature
stress–strain relations and residual stress –strain relations. Results from these tests are compared with
values derived using relevant relations present in codes and literature for conventional (carbon)
structural steel. Also, modi ﬁed strength relations for high-temperature tensile and residual strength
speciﬁc to A572 steel are proposed.
4.1. Tensile strength at elevated temperatures
The variation of stress –strain response of A572 steel at elevated temperatures is evaluated using data
from uniaxial tensile strength tests carried out at various target temperatures. The displacements
recorded at various load levels, during tension tests, are utilized to generate stress –strain response of
A572 steel, and these are plotted in Figure 4. It can be seen in this ﬁgure that the general trend of
stress–strain curve is linear elastic up to yielding of steel, which varies with temperature level,
followed by nonlinear response. Therefore, the stress at which yielding occurs in steel coupon is
different at different temperatures.
No well-de ﬁned yield plateau is recorded in the room temperature test for A572 steel. This
can be attributed to very low levels of carbon content and also presence of alloys such as
vanadium, in the chemical composition of A572 steel as tabulated in Table I. Past the
yielding stress, steel undergoes plastic defo rmation with increasing stress up to reaching
ultimate (stress) point. Once t he ultimate stress is reached, plastic deformation continues
through unloading phase (necking) up to rapture. Also, it can be seen that A572 steel exhibits
some level of strain hardening in the temperature range of 20 –400 °C, while this phenomenon
disappears at temperatures beyond 400 °C. This is in agreement with reported trends in the
literature for conventional carbon steel [14].
From the tensile strength tests data, the yield s tress is evaluated based on a strain level of
0.2%, while the ultimate stress is de ﬁned as the maximum stress recorded during the tensile
strength test on the coupon. The signi ﬁcant points on stress –strain curve from high-
temperature uniaxial tensile strength tests ar e summarized in Table II. The yield and ultimate

4.2. Residual strength
The load –displacement data recorded during uniaxial residual tensile strength tests are utilized to
generate residual stress –strain curves after cooling down from various target temperatures. The
residual stress –stain response of A572 specimens following either air cooling or water quenching is

uniaxial tensile strength tests, are summarized in Tables III and IV, respectively. The strength and

The residual strength factors for heated A572 steel coupons after air cooling and water quenching

quenching). But, A572 steel loses up to 40% of its room temperature yield strength and up to 30%
of its room temperature ultimate strength when it is heated to 800 °C and then air cooled to ambient
temperature. However, when the same steel is heated to 800 °C and then cooled through quenching
