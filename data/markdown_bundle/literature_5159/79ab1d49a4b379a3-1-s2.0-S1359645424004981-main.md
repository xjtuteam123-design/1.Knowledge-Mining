ABSTRACT 
A single-phase or simple-structured alloy does not always possess outstanding combinations of strength and 
ductility over a wide range of temperatures and strain rates for engineering applications. In the present work, a 
high-entropy alloy with multi-heterogeneous microstructures was in-situ fabricated via powder-plasma-arc ad -
ditive manufacturing. The compressive behavior of the additive manufactured high-entropy alloy over a wide 
range of temperatures and strain rates was studied, using an improved split Hopkinson bar system and electronic 
universal testing machine. It shows exceptional combination of strength and ductility within the selected tem -
perature and strain rate ranges. Microstructural evolution was characterized at various temperatures and strain 
rates, providing insight into the intricate relationship between microstructure and property. The multicomponent 
Laves phase is hard yet deformable, while the multicomponent FCC phase is soft and ductile. The deformation 
twins observed all over the selected temperature and strain rate ranges and dynamic recrystallization appearing 
at high temperatures in the FCC phase enhance the ductility of the FCC phase and rise the crack-arresting 
capability. The third-type strain aging occurs at different strain rates, which shifts to a higher temperature 
range as strain rate increases. Ta and impurity atom, Si, acting as “ solute atoms ” form atom atmosphere and 
silicide, pinning the moving dislocations in the FCC phase. Finally, a deformation mechanism map was proposed 
over a wide temperature and strain rate range. The study explored a potentially new avenue to design alloys with 
exceptional combinations of strength and ductility over a wide range of temperatures and strain rates. 
1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) or complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) 
have attracted extensive attention of researchers due to their larger 
composition design space and excellent mechanical properties, 
compared with traditional alloys with single or dual principal elements 
[ 1-3 ]. For traditional alloys (low-entropy alloys), strength and ductility 
are mutually exclusive, referred to as the strength-ductility trade-off [ 4 ]. 
To evade the strength-ductility trade-off in traditional alloys, the most 
potent design aspect was limited to exploiting the local-scale composi -
tional and microstructural heterogeneities [ 5 , 6 ]. HEAs, containing at 
least five principal elements in an equal or near-equal atomic percent -
age, are proposed based on the idea that the high mixing entropy can 
promote the formation of stable single-phase microstructures. The 
development of HEAs offers a multi-dimensional compositional design 
space, an almost limitless design opportunities to surpass the frontiers of

composition-microstructure-property-mechanism correlation, HEAs can

Extensive studies have documented that high mixing entropy can 
promote the formation of stable single-phase microstructures. For 
single-phase HEAs, it is difficult to simultaneously obtain high strength 
and high plasticity. HEAs with body-centered-cubic (BCC) or hexagonal- 
close-packed (HCP) single phases exhibit high strength and relatively 
low plasticity, whereas HEAs with face-centered-cubic (FCC) single 
phases display high plasticity and relatively low strength [ 8 ]. 
Twinning-induced plasticity, transformation-induced plasticity, and 
ultra-fine grains are approaches to combine the strength and ductility of 
HEAs [ 9 ]. However, the microstructures induced by these approaches 
are always unstable at high temperatures, where the strength of the 
relevant HEAs is weak. In other words, they possess low resistance to 
thermal softening. Second-phase intermetallic compounds (IMC) can 
also provide an efficient approach to enhance the strengths of HEAs. 
However, most IMCs with atomically-ordered structures possess high 
strength and low ductility. Thus, the soft and ductile FCC HEA 
strengthened by hard and brittle IMC becomes a widely used design 
strategy, which combines the merits of both the FCC HEA and IMC and 
succeeds in the super strength-ductility synergy [ 10 ]. Due to their 
remarkable combination of strength and ductility, as well as the phase 
stability and good castability, eutectic high-entropy alloys (EHEAs) were 
widely developed in recent years [ 10-12 ]. As we known, IMCs are al -

In comparison with traditional manufacturing techniques, an addi -
tive manufacturing (AM) technology has the potential to improve the 
accuracy and efficiency in both the design and production process while

manufacturing environment [ 14-16 ]. Due to the rapid and repeated 
heating-cooling cycles during the additive manufacturing

tend to form within one step [ 14 ]. HEAs with such heterogeneous mi -

2.3. High strain rate experiments at super high temperatures 
High strain-rate experiments at strain rates of 1000 s
 1 
and 5000 s
 1 
over a wide temperature range from 293 K to 1473 K were performed 
using an improved split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB). For SHPB tests 
at elevated temperatures, it is necessary to heat the specimen to the 
predetermined uniform temperature while keeping the incident bar and 
transmit bar of the SHPB at suitable low temperature (below 523 K for 
steel bars) [ 21 ]. If the incident and transmit bars are in contact with or 
close to the hot specimen within a furnace, a temperature gradient will 
develop at the bars ’ end. This feature affects the elastic constants of the 
bars, leading to changes in the stress-wave propagation in the bars. To 
avoid this influence, the bars are kept far enough away from the heating 
unit of the furnace, while keeping the specimen at the center of the 
furnace. The bars are then pushed in full contact with the specimen 
immediately before the stress wave arrives at the far end of the incident 
bar. SHPB with a synchronically assembled heating system is always 
used for high strain rate experiments at high temperatures. Detailed 
introduction about this enhanced SHPB system is provided in Appendix 
A. 
As discussed in Appendix A, for high strain-rate experiments at super 
high temperatures (above 1273 K), the cold contact time (CCT), which is 
defined as the time interval of the direct contact between the specimen 
and the bars ’ end prior to the arrival of the incident stress wave, must be 
much shorter, and the impact force subjected on the specimen caused by 
the fast synchronic assemble should be avoided. Thus, an improved 
SHPB system for high strain-rate experiments at super high temperatures 
was newly developed in this study, as shown in Fig. 1(a) . Two high- 
precision time delay units were applied to respectively control the 
starting time of the gas gun and the assemble system for the synchronous 
drive of incident and transmit bars. The influence of the impact force 
imposed on the specimen can be completely avoided, and the CCT can be 
accurately controlled. Detailed description about the CCT measurement 
is provided in Appendix B. Fig. 1(b) shows ten repeated measured sig -
nals for obtaining the CCTs. The measured signals can respectively re -
cord the completion time of the assembly between bars and specimen 
and the arrival time of the incident wave. The time interval adding the 
propagation time of the incident wave from the strain gauge to the 
specimen is the CCT. As shown in Fig. 1(b) , the CCT can be accurately 
controlled below 10 ms. Such a short CCT will not lead to an obvious 
temperature drop of the specimen, as presented in Fig. S1(c). Thus, the 
improved SHPB system newly developed in this study was a reliable 
experimental technique for high strain-rate experiments at super high 
temperatures. Using the improved SHPB system, the CCT can be accu -
rately controlled, and the impact force during assembly can be 
completely avoided. Additionally, after the stress wave loading on the 
specimen, the incident and transmit bars were immediately moved far 
away from the heating unit of the furnace, avoiding the continuous 
heating. The improved SHPB system was used to measure the 
compressive behavior of the PPA-AMed CoCrFeNiTa

elevated temperatures and high strain rates. To obtain the deformed

4.2. 3rd SA effect on the combination of strength and ductility 
As displayed in Fig. 4 , the flow stress level increases with increasing 
temperature within a certain temperature range, and an anomalous 
stress peak forms in the flow stress vs. temperature relation. This 
anomalous phenomenon referred to as the 3rd SA is contrary to our 
common knowledge that the flow stress of metals/alloys decreases with 
increasing temperature. Furthermore, as strain rate increases, the 
anomalous stress peak shifts to a higher temperature range and the 
amplitude of the stress peak decreases, as shown in Fig. 5 . To quanti -
tatively describe the variation of the 3rd SA induced stress peak with 
strain rate, several models, including phenomenological models [ 46 , 47 ] 
and physically-based models [ 20 , 48 ], were developed. The introduction 
about the phenomenological model was provided in Appendix D. 
Additionally, it has been widely accepted that the 3rd SA is related to the 
dynamic pinning effect on moving dislocations by solute atom atmo -
spheres formed from diffusing solute atoms, while dislocations are 
waiting at their short-range barriers. The formation of the solute atom 
atmosphere, including Cottrell atmosphere (solute segregation around 
dislocation cores), Sneok atmosphere (dislocation stress-field induced 
short-range ordering), and Suzuki atmosphere (solute segregation on 
stacking faults in extended dislocations), depends on temperature and 
strain rate [ 27 , 49 ]. During plastic deformation, temperature affects the 
diffusion of solute atoms, while strain rate can alter the number and 
mobility of dislocations nucleated in the alloy under stress. When the 
temperature reaches a relationship with the strain rate, solute atom at -
mospheres form at mobile dislocations, pinning the mobile dislocations.

position adjustment of adjacent atoms to reduce the overall strain en -

compressed region [ 52 ]. Consequently, this stress induced migration 
leads to the formation of “ solutes ” atom enriched atmospheres or 
short-range clusters along dislocation lines, thereby pinning the dislo -
cation. A larger stress is required for the unpinning of the dislocation. 
During plastic deformation, temperature influences the migration of 
atoms and further the position adjustment, while strain rate affects the 
mobility of dislocations. When the temperature reaches a relationship 
with the strain rate, e.g., Eq. (2) , atom atmospheres or short-range 
clusters form around mobile dislocations and pin mobile dislocations.
