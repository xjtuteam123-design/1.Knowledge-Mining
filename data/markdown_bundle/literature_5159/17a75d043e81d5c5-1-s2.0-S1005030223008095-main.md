Simultaneous enhancement of strength and ductility in 
high Nb-TiAl by Si alloying 
1. Introduction 
TiAl alloys are lightweight and high-temperature-resistant 
structural materials having applicability in automotive and 
aerospace applications owing to their low density and excellent 
high-temperature properties [1–3] . High-Nb-containing TiAl (High 
Nb-TiAl) alloys are considered as a new generation of TiAl alloys 
for applications at higher service temperatures [4–6] . Components 
of TiAl alloys that have achieved long-term stability in the industry 
are mainly manufactured by casting, which is the most economical 
method [7–9] . However, the mechanical properties of these com- 
ponents are limited at room temperature (RT), signiﬁcantly hinder- 
ing their broad applications [ 7 , 10-12 ]. 
Numerous researchers have attempted to improve the mechan- 
ical properties of cast TiAl alloys, predominantly via alloying and 
heat treatment [ 4 , 13-17 ]. Improving mechanical properties by heat 
treatment is time consuming and ineﬃcient, whereas alloying 
is relatively feasible and eﬃcient [18–20] . Previous studies have 
demonstrated that alloying is an effective method for improving 
the mechanical properties of TiAl alloys, and the most success- 
ful example is the second-generation TiAl alloy Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb 
(4822) with Cr and Nb alloying [ 2 , 21 ]. General Electric equipped 
the last two stages of a low-pressure turbine (LPT) with cast 4822 
blades for the GEnx jet engine; this established a milestone in the 
development of TiAl alloys [ 2 , 4 ]. In recent years, the utilization of 
B alloying to improve the mechanical properties of high Nb-TiAl al- 
loys has also been reported [22] . Furthermore, Si alloying can sig- 
niﬁcantly improve the high-temperature creep and oxidation resis- 
tance of TiAl alloys [ 21 , 23-27 ]. In the reported literature, Si alloying 
TiAl alloys are also in trace amounts (generally not higher than 0.5 
at%) which is mainly utilized for silicide precipitation to improve 
creep resistance during the creep process [ 21 , 24 , 28-30 ]. However, 
studies on high content of Si alloying to improve the tensile prop- 
erties of cast high Nb-TiAl alloys have not been reported. 
In this study, excellent mechanical properties of Ti-45Al-8Nb- 
1.3Si were achieved by casting. The microstructure and tensile de- 
formation characteristics, as well as the in situ precipitation pro- 
cess of micro-/nanosilicides, were observed and thoroughly exam- 
ined. The mechanism of the simultaneous enhancement of strength 
and plasticity by micro-/nanosilicides with Si addition was investi- 
gated.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Tensile properties 
Fig. 1 shows the tensile stress–strain curves of TiAl-0Si and 
TiAl-1.3Si alloys at RT and 850 °C. The ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS) and total strain ( δ) values for TiAl-0Si and TiAl-1.3Si alloys 
at RT are 539.1 ± 17.2 and 860.5 ± 34.4 MPa and 0.29% ±0.03% 
and 0.73% ±0.07%, respectively ( Fig. 1 (a)). The UTS and δ values of 
the alloy improved by 60% and 154%, respectively, with Si alloy- 
ing. At 850 °C, the UTS and δ values are 524.1 MPa and 1.72% 
for TiAl-0Si, and those are 747.5 MPa and 12.64% for TiAl-1.3Si, 
as shown in Fig. 1 (b). With Si alloying, the UTS and δ values im- 
proved by 43% and 635%, respectively. These results indicate that 
both the strength and plasticity of the alloy were signiﬁcantly im- 
proved upon Si addition. 
3.2. Microstructure characteristics 
Fig. 2 shows the microstructures of as-cast TiAl-0Si and TiAl- 
1.3Si. A typical near lamellar (NL) microstructure with β segrega- 
tion is observed for TiAl-0Si ( Fig. 2 (a) and (b)). With the addition 
of Si, the microstructure changed signiﬁcantly, as shown in Fig. 2 (c) 
and (d). 
On the one hand, S segregation is evident, as indicated by the 
yellow arrows in Fig. 2 (c). During the solidiﬁcation of TiAl-1.3Si, 
the β phase solidiﬁed initially, while Al and Si atoms were dis- 
charged into the liquid at the solidiﬁcation front. With the enrich- 
ment of Si atoms in the liquid up to the solubility limit, the silicide 
Ti5 Si3 may precipitate by a combination of Si and Ti atoms. As the 
Ti content decreases and Al content increases, peritectic solidiﬁca- 
tion occurs in the residual liquid. Therefore, signiﬁcant S segrega- 
tion was observed in the as-cast TiAl-1.3Si. 
On the other hand, a signiﬁcant number of micro-/nanosilicides 
precipitated after casting, as shown in Fig. 2 (d). Microsilicides and 
equiaxed γ grains are distributed along the S segregation. The mi- 
crosilicides exhibited eutectic structures with uneven thicknesses. 
S segregation was the last phase of the solidiﬁcation process. In 
this region, silicides were generated from the liquid and exhib- 
ited a rapid growth rate; the silicide size reached the microscale 
level. After silicide generation, the Ti content decreased, whereas 
the Al content remained high. The microregion underwent γ so- 
lidiﬁcation, and equiaxed γ grains were generated. The presence 
of S segregation and equiaxed γ grains is likely favorable for 
plasticity, whereas hard silicides can prevent dislocation gliding, 
thus strengthening the alloy. The nanosilicides were relatively dis- 
persed, predominantly at the edge of the S segregation, as shown 
in Fig. 2 (d). Nanosilicides appeared mainly in small spots and had 
a high density. After complete solidiﬁcation, nanosilicides are gen- 
erated during the cooling process. Around the edge of the S seg- 
regation, a large number of silicides precipitated because of the 
low solubility of Si atoms with a high Al content. Further, nanosili- 
cides tend to disperse in lines 3–4 μm away from the microsili- 
cides. This indirectly demonstrates that the massive precipitation 
of microsilicides leads to the transformation of the residual liquid 
toward γ solidiﬁcation. During the subsequent cooling, a line-like 
distribution of nanosilicides precipitated at the boundary of the 
different solidiﬁcation types. 
The TEM images further revealed that nanosilicides were abun- 
dantly located in γ/ γ twin lamellar boundary ( Fig. 2 (e)), γ/ α2 
lamellar interface ( Fig. 2 (f)), inside γ lamellae ( Fig. 2 (g)), inside 
α2 lamellae ( Fig. 2 (h)), and inside equiaxed γ grains ( Fig. 2 (i)). 
This indicates that no speciﬁc tendency is pursued in the for- 
mation of ﬁne nanosilicides during cooling, provided that the el- 
emental content and nucleation requirements for the precipita- 
tion of the second phase are satisﬁed. These diffusely distributed 
nanosilicides provided further second-phase strengthening. High- 
resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images indicated that the ﬁne nanosili- 
cides were Ti5 Si3 and showed a semi-coherent lattice interface 
with equiaxed γ grains ( Fig. 2 (j) and inset). Therefore, micro- 
/nanosilicides were precipitated in situ during the casting process 
of the alloy. It should be noted that the size of lamellar colonies 
showed no signiﬁcant change being approximately ∼300 μm. This 
was attributed to the fact that both S segregation and silicide pre- 
cipitation occurred at the end of the solidiﬁcation process. At this 
time, the size of phase β, which could cause changes in the size of 
alloy lamellar colonies, has been ﬁxed. Therefore, the size of lamel- 
lar colonies was basically the same.

3.4. Strengthening mechanism on tensile performance 
Fig. 4 shows the bright-ﬁeld TEM images of the TiAl-1.3Si al- 
loy near the fracture after the RT-tensile test. This microsilicide 
is identiﬁed as Ti5 Si3 , as shown in the inset of Fig. 4 (a). Numer- 
ous twins/faults and dislocation tangles are present near the mi- 
crosilicides, whereas no obvious microcracks are observed. This 
suggests that microsilicides can provide second-phase strengthen- 
ing to the alloys. Fig. 4 (b) shows that dislocation movement is hin- 
dered and deﬂected by the nanosilicides. Moreover, a dislocation 
network was generated owing to local stress concentration. High- 
density faults/dislocations are also observed near the nanosili- 
cides ( Fig. 4 (c)). These TEM images demonstrated the strengthen- 
ing effect of the nanosilicides. Fig. 4 (d) shows a dislocation loop 
around the nanosilicides, which indicates the Orowan dislocation- 
precipitate bypass mechanism for the nanosilicides. In summary, 
micro-/nanosilicides together strengthened the TiAl-1.3Si alloy.

ascribed to improvements in strength and plasticity. As shown in

pearance of intersecting twins contributed to their high strength. 
Combined with the second-phase strengthening effect by micro- 
/nanosilicides, high strength and plasticity are achieved at 850 °C. 
4. Conclusions 
In this study, the in situ precipitation of micro-/nanosilicides 
was achieved in the Si-alloyed as-cast high Nb-TiAl alloys. The 
tensile mechanical properties of the alloys were signiﬁcantly im- 
proved. The micro-/nanosilicides were identiﬁed as Ti5 Si3 . Its for- 
mation was caused by local elemental enrichment during the so- 
lidiﬁcation process. Si alloying triggered micro-regional peritectic 
solidiﬁcation, which led to S segregation. The plasticity of the al- 
loys was enhanced by the generation of equiaxed γ grains during 
S segregation. The silicides provided second-phase strengthening to 
the alloys.
