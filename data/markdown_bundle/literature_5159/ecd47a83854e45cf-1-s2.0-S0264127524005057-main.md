ABSTRACT 
The CrCoNiSi
0.3 
MEA exhibits excellent cryogenic mechanical properties upon both quasi-static and dynamic 
tension. Under quasi-static tension at cryogenic temperature, the engineering yield and ultimate tensile strengths 
(UTS) reach 980 MPa and 1800 MPa, respectively, with notable ductility (62 %). The product of UTS and total 
elongation (TE) is 111.6 GPa %, surpassing most cryogenic high strength-ductility alloys. The significant me -
chanical strength enhancement is attributed to the denser deformation twins (DTs), multiple twinning, and 
extensive face-centered-cubic to hexagonal-close-packed (HCP) phase transitions, resulting in a high work 
hardening capacity. Upon dynamic tension at cryogenic temperature, the strength and strain hardening are 
further improved, which originates from the thickening DTs and HCP sequence and localized plastic deformation. 
The effects of temperature and strain rate on phase transition are studied. It is proposed that there is a competing 
relationship between high strain rate and increased stacking fault energy (SFE) due to temperature rise. The 
coupling effect of cryogenic temperature and high strain rate inhibits phase transition due to the deformation 
inhomogeneity in CrCoNiSi
0.3 
MEA. The findings make a valuable contribution to understand the influence of 
temperature and strain rate on the mechanism of FCC-to-HCP phase transition. 
1. Introduction 
Materials with an excellent combination of strength and ductility is a 
key requirement for static and dynamic load-bearing applications at low 
temperatures. Insufficient toughness can lead to catastrophic failure 
since metal materials tend to show a decline in toughness at cryogenic 
temperature [1] . The body-centered-cubic (BCC) and hexagonal-close- 
packed (HCP) alloys usually exhibit high strength than the face- 
centered-cubic (FCC) ones, but they generally present a lower ductility 
or even ductile-to-brittle transition phenomenon at cryogenic conditions 
[2] . However, FCC-structured alloys demonstrate enhanced strength 
and ductility at cryogenic temperature due to the activation of an 
adequate number of slip systems and low stacking-fault energy (SFE) 
[3 – 5] . For instance, at 77 K, 304 and 316 stainless steels show the ul -
timate tensile strength (UTS) of 1521 MPa and 1312 MPa with ductility 
of 42 % and 56 %, respectively, high-Mn steel displays a UTS of 1403 
MPa and a total elongation (TE) of 67 % due to the transformation 
induced plasticity (TRIP) effect [3 – 5] . However, despite the good plas -
ticity, there are still some limitations in these steels, such as a low yield 
strength [6] and a hydrogen delayed fracture [7] upon quasi-static and 
especially dynamic conditions. Therefore, it is significant to develop and 
investigate FCC-structured alloys with superior combination of strength 
and ductility under extreme conditions. 
Medium/high entropy alloys (M/HEAs) have been widely developed 
owing to superior comprehensive mechanical properties than conven -
tional alloys [8,43] . Numerous M/HEAs with FCC structures exhibit

to that of quasi-static one according to the EBSD results ( Fig. 3 (d) and 6 
(a)). This further suggests significant plastic deformation inhomogeneity 
under CT-D deformation. The deformation inhomogeneity is believed to 
generate substantial back stress [35,36] and further enhance the 
strength of alloy. 
The deformation inhomogeneity, denser multiple-twinning, and 
thicker DTs and HCP phase compared with those under quasi-static 
tension are responsible for the higher strain-hardening rate and 
enhanced strength under cryogenic dynamic tension. It is these plastic 
hardening mechanisms that promote the MEA to overcome the thermal 
softening and show stronger work hardening ability. However, the 
deformation inhomogeneity and thicker DTs reduce plastic strain ac -
commodation and lead to a decrease in ductility.

4.2. Coupling effect of strain rate and temperature 
At room temperature, higher strain rates lead to increased fractions 
of DTs and HCP phases [16,17,25] , and vice versa at cryogenic tem -
perature. Under quasi-static tension, lower temperature promotes more 
DTs and HCP phases as previous analyses, while the total fractions of 
DTs and HCP structures at cryogenic temperature are smaller than that 
at room temperature. It is concluded that the coupling effect of cryo -
genic temperature and dynamic loading causes the decreased fractions 
of DTs and HCP phases. However, the DTs and HCP thickness and 
density are larger locally compared to both the CT-Q and RT-D defor -
mation. Consequently, it is attributed to deformation inhomogeneity 
induced by coupling effect of cryogenic temperature and dynamic 
loading that inhibits the DT activation and HCP transformation. 
Due to the higher stress at high strain rate, the rapid proliferation of 
dislocation results in the stronger dislocation entanglement and inter -
action, thus enhancing the work-hardening ability [25] . However, 
during dynamic deformation, the thermal softening from adiabatic 
temperature rise significantly influences the plastic deformation of 
materials. Additionally, the temperature rise increases the SFE and 
makes DT and phase transition more difficult. The existing studies have 
revealed that the deformation at high strain rate could produce positive 
or negative effects on phase transformation [18 – 22] and work hard -
ening [16,18,34,44] . For example, under dynamic tension among the 
strain rate of 2100 to 5000 s

CrCoNiSi
0.3

while the generated HCP phase is decreased as the dynamic tensile strain

for CoCrFeMnNi HEA [22] . It can 
be concluded that a higher strain rate generally tends to inhibit the 
phase transition, while a relatively low strain rate prefers to facilitate 
phase transition over the dynamic loading range. Additionally, it is 
reasonable that a higher strain rate can result in a greater temperature 
increase [24,25] , thus causing a higher SFE and a negative effect on HCP 
transition. Therefore, in the present paper, it is proposed that a higher 
strain rate and increased SFE due to a temperature rise are competitive 
relationship for phase transition. The FCC-to-HCP phase transition is 
promoted by higher strain rate but inhibited by the increased SFE, and 
the eventual effect on phase transition depends on the specific compe -
tition results. Additionally, it can be concluded that the coupling effect 
of cryogenic temperature and high strain rate more easily causes 
deformation inhomogeneity, which is related to the localized plastic 
deformation and smaller activation volume at a high strain rate. This is 
also surely related to the coordination of the deformation mechanism, 
which is worthy of further study.

During dynamic tension at room temperature (RT-D), an increased

HCP sequence compared to quasi-static tension, leading to phase tran -
sition promotion. However, under dynamic tension at cryogenic tem -

the increased DT and HCP density, as well as thickening of the HCP 
sequence in local regions. It is noted that the total fractions of DTs and 
HCP are smaller compared to CT-Q and RT-D due to more serious 
deformation inhomogeneity. Consequently, the combined influence of 
temperature and strain rate inhibits overall phase transition.
