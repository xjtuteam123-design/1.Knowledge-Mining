abstract
The high-temperature oxidation behaviors of Co- and CoNi-base alloys with different Ni and Cr additions
were investigated by isothermal oxidation in air at 900 and 1000 /C176C. The structure, composition, and ele-
ment distribution in the three layers within the oxide scales in different alloys have been explored in
detail by electron microscopy, especially with regard to the ﬁne oxide particles in the intermediate layer.
Based on the microstructure and phase constitution of the oxide scales, the synergetic effects of alloying
elements on the oxidation mechanisms and resistance have been elucidated in detail with the aid of
CALPHAD calculation.

1. Introduction
Conventional carbide and solid-solution strengthened Co-base
superalloys show higher melting temperatures and better corro-
sion resistance than Ni-base superalloys which have been widely
applied as high temperature materials in aircraft engines [1]. Due
to the lack of a stable c’ strengthening phase the application of
conventional Co-base superalloys is rather limited at high temper-
atures compared to Ni-base superalloys. In 2006, Sato et al. discov-
ered the existence of a stable
c’-Co3(Al,W) phase in the Co-Al-W
ternary alloy system, which opened a new era for the development
of such novel Co-base superalloys [2].
High-temperature oxidation resistance is an important factor
determining the service performance of Co-base superalloys, and
thus, it is essential to understand their high-temperature oxidation
behavior and mechanisms to better control their high-temperature
oxidation resistance. During oxidation the formation of continuous

good oxidation resistance of the superalloys as they can effectively
prevent further oxidation of the substrate [3,4]. Thus, by alloying
Cr and Al, the oxidation resistance of superalloys could be greatly
improved [5–7]. Chung et al. showed that the addition of 4 at.%
Cr was able to reduce the oxidation rate of the alloy Co-30Ni-
11Al-2Ti-5.5W-2.5Ta-0.1B (at.% throughout the paper, otherwise
it will be indicated) by two orders of magnitude [8]. However,
the formation of a continuous and dense Al 2O3 protective layer
strongly depends on the alloy composition and oxidation temper-
ature. For the Co-9Al-7W ternary alloy, no indication for the forma-
tion of Al 2O3 could be observed after oxidation at 850 /C176C for 100 h
[9]. But the continuous Al 2O3 layer was reported to form in a tern-
ary Co-9Al-9W alloy with 0.12 at.% B at 800 /C176C [3]. The consider-
able level of boron leads to better oxide layer adhesion,
continuous Al
2O3 layer formation and therefore thinner oxide
scales. In addition, with the addition of Cr into these Co-Al-W base
alloys, the formation of a continuous Al 2O3 layer is accelerated and
promoted at 800 /C176C. Nevertheless, as a c-stabilizing element, Cr
can decrease the c’ solvus temperature and reduce the c’-phase
stability [10–12]. In a Co-7Al-7W-4Ti-2Ta-4Cr alloy, Cr strongly
promoted the precipitation of the v phase which was found after
aging at 1100 /C176C for only 50 h [13]. Thus, the simultaneous addition
of other c’-stabilizing elements, such as Ni, Ta, W, and Ti, to reduce
this adverse effect is advisable [14–16]. In the Co-16Ni-8.5Al-7W
alloy system, further increasing the Ni content could increase the
concentration of Cr 3+ in spinel oxide scales, and thus, promote
the formation of a protective Cr 2O3 layer [18].
In general, adding Ni to improve the c0 phase stability and add-
ing Cr to improve the oxidation resistance have been widely recog-
nized in the research community of Co-base superalloys as
important alloy development trends [19,20]. Most of the recently
reported Cr-containing Co-Al-W-base alloys utilize /C2430 at.% Ni,
which is one of the most widely studied alloy systems in c’-
strengthened Co-base superalloys, to improve the microstructural
stability, expand the c/c’ two-phase region and increase the solu-
bility of other alloying elements [5,8,17]. However, there are only
limited reports about the synergistic effects of Ni and Cr on the oxi-
dation resistance and the detailed structure of oxide scales. In
addition, it should be noted that in multicomponent Co-base
superalloys, the oxidation behavior and formation of oxide scales
could be rather complex as each single element could affect the
diffusion of other alloying elements and the formation energy of
oxides. Cr has been found to reduce the critical Al concentration
to form continuous Al 2O3 layers in Cr-containing Co-30Ni-9Al-
6W-1Ti-4Ta alloys after isothermal oxidation at 1000 /C176C in air
[5]. In Co-30Ni-10Al-5Cr-2Ta-2/4Ti alloys, increasing the Ti con-
tent was reported to be beneﬁcial to accelerate the formation of
Cr
2O3 and promote alloy oxidation resistance [21]. In the IN617
based alloys (Ni-22.2Cr-12.1Co-8.5Mo-1.2Al-0.34Ti-(0.5–2)Ta, wt.
%), Ta impeded the outward-diffusion of Ti toward the surface of
the Cr 2O3 scale, leading to the formation of (Ti,Ta)O 2 inside the
Cr2O3 scale, which ﬁlled the voids of the Cr 2O3 scale and improved
the density of oxide scales [22].
Therefore, an in-depth study on the synergistic effects of alloy-
ing elements on the oxidation performance of multicomponent Co-
base superalloys is of utmost interest. Additionally, most of the
previous oxidation experiments were conducted at 800 and
900 /C176C [23–27], and only limited reports exist about the oxidation
behavior of the novel Co-base superalloys above 900 /C176C [28].
Increasing temperatures has signiﬁcant inﬂuences on oxidation
kinetics, thermodynamics and material transport, thus affecting
the whole oxidation process. It is critical to investigate the oxida-
tion behavior of the alloys at high temperatures of 900–1000 /C176C
as for engineering applications high service temperatures are
demanded for high-temperature alloys like using as turbine blades.
Thus, in this study, Ni-free Co-9Al-5W-2Ti-2Ta alloy, Ni containing
Co-30Ni-9Al-5W-2Ti-2Ta alloy, and Ni/Cr containing Co-9Al-5W-
2Ti-2Ta-30Ni-xCr alloys were designed to test the effects of the
elements Ni and Cr on the high-temperature oxidation behavior
of Co-base and CoNi-base superalloys at 900 and 1000 /C176C. The syn-
ergistic inﬂuence of alloying elements on the formation of oxide
scales were also studied by different advanced characterization
techniques i.e., focus ion beam (FIB), transmission electron micro-
scopy (TEM), scanning TEM (STEM) and energy dispersive spec-
troscopy (EDS) under STEM mode with the aid of CALPHAD
calculation to reveal the relation between the oxide scale
microstructure and chemistry, and the oxidation performance.
2. Experimental methods
The investigated alloys with nominal compositions of Co-9Al-
5W-2Ti-2Ta (Ni-free alloy), Co-30Ni-9Al-5W-2Ti-2Ta (Ni contain-
ing alloy), and Co-9Al-5W-2Ti-2Ta-30Ni-xCr (x = 6, 8 and 10)
(Ni/Cr containing alloy), in this work were prepared by argon arc
melting. They are denoted as 0Ni0Cr, 30Ni0Cr, 30Ni6Cr, 30Ni8Cr
and 30Ni10Cr in the following. The cast ingot of each composition
was remelted at least 8 times to ensure chemical homogeneity. To
further homogenize the microstructure, the cast buttons were
solutionized at 1200 /C176C for 24 h in vacuum followed by water
quenching.
All the samples were ground by SiC sandpapers to grit 1000 and
cleaned by absolute ethanol in an ultrasonic cleaner. Isothermal
oxidation experiments were carried out in air at 900 and 1000 /C176C
in a mufﬂe furnace for 48, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 h, respectively,
followed by furnace cooling. During the tests, the samples were
placed in the alumina crucibles and the crucibles were covered
with a perforated cover before oxidation to avoid weight gain loss
by spalling of the oxide layer. The total weight gains (the samples
and the crucibles) were measured by an analytical balance with an
accuracy of 0.01 mg. The oxidized samples were embedded into
cured resin at room temperature for mechanical grinding and pol-
ishing, and the cross-sections of oxide layers were examined by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quanta 250 FEG). The chemi-
cal compositions of the oxides were determined by energy disper-
sive spectroscopy (EDS) under SEM and scanning transmission
electron microscopy (STEM) conditions. A coarse phase analysis
of oxides was conducted using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Dmax
2550VB). For a ﬁner analysis of the phase constitution and element
distribution in the oxide scales, transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) lamellae were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) in a Ther-
moFisher Scientiﬁc Helios 5 UX. The microstructures and chemical
composition of the TEM slices were investigated with a Thermo
Scientiﬁc
TM Talos F200 equipped with the Super-X detector, and
the EDS results were analyzed using a Thermo Scientiﬁc TM Velox
software.
3. Results
3.1. Isothermal oxidation at 900 and 1000 /C176C
Fig. 1 shows the mass gain of the alloys as a function of oxida-
tion time for all specimens after oxidation tests at 900 /C176C( Fig. 1 a)
and 1000 /C176C( Fig. 1 b). From the proﬁles, it is seen that the mass
gain at the same oxidation time obviously increases with temper-
ature rising from 900 to 1000 /C176C. The phenomenon is more signif-
icant for specimens without Cr addition. Thus, the oxidation
resistance of the 0Ni0Cr, 30Ni0Cr, 30Ni6Cr, 30Ni8Cr and 30Ni10Cr
alloys is obviously reduced at elevated temperatures, which might
be due to the accelerated element diffusion with temperature.
Additionally, the rate of weight gain is found to increase sharply

at the initial stage of the oxidation process, then gradually slows
down till 500 h, and again rises fast after oxidation for 1000 h
(especially for 0Ni0Cr, 30Ni0Cr and 30Ni6Cr at 1000 /C176C). The initial
sharp increase at the beginning of the oxidation process could be
due to the absence of continuous oxide layer on the specimen sur-
face. With prolonging the oxidation time, continuous barrier layers
gradually formed, which effectively inhibited the diffusion of oxy-
gen to the matrix [29]. Thus, the rate of the oxidation weight gain
decreased gradually. After oxidation for 500 h, the weight gain fur-
ther obviously increases, which is probably caused by spallation or
cracking of oxide scale.
The oxidation weight gain of the ﬁve alloys follows the order of:
0Ni0Cr > 30Ni0Cr > 30Ni6Cr > 30Ni(8, 10)Cr. Irrespective if tested
at 900 or 1000 /C176C, the weight gain rate of 0Ni0Cr is much higher
than those of Ni containing and Ni/Cr containing alloys. With
increasing oxidation time and temperature, the weight gain differ-
ence between 0Ni0Cr and the other alloys becomes more pro-
nounced, indicating that the 0Ni0Cr alloy has the worst high-
temperature oxidation resistance. The addition of 30 at.% Ni greatly
decreased the oxidation weight gain of the 30Ni0Cr alloy. However,
for Cr-containing alloys, their oxidation weight gain always retains
at a low level and the mass gain only increases slightly with
temperature.
Generally, if oxidation is controlled by the diffusion of cations or
anions through the oxide ﬁlm, its growth is expected to follow a
parabolic law with time, i.e., ( Dm/A)2 = kpt. Here Dm/A is the mass
gain per unit area (mg cm /C02), kp is the oxidation rate constant
(mg2 cm/C04 h/C01), and t is the oxidation time (h) [30]. The value of
kp could be determined from the weight gain-time proﬁles. In the
present study data at the relatively early stage of the oxidation
process (48–200 h) was used for this due to severe delamination
of the oxide layer after prolonged oxidation. The results in Table 1
show that the oxide layer growth of all investigated specimens (ex-
cept for the 30Ni8Cr alloy at 900 /C176C due to the ﬂuctuation error of
the raw data) ﬁt the parabolic law quite well in the ﬁrst 200 h. The
oxidation rate constant kp of the three alloys increases with tem-
perature rising from 900 to 1000 /C176C. Especially for the 0Ni0Cr alloy,
the oxidation rate constant at 1000 /C176C is increased by almost 9
times compared to that at 900 /C176C, which indicates its inferior oxi-
dation resistance at high temperatures.
3.2. Phase identiﬁcation of oxides by XRD
Fig. 2 shows the XRD proﬁles of the oxidation products of the
0Ni0Cr, 30Ni0Cr, 30Ni6Cr and 30Ni10Cr alloys after oxidation at
1000 /C176C for 500 h. The main oxidation products of the 0Ni0Cr alloy
are CoO, CoAl 2O4 and CoWO4. After adding 30 at.% Ni, the types of
the oxides are quite similar being (Co,Ni)O, (Co,Ni)Al 2O4 and (Co,
Ni)WO4 in the 30Ni0Cr alloy. Thus, the same oxide types are found
but Co is partly replaced by Ni. In both alloys, no reﬂections of the
Al2O3 phase were found in the XRD patterns. This could be due to
the low Al 2O3 fraction or because the upper oxide layer is too thick
to detect signals in the inner layer of the oxide scale. For the
30Ni6Cr and 30Ni10Cr alloys, the detected oxides are mainly com-
posed of (Co,Ni)O, (Co,Ni)Al 2O4, (Co,Ni)Cr 2O4, (Co,Ni)WO 4, and
Al2O3. No reﬂections from the Cr 2O3 phase were identiﬁed in the
XRD pattern, which could be explained by similar reasons as men-
tioned above for Al 2O3.

In the 30Ni0Cr alloy, the morphology of the oxide scale is

the intermediate layer Co, Ni, O, Al and W could be clearly
detected, hinting to the possible co-existence of oxides such as
(Co,Ni)O, (Co,Ni)Al 2O4, (Co,Ni) 2AlO4, and (Co,Ni)WO 4. According
to the chemical compositions of regions 10–13 in Table 2 , the
regions identiﬁed as (Co,Ni)O and (Co,Ni)WO 4 near to the substrate

is more enriched in the oxide layer near to the substrate and Co dif-
fuses outward much faster than Ni. Compared to the 0Ni0Cr alloy,
although there are also many pores existing in the upper oxide
layer, a denser and thus more protective intermediate oxide layer
could be found for the 30Ni0Cr alloy. Below the intermediate oxide
layer, a thin Al and O enriched layer exists adjacent to the substrate
layer, which should be the Al

analysis of region 14. Thus, in the substrate near to the Al 2O3 layer,
the matrix is depleted of Al. Meanwhile, the formation of needle or
plate-like Co3W phase in the 0Ni0Cr alloy has been inhibited in the
30Ni0Cr alloy, which is related to the improved c’ phase stability
caused by Ni alloying [17].
In the 30Ni6Cr alloy, the morphology of the oxide scale is signif-
icantly changed compared to 0Ni0Cr and 30Ni0Cr alloys. With both
Ni and Cr additions, the total oxide scale thickness is greatly
reduced as visible in Fig. 3 c. Meanwhile in some cases the top
oxide layer is divided into two separate layers with the upper layer
enriched in Co, W and O, and the lower layer in Co, Ni and O, which
are determined to be CoWO 4 and (Co,Ni)O, respectively according
to the EDS point analysis of regions 16 and 17. After longer expo-
sure W can diffuse outward and react with CoO to form cobalt
tungstate CoWO 4 in the outmost oxide layer. The intermediate
oxide layer is enriched in O, Co, Al, Cr, Ti and Ta, and based on
EDS point analysis this layer could consist of oxides like (Co,Ni)

Al is greatly depleted. In addition, short needle-like nitrides dis-

The morphology and phase component of the oxide scale in
30Ni8Cr are rather similar to those in 30Ni6Cr. However, the
chemical compositions of oxides, especially for the Ni concentra-
tion in the oxides in the top layer has changed. By comparing the
EDS line analysis results in Fig. 3 c and d , the Ni fraction in the
top layer is found to be obviously increased in the 30Ni8Cr alloy.
With further increasing the Cr content in the overall alloy com-
position, the top oxide layer is again determined to be (Co,Ni)O in
the 30Ni10Cr alloy in Fig. 3 e. The presence of the elements O, Co,
Ni, Al, Cr and W in the intermediate layer indicates the existence
of similar oxide phases as those in 30Ni6Cr and 30Ni8Cr.
Fig. 4 shows the element distribution over the oxide scales in
the ﬁve alloys after oxidation at 900 /C176C for 500 h. For the 0Ni0Cr
alloy, Co and O are uniformly distributed in the top layer. This is
followed by a Co-depleted intermediate oxide layer, where Al
enrichment can be observed. The elements W, Ti and Ta are dis-
tributed in the intermediate oxide layer and substrate without
obvious segregation. With 30 at.% Ni addition, the distribution of
Co, Ta, Ti and O in the 30Ni0Cr alloy is nevertheless similar with
that in the 0Ni0Cr alloy. While some regions enriched by W are
observed in the top oxide layer, which is due to the formation of
the CoWO 4 phase based on the EDS quantitative results, and Al is
mainly distributed in the intermediate layer and a rather thin Al-
enrichment layer is located near to the substrate. Ni exhibits a dif-
ferent distribution behavior over the oxide scale compared to Co
and in the intermediate oxide layer, especially for the regions near
to the substrate, and a signiﬁcant Ni fraction could be detected. The
addition of Cr obviously affects the distribution of Al, W, Ti, Ta and
Ni in the oxide scale. In the 30Ni6Cr and 30Ni8Cr alloys, W enrich-
ment at the top oxide layer can be found which corresponds to the
presence of the CoWO 4 phase according to Table 2. Unlike the uni-
form distribution of Al in the intermediate layer in the 30Ni0Cr
alloy, enrichment of Al at the top and bottom of the intermediate
oxide layer can be observed in Cr-containing alloys. Based on the
quantitative EDS results, the CoAl 2O4 spinel phase forms at the
top and Al 2O3 at the bottom of the intermediate layer. In the
regions between the two Al enriched layers, Co and Ni are
depleted, while layers of W, Ti, Ta and Cr enrichments could be
detected. In the 30Ni10Cr alloy, in most cases, the top layer is again
enriched by Co, Ni and O. However, in the intermediate layer, the
element distribution is quite similar to those in the 30Ni6Cr and
30Ni8Cr alloys. Close to the substrate a layer enriched in Cr and
a layer enriched in Al were found, in which Cr
2O3 and Al2O3 oxides
exist, respectively. The formation of continuous Cr 2O3 and Al 2O3
layers slows down or prohibits the further diffusion of oxygen into
the substrate, resulting in a good oxidation resistance of the Cr
containing alloys.

5. Conclusions
The oxidation behavior of Co-9Al-5W-2Ti-2Ta-xNi-yCr alloys
with different Ni and Cr contents was investigated at 900 and
1000 /C176C. The formation of oxide scales, their microstructure and
chemical composition were analyzed in detail by various charac-
terization techniques. The main conclusions can be drawn as
follows:
(1) The oxide scale of all alloys roughly consists of three oxide
layers. The addition of Ni and especially Cr can signiﬁcantly
reduce the thickness and change the microstructure and
phase composition of the oxide scales.
(2) The addition of 30 at.% Ni can improve the oxidation resis-
tance of the investigated alloys at 900 and 1000 /C176C. It can
reduce void formation in the oxide scale and has a positive
effect on avoiding oxide layer spallation.
(3) Cr promotes the formation of a continuous Al 2O3 layer,
which together with the chromium oxide layer impeded fur-
ther outward diffusion of alloying elements and inward dif-
fusion of oxygen.
(4) CALPHAD calculations showed that the alloying of Ni and Cr
has different effects on the chemical activities of alloying
elements. The chemical activity of Al reaches a maximum
and the Gibbs free energy of formation of Al 2O3 reaches a
minimum at a Cr content of 8 at.% in Co-30Ni-9Al-5W-2Ti-
2Ta-yCr alloys.
