1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), which comprise multiple principal ele -
ments in near-equimolar proportions, have garnered significant atten -
tion owing to their exceptional combinations of mechanical, thermal, 
and chemical properties [ 1 – 4 ]. A notable subclass of HEAs, refractory 
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), is based predominantly on refractory 
metals such as titanium, hafnium, niobium, tantalum, zirconium, mo -
lybdenum, and tungsten [ 5 – 10 ]. Pioneering work by Senkov et al. has 
demonstrated the promising mechanical properties of body-centered 
cubic (BCC) RHEAs, including NbMoTaW [ 5 ], VNbMoTaW [ 11 ], 
CrNbTiVZr [ 12 ], and TiHfNbTaZr [ 6 ]. These alloys exhibit high yield 
strengths ranging from 900 to 1600 MPa at room temperature [ 13 ], 
retain substantial strength at elevated temperatures [ 14 ] and display 
excellent corrosion resistance [ 15 ]. Such attributes render RHEAs strong 
candidates for demanding applications in aerospace and biomedical 
engineering [ 13 , 16 – 19 ].
RHEAs and the derived refractory medium-entropy alloys (RMEAs) 
typically suffer from poor room-temperature plasticity and strong 
sensitivity to their preparation and processing routes, which severely 
hinder their engineering applications. Most existing studies prioritize 
alloy compositions over processing technologies; the as-cast micro -
structures investigated in these works commonly exhibit issues 
including coarse grains, compositional segregation and precipitation of 
brittle phases, thereby greatly limiting the optimization of mechanical 
performance. To date, only the equiatomic NbTaTiHfZr HEA and its sub- 
system MEAs, such as TaHfTiZr, NbTaTiHf and TiZrHf, have demon -
strated considerable room-temperature tensile plasticity. Limited 
studies on these alloy systems have revealed that different processing 
histories, including casting, mechanical processing (e.g., cold rolling, 
hot rolling, forging) and subsequent heat treatment, can significantly 
alter the alloy microstructure (e.g., phase constituents, grain size, and 
distribution of precipitated phases) as well as its evolution mechanism, 
particularly the strength-ductility synergy. Chen et al. [ 20 ] reported that 
cold-rolled NbTaTiHfZr alloy annealed above 1000 
◦
C forms a 
single-phase BCC structure, whereas phase separation occurs at inter -
mediate annealing temperatures. Senkov et al. [ 21 ] observed that 
although high-temperature annealing produced a single-phase

In this study, an equiatomic NbTaTiHf RMEA was fabricated via cold 
crucible induction melting (CCIM). The ingot was subsequently hot- 
rolled to produce sheet material, followed by annealing at various 
temperatures to mitigate casting-induced porosity. The microstructure 
of the hot-rolled alloy, both in the as-deformed state and after post- 
rolling annealing, was systematically characterized to elucidate the 
evolution of structural features in response to distinct thermomechan -
ical processing histories. Quasi-static tensile tests were performed to 
evaluate the tensile strength and ductility under each condition. Com -
plementary fractographic analyses of the fracture surfaces were con -
ducted to discern the operative failure mechanisms associated with 
different heat treatments.

4. Discussion
4.1. The formation mechanism of kink bands of RHEA/MEAs during hot 
rolling
The primary slip systems in BCC metals and alloys include the {110}, 
{112}, and {123} planes, with the slip direction being < 111 > . At 
ambient or intermediate temperatures, initiating slip along the < 111 >
direction is challenging due to the high Peierls barrier. This difficulty is 
exacerbated in HEAs, where significant lattice distortions induced by the 
mixing of multiple principal elements severely impede dislocation mo -
tion. Consequently, during intense plastic deformation processes such as 
hot rolling, when traditional slip mechanisms fail to adequately 
accommodate deformation, unconventional mechanisms, including 
mechanical twinning, phase transformations, and kink band formation, 
become active. Kink bands are localized shear deformation zones char -
acterized by rigid rotations around an intragranular axis, resulting in 
abrupt changes in orientation. These formations typically occur in sit -
uations where slip is restricted yet significant strain accommodation is 
required, particularly in materials with low symmetry or high critical 
resolved shear stresses.
Physically, the rotation axis lies in the slip plane and is perpendicular 
to the Burgers vector. The lattice rotation axis T can be determined by 
the equation of T = γ b × n , where b and n are Burgers vector and the 
normal vector of the relevant slip system respectively [ 30 ]. The kink 
band formation and the crystallographic relationship with the slip plane 
and slip direction are shown in Fig. 14 . In BCC alloy, the 1/2 < 111 > type 
Burgers vector b dictates the plastic deformation and the dislocation slip 
usually occurs on {110}, {112} and {123} planes. Based on the com -

occurrence of kink bands with the lattice rotation axis of < 110 > cor -

rolling process.
In HEAs, the pronounced lattice distortion and kinetic constraints 
contribute to increased kinking tendencies. Large atomic size mis -
matches among constituent elements intensify local stress fields, 
impeding dislocation mobility. Additionally, kinetic constraints impede 
dislocations from bypassing obstacles via mechanisms such as disloca -
tion climb. During hot rolling, variations in strain distribution across 
different areas (such as surface versus core regions, or shear zones versus 
compression zones) can result in localized stress concentrations, favor -
ing the formation of kink bands in grains with specific orientations. 
Thus, even though elevated temperatures during hot rolling aid in 
activating slip, localized stress concentrations may still cause uneven 
slip, leading to kink band development.
In summary, the formation of kink bands rotating about the < 110 >
axis in BCC HEA following hot rolling represents an alternative defor -
mation mechanism activated to accommodate large plastic strains under 
conditions where conventional slip is impeded. This preference for 
< 110 > as the rotation axis stems from the inherent symmetry of the BCC 
lattice and the favorable energetic and geometric characteristics asso -
ciated with this orientation. The pronounced chemical complexity of 
high-entropy alloys further suppresses dislocation-mediated slip, 
thereby facilitating the emergence of such non-conventional deforma -
tion features.

limited. Consequently, the resulting microstructure, and hence me -

4.3. Processing history effect on the tensile properties of RHEAs/MEAs
A comparison of the tensile properties of the NbTaTiHf RMEA across 
different processing histories reveals that the processing history exerts

the NbTaTiHfZr RHEA, where microstructural and grain size differences 
induced by processing resulted in yield strength variations of merely 
~14%. A second common feature of these BCC RHEAs/RMEAs is their 
negligible work-hardening capacity, as evidenced by yield-to-ultimate 
strength ratios consistently exceeding 95%. For single phase HEAs/ -
MEAs, room-temperature mechanical behavior is typically governed by 
three classical strengthening mechanisms: solid-solution strengthening, 
work hardening, and grain-boundary (Hall – Petch) strengthening. 
Quantifying the relative contributions of these mechanisms in BCC 
RHEAs/RMEAs is essential to elucidate their underlying deformation 
physics and guide rational alloy design.
In HEA solid solutions, the nature of solute – dislocation interactions 
evolves from isolated solute atoms in dilute alloys to complex, multi- 
component “ collective solute ” environments. Phenomenological 
models attribute solid-solution strengthening primarily to atomic size 
and elastic modulus mismatches among principal elements, which 
elevate lattice friction stress. BCC metals and their solid solutions are 
distinguished by screw dislocations with non-planar core structures that 
glide via the nucleation and migration of kink pairs. Consequently, the 
yield strength of BCC RHEAs/RMEAs comprises both thermally acti -
vated and athermal components. The latter exhibits weak temperature 
dependence, while alloying significantly raises the thermal activation 
barrier, widely regarded as the origin of their high strength at ambient 
temperature.

extreme grain-size contrasts (e.g., nanocrystalline vs. coarse-grained),

grain-size changes during annealing, potentially activating alternative 
strengthening mechanisms. Thus, definitive conclusions regarding the 
role of grain size in BCC HEAs/MEAs require further controlled 
investigations.
Work hardening, the increase in flow stress with plastic strain, arises 
from dislocation multiplication and interactions, reflecting a material's 
capacity for uniform plastic deformation. FCC HEAs/MEAs generally 
exhibit strong work-hardening capability, with yield-to-ultimate 
strength ratios ranging from 0.5 to 0.9 and uniform elongations often 
exceeding 20% [ 3 , 41 – 43 , 46 , 47 ]. In Cantor alloy (CrMnFeCoNi), George 
et al. observed progressive dislocation structure evolution: at low strains

at high strains, dislocation cells form. Taylor-type analysis, Δ σ = α Gb √ ρ , 
yields α = 0.4, comparable to conventional FCC metals [ 48 , 49 ], con -
firming that FCC HEAs/MEAs follow classical dislocation-mediated 
hardening.
Conversely, despite exhibiting tensile ductilities > 15%, BCC RHEAs/ 
RMEAs typically display flat stress – strain curves with yield-to-ultimate 
strength ratios > 95% [ 13 , 21 , 22 , 50 – 53 ]. Guillot et al. [ 24 ] investigated 
dislocation behavior in as-cast Senkov alloy under compression. Screw 
dislocations with b = a/2 〈 111 〉 dominate, evolving through three stages: 
(i) at low strain, long, straight screw dislocations form band-like struc -
tures with sparse loops/dipoles; (ii) at intermediate strain, dislocation 
density increases within bands, loops proliferate, and low-density dis -

cross-slip and entanglement. This behavior closely mirrors that of con -

model to NbTaTiHfZr and obtained α = 0.16, comparable to traditional 
BCC metals but less than half that of Cantor alloy, which confirmed the 
limited contribution of forest hardening to work hardening in these 
alloys.
In summary, current evidence indicates that for single-phase BCC 
RHEAs/RMEAs, both grain refinement strengthening and work hard -
ening are markedly less effective than in their FCC counterparts. Their 
high strength is predominantly attributed to pronounced solid-solution 
strengthening arising from multi-principal-element interactions. 
Consequently, the choice of alloying elements, and their physicochem -
ical contrasts, exerts a far greater influence on mechanical properties 
than the number of components or post-solidification processing history.
