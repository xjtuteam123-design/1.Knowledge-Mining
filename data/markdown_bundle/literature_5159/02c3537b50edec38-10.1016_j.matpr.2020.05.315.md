abstract
The conventional titanium-based alloys, Ti-6Al-4 V in particular, have shown biological toxicity and
mechanical incompatibilities which leads to implant loosening eventually requiring revision surgery.
For that reason, there has been a growing interest in the development of new generation b-titanium
biomedical alloys with improved mechanical properties speciﬁcally a lower Young’s modulus and
enhanced biocompatibility. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the inﬂuence of Nb content on
microstructural evolution and tensile properties of Ti-Nb alloys. Ti-Nb alloys containing 13 and 28 wt%
Nb were cast using the vacuum arc remelting process. Microstructural changes were evaluated using
the optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy dispersive
spectrometer (EDX). Phases present were determined by means of X-ray diffraction (XRD). The Vickers
micro-hardness and tensile tests were performed to determine the mechanical properties of the Ti-Nb
binary alloys. Experimental ﬁndings indicate that the microstructural and mechanical properties of the
as-cast Ti-Nb alloys are both sensitive to Nb content. With increased Nb content, the microstructure
evolved from the martensitic structure to the b-phase whereas the yield strength, ultimate tensile
strength, and Young’s modulus all decreased and conversely, the micro-hardness increased.

1. Introduction
Metallic biomaterials have been used for different medical
applications as implants to replace certain parts of the human body
alone or as a part of a complex system to restore the lost form or
function of the biological structure such as total hip replacements,
spinal ﬁxation devices, dental applications and cardiovascular
tents [1-3]. It has been estimated that by 2030 there will be
approximately 300 000 total hip replacements done annually and
due to degenerative diseases such as arthritis, musculoskeletal dis-
orders [2,3] and in China alone approximately 400 000 total hip
replacements were done in 2015 [4].
Metallic biomaterials that have been used are cobalt-chromium
alloys, stainless steels, titanium and its alloys. However, titanium
alloys show advantages compared to the other biomaterials with
lower elastic moduli and enhanced biocompatibility [3,5,6].
To-date, the use of titanium as an implant has been primarily lim-
ited to the widely available commercially pure titanium and Ti-
6Al-4 V.
Recent studies have uncovered two particularly adverse biolog-
ical and mechanical incompatibilities. Firstly, Ti-6Al-4 V has a
Young’s modulus (/C24110 GPa) signiﬁcantly higher than that of the
human bone (10 /C2440 GPa) [7-9]. Studies have shown that this
mismatch in elastic moduli leads to insufﬁcient mechanical load
transfer from the implant to the bone and this results in bone
resorption thus implant failure or the stress-shielding effect [7-
10]. Secondly, the adverse release of the alloying elements (as Al
and V ions) from Ti-6Al-4 V has also been associated with cytotoxic
effects and neurological disorders. The wear debris causes adverse
allergic reactions that stimulate the release of pro- inﬂammatory
and osteolytic mediators that causes loosening of the implant
and subsequent revision of the medical procedure [11,12].
An ideal next- generation implant biomaterial must exhibit
both biological and mechanical compatibility [13]. Beta-titanium
alloys have attracted interest as such due to their versatile

the microstructures as well as their effect on the mechanical prop-

2. Materials and method
Titanium-niobium alloys (13 and 28 wt%) were prepared by
vacuum arc remelting process. They were prepared using titanium
(Ti) (99.8% purity, /C0150 mm) and niobium (Nb) (99.8% purity,
1–5 mm) elemental powders. The Ti-Nb alloys were weighed
according to their respective compositions and cold-pressed using
a manually controlled hydraulic press with a pressure of about
500–800 MPa, into 100 g green compacts [17]. The green compacts
were arc melted using a tungsten electrode on a water-cooled cop-
per hearth in a button arc furnace. The furnace was evacuated and
purged with argon three times and a titanium ingot was melted
prior to each melting step to remove impurities such as oxygen
and nitrogen. The samples were turned and re-melted three times
in order to achieve chemical homogeneity and in order to avoid
microscopic segregation that usually occurs in conventional cast
products.
The as-cast ingots were precision-cut then hot mounted using a
Bakelite resin. The samples were mechanically polished to a
mirror-like ﬁnish surface using SiC paper up 2000 mesh, 3 mm dia-
mond suspension and colloidal silica. The Ti-Nb polished samples
were etched using Kroll’s reagent (2 ml HF, 6 ml HNO
3, 92 ml dis-
tilled H 2O). The etched samples were analyzed using the optical
microscope (Leica DMI5000 M) and the Jeol JSM651 scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy dispersive spectrom-
eter (EDX) to determine the microstructural evolution. X-ray
diffraction (XRD) for phase analysis of the samples was done using
the PANalytical X’PertTM Pro powder diffractometer in 2 h conﬁgura-
tion with an X’CeleratorTM detector with Fe ﬁltered Co-K a radiation
(k = 1.789 Å). The powder diffraction patterns were collected using
the Bragg-Brentano geometry over a 20 /C176– 100 /C1762h range in steps
on 0.02/C176at a sampling time of approximately 2 s per step and ana-
lyzed. The voltage on the anode of the X-ray tube was 50 kV, and
the current was 30 mA. The hardness of the as-cast samples was
measured using a Vickers micro-hardness tester (FM-700) under
a load of 0.5 kg with a dwell time of 15 s. The tensile properties
were determined using the Instron TM 1542 tensile testing system
at room temperature at a constant strain rate of 0.5 mm/min.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and phase analysis
The microstructural evolution of the as-cast Ti-Nb binary alloys
is illustrated in Fig. 2. Fig. 2(a and b) demonstrates that the Ti-13Nb
alloy is characterized predominantly by acicular a’-martensite
microstructure and a”-martensite as identiﬁed by XRD analysis
as the acicular martensitic microstructures are morphologically
similar. The b-phase was observed, with the a”-martensite
microstructure retained within b-phase grains, when the Nb con-
tent increased to 28 wt% as shown in Fig. 2 (c and d).
Fast cooling from the b-phase ﬁeld of pure Ti and dilute Ti-
alloys can result in two different crystal structures of microstruc-
tures, the hexagonal aʹ-martensite and the orthorhombic a”-
martensite [19,20]. The martensitic transformation start ( Ms) and
martensitic ﬁnish ( Mf) temperatures are dependent on the concen-
tration of the b-stabilizing element (Nb), as the content increases
both transformation temperatures decreases [20]. Therefore, when
the Nb content increased from 13 wt% to 28 wt%, the Mf declined
below room temperature and the b-phase was retained. As the
Nb content increased, the microstructures evolved from the
martensitic structure to a mixture of the b-phase and martensite.
This observation is also consistent with previous ﬁndings [10,20].
The phases present in the Ti-13Nb and Ti-28Nb alloys are
shown in Fig. 3 . The Ti-13Nb XRD pattern conﬁrms the predomi-
nant a’-martensite microstructure shown in Fig. 2 . The observed
splitting of the Ti-13Nb XRD pattern peaks is a typical indicator
of the presence of the orthorhombic a00 phase that other studies
on titanium-niobium alloys have reported [10,20]. The Ti-28Nb
XRD pattern exhibits a mixture of a00-orthorhombic phase and
body centered cubic b-phase (BCC) which is consistent with the
microstructural observations. There is also a possibility that the
x-phase is present in Ti-28Nb alloy, however, it was not detected
due to the detection limit in the XRD instrument [9]. The hexago-

3.2. Mechanical properties
Table 1 below shows the Vickers micro-hardness and tensile
properties of the two Ti-Nb alloys, while Fig. 4 illustrates the
strain–stress curve of the alloys. The micro-hardness increased
with increasing Nb content from 276 HV 0.5 for Ti-13Nb to 287
HV0.5 for Ti-28Nb. The increase in hardness may be attributed to
the increase in the possible presence of the x-phase in the alloy
as it has been reported to be present in alloys with compositions
of 25–35 wt% Nb [10,21].The Young’s modulus decreased as the
Nb content increased with Ti-28Nb having the lower E-value of
80.99 GPa; the decrease is due to the reduction of the proportion
of the martensitic phase and the b-phase being retained. The b-
phase has been reported to have the lowest Young’s modulus
whilst the x-phase has the highest [23]. The ultimate tensile
strength (UTS), yield strength (0.2% offset YS) and the tensile duc-
tility all decreased as the Nb content increased. Like the elastic
modulus, the change in the elongation with increase of Nb content
is consistent with previously published data [24]. The b-phase has

4. Conclusions
The effect of Nb content in binary Ti-Nb alloys was evaluated
after vacuum arc remelting process, microstructural analysis and
tensile testing. Findings from the study indicate that the
microstructural and mechanical properties of the as-cast binary
Ti-Nb alloys are generally sensitive to Nb content variations. The
following conclusions were drawn:
/C15The microstructure evolved from a fully-martensitic structure
to the beta-phase with martensite in the b-grain structure,
/C15The Young’s modulus decreased due to the presence of the b-
phase being retained as the Nb content increased,
/C15The ultimate tensile strength, yielding strength and elongation
of the alloys decreased with increasing Nb content from 13 to
28 wt%, and
/C15The Vickers micro-hardness increased as the Nb content
increased.
