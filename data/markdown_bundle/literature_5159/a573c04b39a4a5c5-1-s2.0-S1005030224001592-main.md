a b s t r a c t 
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are potential materials for high-temperature structural applica- 
tions due to their excellent high-temperature performance. However, the early onset of plastic instability 
in RHEAs is a critical issue to be addressed. In this study, a novel Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 RHEA was subjected 
to 95% cold rolling and annealing, achieving outstanding mechanical properties. Notably, the cold-rolled 
alloy exhibited the highest reported tensile strength ( ∼2.0 GPa) among RHEAs with a moderate ductil- 
ity of ∼8.5%. Moreover, Additionally, the subsequent annealing process enabled the alloy to achieve an 
outstanding combination of yield strength (1.1 GPa) and fracture elongation (20%). The cold-rolled alloy 
has a heterogeneous lamella structure with high-density dislocation cells and dislocation pile-ups, and 
lattice distortion in the locally chemically ordered structure. The high work hardening behavior origi- 
nates from the heterogeneous deformation-induced (HDI) hardening, which makes the HDI stress and 
hardening rate increase with increasing strain. In the annealed alloy, lattice distortion increases signiﬁ- 
cantly with increasing strain, promoting dislocation cross-slip. The dislocation pile-ups and interactions 
facilitate the transition of microbands, and the relatively stable work-hardening behavior of the alloy is 
mainly attributed to the microband-induced plasticity effect. This study provides novel insights into the 
design and optimization of highly formable RHEAs with excellent mechanical properties.

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new class of metallic mate- 
rials composed of ﬁve or more primary elements in near-equal 
atomic proportions [1] . Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are 
a subgroup of HEAs primarily composed of refractory elements (Zr, 
Ti, Nb, Cr, Hf, Mo, V, Ta, and W), optionally alloyed with non- 
refractory elements such as Ni, Al, Co, and Si [2] . Compared to con- 
ventional alloys, RHEAs present a high degree of microstructural 
controllability and exceptional mechanical properties, including el- 
evated ultimate strength, resistance to fatigue and oxidation, and 
improved mechanical performance at high operating temperatures 
[3–7] . These superior properties open up extensive prospects for 
the future design of advanced materials. 
RHEAs typically exhibit a body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal 
structure, which restricts their slip systems and results in high

yield strength but low ductility and toughness at room tempera- 
ture [8] . According to the Considère criterion [9] , necking instabil- 
ity occurs when the strain hardening rate ( θ) is lower than the 
ﬂow stress ( θ < σ). Therefore, maintaining a high strain hardening 
rate is crucial under high stress to avoid necking instability. Reduc- 
ing the stacking fault energy is an effective approach to increase 
the strain hardening rate, as it can promote the formation of de- 
formation twins [ 10 , 11 ]. Twin boundaries can serve as sources of 
partial dislocations that hinder their movement, thereby enhanc- 
ing the strain-hardening ability of the materials [12] . However, this 
strategy is generally applicable to alloys with low yield strength 
and may not be suitable for BCC RHEAs with high yield strength 
[13–15] . 
Recently, dual-phase HEAs with heterogeneous microstructures 
created by severe plastic deformation (SPD) are an effective ap- 
proach for achieving high strain hardening rates [16–19] . In these 
complex heterogeneous structures, which include multiscale (such 
as gradient, bimodal, lamellar, etc.) and multicomponent (such as 
grains and phases) heterogeneities, large strain gradients are gen- 
erated during deformation, which can effectively realize hetero-

ample, Zhou et al. [24] adjusted the microstructure (equiaxed, 
lamellar, and bimodal) of a Ti-6Al-2Sn-4Zr-2Mo-0.1Si alloy through 
thermal deformation and heat treatment, achieving a synergistic 
improvement in the strength and ductility through the coordi- 
nated action of multiscale α-grains. Mu et al. [25] combined a 
high-density dislocation structure with high-volume-fraction duc- 
tile nano-precipitates to produce dislocation-precipitation skele- 
ton structures and multilayered fault structures, resulting in an 
ultra-high tensile strength of ∼1.8 GPa and a maximum elon- 
gation of 16% in Fe28 Co29.5 Ni27.5 Al8.5 Ti6.5 alloy. Additionally, the 
microband-induced plasticity (MBIP) effect has been observed in 
some heterogeneous-deformation RHEAs with FCC or FCC/BCC dual 
phases [ 19 , 21 ]. When the MBIP occurs, microbands (MBs) can pro- 
vide additional obstacles for dislocation motion, interact with other 
deformation mechanisms (such as stacking fault formation, phase 
transformation, and martensitic transformation), and lead to sus- 
tained strain hardening, suppressing the occurrence of plastic in- 
stability [ 26 , 27 ]. However, it should be noted that these studies 
were only reported in FCC single-phase or FCC/BCC dual-phase 
HEAs, which have good formability [ 28 , 29 ]. BCC RHEAs are sig- 
niﬁcantly diﬃcult to process by thermomechanical methods, and 
thus till now, the introduction of HDI or MBIP in BCC RHEAs has 
been scarcely reported. BCC RHEAs have high strength with low 
strain hardening ability, which leads to instability after yielding 
[30] . Therefore, introducing HDI hardening or MBIP mechanism 
into RHEAs to improve the post-yield stability of formable RHEAs 
seems to be a feasible strategy. 
According to the empirical criterion of valence electron concen- 
tration (VEC) [31] , when the VEC of BCC RHEA is ≤ 4.6, it exhibits 
inherent plasticity and toughness. The VEC of Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 
RHEA is 4.15. In the meantime, this RHEA has been proven 
to possess excellent tensile ductility [32] , which enables the 
Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 RHEA as a potential material for SPD treatment. 
The Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 RHEA exhibits a signiﬁcant atomic size dif- 
ference and a notably negative mixing enthalpy between Zr and Al 
elements [32] . These characteristics are utilized to induce a sub- 
stantial level of locally chemical ordered (LCO) and lattice distor- 
tion [33] . In this study, the HDI hardening and MBIP mechanisms 
were applied to the Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 BCC RHEA developed by 
using 95% cold rolling and annealing, successfully overcoming the 
room temperature plastic instability of the RHEA and achieving re- 
markable mechanical properties. At room temperature, the cold- 
rolled alloy exhibited an ultra-high tensile strength of ∼2.0 GPa 
and a tensile elongation of 8.5%, while the annealed alloy exhib- 
ited a high yield strength of 1.1 GPa and a high elongation of 
20%. The aim of this study was to prepare heterogeneous mate- 
rials through SPD and investigate the effect of HDI hardening on 
the strength and strain hardening ability of the cold-rolled alloy 
by analyzing its loading-unloading hysteresis loops. Moreover, this 
work aimed to reveal the steady-state effect of the MBIP mecha- 
nism on the work hardening behavior by analyzing the microstruc- 
tural changes at various tensile deformation amounts of the an- 
nealed alloy. These ﬁndings provide new insights for the develop- 
ment of high-performance RHEAs in the future. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Alloys processing 
The RHEA with a composition of Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 (at.%) was 
synthesized via vacuum arc melting, using raw materials with pu- 
rity exceeding 99.95 wt.% in a high-purity argon atmosphere. The 
alloy was subjected to a minimum of eight times melting and sub- 
sequently cast into a cylindrical ingot of ϕ 20 mm × 300 mm 
using suction casting. Subsequently, the ingot was cold rolled at 
room temperature to a ﬁnal thickness of 1 mm (a reduction of 
95%) in each pass of 0.2–0.3 mm. The cold-rolled alloy was an- 
nealed at 850 °C for 5 min in a quartz tube ﬁlled with argon gas 
and then water-quenched after breaking the quartz tube. The cold- 
rolled and annealed alloy plates were named CR95 and CR95-850, 
respectively. To achieve a microstructure with homogeneous grains, 
the cold-rolled alloy was annealed at 850 °C for 10 min in a quartz 
tube ﬁlled with argon gas and then water-quenched after break- 
ing the quartz tube. The annealed alloy was named as HG-850. 
Dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with effective dimensions of 
1.0 mm (thickness) × 3.5 mm (width) × 21.5 mm (length) were 
prepared using an electrical discharge cutting machine and pol- 
ished with 20 0 0 grit sandpaper. 
2.2. Characterization methods 
The phase composition and lattice parameters of the CR95, 
CR95-850, and HG-850 alloys were tested using a Bruker D2 Phaser 
X-ray diffractometer with Cu-K α radiation. The scanning range was 
10 °–100 °, and the scanning rate was 5 ° min–1 . Electron backscat- 
tered diffraction (EBSD) was conducted using a Zeiss Sigma ZESS 
Ultra55. The geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) density 
was determined from the EBSD data using the MTEX toolbox im- 
plemented in Matlab. Prior to EBSD measurement, the samples un- 
derwent mechanical polishing followed by twin-jet polishing at 
a temperature of –30 °C and an applied voltage of 30 V. The 
polishing was executed using a solution containing 5% perchloric 
acid, 35% 2-butoxyethanol, and 60% methanol. The microstructure 
was characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hi- 
tachi SU50 0 0) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) for elemental analysis. The microstructure was further an- 
alyzed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos 
F20 0) at 20 0 kV to investigate the stable work hardening behav- 
ior of the CR95-850 alloy. The deformation zones after interrupted 
tensile tests at true strains of ε = 4%, ε = 10%, and ε = 18% were 
analyzed using TEM. 
2.3. Mechanical testing 
Tensile tests were carried out at room temperature using an 
Instron 5566 machine, employing a strain rate of 1.0 × 10–3 s–1 . 
Each alloy was subjected to no less than three tests to ensure the 
validity of the results. Load-unload-reload tests at room temper- 
ature were also performed using the same apparatus. The CR95, 
CR95-850, and HG-850 alloys were stretched at a strain rate of 
1.0 × 10–3 s–1 to a certain strain, then unloaded at a load rate of 
20 0 0 N min–1 to 30 N in load control mode and reloaded at the 
same strain rate to the next unloading strain.

3.3. Microstructure of the CR95-850 alloy at different tensile strains 
Fig. 4 (b) shows that the CR95-850 alloy exhibited stable work 
hardening behavior in the strain range of 4%–16%, which is dif- 
ferent from conventional RHEAs. To investigate the microstruc- 
tural transitions responsible for this unique work-hardening be- 
havior, TEM characterization was performed on the deformed re- 
gions of interrupted tensile samples at true strains of ε = 4% (the 
beginning of stable work-hardening behavior), ε = 10% (during 
stable work hardening behavior), and ε = 18% (the end of sta- 
ble work hardening behavior). Fig. 6 shows the TEM images of 
the microstructure of the CR95-850 alloy after 4% tensile defor- 
mation. Fig. 6 (a) displays a BF-TEM image of the CR95-850 alloy, 
from which dislocations and microbands are observed, indicating 
the occurrence of the MBIP mechanism during tensile deforma- 
tion [29] . Fig. 6 (b) shows the HR-TEM image of the interior re- 
gion of a grain, revealing the presence of lattice distortion in the 
LCO atomic structure of the matrix. To observe the microstructure 
more clearly, DF-TEM images of the CR95-850 alloy after 4% defor- 
mation are shown in Fig. 6 (c, d). The images reveal high-density 
dislocation walls (HDDWs) and a large number of dislocation pile- 
ups (with diameters of ∼100 nm) inside the grains, which hin- 
der the movement of dislocations and can lead to sustained work 
hardening. 
Fig. 7 shows the TEM images of the CR95-850 alloy after tensile 
deformation to 10%. Fig. 7 (a, c, d) reveals the presence of HDDWs, 
dislocation pile-ups, dislocation pinning, dislocation cross slip, and 
microbands. Compared to the 4% strain condition, the alloy at 10% 
strain exhibited more frequent dislocation cross slip, dislocation 
pile-ups, and HDDWs, which can transform some of the disloca- 
tion slip into more stable three-dimensional dislocation pile-ups, 
providing sustained strain hardening [40] . Fig. 7 (b) shows the HR- 
TEM image, with the IFFT image revealing an obvious increase in 
lattice distortion at the nanoscale. This is due to the fact that with 
the increasing deformation, lattice distortion will slip along the slip 
plane towards the slip direction, leading to an increase in disloca- 
tion density [41] . Moreover, the presence of lattice distortion en- 
hances the hindrance effect on dislocation motion, promoting dis- 
location cross slip and enhancing the interaction between disloca- 
tions [42] .

4.1. Effect of HDI hardening mechanism on CR95 alloy 
Under normal circumstances, an alloy cannot maintain sus- 
tained work hardening without necking and dislocation dynamic 
annihilation during SPD [ 43 , 44 ]. However, the CR95 alloy exhibits 
high work hardening behavior after 95% cold rolling, as shown 
in Fig. 4 (a, b), which is attributed to the inﬂuence of the het- 
erostructure produced by SPD. It has been reported that a het- 
erostructure can further enhance the strain-hardening rate [45] . 
The IPF image in Fig. 1 (b1) shows that the CR95 alloy has an 
HL structure. The KAM map shows a high local orientation dif- 
ference at ∼2 ° ( Fig. 1 (b3)), indicating the presence of many de- 
formed ﬁne grains within the HL structure, which increases the 
yield strength and tensile strength [46] . In addition, a high den- 
sity of GNDs accumulates at the boundary of the HL structure do- 
main ( Fig. 1 (b4)), which can resist dislocation motion and lead to 
signiﬁcant strain hardening [47] . The BF-TEM image in Fig. 3 (a) 
shows the presence of high-density dislocation cells and disloca- 
tion pile-ups in the CR95 alloy. This makes the stress distribu- 
tion inside the material uneven, increases the resistance to dis- 
location motion, and thus enhances the strength of the materials 
[48] . The IFFT image in Fig. 3 (b) shows the presence of lattice dis- 
tortion in the LCO atomic arrangement. Wang et al. [33] showed 
that SPD gradually destroyed the LCO, resulting in excessive lat- 
tice distortion, which promoted elastic strain and residual dis- 
locations to trigger dynamic hardening. The back stress is pro- 
duced by GNDs [20] . In hetero-structured deformation, the un- 
even distribution of stress within the material necessitates the 
accommodation through GNDs [ 47 , 49 ], which in turn produces 
back stress-induced hardening, thus achieving HDI hardening 
[20] . 
At a macroscopic scale, the magnitude of HDI stress can be 
evaluated by the loading-unloading hysteresis loop, which is re- 
ﬂected by the back stress measured in the loading-unloading- 
reloading test [50] . Fig. 9 (a) presents the loading-unloading- 
reloading test results of the CR95, CR95-850, and HG-850 alloys, 
while Fig. 9 (b) speciﬁcally shows the third unloading-reloading 
loop. The loading-unloading-reloading tensile curve of the CR95 
alloy exhibits a clear hysteresis loop, indicating the occurrence 
of back stress hardening during the tensile process [51] . In con- 
trast, no hysteresis loops are observed in the loading-unloading- 
reloading curves of the CR95-850 and HG-850 alloy, suggesting the 
absence of back-stress hardening in both samples [51] . The HG- 
850 alloy possesses a homogeneous grain structure with an av- 
erage grain size similar to that of the CR95-850 alloy ( Fig. 1 (c1, 
d1)). In comparison to the HG-850 alloy, although the CR95-850 
alloy exhibits microscale grain structure heterogeneity with small 
recrystallized grains and large deformed grains, the distribution of 
GNDs within the heterogeneous microstructure does not show sig- 
niﬁcant heterogeneity ( Fig. 1 (c5)), resulting in the absence of HDI 
hardening. This result indicates that, for the Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 al- 
loy, regardless of whether it possesses homogeneous or heteroge- 
neous equiaxed grains, the variation in grain size distribution is not 
the primary factor contributing to HDI hardening. In contrast, the 
CR95 alloy possesses an HL structure with high-density GNDs ac- 
cumulated at the deformation band boundaries ( Fig. 1 (b4)). These 
high-density GNDs generate elevated HDI stresses during stress re- 
distribution, resulting in HDI hardening [ 52 , 53 ]. The parameters σly 
and σuy represent the yield stress during loading and unloading, 
respectively, while E denotes the effective elastic modulus. Since 
the dislocation conﬁguration is reversible during the unloading and 
reloading processes [54] , the back stress derived from the reload- 
ing curve is considered equivalent to that from the correspond- 
ing unloading curve. Therefore, calculations were performed using

4.2. Effect of MBIP deformation on CR95-850 alloy 
The CR95-850 alloy exhibits outstanding mechanical properties, 
with a yield strength of 1.1 GPa and a fracture elongation of 20%. 
Short-time annealing makes the severely plastically deformed alloy 
partially recrystallize, which leads to microstructural differences 
in grain structure at the microscale and defect heterogeneity at 
the nanoscale, as depicted in Figs. 1 (c1, c3) and 3 (f). Moreover, 
the alloy exhibits a relatively steady work hardening behavior in 
the strain range of 4%–16% ( Fig. 4 (b)), which is typically associ- 
ated with TWIP or TRIP effects [ 62 , 63 ]. However, due to the high 
stacking fault energy of RHEAs, TWIP, or TRIP effects are not eas- 
ily induced during plastic deformation. Thus, this unusual work- 
hardening behavior is dominated by other mechanisms.

5. Conclusions 
In this study, the microstructure and mechanical properties of 
the Zr35 Ti30 Nb20 Al10 V5 RHEA with a BCC + B2 structure were reg- 
ulated by 95% cold rolling and annealing treatment. The main con- 
clusions are as follows: 
(1) The CR95 alloy possesses an ultra-high ultimate tensile strength 
(2.0 GPa), the highest reported strength value among stretch- 
able RHEAs, and good tensile ductility (8.5%). The SPD in- 
duces the formation of a heterogeneous structure, which con- 
tains abundant GNDs, high-density dislocation cells, and dis- 
location pile-ups, as well as lattice distortion in the nanoscale 
LCO atomic structure. The HDI strengthening mechanism is the 
main reason for the strength and strain hardening improvement 
of the CR95 alloy. 
(2) The CR95-850 alloy achieves a yield strength of 1.1 GPa and a 
fracture elongation of 20%, demonstrating an outstanding com- 
bination of strength and ductility within the RHEAs system. The 
CR95-850 alloy exhibits a heterogeneous grain structure at the 
microscale (composed of small recrystallized grains and large 
deformed grains) and heterogeneous defect distribution at the 
nanoscale (lattice distortion appearing in the LCO atomic struc- 
ture). In addition, during the tensile deformation process, the 
MBIP effect makes the alloy exhibit relatively stable work hard- 
ening behavior.
