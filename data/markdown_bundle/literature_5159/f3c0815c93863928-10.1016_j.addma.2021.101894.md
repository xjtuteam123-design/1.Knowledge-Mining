ABSTRACT 
The ultra-high strength of high γ’ nickel (Ni)-based superalloys with low ductility comes with the price of high 
susceptibility to cracking and a high residual stress state when processed with conventional powder bed fusion 
technologies. Binder jet additive manufacturing (BJAM) is an interesting alternative to other additive 
manufacturing (AM) modalities since it does not require melting to fuse powder material but selectively adheres 
powder with a binder. While there are several studies on the use of melting and solidification-based AM tech -
nologies, limited literature is available on the use of BJAM for high γ’ Ni-based superalloys. This study presents 
the advantages and disadvantages associated with the BJAM of REN
´
E 108 superalloy. The BJAM microstructure 
is characterized in the green, brown and sintered states. The formation of parallel primitive lines perpendicular 
to the build direction is observed after printing. This leads to persistent lines of porosity and a relative density 
above 96% after a sintering cycle. A fully dense microstructure is obtained after hot isostatic pressing (HIP) of the 
material. The formation of HfO
2 
and Ta-rich carbides along prior particle boundaries (PPBs) is also observed 
during the sintering stage. Precipitation along PPBs is intrinsic to the powder metallurgy of Ni-based superalloys 
and influenced by the debinding conditions of the parts. They have a strong impact on the mechanical properties 
of the material. At low temperatures ( < 760 
◦
C), the deformation mode is transgranular, and the BJAM material 
has better tensile properties than the directional solidification (DS) material. At high temperatures ( > 760 
◦
C), 
the deformation becomes intergranular, and fracture occurs at the PPBs. The ductility of BJAM REN
´
E 108 be -
comes slightly lower than the DS material while the strength of the materials remains comparable. 
1. Introduction 
Since they first emerged in the 1950s, the development of γ’ Ni-based 
superalloys is extremely strategic for the energy and transportation in -
dustries. Their complex composition, with sometime over a dozen 
alloying elements, leads to outstanding high-temperature mechanical 
properties along with good resistance against oxidation when processed 
using conventional routes [1,2] . In recent years, the research on the use 
of additive manufacturing (AM) for Ni-based superalloys has attracted 
much attention due to the possibility of building complex geometries not 
achievable using conventional techniques. 
The fabrication of high γ’ Ni-based superalloys by AM techniques, 
such as selective laser melting (SLM) [3 – 5] and electron beam melting 
(EBM) [6 – 8] has been studied over the past few years. Nonetheless, the 
complex alloy composition is prone to the formation of detrimental 
phases that can result in cracking and high residual stress state after 
processing [9] . 
Binder jetting additive manufacturing (BJAM) is another layer-by- 
layer AM approach offering freedom in terms of designing complex 
geometries [10] . It is an inherently scalable and cost-effective technol -
ogy, which has been demonstrated in many commercial systems. It can 
be faster than other AM techniques since print heads typically work 
faster than the laser, and more than one nozzle is available during raster 
scanning [11,12] . Unlike laser AM printing processes, BJAM utilizes 
inkjet printing technologies to bond the particles layer-by-layer at room 
temperature (RT). Thus, it does not induce residual stress or distortions 
assigned to large thermal gradients. Solidification cracking commonly 
encountered in SLM of high γ’ Ni-based superalloys can thus be avoided

2.2. Printing method 
A commercial binder-jet 3D-printer was used to manufacture 3D 
tensile blanks from REN
´
E 108 powder with dimensions of 60 mm in 
height and 10 mm in diameter. Fig. 3 shows a schematic of the printing 
strategy used in this study. The printed samples were oriented horizontal 
(parallel) to the powder bed, with their longitudinal directions aligned 
parallel to the X-axis. BJAM parts were produced using a binder devel -
oped by GE with binder saturation of 100 – 120%, recoat speed of 
100 – 170 mm/s, roller speed of 200 – 500 rpm, roller traverse speed of 
8 – 20 mm/s and drying speed of 8 – 12 mm/s. The layer thickness was 
100 µ m in order to optimize the build speed without significantly 
impacting the resolution of the parts. 
2.3. Debinding and sintering method 
After a part is fabricated and removed from the printing machine, it 
is in green state with a relative density of 60 – 62%. The density of the 
green state was determined by measuring the dimension and mass of the 
samples, which consist of loosely bound powder particles. The parts 
were thermally treated at 700
◦
C for 1.5 h under vacuum to remove the 
binder. The carbon content after debinding (0.1 – 0.12 wt%) is slightly 
higher than the initial powder in Table 1 (0.09 wt%) and remains con -
stant during subsequent heat treatments. The resulting, fragile and 
porous part is in brown state, which should be handled carefully. 
Finally, the part was sintered at 1150
◦

1320

and enhance mechanical properties. The heat treatment profile is shown

step closes the open porosity to allow complete densification through 
hot isostatic pressing (HIP) process.

3. Results 
3.1. Sintering microstructure of BJAM high γ ’ Ni-based superalloy 
Fig. 5 (a) and (b) shows the non-polished surface of REN
´
E 108 green 
state part. The formation of periodic parallel primitive lines (sub-surface 
pores) is observed. The lines are parallel to the longitudinal axis of the 
tensile blanks (X-axis in Fig. 3 ) and perpendicular to the building di -
rection (Z-axis). These features are inherent to the binder jetting process 
and are commonly observed by other researchers [20 – 22] . It is the 
impact of the high velocity binder droplet on the powder bed that causes 
movement and ejection of the powder particles and leads to the for -
mation of primitive lines. Metallic powder particles tend to agglomerate 
with the droplet impact to form spherical primitives (granules) with a 
continuous jetting droplet print-head. This leads to the formation of an 
impact crater around the primitive due to deformation of the powder 
bed from the moving primitive [23] . The depth of disturbance corre -
sponds to the distance between the primitives (~112 µ m in Fig. 5 ). 
Fig. 6 (a) shows that the periodic parallel lines, in the form of elon -
gated pores, remain within the structure during sintering. This is 
because the primitive lines are made of dense layers of powder over 
more porous areas. During consolidation, layers of dense powder 
consolidate faster than the porous areas. The overall relative density in 
Fig. 6 is 87.7%. The distance between the lines is reduced from 112 µ m 
to 104 µ m indicating the packing of lines due to sintering. The neck 
formation is clearly observed in Fig. 6 (b).

sintering cycle. The relative density is now above 96%. Two types of

less sintering and (ii) oval pores originating from the primitive lines. The

The periodic parallel lines are not apparent anymore in the HIP micro -

The binder jetting process generates a fine and equiaxed grain structure

3.2. Precipitation mechanisms along prior particle boundaries in BJAM 
high γ ’ Ni-based superalloys 
The use of powder metallurgy (PM) techniques for superalloys leads 
to different microstructures than those observed in the cast form. This is 
primarily due to different patterns of segregation and precipitation in 
the conventional and powder forms. Although PM typically contributes 
to more homogenous microstructures, it introduces other microstructure 
defects like gas porosities and precipitation at PPBs [25 – 27] . In Fig. 8 (a), 
these precipitates appear in BJAM superalloy microstructure as white 
phases situated along the grain boundaries. In superalloys they originate 
from two sources [27] . Firstly, the surface of powder particles is made of 
a thin oxide layer. When sintering temperature exceeds 850
◦
C, the 
oxide layer breaks down into fine particles rich in elements like Zr, ti -
tanium (Ti) and Hf. Secondly, carbides nucleate preferentially at the 
surface of metallic powder particles since diffusion kinetics are signifi -
cantly higher than in the bulk material. This leads to a network of car -
bides and oxides like the ones observed in Fig. 9 (a). Fig. 9 (b) shows that 
oxides and carbides in REN
´
E 108 are rich in Hf and Tantalum (Ta). 
A third source of precipitation along the PPBs is observed in binder 
jetting. Carbon residue from the binder also contributes to the formation 
of these precipitates in the form of Ta-rich MC carbides in REN
´
E 108. 
The high amount of reactive alloying elements like Ti, Ta and Hf makes 
the debinding challenging for high γ’ Ni-based superalloys. When the 
debinding sequence is not properly done, a char residue from the binder 
forms a film at the surface of the powder particles, as shown in Fig. 10 
(b). The char residue leads to large carbide agglomerations along triple 
points after a complete sintering cycle, as observed in Fig. 8 (b). 
Precipitates along PPBs have a strong impact on the final micro -
structure. They control the grain size through Zenner pinning mecha -
nism [28] . Various processing strategies, like heat treatments, alloy 
composition, etc., have been established over the past 50 years to modify 
the volume fraction of precipitates [27,29] . In BJAM, grain sizes as small 
as 10 µ m can be obtained when the volume fraction of precipitates is 
high. In the present work, the number of precipitates along PPBs was 
controlled to obtain a larger grain size (~60 µ m ). This is significantly 
finer than what is observed in DS materials [30] .

BJAM high γ’ Ni-based superalloy REN
´
E 108 has the following 
characteristics: 
1. The wide solidification range allows liquid phase sintering to be 
accomplished without mechanical alloying. High density ( > 96%) 
material can thus be obtained using binder jetting technology. 
2. BJAM microstructure is more homogenous compared with conven -
tional DS cast structure. It is free of macro-, micro-segregation and 
cast-type defects. Solution heat treatments can thus be accomplished 
without causing incipient melting or requiring pre-homogenization 
treatment. This allows the γ’ structure to be easily modified and 
mechanical properties to be optimized. 
3. SEM EDS mapping revealed the formation of Hf-rich oxides and Ta- 
rich carbides along prior particle boundaries. The particles originate 
from three sources: (i) initial oxide layer at the surface of the powder 
particles, (ii) initial carbon content in the alloy and (iii) carbon 
residue left from the binder. 
4. BJAM microstructure is significantly finer than conventional DS cast 
microstructure. This is because precipitation along PPBs reduces 
grain boundary mobility and controls the final grain size. After sin -
tering and solutioning, the oxides and carbides decorate the grain 
boundaries. The final grain size can be modified by controlling the 
size and volume fraction of these precipitates. 
5. BJAM high γ’ Ni-based superalloy REN
´
E 108 and DS cast REN
´
E 108 
have similar mechanical properties. BJAM is slightly stronger and 
ductile at lower temperatures when deformation mode is essentially 
transgranular, while DS cast become more ductile at higher tem -
peratures when deformation becomes intergranular. 
There are challenges associated with the fabrication of high γ’ Ni- 
based superalloy REN
´
E 108 with BJAM technology. Firstly, the forma -
tion of primitive lines can reduce the final part density. This can be 
improved by modifying the powder particle size distribution and opti -
mizing the printing parameters. Secondly, high γ’ Ni-based superalloys 
are made of reactive alloying elements that make the debinding opera -
tion critical. Finally, the level of precipitation along PPBs must be 
controlled to achieve acceptable ductility for industrial application.
