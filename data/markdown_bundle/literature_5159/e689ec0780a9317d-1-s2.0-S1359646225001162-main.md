ABSTRACT
The impact of solute elements on the stacking fault energies (SFE) in γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb is imperative to ascertain to design 
of new Ni-based superalloys. Although few experimental studies are available that determine the SFE in Ni alloys, 
a comprehensive and systematic study on the effect of solute elements on γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb is unknown. In this study, 
first-principles calculations based on density functional theory were employed to investigate the effect of solutes. 
All the relevant D -block elements were evaluated. Substitutions at Ni and Nb sites were examined to assess their 
effects on intrinsic stacking fault (ISF), superlattice intrinsic stacking fault (SISF), and anti-phase boundary (APB- 
I) energies. The study elucidated that Ti, V, Co, and Fe are expected to inhibit the formation of detrimental delta 
phase and improve the mechanical properties. These findings offer insights into optimizing γ ′ ′ -strengthened Ni- 
based superalloys through tailored alloying strategies for enhancing the application of these alloys.
The γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb (D0
22
, tetragonal, I4/mmm) is the primary strength -
ening phase in a wide range of nickel-base superalloys such as Inconel 
718 (the most widely used Ni-based superalloy), Inconel 713, and 
Inconel 618, as well as in recently developed Nb containing high entropy 
alloys and bulk metallic glasses [ 1 – 3 ]. The coherent γ ′ ′ phase in the 
matrix predominantly strengthens the alloy by order strengthening and 
coherency strengthening, resulting in exceptional high-temperature 
properties, such as creep and fatigue strength [ 4 , 5 ]. Both factors 
depend on the composition of the alloy, which controls the order 
strengthening in γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb arises from the generation of stacking faults 
during dislocation reactions at ordered γ ′ ′ precipitates and disordered γ 
matrix interface, which impede dislocation motion.
In γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb, five distinct stacking fault types can form on the close- 
packed {111} planes (equivalently {112} in D0 ₂₂ Miller indices), as 
illustrated in Fig. 1 (a). Using first principle calculations, Lv et al. [ 6 ]. 
reported that among the five faults, only the anti-phase boundary fault 
(APB-I), intrinsic stacking fault (ISF), and superlattice intrinsic stacking 
fault (SISF) are stable, while the anti-phase boundary-like fault (APB-II) 
and complex stacking fault (CSF) are unstable due to the absence of local 
minima on the generalized stacking fault energy surface. These findings 
align with experimental observations in IN718 samples, where the 
appearance of paired dislocations suggests the formation of APB-I and 
numerous ISF due to its low energy [ 7 , 5 ]. Furthermore, Xiao et al. [ 8 ] 
found that planar slip is the primary deformation mechanism in IN718 
during low-cycle fatigue at room temperature and 650 
◦
C, where dis -
locations shear precipitates aid APB-I formation. In contrast, the lattice 
mismatch between the precipitate and matrix influences the coherency 
strain, which plays a curtail role in determining the size, morphology, 
and distribution of γ ′ ′ precipitates, thereby influencing the mechanical 
properties of the alloy [ 9 , 10 ]. Reduction in lattice mismatch improves 
precipitate stability, as the coarsening rate of precipitates is proportional 
to the degree of lattice mismatch [ 11 ]. Notably, a zero lattice misfit can 
enhance the creep-rupture life of an alloy by up to 50 times compared to 
a 0.2% misfit, significantly mitigating precipitate coarsening [ 12 , 13 ].
Fault energies and lattice mismatch significantly influence alloy 
stability and mechanical properties. However, a thorough analysis of 
solute addition at the Ni and Nb sites in γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb remains unknown, 
especially concerning the stable fault energies of APB-I, ISF, and SISF. 
Measuring fault energies is particularly challenging, as traditional 
experimental techniques like transmission electron microscopy (TEM) 
require sufficiently large particle sizes for accurate assessment, which 
the γ ′ ′ phase often fails to achieve due to transformation to the δ phase 
[ 5 , 14 ]. In contrast, density functional theory (DFT), based on ab initio 
quantum mechanics, offers a precise method to examine the effect of 
alloying on fault energy variations and lattice parameters of γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb.
The current study employs density functional theory to explore the 
influence of ternary alloying elements on lattice parameters and fault 
energies of stable faults. All commonly used alloying elements are taken

To evaluate the effect of alloying elements on the lattice parameter of 
γ ′ ′ -Ni

vectors aligned along [100], [010], and [001]directions. Ni and Nb sites 
are substituted separately by one ternary element, and complete struc -
tural relaxation is performed. For examining stacking fault energies, a 
48-atom supercell is constructed with three orthogonal vectors aligned 
along [ 110 ] , [11 2 ] , and [001] directions ( Fig. 1 (c)), containing six layers 
perpendicular to the slip plane. A substitutional atom is placed in the 
shearing layer, replacing Ni or Nb, and the system is fully relaxed. One

Here, the substitution effects were examined only for APB-I, ISF, and 
SISF faults, as they are stable. Nevertheless, energy values for all the 
possible faults in the unsubstituted γ ′ ′ -Ni
3

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations are conducted using 
Quantum ESPRESSO [ 16 ] with the generalized gradient approximation 
(GGA) employing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [ 17 ]. 
The core electrons are treated with projector-augmented-wave (PAW) 
pseudopotentials [ 18 ]. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 600 eV is used, 
with k-point sampling set to 5 × 9 × 2 for the 48-atom supercell and 6 ×
6 × 6 for the 32-atom one. Structural relaxations are performed under 
the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno [ 19 ] scheme until forces on 
atoms fall below 0.02 eV/Å, with convergence thresholds of 10 ⁻⁵ eV for 
total energy and 10 ⁻⁴ eV/Å for forces.
The stacking fault energies of different stacking faults (both stable 
and unstable) in γ ′ ′ - Ni
3
Nb are given in Table 1 under conditions of both 
complete relaxation and partial (only out-of-plane) relaxation. The en -
ergy values for partial relaxation agree with previous literature data 
obtained using the same relaxation scheme [ 6 ]. The energy obtained 
under complete relaxation is significantly lower than partial relaxation, 
which recognizes the necessity of in-plane and out-of-plane relaxation. 
Additional “ in-plane ” atomic shuffling reduces GSFE by varying slip 
amplitude and crystal distortion from single to several atomic planes 
(See section S1 of the Supplementary File for additional details) [ 20 ]. 
Therefore, all the subsequent calculations on the effect of alloying ele -
ments on fault energies were performed using the complete relaxation 
technique. Fault energies for system sizes of 48 atoms (6 layers) and 96 
atoms (12 layers) differ by less than 2%, indicating that 48 atoms (6 
layers) sufficiently mitigate periodic image effects. Therefore, this size is 
used for all studies in this work.

The ‘c/a ’ ratio shows an inverse trend to the lattice parameter, 
increasing as the latter decreases, as seen in Fig. 2 (c) and (d) for Nb and 
Ni substitutions. This behavior occurs because four out of twelve nearest 
neighbor atoms along the (001) plane on which the atom is substituted 
are positioned closer together (2.557 Å versus 2.594 Å for the other 
eight). Substituting Nb/Ni with different atoms generates more strain 
along the ‘a ’ and ‘b ’ directions than the ‘c ’ directions. Consequently, 
larger atoms lead to more significant expansion along the ‘a ’ and ‘b ’ 
direction, reducing the ‘c/a ’ ratio. In comparison, smaller atoms cause 
more significant contraction in the direction, decreasing the ‘c/a ’ ratio. 
Specific findings from this study align with those of experiments, vali -
dating the results of the present work. For instance, Rodenkirchen et al. 
found that substituting Nb with Mo and W in IN718 reduces the lattice 
misfit, refining the microstructure and slowing the coarsening rate of 
Ni
3
Nb by lowering interfacial energy [ 22 ]. These results align with our 
findings that replacing Nb with W and Mo decreases the lattice param -
eter of γ ′ ′ -Ni3Nb, thereby reducing the lattice misfit. Other elements 
expected to reduce the lattice mismatch include Fe and Co for both Ni 
and Nb substitution, as well as Ru, Rh, Re , Os, and all the elements of the 
3d series except Sc for Nb substitution.
The overall trend of the fault energy variations forms a triangular 
ribbon with alloying element substitution at Nb and Ni sites, as shown in 
Fig. 3 . It is evident in APB-I and SISF fault energies reach their peak 
when the substituted element belongs to the same or adjacent periodic 
groups. Additionally, the 4d and 5d series exhibit nearly identical but 
significantly lower fault energies compared to the 3d series in most 
cases, due to their larger atomic radii compared to 3d elements within 
the same group. Larger atoms lead to more lattice distortion and weaker 
bonding, making the formation of stacking faults more favorable 
[ 23 – 25 ].
The variation of APB-I with the alloying element upon Nb substitu -
tion (see Fig. 3 (a)) indicates that, except Ta, all other elements decrease 
APB-I energy. As the elements move further from Nb across each series, 
the energy values decrease nearly linearly. In contrast, elements within 
the same group as Nb or those nearby exhibit similar energy values. For 
example, Ti, V, Zr, Mo, and Hf, which are in the same groups as Nb or 
adjacent to it, show only a slight reduction in energy values, approxi -
mately 3%. Conversely, substituting Nb with Pd, positioned far from Nb, 
results in the most significant reduction of up to 40%.
The variation in ISF energy with elemental substitution at the Nb 
site, shown in Fig. 3 (c), is less evident than APB-I and SISF, though the 
overall trend remains consistent. ISF energy values are negative, indi -
cating that ISF formation is energetically favorable. Substitutions with 
V, Ti, Sc , Cr, Mn, Hf, W, and Zr significantly increase ISF energy.
The fault energies for Ni substitution show a similar pattern to Nb 
substitution, with maxima occurring when the alloying element is in the 
same group, as shown in Fig 3(d-f). Substituting Ni with various ele -
ments reduces both APB-I and SISF energies, with the D -series showing 
similar variations. For example, replacing Ni with Y leads to a 65% 
reduction in APB-I and a 69% reduction in SISF. ISF energy also de -
creases with Ni substitution, but the change is less significant than for 
APB-I and SISF, with elements like Fe, Co, and Pd causing only minimal 
reductions.

ternary element X, new Nb-X or Ni-X bonds form, affecting APB-I and

According to the study by Kennedy et al. [ 26 ], the creep life and 
thermal stability of Inconel 718 significantly improve with cobalt (Co), 
reaching a peak at 9 wt. % of Co, which aligns with our findings. Fig. 2
(b) illustrates that Co reduces the lattice parameter ‘a ’ of γ ′ ′ -Ni

subsequently decreasing the lattice mismatch. The coarsening rate is 
proportional to the lattice mismatch, which may explain why Co en -

indicate that Co addition lowers the APB-I and SISF energy when 
incorporated into the Nb and Ni sites. This reduction in fault energy 
increases the fault width, thereby minimizing cross-slip and, conse -
quently, the steady-state creep rate. Other elements expected to improve 
the creep resistance of the alloy by reducing APB-I and SISF include Sc , 
Y, V, Ti, Cr, Ir, Rh, and Fe.
The variation of ISF seen in Fig. 3 (c, f) is less pronounced with the 
substitution than APB-I and SISF, which can be explained based on 
changes in the nearest neighbor in different faults. In the case of an ISF, 
only the third nearest neighbor is altered, where the interactions are less 
intense than in the first and second nearest neighbors. In contrast, APB-I 
and SISF also affect the first and second nearest neighbors. The forma -
tion of the ISF leads to an ABAB stacking sequence, which acts as a 
nucleation site for the delta phase [ 5 ]. The ISF energy of pure γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb 
has a negative value, indicating that the delta phase is more stable than 
the γ ′ ′ phase, which is also known from experiments [ 27 ]. Therefore, 
elements that increase the ISF energy can inhibit the formation of the 
delta phase. Substitution of Ti and V at the Nb site has the most pro -

Previous studies have revealed that fault energy mainly depends on 
atomic radii and valence electron concentration [ 23 , 28 , 29 ]. Fig. 4 (a) 
depicts the correlation between the valence electron concentration in 
terms of valence electron difference (VED) of the two slipping layers and 
fault energy values for different substitutional elements. The outcome 
indicates that fault energy values decrease linearly as the VED increases, 
regardless of whether the alloying element substitutes the Nb or Ni site. 
However, the dependence of intrinsic stacking fault energy (ISF) on the 
VED is weaker than that of APB and SISF. This is due to the dissimilarity 
in the change in nearest neighbors upon the formation of different faults 
described in the previous paragraph. In contrast to the above findings, 
the correlation of APB-I and SISF with the atomic size difference is ab -
sent, as shown in Fig. 4 (b). However, the contribution from lattice strain 
is slightly more significant for ISF, which can be due to weaker VED 
interaction.
The primary focus of the present study is to demonstrate a general 
trend in the variation of fault energies by substituting different solute 
atoms and identifying elements that could enhance high-temperature 
properties. The results indicate that adding Ti, V, Co, and Fe in 
γ ′ ′ -strengthened Ni-based superalloys is particularly promising, with Ti 
and V exhibiting the most significant influence. These elements reduce 
the lattice parameter of γ ′ ′ -Ni
3
Nb, which minimizes lattice mismatch, 
thereby increasing the precipitate stability by lowering the coarsening 
rate of precipitates. They also decrease the values of APB-I and SISF, 
making cross-slip more difficult and improving creep resistance. 
Furthermore, these elements mostly increase the intrinsic stacking fault 
energy (ISF), effectively delaying the onset of the delta phase formation.
