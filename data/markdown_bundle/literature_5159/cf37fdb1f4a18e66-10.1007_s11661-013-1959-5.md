Tensile Properties and Deformation Characteristics
of a Ni-Fe-Base Superalloy for Steam Boiler Applications
ZHIHONG ZHONG, YUEFENG GU, YONG YUAN, and ZHAN SHI
Ni-Fe-base superalloys due to their good manufacturability and low cost are the proper
candidates for boiler materials in advanced power plants. The major concerns with Ni-Fe-base
superalloys are the insuﬃcient mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. In this paper,
tensile properties, deformation, and fracture characteristics of a Ni-Fe-base superalloy primarily
strengthened by c¢ precipitates have been investigated from room temperature to 1073 K
(800 /C176 C). The results showed a gradual decrease in the strength up to about 973 K (700 /C176 C)
followed by a rapid drop above this temperature and a ductility minimum at around 973 K
(700 /C176 C). The fracture surfaces were studied using scanning electron microscopy and the
deformation mechanisms were determined by the observation of deformed microstructures
using transmission electron microscopy. An attempt has been made to correlate the tensile
properties and fracture characteristics at diﬀerent temperatures with the observed deformation
mechanisms.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE development of advanced ultra-supercritical
(A-USC) coal-ﬁred power plants with steam conditions
of up to 973 K (700 /C176 C) and 37.5 MPa requires the use
of Ni-base superalloys in the hottest boiler and turbine
sections. Ni-base alloys such as CCA 617, [1,2] Nimonic
263,[3,4] and Inconel 740 [5,6] have been evaluated as
candidate boiler materials for A-USC applications.
However, those alloys are prohibitively expensive due
to a high content of Co (10 to 20 wt pct) and/or Mo and
W (6 to 8 wt pct). A low cost austenitic Ni-Fe-base alloy
designated as GH2984 (developed by the Institute of
Metal Research, China) has been used as superheater
tube alloy in marine environments at temperatures
between 923 K and 973 K (650 /C176 C and 700 /C176 C) for
several years
[7–9] and it has recently attracted much
attention as a promising candidate alloy for use as boiler
material in A-USC power plants. This alloy contains a
high amount of Fe (33 wt pct), no Co, and low amount
of Mo (2.2 wt pct). It is primarily strengthened through
precipitation of homogeneously dispersed ﬁne c¢ phase
in c matrix. Some strength is also derived from the
precipitation of MC and M
23C6 carbides at both the
grain boundary and grain interior. [10] Alloy GH2984
possesses good oxidation and hot corrosion resis-
tance.[11,12] Also, this alloy has good workability which
makes it easy to form large and complex-shaped
components.[7]
The operation of heat-resistant structural components
such as boilers in power plants requires good under-
standing of the overall mechanical properties and
related deformation mechanisms in a wide range of
conditions under the loads of tensile stress, creep, and
fatigue.
[13–21] However, a limited number of articles on
the mechanical properties and deformation behavior of
GH2984 alloy are available. Guo and Du
[7] reported the
tensile properties of GH2984 alloy at room temperature
and at 973 K (700 /C176 C), but the properties of GH2984
alloy at other temperatures of interest have not been
studied. In addition, deformation mechanisms of
GH2984 alloy have not been reported yet. To evaluate
the potential applications of GH2984 alloy for 973 K
(700 /C176 C)-class A-USC boilers, this paper, one of a series
of studies, investigated the tensile properties and deter-
mined the deformation mechanism of GH2984 alloy by
means of tensile tests, scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS), and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Results are
discussed in terms of fractography and evolution of
dislocation substructures deformed from room temper-
ature to 1073 K (800 /C176 C).

III. RESULTS
A. Microstructure
Figure 1 shows the microstructure of GH2984 after
solution and aging heat treatment. The average grain
size of the alloy is about 70 lm (Figure 1(a)). Many
irregular particles ranging in size from 0.3 to 3 lm are
located mostly within the grains and some along the
grain boundaries (Figure 1(b)). These irregular particles
were determined using EDS to be rich in Nb with
modest levels of Ti and were presumably (Nb, Ti)C
carbides. These carbides may form during solidiﬁcation
and the hot working process. In addition, Cr
23C6-type
carbides with small levels of Mo and Fe were also
observed along grain boundaries (Figure 1(b)). Anneal-
ing twins were noticed in the grain interior. The TEM
image revealed that the spherical-shaped c¢ particles
with a mean diameter of 49.3 nm precipitated in the c
matrix (Figure 1(c)). The volume fraction of c¢ phase is
about 6 pct.
B. Tensile Properties
Tensile engineering stress–strain curves for the alloy
from room temperature to 1073 K (800 /C176 C) are shown
in Figure 2. In order to avoid long testing times, the
loading rate increased after the deformation reached
about 3 pct for tests conducted at temperatures higher
than 773 K (500 /C176 C). It can be found that an increase of
loading rate will enhance slightly the maximum tensile
strength (<5 pct). Figure 3 summarizes the tensile prop-
erties of GH2984 from room temperature to 1073 K
(800 /C176 C). Yield strength decreased gradually from room
temperature to about 973 K (700 /C176 C) and then
decreased drastically at higher temperatures. Similar to
the temperature dependence of yield strength, ultimate
tensile strength decreased gradually with increasing
temperature to 923 K (650 /C176 C) and thereafter decreased
steeply. It should be noted that the yield and ultimate
tensile strength diﬀered considerably at room tempera-
ture, while this diﬀerence reduced to a marginal level at
temperatures higher than 973 K (700 /C176 C). Both elonga-
tion (Figure 3(b)) and reduction in area (Figure 3(c))
were found to be relatively insensitive to temperatures
up to 773 K (500 /C176 C). Beyond this temperature, the
elongation and reduction in area decreased with increas-
ing temperature, though to diﬀerent extents, and both
reached minimum levels at 973 K (700 /C176 C), which are
17.3 and 21.4 pct, respectively. The elongation and
reduction in area increased linearly with the temperature
above 973 K (700 /C176 C).
C. Fracture Surface Characterization
The fracture surfaces of tensile tested samples were
investigated to identify the fracture characteristics at
various testing temperatures. At room temperature,
failure occurred by a transgranular mode with typical
ductile fracture dimples and microvoids within the grain
interior (Figures 4(a) and (b)). It is clear that before
fracture, the specimen experienced considerable plastic
deformation. The dimples were about 1 to 10 lm in size,
which is evidence of microvoid nucleation and growth
during plastic deformation. A similar dimple ductile
fracture occurred at 923 K (650 /C176 C) (Figure 4(c)). Some
particles are presented at the bottom of the dimples on
the fracture surface (Figure 4(d)). The size of these
particles and EDS analysis conducted on them revealed
that they are MC carbides, suggesting that voids formed
around the intragranular carbides. On careful examina-
tion of the fractograph in Figure 4(c), one can ﬁnd
intergranular fracture at 923 K (650 /C176 C), while fracture
was entirely transgranular at room temperature. At
973 K (700 /C176 C), about half of the fracture (the ﬁnal
stage) occurred by shear with the fracture surfaces
(cleavage fracture) separating along a plane inclined at
45 degree to the tensile axis. The remaining half of the
fracture surface exhibited a mixed dimple ductile and
intergranular fracture as illustrated in Figures 4(e) and
(f). The area fraction of intergranular fracture is much

IV. DISCUSSION
The yield strength of an alloy with a c/c¢ two-phase
structure exhibits diﬀerent types of temperature depen-
dence depending on the volume fraction of c¢ precipi-
tates. It was reported
[18,22] that the yield strength of an
alloy with a low volume fraction ( <12 pct) of c¢ phase
decreased monotonically with an increase in tempera-
ture. As mentioned before, GH2984 alloy is a c¢
precipitation-strengthened superalloy with a low volume
fraction of c¢ (6 pct). Therefore, such a feature is
expected to occur in this alloy, as indeed experimentally
observed.
During tensile testing, the deformation behavior of
GH2984 alloy is strongly dependent on the temperature.

At room temperature, the observed well-deﬁned slip
bands indicate that most dislocations moved within slip
planes. Super dislocations travel through the c¢ particles
in the form of pairs (Figure 6(b)) in order to minimize
the antiphase boundary area created by the a/2h110i
displacement of the superlattice,
[21] and this leads to the
high strength at room temperature. The observed
dislocation loops around the larger c¢ precipitate indi-
cate that the Orowan mechanism also occurs during
tensile deformation at room temperature (Figure 6(c).
When temperature increased to 923 K (650 /C176 C), slip
bands have not been observed. This means that the
movement of dislocation is not conﬁned in the slip
bands. Extensive dislocation activities, including loop-
ing and bowing-out reactions (Orowan mechanism),
operated at this temperature. Hence, the eﬀectiveness of
c¢ particles as dislocation barriers is decreased when
compared to the shearing eﬀect and will cause a decrease
in strength, as shown in Figure 3. Similarly, at 973 K
(700 /C176 C), no shearing of c¢ precipitates by dislocations
was observed, but Orowan bypassing was observed.
This resulted in a further decrease in yield strength
owing to temperature eﬀect. At 1023 K (750 /C176 C),
dynamic recovery and recrystallization driven mainly
by thermal activation are observed (Figure 9(b)). It is
reported that if dynamic recovery occurs, work harden-
ing due to the increase in dislocation density with
increasing strain is counteracted by the softening arising
out of the dynamic recovery under low strain rates
(<0.01 s
/C0 1) in tensile or compression tests; [22] hence, the
stress–strain curve exhibits a small strain-hardening
portion followed by a segment of steady ﬂow stress
saturation,
[23] which means there is a small diﬀerence
between yield strength and maximum strength. There-
fore, the observed marginal diﬀerence between yield
strength and ultimate tensile strength at 1023 K and
1073 K (750 /C176 C and 800 /C176 C) (Figure 3) may be the
result of the occurrence of dynamic recovery and
recrystallization. Thus, the rapid reduction of strength
at temperatures above 1023 K (750 /C176 C) could be partly
attributed to the onset of dynamic recovery and/or
recrystallization. A similar dynamic recovery and
recrystallization softening mechanism, and its inﬂuence
on the tensile strength, has been reported in other Ni-
base superalloys.
[20,24]
The fracture behaviors of GH2984 exhibited a trans-
granular ductile fracture at room temperature and high
temperatures [above 1023 K (750 /C176 C)], and a mixed
transgranular and intergranular fracture at intermediate
temperatures [923 K to 973 K (650 /C176 C to 700 /C176 C)]. At
room temperature, grain boundary strength is high and
the deformation is concentrated in the grain inside. The
process of microvoid nucleation and coalescence results
in ductile fracture and high ductility. A ductility
minimum occurred at intermediate temperatures of
923 K to 973 K (650 /C176 C to 700 /C176 C). The most important
factors that cause the ductility minimum of superalloys
are
[25] (1) embrittlement of grain boundaries, (2) defor-
mation mechanisms which lead to strain localization,
and (3) coarsening of c¢ particles. Given the high
stability of c¢ particles in GH2984, the ﬁrst two factors
are the most important for the present alloy. The
extensive dislocation activity found throughout the
matrix (Figures 7 and 8) indicates that considerable
plastic deformation occurred prior to failure. This oﬀers
a high opportunity for the interactions between dislo-
cations and intergranular carbides, leading to stress
concentrations at the interface between carbides and c
matrix and subsequently decohesion between them.
Then, growth and coalescence of these voids along the
grain boundaries result in the observed intergranular
dimple rupture (Figure 4(f)). The cleavage fracture in
the ﬁnal stage of the fracture process also contributes to
the ductility minimum. It should be mentioned that,
however, the ductility minimum for the present alloy
may also be associated with the segregation of oxygen
and other trace impurities to the grain boundaries.

Similar observations have been reported in other super-
alloys.[26,27] At temperatures higher than 1023 K
(750 /C176 C), dynamic recovery of deformation dislocation
becomes strong and dynamic recrystallization of c
matrix retards the microcrack growth, [24,28] thereby
enhancing the fracture ductility. Therefore, the im-
proved ductility at 1023 K and 1073 K (750 /C176 Ca n d
800 /C176 C) is believed to be associated with the dynamic
recovery and recrystallization.
Clearly, compared with other candidate Ni-base
alloys such as CCA 617, [1,2] Nimonic 263, [3,4] and
Inconel 740, [5,6] the Ni-Fe-base superalloy GH2984 has
great cost beneﬁts for superheater and reheater applica-
tions in A-USC power plants. However, the mechanical
properties of GH2984 seem to be insuﬃcient for the
application in 973 K (700 /C176 C)-class A-USC power
plants due to the relatively low strength.
V. CONCLUSIONS
Tensile tests on a precipitation-strengthened austenitic
Ni-Fe-base superalloy GH2984 were carried out from
room temperature to 1073 K (800 /C176 C). The major con-
clusions drawn from this study relating to the deformation
behavior and fracture characteristics are given below:
1. Yield strength and ultimate tensile strength decreased
gradually from room temperature to 973 K (700 /C176 C)
and then decreased markedly at higher temperatures.
The minimum value of tensile elongation and reduc-
tion in area was observed at 973 K (700 /C176 C).
2. A transgranular dimple ductile fracture was observed
at room temperature, a mixed transgranular and inter-
granular fracture at intermediate temperatures [923 K
to 973 K (650 /C176 Ct o7 0 0 /C176 C)], and a dimple ductile
fracture at temperatures higher than 973 K (700 /C176 C).
3. The predominant deformation mechanisms were
planar slip and c¢ shearing by dislocation pairs at
room temperature, bypassing of c¢ particles by the
Orowan mechanism at intermediate temperatures
[923 K to 973 K (650 /C176 C to 700 /C176 C)], and disloca-
tion rearrangement and dynamic recovery above
1023 K (750 /C176 C).
