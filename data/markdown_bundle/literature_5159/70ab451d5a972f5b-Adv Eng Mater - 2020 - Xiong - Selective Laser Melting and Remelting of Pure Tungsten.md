1. Introduction
Tungsten, the metal with the highest melting point, has many
unique physical properties such as high density, high hardness,
low vapor pressure, low thermal expansion coef ﬁcient, and excel-
lent thermal conductivity. [1– 3] Due to these outstanding proper-
ties, tungsten and its alloys have been widely applied in the ﬁelds
of defense, medical, and nuclear fusion. However, the brittleness
of tungsten is an inherent property related to the nature of atomic
bonds. The high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)
of tungsten is governed by its intrinsic and extrinsic properties. [4]
Tungsten, as a transition metal, has a certain proportion of
covalent bonding in the bonding structures because the inner-
shell electrons are not full. Yet, this covalent bonding has strong
directionality, which does not allow the
relative position between the atoms to be
changed, so that the material does not
have plasticity. In addition, the interstitial
impurities have a signi ﬁcant effect on
the brittleness, which is extremely suscep-
tible to oxygen, [5] and a trace amount of
oxygen causes the DBTT rise sharply. [6]
Accordingly, tungsten is arduous to the
machine.
However, conventional machining
methods have limitations in producing
components with complex internal and
external structures. It is necessary to adopt
a new approach to produce tungsten.
Selective laser melting (SLM) is a kind of
state-of-the-art processing technology that
manufactures components in an incre-
mental layer-by-layer means using the laser
to melt, bond, and solidify powder particles
on powder bed.[7] It has been widely used in
the production of a variety of alloys including titanium alloys,
aluminum alloys, and stainless steel, [8– 12] providing the possibil-
ity for its application in producing tungsten. Tan et al. [13]
fabricated pure tungsten samples with a relative density of 98.5%
using SLM. Recent literature [6,14,15] also revealed that nearly
full-dense tungsten could be prepared by SLM. In view of the
characteristics of SLM, however, the manufacture of high melt-
ing point tungsten still faces some technical challenges. Balling
phenomena occur because of the high surface tension of tung-
sten.[16] On the contrary, cracks also occur due to the presence of
oxygen impurities and high-temperature gradients. [14– 19] All
these factors hinder the increase in density of SLM-processed
tungsten. As Sidambe et al. [15] reported, the residual defects of
pure tungsten parts manufactured using the current scanning
strategy may not be eliminated.
The improvement of material performance is usually achieved
by alloying to stabilize grain boundaries (GBs). As the GBs
hinder dislocation motion, metals and alloys can be strengthened
by reducing grain size. Alja ž Ivekovi´c et al.[18] added tantalum as
an alloying element, revealing that the addition of tantalum
caused a reduced grain size due to constitutional undercooling.
Compared with pure tungsten, cracks in the W-6Ta alloy were
reduced by 80%, which was attributed to the observed submicron
intragranular cellular structure. [19]
Nevertheless, alloying is unfavorable for the application and
recycling of pure materials under certain circumstances.
Against this backdrop, it is considered to improve the material
properties by tailoring the microstructure without adding
other elements, thereby achieving resource sustainability.

In conventional processing, heat treatments after cold deforma-
tion are often used to create recrystallization and alter the grain
structure. Sidambe et al. [15] stated that the heat treatments of
tungsten after SLM processing were not available owing to the
lack of high dislocation density, and any heat treatments would
only result in slow growth of grain. Motivated by this question,
the remelting is adopted to conduct heat treatment.
Selective laser remelting (SLRM) is a scanning strategy with
additional remelting step in the SLM process. In situ heat treat-
ment is carried out by remelting the solidi ﬁed metal layer by
layer, which can eliminate thermal stress and inhibit crack
initiation and propagation. [20] Li et al. [21] successfully fabricated
crack-free Al 85Ni5Y6Co2Fe2 bulk metallic glass composites by
re-scanning with low-energy density. On the contrary, SLRM
also improves the relative density and surface quality of compo-
nents,[22– 24] and forms the shallower molten pools from melting
previously obtained columnar crystals, thus ﬁne grains were
achieved.[23] During the SLM process, Gustmann et al. [22] argued
that the remelting procedure altered the microstructure and the
properties in shape-memory alloy without compromising the
relative density and proved to be an interesting tool for related
alloys to optimize their performance without additional post-
processing steps. However, there were rarely reported investiga-
tions on SLRM of tungsten.
In this work, pure tungsten was produced by the SLM
technique, followed by a series of process parameters optimi-
zations to obtain high-performance components. Afterward,
an additional laser remelting step is implemented to improve
their performance. Our primary focus, yet, is to explore the den-
siﬁcation behavior and the microstructure and obtain the parts of
SLM and SLRM with optimal process parameters. The surface
quality, relative density, and microstructure of samples proc-
essed with different parameters were characterized in detail.
2. Experimental Section
2.1. Materials and Processing
The raw material used in this work was high purity, plasma
spheroidized tungsten powder (purity 99.9%) supplied by
Tekna Advanced Materials Inc. (Quebec, Canada). Figure 1 a
shows a scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the highly
spherical powder carried out using a Nova NanoSEM 450
(Hillsboro, USA), and Figure 1b shows the size distribution
of the powder measured by a Mastersizer 3000 laser scattering
particle size analyzer (Tokyo, Japan). A smaller powder size
(D50 ¼ 15.8 μm) and size distribution of tungsten played an
important role during the SLM process, which increased the
laser heat ﬂux density absorbed by the powder. [25]
The pure tungsten powder was processed in an EOS M290
SLM system (EOS GmbH, Krailling, Germany) equipped with
a 400 W laser and laser beam diameter of 100 μm. Due to the
small size of the powder particles, the minimum layer thickness
of 20 μm was selected. When the hatch spacing and layer thick-
ness were ﬁxed during the SLM process, the volumetric energy
input, EV, can be adapted to calculate the energy deposited in a
single track. Thus, the linear energy is de ﬁned by the equation[13]
EL ¼ P=ν (1)
where P is the laser power and ν is the scanning speed. Based on
the preliminary experiment, the laser power ( P) and scanning
speed (ν) were set in the range of 150–350 W and 200–800 mm s/C0 1,
respectively.
All experiments were conducted in a ﬂowing argon atmo-
sphere with resulting oxygen content below 0.1 vol%. Prior to
SLM, a substrate was preheated to 180 /C14 C to decrease the
temperature gradient. To reduce the in ﬂuence of thermal stress
on samples, the scanning rotations of 67 /C14 between adjacent
layers were applied during the SLM process. In addition, an addi-
tional laser exposure was added after the ﬁrst layer powder had
melted. In other words, the ﬁrst layer powder was melted twice
and spread completely on the substrate to ensure stability during
the subsequent processing.

3. Results and Discussion
The following discussion is divided into four parts. First, we
elaborate on how different process parameters affect the relative
density of the as-built specimens. The second section focuses on
the interrelation between the microstructure and the mechanical
properties. Afterward, we will describe the surface morphology
and crack distribution in detail. Finally, the additional remelting
steps are implemented, and their effects on the microstructure
and the relative density of samples are discussed.
3.1. SLM Processing and Densi ﬁcation
To better understand the effect of process parameters on densi ﬁ-
cation in SLM process, we produced pure tungsten samples with
different process parameters, as shown in Figure 2. In fact, due
to the characteristics of SLM, there are usually more defects
along the building direction (BD), that is to say, the cross section
has a higher relative density than BD for the SLM-produced
samples. It is worth noting that the relative density in
Figure 2 was measured by the image analysis method, which
only characterized the horizontal cross section ( x– y), hence there
may be some limitations in not re ﬂecting the actual density of
pure tungsten.
Undoubtedly, the experimental results proclaimed that the
relative density was improved remarkably with the increase in
laser power. If the laser power is excessively low or the scanning
speed is too fast, the metallurgical defects, such as unmolten
powder, will emerge from the molten pool under the low-energy
density, thus the relative density of tungsten parts is signi ﬁcantly
reduced (as shown in the low-energy density zone of Figure 2).
We also found that the average microhardness of tungsten sam-
ples increases with the growth of laser power and after passing
through a maximum of 456 HV 0.2 the values decrease, as shown
in Figure 2. A relatively high laser power with high heat input is
beneﬁcial for tungsten melting. [26] Unfortunately, further
increasing laser power would lead to sintering loss and surface
wrinkling for tungsten components, which can destroy the
forming quality. [13]
To further optimize the proces s parameters, th el a s e rp o w e r
was changed in the range of 200 /C0 300 W, while the scanning
speed was ﬁxed at 400 mm s /C0 1. Figure 3 shows the optical
macrographs of tungsten specimens in the horizontal cross
sections with different laser power. The densi ﬁcation of
tungsten was clearly found to increase with an increase in laser
power. The result is also consistent with Figure 2. When the
laser power is 200 W, there are many pores distributed
in the specimen and the relative density is 97.81%, as shown
in Figure 3a. When the laser power is 250 W, the pores decrease
evidently and the relative density is 99.53%, as shown in
Figure 3b. When the laser power is 300 W, the specimen is
nearly fully dense and the relative density is 99.98%, as
shown in Figure 3c. The elevated power is instrumental in
raising the width and penetration depth of molten pool, [17]
which provide an appropriate o verlap between adjacent scan
tracks to produce dense tungsten specimens. Therefore, when
the laser power is 300 W, fully dense tungsten samples can be
obtained.

3.2. Microstructural Analysis
To fully understand the in ﬂuence of process parameters on pure
tungsten, we further studied the microstructure and the defect of
SLM-produced samples. Figure 4 shows the single molten pool
track on pure tungsten specimens at different scanning speeds.
Likewise, regardless of the laser power, excessive scanning speed
created instability in the molten pool due to the high cooling rate.
The unstable molten pool implemented subsequent crack prop-
agation and consequent fracture (as shown in Figure 4c). On the
contrary, high surface tension of tungsten tends to cause agglo-
meration and balling of the powder particles. [16] Accordingly, a
relatively stable molten pool can be obtained at the scanning
speeds of 400 – 600 mm s/C0 1, which is selected for further study.
In the powder bed, the metal melt was subjected to the heat
ﬂow impact continuously, and the solidi ﬁed structure was in a
state of repeated melting and solidi ﬁcation, which caused the
crystal grains to undergo repeated melting and growth.
Meanwhile, the direction of grain growth was disturbed by the
scanning strategy of interlayer rotation, [12] thereby forming irreg-
ular microstructures. Figure 5 a– c shows the microstructural
morphologies of pure tungsten at different scanning speeds.
As mentioned earlier, the obtained samples possess satisfactory
density. In addition, with the acceleration of scanning speed, the
grain size of pure tungsten was reduced due to high cooling rate.
On the one hand, according to the Hall– Patch relationship, the
decrease in grain size leads to an increase in yield strength. [27]
The ultimate compressive strength of the specimens with a scan-
ning speed of 600 mm s /C0 1 is 1197 MPa, as shown in Figure 5d,
which reaches the highest value among our specimens and
exceeds the value reported in other literature. [13,28] On the other
hand, the reduction of laser energy input elevated the defect level
inside the samples, which slightly reduced the density of pure
tungsten. Their densities determined by the Archimedes method
are 18.77 – 18.93 g cm/C0 3, which is 97.3 – 98.1% density of pure
tungsten (19.30 g cm /C0 3), and the results are shown in Table 1 .
In addition to the inherent brittleness of tungsten, internal
defects also affect mechanical properties. Table 1 shows that
the decrease in relative density leads to a decrease in the bending
properties, which might be ascribed to the sensitivity of bending
properties to defects inside the samples.
The heat dissipation in the BD is faster than other directions
during the SLM process, which creates the heat dissipation
condition of a single direction during the melt solidi ﬁcation
and promotes the formation of columnar crystals in the prefer-
ential orientation against the direction of heat ﬂux.[29] Generally,
pure metals and the alloys with narrow solidi ﬁcation range, such
as pure tungsten, tend to form columnar crystals without suf ﬁ-
cient constitutional super-cooling to allow nucleation. [12,30]
Figure 6 shows the SEM image of typical columnar grains for
SLM-produced pure tungsten. The columnar grains grew epitax-
ially on the previously solidi ﬁed track. Simultaneously, the
oxygen solute atoms were expelled into the GBs and formed
oxides.[6] Such columnar crystals lead to extremely fragile grain
bonding for brittle tungsten, which will exert an adverse effect on
the mechanical properties. Table 1 shows that tungsten speci-
mens with the microstructure possessed low bending strength.
The GBs between columnar grains are high angle grain
boundaries with signi ﬁcant orientation difference. [6,15] In the
plastic deformation, the deformation of grains is not arbitrary,
and such high angle grain boundaries need to coordinate the
deformation between adjacent grains, so they often become the
crack sources under stress.

3.3. Surface Morphology
The generation of cracks is inevitable in the SLM process of pure
tungsten. Figure 7 shows the surface morphology of samples.
It can be observed that the surface presents “Y”-shaped cracks,
which exhibit some certain regularity. The cracks of pure tung-
sten produced by SLM can be classi ﬁed into three types accord-
ing to their orientation. 1) First, the cracks near the molten
pool centerline, i.e., along the scanning direction, as shown in
Figure 7a. Because the laser energy satis ﬁes the Gauss distribu-
tion,[31] the nonuniform heating in the molten pool results in a
large temperature gradient in the centerline. 2) Second, the
cracks cross along the surface ripples of the scanning track,
i.e., perpendicular to the solidi ﬁcation isotherm, as shown in
Figure 7b. There is a large temperature gradient in the vertical
direction of the isotherm. 3) Third, the cracks extend between
layers, i.e., parallel to the BD, as shown in Figure 7c. Due to
the high thermal conductivity of tungsten, the heat input was
rapidly transferred to the substrate in the form of heat conduc-
tion, which had a thermal in ﬂuence on the previously solidi ﬁed
layers, resulting in a large temperature gradient in the BD.

In summary, the cracks occurred along the temperature
gradient on the surface of specimens. As the laser energy is
input, pure tungsten particles in the powder bed are rapidly
melted, and then the melted droplets are quickly wetted and
spread on its own solidi ﬁed substrate. The droplets, simulta-
neously, are subjected to tensile stress at their top during rapid
solidiﬁcation,[32] resulting in an increase in the lattice constant of
tungsten. Consequently, the residual oxygen atoms, inside the
droplets and the forming chamber, diffuse into the top tension
zone, which may cause intergranular fraction at the top of solidi-
ﬁed droplets. [18] In addition, the attracted oxygen atoms hinder
the movement of dislocations, which are not conducive to plastic
deformation for brittle tungsten. [4,5] Under the extreme temper-
ature gradient, tungsten thereupon cracks once the triggered
thermal stress exceeds its yield strength in this state. [18,21]
Figure 8 shows the effect of linear laser energy on Ra, which
was measured from the top surface of the as-built pure tungsten
specimens. One can reveal from the present data that increasing
energy input results in increased roughness. As mentioned
earlier, the elevated laser energy input was instrumental in
raising the overlap of adjacent tracks, which created rougher
surface for tungsten specimens. Simultaneously, the splashes
adhered to the surface. [17] Such rough surface would cause
samples to be scraped destructively by the recoater, which further
makes it dif ﬁcult for tungsten powder to spread and process
(as shown in the high-energy density zone of Figure 2).
It is noteworthy that the Ra value is 14.72 μm at the linear
energy density of 0.75 J mm /C0 1 (the laser power of 300 W and
scanning speed of 400 mm s /C0 1). Accordingly, it is necessary to
take measures to polish the rough surface for the SLM-processed
tungsten specimens.
3.4. Remelting Strategy
To improve the surface quality and further ensure the formability
of pure tungsten, SLM-processed specimens were remelted layer
by layer. The additional remelting step was implemented on
substrates with laser power of 300 W and scanning speed of
400 mm s/C0 1. Figure 9a shows a schematic diagram of the remelt-
ing strategy. Therefore, we selected four different process para-
meters with lower power, as shown in Table 2 , and further
explored the effect of additional laser remelting parameters on
solidiﬁed samples. It is clearly shown that the lower power
remelting parameters improved the surface quality in varying
degrees, and the additional energy input of 1/2 E has a more pro-
nounced effect. Figure 9b shows the effect of these parameters
on the relative density. Interestingly, the remelting changed the
Archimedes density of tungsten samples, and only the SLRM (3)
sample improved its relative density from 98.1% to 98.4%. For
the SLRM (1), (2), and (4) samples, their relative densities were
lowered by implementing the remelting step, and the averages
were 97.6%, 97.8%, and 97.6%, respectively.
To further explore the effect of remelting strategy on pure
tungsten, we selected the SLRM (3) sample to compare its
surface morphology and microstructure between before and after
remelting. Figure 10 shows the 3D topography taken from the
untreated fresh surface of pure tungsten. As we expected, the
strategy using laser remelting effectively reduced the Ra value
from 14.72 to 10.62 μm for the SLRM (3) sample surface, which
is 28% lower than that produced without remelting. In addition,
the strategy also signi ﬁcantly reduced the grain size of pure
tungsten compared with the method without remelting, as
shown in Figure 11. The grain size of tungsten was re ﬁned from
29 /C6 10 μm (Figure 11a) to 14 /C6 3 μm (Figure 11b). It should be
stressed that the grain size, here, was characterized by evaluating
the average of intercepts from different OM images.
After remelting, a large number of ﬁne grains were obtained
and such grains have also been found in other remelting param-
eters. The reasons are explained below: a large temperature
gradient accompanied by temperature ﬂuctuation in the exposed
molten pool, which causes the density differences of ﬂuids.

The heat ﬂow remelts the previously formed columnar crystals
and triggers them to peel off. [23,35] The detached grains are
engulfed into the molten pool and formed free grains, which may
become the source of crystal nuclei in ﬁne-grain zone (FGZ).
Additional heating from laser rementing can also in ﬂuence the
grain microstructure. [35] The remelted microstructures, which

the chilled grains are less likely to grow and form the FGZ near
the centerline of the melt pool. [37] Therefore, the ﬂow of ﬂuids

As expected, the formation of FGZ reduces the oxygen concen-
tration at GBs, which hinders the initiation and propagation of
cracks. In addition, the remelting healed the remaining voids and
cracks to reduce partial defects. Figure 13a,b shows OM images
of the horizontal cross section ( x– y) and the vertical section ( x– z)
for the remelted polished specimens, respectively. It is demon-
strated that there are more defects in the vertical section ( x– z)
than cross section ( x– y) as mentioned earlier. It is worth men-
tioning that few cracks were found in the core of specimens
for both remelted method and without, as shown in Figure 13c.
Yet, macroscopic cracks occurred near the edge of specimens,
which may be attributed to the oxygen contamination at the edge.
Paradoxically, the remelting may also entrain the gas into the
molten pool when the ﬂuids ﬂowed. The rapid cooling of ﬂuids
made it too late for the residual gas to escape, resulting in a
decrease in the density of tungsten samples. Therefore, the ﬂow
of ﬂuids caused by remelting has an opposite effect on the
densiﬁcation behavior. The reduction and increase in porosity
constitute a competing relationship, and the ﬁnal result is shown
in Figure 9b.
As Gustmann indicated, increasing the relative density by
optimizing the remelting parameters seems a matter of trial
and error. [22] But undoubtedly, remelting strategy provides a
way to regulate the microstructure, which points out a new direc-
tion for SLM processing pure tungsten and is subject to be fur-
ther studied.

4. Conclusions
Tungsten is hard to be processed owing to its intrinsic properties,
but high-density pure tungsten parts using SLM technique were
fabricated successfully. The main conclusions were summarized
as follows: 1) the densi ﬁcation of pure tungsten was found to
promote with an increase in laser power. Dense tungsten parts
(98.1 /C6 0.3%) with the bending property of 131 MPa were
obtained by optimizing process parameters (the laser power is
300 W and the scanning speed is 400 mm s /C0 1). 2) The micro-
structure and the formation mechanism of crack for SLM pure
tungsten were systematically elucidated. The highest ultimate
compressive strength of about 1200 MPa was achieved due to
the ﬁner grains by increasing the scanning speed to 600 mm s /C0 1,
yet higher scanning speed created the unstable molten pool and
consequent crack. 3) With a growing of laser energy input, the
average roughness of specimens increased gradually. The remelt-
ing with an additional energy input of 1/2 EV effectively reduced
the surface roughness to improve the formability of pure tung-
sten. 4) The laser remelting, through trial and error, reduced the
grain size and the defect level in specimens. Pure tungsten parts
with high relative density (98.4 /C6 0.5%) were obtained in the end.
