ABSTRACT
High-temperature oxidation poses a significant challenge in applications such as aviation and turbines. While 
refractory high-entropy alloys maintain good strength at elevated temperatures, their resistance to high- 
temperature oxidation remains problematic. To explore an antioxidant strategy for refractory high-entropy al -
loys, the oxidation behaviour of Ti2ZrNbNi3 with an ultrafine body-centered cubic disordered and ordered so -
lution (BCC + B2) lamellar microstructure was studied at different temperatures. When the oxidation 
temperature is greater than 800 
◦
C, O atoms diffuse inwards along the boundaries of the ultrafine lamella and 
combine with Zr and Ti to generate low-valent oxides. With increasing time, high-valent oxides of Zr and Ti are 
generated, and Ni atoms originally belonging to the B2 phase can diffuse outwards and then form a dense NiO 
antioxidation layer. As the oxide rate of Ti2ZrNbNi3 is as low as that of pure Ni, the effectiveness of an anti -
oxidation strategy utilizing elemental diffusion along an ultrafine lamellar microstructure has been 
demonstrated.
1. Introduction
Refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA) is a type of multicomponent 
alloy composed of several refractory metals, such as Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, 
and Ta [ 1 , 2 ]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that RHEAs, such as 
Ti50Zr18Nb15V12Al5 [ 3 ], (TiZrHfNb)98O2 [ 4 ] and (W1.5Ni2.25Fe) 
95Ta5 [ 5 ], exhibit an excellent combination of strength and plasticity at 
room temperature. Additionally, they exhibit high strength at elevated 
temperatures, as seen in NbMoTaW [ 6 – 8 ] and VNbMoTaW [ 7 , 9 – 11 ]. 
Their strength and specific strength at temperatures exceeding 1000 
◦
C 
surpass those of Haynes 230, MAR-M 247 and INCONEL 718 [ 12 ]. 
Consequently, RHEAs are believed to possess the potential to increase 
the temperature capabilities of blades and disks beyond those of current 
superalloys [ 12 ].
However, the application of RHEAs in high-temperature structural 
materials faces two major challenges. On the one hand, the manufacture 
of large, high-quality RHEAs is very difficult because of their high 
melting point, poor casting fluidity and high activity [ 13 – 18 ]. On the 
other hand, the antioxidation performance of RHEAs is poor, requiring 
coating even at 600 
◦
C because of the characteristics of refractory metals 
[ 19 – 22 ].
Eutectic high-entropy alloys (EHEAs) can solve the first problem of 
RHEAs without sacrificing their mechanical properties at high temper -
atures. On the one hand, owing to the inheritance of eutectic alloys, 
EHEAs exhibit excellent casting fluidity, including a low melting point 
and a small difference between the melting and solidification tempera -
tures [ 23 , 24 ]. On the other hand, EHEAs typically have a two-phase 
lamellar structure with many phase interfaces [ 23 , 25 , 26 ]. A semi -
coherent face-centered cubic (FCC)/body-centered cubic (BCC) inter -
face with a Kurdjumov ‒ Sachs orientation relationship can provide high 
interfacial shear strength [ 27 ]. According to reports by Lu et al. [ 28 ], the 
compressive yield strengths of the C0.25Hf0.25NbTaW0.5 EHEA at 
1200 
◦
C and 1400 
◦
C are 792 MPa and 749 MPa, respectively. Shu et al. 
[ 29 ] reported that the tensile yield strength at 1100 
◦
C of VNbTiSi-based 
EHEAs reached 300 MPa. It can be expected that the EHEA composed 
mainly of refractory metals (R-EHEAs) displays greater strength at high 
temperatures.

are achieved for large R-EHEA samples, good antioxidation performance 
is also expected. The atom diffusion rate in high-energy regions, such as

In this work, a novel EHEA composed mainly of the highly active 
refractory metal Ti2ZrNbNi3 is proposed. The oxidation behaviour of 
the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA, including the oxidation type, diffusion 
behaviour of O and its constituent elements, morphology evolution, and 
effect of various oxidation layers, is systematically investigated. A novel 
and effective antioxidation strategy for the RHEA using the effects of an 
ultrafine lamellar microstructure on element diffusion and the oxidation 
rate is demonstrated. The application of R-EHEAs in the field of high- 
temperature structural materials is further explored.

4. Discussion
4.1. Oxidation type of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA
According to the theory of metal oxidation, metal oxidation can be 
divided into two main types: internal oxidation and external oxidation 
[ 37 ]. Internal oxidation, also known as diffusion oxidation, involves the 
gradual diffusion of oxygen atoms into the alloy matrix, where they 
undergo oxidation reactions. In contrast, external oxidation, or reaction 
oxidation, occurs when certain elements diffuse to the surface of the 
matrix and react with oxygen. During the oxidation of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 
R-EHEA at 600 
◦
C, the relatively low temperature allows oxygen from 
the environment to diffuse into the alloy matrix without altering the 
constituent elements (as shown in Fig. 5 ). Consequently, incomplete 
oxidation reactions occur (as shown in Fig. 3 (a)). Thus, the oxidation 
type experienced by the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA oxidized at 600 
◦
C is 
classified as internal oxidation, which is consistent with observations in 
other R-HEAs and R-MEAs [ 38 – 41 ].
As the oxidation temperature increases to ≥ 800 
◦
C, the distributions 
of the constituent elements in different oxidized areas and the matrix 
change, resulting in the formation of several oxidation layers, as shown 
in Figs. 6 and 7 . Moreover, the inward diffusion of oxygen persists, as 
indicated by the reduced oxygen gradient ( Figs. 6 and 7 ) and the 
increasing thickness of the oxidation layer with prolonged oxidation 
time ( Fig. 8 ). Therefore, the oxidation process of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R- 
EHEA oxidized at temperatures above 800 
◦
C comprises a combination 
of external oxidation and internal oxidation mechanisms. The weight 
gain curves of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA oxidized at 800 
◦
C and 1000 
◦
C 
display a segmented trend, including an O diffusion-controlled stage for 
oxidation times t < 3 h and an oxidation reaction-controlled stage for t 
> 3 h, as shown in Fig. 2 .

4.2. Diffusion behaviour of oxygen in the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA
As mentioned above, the oxidation behaviour of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R- 
EHEA is influenced primarily by the diffusion of oxygen during low- 
temperature oxidation or the initial stages of oxidation at elevated 
temperatures. Oxygen atom diffusion in solids includes boundary 
diffusion and bulk diffusion. The rate of boundary diffusion is generally 
higher than that of bulk diffusion and affects the diffusion behaviour of 
multiphase materials. Thus, oxygen atoms diffuse into the Ti2ZrNbNi3 
R-EHEA matrix along the boundaries of the B2 and BCC phases, as 
shown in Fig. 13 (a). Essentially, the ultrafine lamellar microstructure of 
the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA with a wavelength of 100 nm ( Fig. 1 ) provides 
numerous dense diffusion channels for oxygen atoms. Therefore, the 
difference in the composition of lamellar structures increases as oxygen 
atoms diffuse, as shown by the increasing contrast difference in Figs. 5 
and 6 . Thus, the oxidation rate constant kp of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA 
when t < 3 h is primarily determined by the diffusion rate of oxygen 
atoms, which increases with increasing oxidation temperature, as indi -

greater than that of TiO2, ZrO2 and Nb2O5, and the electronegativity of 
Ni (1.88) is greater than that of Ti (1.38), Zr (1.32), and Nb (1.41) [ 47 ]; 
this suggests that TiO2, ZrO2, and Nb2O5 form preferentially over NiO. 
Based on this inference, if outward diffusion of the constituent elements 
occurs, Ti, Zr and Nb are expected to diffuse rather than Ni, which ap -
pears to be inconsistent with the experimental results ( Figs. 6 and 7 ).
In fact, the oxidation of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA involves not only 
external oxidation but also internal oxidation, as previously mentioned. 
More precisely, it entails internal oxidation followed by external 
oxidation. The oxygen atoms present in the R-EHEA matrix are more 
likely to combine with Zr and Ti due to the low Gibbs free energy of ZrO2 
and TiO2 ( Fig. 14 ), low oxygen potential ( Fig. S6 ) and the low electro -
negativity of Zr and Ti [ 48 ]. Thus, at 600

The formation of oxides changes the bonding between constituent 
atoms. The bonding between Zr, Ti and O atoms causes the Ni atoms, 
which were initially bound with Zr and Ti atoms to form the B2 phase (as 
shown in Fig. 1 ), to become free. This weakening and disappearance of 
the bonds with Ni atoms significantly enhances their diffusion rate and 
probability [ 48 – 50 ]. In essence, as the amounts of Zr and Ti oxides in -
crease, the diffusion coefficient of Ni also increases, enabling outward 
diffusion of Ni. While Nb atoms in the BCC phase are bound by weak 
metallic bonds, their large atomic masses (47.867 μ m, 91.22 μ m, 
92.9064 μ m and 58.6934 μ m for Ti, Zr, Nb, and Ni, respectively) greatly 
reduce the possibility of diffusion. Therefore, the diffusion of Nb and Zr 
atoms is rarely observed in alloys with multiple principal elements [ 41 ]. 
More importantly, if there is a sufficient diffusion driving force, the Ni 
atoms can diffuse to the surface of the alloy.
The driving force for Ni atom diffusion is its tendency to combine 
with oxygen. The enthalpy and total energy of a free atomic system, or 
the total energy in an elemental system, are much greater than those of a 
compound system. Thus, Ni atoms in the free state tend to combine with 
other atoms to form compounds due to the principle of minimum en -
ergies. Compared with intermetallic compounds, ionic bond compounds 
have lower binding energies. Therefore, Ni atoms in the free state are 
more inclined to combine with oxygen to form oxides. When the oxygen 
content is insufficient, oxygen atoms preferentially combine with Zr and 
Ti atoms, as mentioned above. Hence, Ni atoms that possess diffusion 
driving forces and abilities diffuse outwards to the oxygen-rich region 
along numerous boundary diffusion channels, leading to the formation 
of NiO on the surface of the oxidized Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA, as shown in 
Figs. 6, 7 and 13(b) .
4.4. Morphology evolution during the oxidation of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R- 
EHEA
The diffusion of elements and the formation of oxides also affect the 
morphology of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA. On the one hand, oxide for -
mation increases crystal volume and alters the phase boundaries in the 
Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA. Specifically, the number of coherent interfaces 
between the BCC and B2 phases decreases, whereas the number of 
incoherent interfaces between various oxides and the original matrix 
phases increases. This increase in the interfacial energy may lead to a 
transformation of the microstructure of the alloy. When the types and 
quantities of oxides are relatively small, as is the case in Ti2ZrNbNi3 
oxidized at 600

activation energy for structural transformation increase, which can both 
be obtained by increasing the oxidation temperature ( Fig. 3 ) and

On the other hand, with the diffusion of elements and the formation 
of oxides, particularly the development of needle-like structures, some 
pores also emerge within the oxide region of the Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA, as 
depicted in regions B and C of Fig. 7 . As the oxidation process continues, 
the oxide crystals continue to grow. Consequently, the needle-like 
structure disappears, and the volume of pores among the oxide crys -
tals decreases, indicating that the size of the pores in region B in Fig. 7 is 
much smaller than that in region C, which has a lower oxygen content 
and a shorter oxidation time.
Importantly, the development of needle-like structures and the 
combination of Ti, Zr and O are prerequisites for the outward diffusion 
of Ni atoms in the free state. This is because the Ni content in the region 
with a needle-like structure (region C in Fig. 7 ) does not decrease 
significantly, but the Ni content in the nonsegregated oxide region that 
evolved from the needle-like structures (region B in Fig. 7 ) is very low, as 
shown in Fig. 7 .

was prepared and investigated to study its oxidation behaviour. The 
following conclusions are drawn:

During the initial stage of oxidation at T ≥ 800 
◦
C, oxygen diffuses 
inward along the BCC/B2 boundaries of the ultrafine lamellar structure 
and preferentially combines with Zr and Ti to generate low-valent ox -
ides. With increasing time, the bonds between Zr, Ti, and O atoms 
combine to form high-valent oxides. Concurrently, free Ni atoms diffuse 
outward along the mass boundary diffusion channels and form dense 
NiO.
The formation of oxides leads to the expansion of the crystal volume 
and a change in the phase boundary, resulting in a transformation of the 
microstructure of the alloy and the formation of needle-like structures.
While lamellar and needle-like structures provide numerous diffu -
sion channels for oxygen and Ni atoms, dense NiO effectively prevents 
the infiltration of oxygen, resulting in the oxidation performance of the 
Ti2ZrNbNi3 R-EHEA being similar to that of pure Ni and nickel-based 
superalloys.
This work proposes and demonstrates the effectiveness of a novel 
antioxidation strategy for R-HEAs via the effects of an ultrafine lamellar 
microstructure on element diffusion and the oxidation rate. The appli -
cation potential of R-EHEAs as a high-temperature structural materials is 
further illustrated.

2. Experiment
Bulk Ti2ZrNbNi3 was prepared via vacuum arc melting. High-purity 
Ti, Zr and Nb raw materials were weighed, mixed and remelted at least 
five times. The raw Ni material was subsequently added and remelted 
three times to ensure the uniform distribution of elements, resulting in a 
10 × 10 × 75 mm ingot obtained by vacuum casting. Samples measuring 
8 × 8 × 8 mm were used for the cyclic oxidation test.
The cyclic oxidation test was carried out in a muffle furnace at 
600 
◦
C, 800 
◦
C and 1000 
◦
C in air. Each sample surface was polished 
with 1000# sandpaper and cleaned by ultrasonic waves after wire 
electrical discharge machining to ensure the removal of scales and oil 
stains. The cleaned samples were then placed into crucibles and put into 
a muffle furnace before the heating program was started. During the 
cyclic oxidation procedure, the sample was removed and weighed after 
cooling. The above process was repeated until the end of the cyclic 
oxidation test. To eliminate the influence of accidental factors, the 
oxidation cycle experiment was repeated three times for each 
temperature.
The crystal structure of Ti2ZrNbNi3 was characterized via X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) via a Rigaku SmartLab 9 KW instrument with a 
scanning rate of 5
◦
/min. The microstructure and elemental distribution 
of the preoxidized and oxidized alloys were analysed by scanning elec -
tron microscopy (SEM; JXA-8530) equipped with backscattered electron 
(BSE) and energy dispersion (EDS) detectors, as well as transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM; FEI Talos F200X). TEM samples were pre -
pared via focused ion beam (FIB; ZEISS Gemini Crossbeam 350). 
Initially, both sides of the TEM sample were thinned at 30 kV, with the 
current being gradually decreased. After preliminary processing, the 
sample surface was cleaned at 5 kV with low current to remove parts 
that were previously thinned and that caused surface damage. The 
valence states of the elements on the surface of the oxidized alloys were 
analysed via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Scientific 
Nexsa).
