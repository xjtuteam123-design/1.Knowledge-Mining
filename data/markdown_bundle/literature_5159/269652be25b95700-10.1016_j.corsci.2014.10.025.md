abstract
The effects of Al and Ta on the high temperature oxidation of Ni–8Cr–9.5Co–2.5Mo–6W–(4–5)Al–2Ti–(3–6)
Ta–0.1C–0.01B (wt.%) superalloys were examined at 850 /C176C and 1000 /C176C by cyclic oxidation tests. The oxi-
dation resistance of the superalloys was improved with an increase in the Al content in the alloy at both
temperatures, although the degree of this inﬂuence was dependent on Ta concentration at 850 /C176C. Ta
was found to be detrimental to formation of a continuous Al
2O3 layer at 850 /C176C, but it reduced oxidation rate
at 1000 /C176C or when its concentration is over 5 wt.% at 850 /C176C.

1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are highly resistant to oxidation and wear
and also they has remarkable mechanical properties at high tem-
peratures. These alloys are suitably used in highly demanding
industrial components such as gas turbine blades, jet engines,
aerospace components, and chemical plants. The gas turbine
blades, in particular, are exposed to very high temperature over
1000 /C176C. Therefore the alloy must have extraordinary oxidation
resistance otherwise it fails shortly with a large loss of metals or
due to weakened creep and fatigue strength involved by severe
oxidation [1,2]. Although ceramic coatings are used to protect
the alloy surface, fully resistant alloys are necessary because the
coating cannot always protect the alloy perfectly.
The oxidation resistance of a superalloy is primarily determined
by its composition. Al and Cr are critical because they form protec-
tive oxides, i.e. Al 2O3 and CrO 3, respectively, which inhibit further
oxidation. Cr 2O3 act as protective barrier up to about 1600 /C176F
(871 /C176C). Cr 2O3 begins to be unstable above this temperature but
Al2O3 dominates the protection [3–5]. As the roles of Cr and Al
are exclusively important, the effects of other alloying elements
on oxidation properties have received less attention. Nevertheless,
Si, Y, and Hf are known to promote selective oxidation of Al and
enhance the oxidation resistance [3]. Recent research results show
that rare earth elements such as Dy, Hf, Zr, and La can reduce oxi-
dation rate of b-NiAl by single- or co-doping [6,7], although Dy and
La sometimes promote internal oxidation [7,8]. Si and B are consid-
ered to improve oxide layer adhesion of Co-based superalloys [9].
Alloying Fe and Cr to a Co-based superalloy was also reported to
be beneﬁcial as well [10]. On the contrary, Mn was found to be det-
rimental to the oxidation resistance of Ni–Cr–W–Mo alloys but its
effect could be suppressed by the addition of Al and Si [11]. There
are conﬂicting opinions about the role of Ta since Ta appears to
improve or degrade the oxidation resistance from case to case
[9,12–14]. Yang [12] reported that a small addition of Ta as 1 at.%
improved the oxidation resistance signiﬁcantly, promoting early
establishment of a protective Al 2O3 layer. However they also found
that complex oxides such as NiMoO 4, NiTa 2O6, TaO 2, MAl 2O4, and
M2CrO4 were formed in the alloy with 3 at.% of Ta, leading to a
decrease of oxidation resistance. Klein et al. [9] presented that
2 at.% of Ta impairs the oxidation resistance of Co-based superal-
loys. It is suggested by Lu et al. [13] and Guo et al. [14] that Ta
restrains outward diffusion of Al and hence impinges on the
growth of oxide scales on a single-crystal Ni-based alloy [13] or
b-NiAl [14]. The previous works, as reviewed above, agree exten-
sively that the minor elements affect the rate to form Al 2O3 or
adhesion of the oxide layer. Nevertheless the effects of Ta, in par-
ticular, are still open to debate.
In this work, the effects of Al and Ta on the oxidation resistance of
Ni-based superalloys were investigated with a prior interest in the
interactions of the two alloying elements. The oxidation resistance
of Ni–9.5Co–8Cr–2.5Mo–6W–(4–5)Al–2Ti–(3–6)Ta–0.1C–0.01B
(wt.%) alloys was evaluated by cyclic oxidation tests. The oxide
scales were examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) along with energy dispersive spectroscopy

3. Results
The picture of specimens after oxidation tests are presented in
Fig. 1 . Oxide scale covered the entire surfaces of all specimens.
The oxidized surface was smooth and any evidence of spallation
was not found after oxidation at 850 /C176C( Fig. 1(a)). However spalled
oxides were found in every crucible after tests at 1000 /C176C
(Fig. 1 (b)). Further, spallation appears more severe for lower Al
or lower Ta alloys ( Fig. 1 (b)).
Fig. 2 shows the mass gain during the oxidation tests at 850 /C176C
(Fig. 2 (a)) and at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 2 (b)). The weight of specimens
increased gradually by oxidation. After 20 cycles of oxidation at
850 /C176C, the weight of the alloys increased by 0.60125 mg/cm 2–
0.97290 mg/cm2 (Fig. 2 (a)). The mass gain of the 4Al–5Ta alloy
was the largest while that of the 5Al–6Ta alloy was the smallest
among the specimens tested at 850 /C176C. The weight gain was more
signiﬁcant for all alloys at 1000 /C176C than at 850 /C176C( Fig. 2 (b)). The
mass gain of the 4Al–4Ta alloy after oxidation at 1000 /C176C was
1.72522 mg/cm2, which was remarkably higher than the other
alloys. The 5Al–6Ta alloy showed the best oxidation resistance
among all test specimens at 1000 /C176C as well.
The X-ray diffraction spectra of the oxidized 4Al–(4, 5, 6)Ta
alloys showed that various types of oxides were formed by oxida-
tion tests at 850 /C176C( Fig. 3 (a)). The spectra include NiO, NiCr 2O4,
Cr2O3, CrTaO 4, a-Al2O3, and very weak sign of TiO 2. The peaks of
NiCr2O4 were stronger but those of Al 2O3 were weaker for 4Al
alloys than the 5Al alloys. NiCr 2O4 is thought to be formed by a
reaction of Cr 2O3 +N i+½ O 2 ? NiCr2O4 or Cr 2O3 + NiO ? NiCr2O4
[15–20]. Thus existence of more NiCr 2O4 means a low stability of
Cr2O3 or high activity of oxygen in the scale, leading to a poor oxi-
dation resistance.
The scales formed at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 3 (b)) were found to include
NiAl2O4,A l 2O3, AlTaO 4, NiCr 2O4,C r 2O3, NiO, and CoO for some
cases. The signals from Cr 2O3 and NiCr 2O4 were very weak,
evidencing Cr-containing oxides are unstable at this temperature.
The NiAl 2O4 is known to be formed by the reaction of Al 2O3 +
Ni + ½O2 ? NiAl2O4 or Al 2O3 + NiO ? NiAl2O4 [15–18]. In other
way, NiAl 2O4 transforms to Al 2O3 by reaction of 3NiAl 2O4 +
2Al ? 4Al2O3 + 3Ni [21].
Fig. 4 shows the cross section SEM images of alloys oxidized at
850 /C176C. The oxide scales consist of outer NiCr 2O4 layer, Cr 2O3 sub-
layer, and inner Al 2O3 layer ( Fig. 4 ). Al 2O3 formed a nearly contin-
uous layer, even though it is not smooth, below the Cr 2O3 on the
4Al–4Ta alloy ( Fig. 4 (a)). However it is clearly seen that a metallic
region separates outer Cr 2O3 layer and inner Al 2O3 layer on the
other alloys ( Fig. 4 (b)–(f)). The Al 2O3 forms large agglomerates in
the 4Al–5Ta alloy ( Fig. 4(b)). The 4Al–6Ta alloy had an almost con-
tinuous Al 2O3 layer and also a discontinuous series of small parti-
cles ( Fig. 4 (c)). The Al 2O3 layers in the 5Al alloys were continuous
or partly continuous ( Fig. 5 (d)–(f)). The alloy with higher Ta con-
tent had more irregular and discontinuous Al 2O3.
Some regions with Cr, Ni, Ti, Al, Co, Ta and O were also found
and they may be designated to CrTaO 4, TiO 2, and other complex
oxides in Cr oxide layer ( Fig. 5). These complex oxides with various

4. Discussion
The inﬂuences of Al and Ta on the mass gain by oxidation and the
interactions between them are shown in Fig. 8 . It depicts the ﬁnal
values of mass gain fromFig. 2 as functions of Al or Ta concentration.
Al tends to decrease the mass gain at both test temperatures
(Fig. 8(a) and (c)). The gradient of the mass gain vs. Al content graph
is steeper when Ta content is lower as shown in Fig. 8(a), although
this dependence was not clear at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 8 (c)). Ta increased
the mass gain with its concentration up to 5 wt.% and then
decreased at 850 /C176C( Fig. 8(b)), while it reduced the mass gain con-
sistently at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 8 (d)). It corresponds to the report of Guo
et al. [14] that Nb and Ta reduced the oxidation rate of NiAl at
1200 /C176C. The mass gain vs. Ta content plots were steeper when the
Al content was lower at both temperatures. It proposes a possibility
that these two elements have interactions so the effectiveness of
each element degrades when the other’s concentration is high in
the alloy, although only 2 levels of Al concentration are tested in this
work. This proposition may be conﬁrmed by a statistical approach,
which is now in progress by our research group.
Yang [12] reported that 3 at.%, i.e. about 9 wt.%, of Ta degraded
the oxidation resistance of Ni–4.2Co–4.8Cr–12.8Al–1.6W–3.1Mo–
1.0Re–(1, 3)Ta (at.%) alloys at 1000 and 1100 /C176C. It does not

the beneﬁcial effect of Ta as we remind that the effect of Ta is weak
with 5 wt.% of Al in our result ( Fig. 2(d)). Further, the different con-

might lead to the discrepancy.
The composition of alloys changed the oxide morphology as
well as the mass gain. In particular, the morphology of Al 2O3 layer
was affected by the composition as shown in Figs. 4 and 6 . The oxi-
dation resistance of the alloys seems to depend on the concentra-
tion of Al and Ta because of the difference in oxide scale structure
or growth mechanism. The Al 2O3 scale is grown by inward diffu-
sion of oxygen and outward diffusion of Al. Previous studies sug-
gested two mechanisms for Ta retarding growth of Al oxide; one
is reducing oxygen activity [22,23] and the other is decreasing dif-
fusion of Al [13,14].
Sato et al. [22] suggested Ta inhibits rapid formation of Al 2O3
because it reduces inward diffusion of oxygen due to its higher
valance than Al. He calculated the possibility to have a continuous
Al2O3 layer thermodynamically. Additions of Al, Si, and Cr are
expected to be beneﬁcial to formation of Al 2O3 but W, Ta, Nb, Ru,
Ti, and Mo are to be detrimental. This prediction is based on a
defect chemistry in that the impurities with the higher valence
than Al 3+ in oxides retard formation of Al 2O3 because they reduce
concentration of oxygen vacancies. Si was regarded to be excep-
tional due probably to the ability to promote the nucleation of
Al2O3. Irving et al. [23] also suggested that Ta reduces oxygen
activity at the surface of a Co-based alloy.
On the other hand, Guo et al. [14] suggested for NiAl alloys that
Nb and Ta diffuse more slowly than Al due to their larger sizes and
diffusion of Al is also suppressed for them consequently. The same
research group also found that the oxide has granular grains when
diffusions of Al and O simultaneously occur while columnar grains
dominate with inward oxygen diffusion [7,24]. Lu et al. [13] pre-
sented a cross-section structure of oxide scale that has strip-like
Al oxides for Ni–4.04Al–6.74Ta–5.16Co–1.35Mo–6.29 W–12.59Cr
alloy, attributing this morphology to slow outward diffusion of Al
due to high concentration of Ta.
In this respect, high Ta concentration is thought to make Al 2O3
less continuous by reducing the activity of oxygen or decreasing
outward diffusion of Al, although it is not clear which mechanism
is dominant in this work. But it is conﬁrmed that the effects of Ta is
related to diffusion processes because formation of Al 2O3 was sup-
pressed more effectively by high Ta concentration at lower temper-
ature as recognized in Figs. 4 and 6 .
The effects of Al and Ta on the mass gain at 850 /C176C( Fig. 2(a)) are
explained in the light of the scale structure ( Fig. 4) as follows. More
Ta causes lower oxidation resistance since it inhibits formation of
highly protective Al 2O3 layer for the 4Al–5Ta alloy in comparison
with the 4Al–4Ta alloy. However Ta impedes inward diffusion of
oxygen and hence is able to improve oxidation resistance at the
same time. This effect was notable with an increase in Ta concen-
tration from 5 to 6 wt.% at 850 /C176C. The reducing effect of Al on the
mass gain is weak for the alloys with high Ta concentration
because the Al
2O3 layer is imperfect. On the contrary, when Ta con-
centration is low or Al concentration is high enough, a continuous
Al2O3 layer is formed and the protection of the alloy depends more
on Al than Ta.
The Al2O3 layers were continuous on all specimens regardless of
Ta concentration at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 6 ) unlike at 850 /C176C. It indicates
that the diffusion of oxygen and aluminum was fast enough to
form continuous Al 2O3 and the hindering effect of Ta was negligi-
ble at this temperature. Since the morphology of Al 2O3 layer does
not depend on Ta concentration, the ability of Al to protect the
alloy against oxidation will not depend as well. This is conﬁrmed
by Fig. 2 (b) in which the slopes of mass gain vs. Al concentration
plots are almost constant. The role of Ta to decrease oxygen activ-
ity is thought to be prominent when Al concentration is low
because oxygen will react preferentially with Al rather than with
Ta under high Al concentration. Further, outward diffusion of Al
will be less restrained by Ta when Al concentration is high.

In the scale formed at 850 /C176C, the areas between Cr 2O3 and
Al2O3 were composed of metal phase with very high concentration
of Ni ( Fig. 4 (b)–(f)). Such a feature may be related to reduction of
NiO by Cr. It is known as that NiO, Cr 2O3, and Al 2O3 are formed
initially but Cr can reduce NiO through a reaction of 3NiO + 2Cr ?
Ni + Cr2O3 [25,26]. Selective oxidation of Cr is promoted, leaving Ni
as a metallic phase, when oxygen activity is low [26]. Formation of
NiCr2O4 would be also limited in such a situation. Therefore, it can
be considered that the oxygen activity was lowered by an inﬂuence
of Ta and it caused less Ni-containing oxide and more Ni-rich metal
phase for the alloy with higher Ta concentration.
Ta-containing oxides were found as complex oxides as CrTaO 4
at 850 /C176C( Figs. 3 (a) and 5(a)) and AlTaO 4 at 1000 /C176C( Figs. 3 (b),
7(a), and (c)). Sometimes Ta-rich oxide was found without Al at
1000 /C176C( Fig. 7 (b)) along with Ti, Cr, and small amount of W. It
may be Ta-rich oxide such as TaO 2 [12], (Ti, Ta)O 2 [27],o rT a 2O5
[13] although the spectrum of it was not detected by XRD analysis
due probably to its low intensity. At 1000 /C176C, it is notable that the
Ta-containing oxide occupies more areas along the interface
between NiAl 2O4 and Al 2O3 in the alloy with a higher Ta content
as we examine Fig. 7 (a) and (b) in comparison. It is considered to
be due to high concentration of Ta would favor a larger or broader
form of its oxide.
Ta-containing oxides such as CrTa 2O4 and AlTa 2O4 were found
from some areas at the outer part of the scale at 850 /C176C
(Fig. 5 (a)), but at the sublayer between NiAl 2O4 and Al 2O3 at
1000 /C176C( Fig. 7 (a)–(c)). From a previous report [28], CrTaO 4 was
located beneath NiO or CoO and over Al 2O3 layer on a Ni-based
superalloy with 6.3 wt.% Al and unknown concentration of Ta after

in Li et al.’s studies [28,29] that CrTaO 4 and other oxides form

in scale structure and morphology with the oxidation temperature
might be caused by dependence of diffusion rate of elements and
growth rate of Al 2O3 on the temperature. Rapid outward diffusion
of Al will promote continuous growth of Al 2O3 and also enable
Al2O3 to form at outer part of the scale. Al 2O3 would grow primar-
ily at inner layer at a low temperature of 800–900 /C176C. At this tem-
perature, Ta-containing oxides would form at outer layer along
with Cr oxide or spinels over Al oxide as shown in Fig. 5 (a) and
the Li et al.’s report [28] because of lower diffusion rate of Ta.
On the contrary, fast outward diffusion of Al at high temperature
would facilitate growth of Al 2O3 or NiAl 2O4 at outer part of the
scale. Consequently, Ta oxides would be located between NiAl 2O4
and Al 2O3 (Fig. 7 ), in the middle of the layers with Al 2O3 [29],o r
beneath the Al 2O3 [27]. Nevertheless, Al oxide would not be
located over Ta oxides due presumably to the diffusion rate of Al
is impinged by Ta in a high Ta alloy [13] or due to various exper-
imental conditions including concentration of other alloying ele-
ments and oxidation time.
Cracks in scales were often found at the interfaces between
NiCr2O4 and Cr 2O3 at 850 /C176C( Fig. 4 (b)) or between NiAl 2O4 and
Al2O3 at 1000 /C176C( Fig. 6 (b)) but rarely between Cr 2O3 and Al 2O3.
Sometimes they developed along the Al 2O3 layer at the metal/
oxide interface ( Fig. 6 (e) and (f)). Cracking resulted from mainly
by the large difference in the thermal expansion coefﬁcients
[30,31]. The thermal expansion coefﬁcients of Al 2O3 and Cr 2O3
are small as 8.1 /C210/C06 K/C01 [32] and 8.7 /C210/C06 K/C01 [33], respec-
tively, being close to each other. However those of both NiAl 2O4
and NiCr 2O4 are 10 /C210/C06 K/C01 [34] so these complex oxides
expands much more than Al 2O3 or Cr2O3. NiO has even larger ther-
mal expansion coefﬁcient of 17.1 /C210/C06 K/C01 [35], thus spallation
can occur easily if NiO is in contact with other oxides. A large stress
is expected at the interface of Al 2O3 and alloy which has thermal
coefﬁcient of 18.6 /C210/C06 K/C01 [36], of course.
Considering the large difference in the thermal expansion
coefﬁcients, it is possible that Ni and Cr oxides might be taken
off from the surface of the alloy at 1000 /C176C leaving NiAl 2O4 or
Al2O3 (Fig. 6 ) during oxidation cycles [27]. Rapid growth of oxides
at this temperature involves a high stress at the interfaces.
Similarly, NiAl2O4 might be spalled off due to high interfacial stress
between NiAl2O4 and Al2O3 (Fig. 6(d)–(f)). In addition, the transfor-
mation of NiAl 2O4 into Al 2O3 by the reaction of 3NiAl 2O4 + 2Al ?
4Al2O3 + 3Ni [21], promoted by high Al concentration, would accel-
erate the dissipation of NiAl 2O4 layer.
5. Conclusions
The oxidation resistance of Ni–8Cr–9.5Co–2.5Mo–6W–(4–5)
Al–2Ti–(3–6)Ta–0.1C–0.01B superalloys was evaluated by cyclic
oxidation tests at 850 /C176C and 1000 /C176C. The oxidation scales were
composed mainly of outer NiCr 2O4 layer, Cr 2O3 sublayer, and
inner Al 2O3 layer at 850 /C176C but outer NiAl 2O4 and inner Al 2O3 lay-
ers at 1000 /C176C. High Al content suppressed formation of complex
oxides such as NiCr 2O4 and NiAl 2O4 and reduced the mass gain
by oxidation at both temperatures. Al reduced mass gain by oxi-
dation but the protecting effect of Al depends on Ta content at
850 /C176C, since high Ta concentration hinders formation of continu-
ous Al 2O3 layer. However a continuous layer of Al 2O3 was estab-
lished regardless of Ta content and the inﬂuence of Al on the
oxidation resistance did not depend on Ta concentration at
1000 /C176C. Small addition of Ta up to 5 wt.% degraded oxidation
resistance at 850 /C176C as it retards formation of Al 2O3. However,
Ta exerted a beneﬁcial effect on oxidation resistance when its
