Effect of the c’ volume fraction on the creep
strength of Ni-base superalloys
Dedicated to Professor Dr . Otmar Vöhrunger on the occasion of his 65th birthday
Three single-crystalline Ni-base superalloys with varying
content of the c0 phase are investigated with respect to their
microstructure and creep behaviour at 1000 °C. It is shown
by convergent beam electron diffraction, X-ray diffraction
and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy that the compo-
sition of the matrix and precipitates as well as the lattice
misfit between both phases are similar for all three model
alloys despite a variation of the c0 content from about 50 to
about 65 %. This allows to study the influence of the precip-
itate volume fraction on high-temperature behaviour in sep-
aration from other microstructural parameters. Highest
creep strength is found for the alloy with intermediate parti-
cle content (about 58 %), which is significantly below the
particle volume fraction of contemporary single-crystal
superalloys such as CMSX-4.
Keywords: Ni-base superalloys; Creep; Single-crystalline
superalloys

1. Introduction
The high-temperature strength of single-crystal Ni-base
superalloys results mainly from the presence of intermetal-
lic Ni
3Al precipitates ( c0). The precipitates are coherently
embedded in the Ni solid solution matrix ( c phase). In these
two-phase materials, stacking faults or antiphase bound-
aries must be created in the ordered c0 phase (L1 2 crystal
structure) before the matrix [face-centred cubic (fcc) crystal
structure] dislocations can penetrate the precipitate [1]. In-
teraction of matrix dislocations with the uniformly dis-
persed precipitates, where the precipitates form barriers to
dislocation motion, is the principal strengthening mecha-
nism [2]. Therefore, like in other precipitation hardened al-
loys, increasing the c0 volume fraction in Ni superalloys in-
creases the alloy strength by creating more barriers to
dislocation motion. However, for precipitation hardening
to be effective, it is essential that the c matrix remains the
continuous phase in the microstructure and the c0 precipi-
tates are uniformly distributed in the matrix [3]. At very
high c0 volume fractions, microstructure inversion occurs,
i. e., the c0 becomes the continuous phase with a relatively
small amount of c precipitates. Thereby, the density of bar-
riers diminishes and a loss of strength is then expected.
Modern single-crystal superalloys contain about 60 –
70 % c’ particles as this is believed to be the optimum con-
tent. However, it is also clear from the above discussion that
this optimum may depend on the loading condition. For ex-
ample, an alloy containing less c’ phase may be softer com-
pared to a counterpart with higher c’ content in the early
stage of creep deformation, as the initial c/c’ interface den-
sity is less at comparable particle size. However, c’ coales-
cence by directional coarsening (i. e., rafting) may also be
more sluggish due to the larger interparticle spacing so that
loss in strength may be less pronounced during long-term
service at elevated temperatures.
It is the purpose of this work to study the influence of the
c’ content on creep behaviour. A relatively simple quarter-
nary model system, namely Ni – Al – Ta – W, is used in con-
junction with thermodynamic calculations utilising the
THERMOCALC software [4] to keep other important
parameters such as c/c’ misfit and composition of the two
phases essentially constant. This is necessary to clearly dis-
cern the effect of the c’ content from other factors. Selection
of the alloy compositions takes also into account the follow-
ing requisites: (i) microstructural stability against undesir-
able Topologically Close Packed (TCP) phase formation
after long-term high-temperature exposure; (ii) sufficient
heat treatment window for complete c
0 solutioning, and
(iii) low c/c0 lattice misfit (< 0.3 %). Based on these princi-
ples, three alloys were designed, covering a c’ content of
about 50 to 65 % at 1000 °C. In this paper, we determine
the c’ volume fraction and c/c’ misfit quantitatively and dis-
cuss the observed creep behaviour.

2. Experimental
In order to achieve the above-mentioned goals, it was neces-
sary to first evaluate a number of experimental polycrystal-
line alloys melted in small quantities (2 kg). They were
characterised with respect to their microstructure and lattice
misfit. Quaternary alloys containing W as solid solution
strengthening element and Al as well as Ta for c0 formation
were produced in a vacuum induction furnace and cast in
metal moulds. The amount of W was varied between 10
and 15.5 wt.%. The initial compositions were selected on
the basis of thermodynamic calculations using the THER-
MOCALC software [4]. Cr and Mo were added in further
trials to adjust the c/c0 lattice misfit to the desired level.
The alloy compositions are given in Table 1. The three al-
loys which were finally selected for single-crystal casting
are marked by (*) in Table 1. Single-crystal bars of the se-
lected alloys were manufactured at Doncasters, Bochum,
in the form of 20 mm diameter rods, 120 mm long. The
compositional inhomogeneity due to dendritic coring af-
fected the precipitate morphology in the as-cast condition,
see Fig. 1. This effect was the more pronounced the higher
the W content was.
Homogenisation of the composition and desired precipi-
tate morphology were obtained through heat treatment,
which consisted of a solutionising step, followed by two-
step ageing. It is well known that the best solutionising in
Ni superalloys is achieved in the single-phase c region.
However, the solution window between c0 solvus and the in-
cipient-melting point was narrow. Therefore, the solution
treatment had to be optimised for each alloy experimen-
tally. For the alloy with the highest c0 volume fraction
(W11), a three-step solution heat treatment (1250 °C/
1 h + 1270 °C/1 h + 1290 °C/3 h/argon quench) was neces-
sary while a single-step solution treatment (1260 °C/3 h/ar-
gon quench) was adequate for alloys W21 and W31. All
alloys were subsequently aged at 1100 °C/4 h/air
cool + 850 °C/24 h/air cool. A typical fully heat-treated mi-
crostructure, containing uniformly distributed cuboidal c0
precipitates of size 400 – 500 nm cube edge length, is shown
in Fig. 2.
The microstructure of the alloys was characterised by
optical microscopy, scanning and transmission electron
microscopy (SEM, TEM). Energy-dispersive X-ray spec-
troscopy (EDS) in TEM was used to determine c and c0
phase compositions and volume fractions [5]. Lattice
parameters of matrix and precipitate phases were measured
by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and convergent beam electron
diffraction (CBED) [6, 7]. TEM samples were prepared by
twin-jet electro-polishing in a solution of 10 % perchloric
acid in 90 % ethanol with 7 g thiourea. All TEM measure-
ments were carried out in a Philips CM120 microscope
equipped with an EDS system. The beam size for the EDS
measurements was usually about 5 nm. Constant load creep
tests were performed at 1000 °C generally until rupture.
However, some tests were interrupted after a predetermined
strain value to monitor the microstructural evolution during
deformation.
3. Results and discussion
The ageing heat treatments used in this study, as described
above, produced secondary spherical c0 precipitates
(Fig. 3a) between the large cuboidal c0 precipitates due to
fast cooling from 1100 °C followed by ageing at 850 °C.
The secondary precipitates interfere with the measurement
of the c phase lattice parameter and composition by CBED
and EDS. In order to eliminate their influence, a modified
ageing heat treatment (1100 °C/4 h/slow cool to 850 °C+
850 °C/24 h/air cool) was used to avoid precipitation of
secondary c0 (Fig. 3b). As can be seen in Fig. 3, the
modified ageing treatment does not alter the morphology
of the cuboidal c0 precipitates or their size (average size
/C25 450 nm).

3.1. V olume fraction and phase composition
The c0 volume fraction, fv, which is an important parameter
in the present study, was measured by different methods. It
can be determined from the partitioning of any given alloy-
ing element to the two phases [5] by the following relation:
f
v =( C – Cc)/(Cc0 – Cc)
where C, Cc and Cc0 are the concentration of the given ele-
ment in the alloy, the c and the c0 phases, respectively. The
c and c0 phase compositions of alloys W11, W21 and W31
were measured by EDS in TEM and are listed in Table 2.
The concentration differences ( C – Cc) and ( Cc0 – Cc) are
shown in Fig. 4 as an example of alloy W11 for each alloy-
ing element. The slope of the least-square fit gives then the
c
0 volume fraction. The results are listed in Table 2 for all
three alloys. As the compositions were measured after
long-term ageing at 850 °C, the measured volume fractions
relate to a different temperature than the calculated values
listed in Table 1 (temperature T = 1000 °C). Nevertheless,
calculated and measured fv values show reasonable agree-
ment.
The precipitate volume fractions were also measured by
c0 extraction via electrochemical dissolution of the c phase
[8]. The extracted precipitates are collected through a filter-
ing procedure [8] using a filter with submicron pore size.
The volume fraction is determined comparing the weight
of the initial sample with that of the extracted c
0 particles
and the results are listed in Table 3.
The volume fraction values determined by the two differ-
ent methods correspond closely. The observed deviations
result from the inaccuracy of the EDS measurements and
the thermodynamic database. For alloys with high c0 con-
tent as investigated here, measurement of the c-phase com-
position is particularly difficult in view of the small c-chan-
nel width.

3.3. Creep testing
Single-crystal specimens oriented in [001] direction were
used for creep testing of alloys W11SX, W21SX and
W31SX. (The suffix “SX” is used to distinguish data ob-
tained on single crystals from those obtained on polycrys-
talline samples.) All tests were performed at 1000 °C and
the applied stress was varied between 140 and 210 MPa. In-
terrupted tests were conducted to monitor the evolution of
microstructure as a function of creep strain. The creep
curves for all three alloys are compared at a stress level of
190 MPa in Fig. 9. The rupture life is compiled in Table 5.
It is seen that alloy W21SX with intermediate c
0 content
(approx. 58 %) is stronger than the other two alloys. While
W11SX and W31SX show similar creep life despite vastly
different c0 contents, a noteworthy difference is that
W11SX (high c0 content) exhibits initially a lower creep
strain rate _ee than W31SX (low c0 content). This is illustrated
more clearly in Fig. 10, which shows that the minima in the
creep rate is reached at a lower strain value, for W11SX
compared to W31SX. The applied stress value however,
has an influence and the details will be published later [13].

To rationalise the above behaviour, it is useful to inspect
the microstructural evolution during creep testing, as shown
in Fig. 11 for alloy W31SX tested at 170 MPa. When the
creep experiment is interrupted at 0.4 % strain, i. e., before
the creep rate minimum _ee
min is reached, individual c0 parti-
cles, decorated with a dislocation network at the c/c0 inter-
faces, are observed. Strain hardening is occurring during
this early stage of creep deformation as long-range back
stresses result from the arrested interfacial dislocations
[14]. At 0.75 % strain, i. e., shortly after _eemin is reached, par-
ticle coalescence has already started and substantial nar-
rowing of remaining vertical c channels is noted. Horizon-
tal and vertical c channels between the c0 precipitates are
distinguished by their orientation relative to the loading
axis. The stress axis is parallel to the vertical channels and
perpendicular to the horizontal channels. Somewhat later,
i. e., after 1.5 % creep deformation, well-defined rafts have
been formed and entrapped interfacial dislocations are ob-
served at numerous locations within the c0 phase (at posi-
tions marked a, b and c in Fig. 12).
From the above observation, the following qualitative
scenario is derived: Before _eemin is reached, creep deforma-
tion occurs exclusively in the c matrix. Strain hardening
takes place because (i) long-range back stresses are gener-
ated by the interfacial dislocations and (ii) deformation of
the vertical channels becomes increasingly difficult as they
narrow. More and more load is transferred onto the c0 parti-
cles in the course of this process until creep deformation of
the c
0 phase starts as well. This must happen once the
c0 phase has formed an interconnected network by rafting
but my also occur earlier. At this point in time, the strength-
ening contribution of the c0 particles is fully utilised and
further deformation leads to softening as the two-phase mi-
crostructure coarsens. Alloys with higher c0 content are
stronger in the early phase of creep deformation as (i) the
c channels are narrower at comparable c0 size and (ii) the
interface density is higher. However, coalescence of the c0
particles happens also earlier as the spacing between adja-
cent side faces is less. Thus, deformation of the c0 phase,
which marks the onset of softening, is occurring earlier. In
consequence, _eemin is reached at smaller strains and accelera-
tion of the creep rate takes place when an alloy with lower
c0 content is still hardening.
4. Conclusions
The effect of the c’ content on the creep strength of single-
crystalline Ni-base superalloys was studied. It was ensured
by careful design of the chemical compositions, using the
THERMOCALC software, that the three inspected model
alloys exhibited similar composition of the c and c’ phases
as well as similar misfit between them. Otherwise, it would
have been impossible to discern the effect of the c’ content
from other factors. The particle content at the testing tem-
perature of 1000 °C was about 50 %, 58 and 65 % for alloys
W31SX, W21SX and W11SX, respectively. Important ob-
servations are summarised as follows:
• The highest creep strength was observed for alloy
W21SX with intermediate c’ content. This is note-
worthy, considering that contemporary single-crystal-
line superalloys, such as CMSX-4, PW A 1484 or Rene
N5, contain typically 65 % of the c’ phase.
• The rupture lives of alloys W11SX and W31SX are
similar under the conditions tested here (1000 °C/140 –
210 MPa), despite vastly different particle contents.
• At the onset of creep deformation, the creep rate is con-
trolled by the initial width of the c channels. They are
