ABSTRACT
The tensile behavior and deformation mechanism of Ti-10Mo alloy at room temperature (298 K) were investi -
gated. The results show that the alloy has an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 744 MPa and a high yield strength 
(YS) of 662 MPa, as well as an excellent elongation (El) after fracture of 38 %, outperforming Ti-15Mo alloy 
under the same conditions. The superior strength and plasticity of Ti-10Mo alloy at 298 K are attributed to the 
activation of both {332} 〈 113 > twinning and SIM α “ during deformation. The sequence of different deformation 
mechanisms is as follows: in the initial stage of deformation, dislocation slip can be extensively activated and 
continues to occur during subsequent deformation; in the middle stage of deformation, primary and secondary 
{332} 〈 113 〉 twinning and SIM α ” are activated simultaneously, resulting in a continuous increase in work 
hardening rate; in the later stage of deformation, the alloy can further deform through {1 1 1}
α “ 
twinning. The 
higher YS may be due to the optimal quantity and size of athermal ω phase in Ti-10Mo alloy, which increases the 
YS without significantly affecting plasticity. Meanwhile, Quenching vacancies can also increase the YS of the 
alloy. The three stages of the work hardening rate curve correspond to the activation of the dislocation slip 
mechanism, the activation of the {332} 〈 113 〉 twinning and SIM α ” and the stop of the {332} 〈 113 〉 twinning and 
SIM α “ .
1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are widely favored in aerospace, petrochemical, 
marine engineering, biomedical, automotive and shipbuilding fields due 
to their ultra-high specific strength, good hardenability, excellent 
corrosion resistance, biocompatibility and non-magnetic characteristics 
[1 – 5] . In recent years, metastable β titanium alloys have received 
widespread attention because of their complex deformation mechanisms 
and various excellent properties. It is reported that the deformation 
mechanism of metastable β titanium alloys can change with the content 
of β -stabilizers [1,6] . With the change of β stabilizing elements, the 
deformation mechanisms include dislocation slip, {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twin -
ning, {1 1 2} < 1 1 1 > twinning, ω phase transformation, α “ martensitic 
transformation and the combination of several mechanisms [1,6,7] . 
Different deformation mechanisms endow the alloys with different 
properties. For example, α ” martensite can cause the alloy to have su -
perplasticity and shape memory effect [8] , and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning 
and {1 1 2} < 1 1 1 > twinning can cause the alloy to have good strength- 
plasticity matching [1,6] .

For Ti-Mo binary alloy, different Mo element contents can make the 
alloys show different deformation mechanisms and mechanical prop -

nism is dislocation slip, and the alloy often shows a higher yield strength 
(YS), but the elongation-to-fracture (El) is poor [17] , such as Ti-22.5Mo

can obtain a higher El through a significant work hardening process [1] , 
such as Ti-15Mo [15] . When the Mo element content is further reduced, 
{3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning and SIM α “ can be activated simultaneously, 
such as Ti-12Mo [5,18] . The simultaneous activation of twinning- 
induced plasticity (TWIP) and transformation-induced plasticity 
(TRIP) effects can make the alloy have high strength, high plasticity and 
excellent work hardening rate [19 – 21] . However, compared with TWIP 
alloys, the current research on the room temperature deformation 
mechanism of TWIP and TRIP metastable β titanium alloys is relatively 
less. In addition to Ti-12Mo being studied in partial, other alloys, such as 
Ti-10Mo [22] , Ti-4Al-7Mo-3V-3Cr [12] , Ti-9Mo-6W [21] and Ti-7Mo- 
3Nb-3CR-3Al [23] have been proven to be TWIP/TRIP alloys, but the 
work hardening and microstructure evolution process of the alloy during 
deformation are still unclear. But this is very important for under -
standing the strength-plasticity and work hardening behavior of meta -
stable β titanium alloys, so more evidence is still needed at present.

alloy from undergoing martensitic transformation when quenched and 
cooled to room temperature [22] . Secondly, using the d-electron theory

4. Discussion
The Ti-10Mo alloy with dislocation slip, {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning and 
SIM α “ as deformation mechanisms, which has high work hardening rate 
and El at room temperature. Previous researches have shown that alloys 
with SIM α ” and twinning involved in deformation often leads to higher 
work hardening rates and El [19,32 – 34] , which is consistent with the 
results of this experiment. This is mainly because the metastable β ti -
tanium alloy Ti-10Mo with TWIP and TRIP effects can produce the 
following four effects during deformation: (1) The large number of twins 
and martensite interfaces produced during the deformation process can 
act as obstacles to dislocation movement, thereby producing a signifi -
cant dynamic Hall-Petch effect, which making the alloy have a better 
work hardening rate [1,13] ; (2) The α “ phase with an orthogonal 
structure has a far smaller elastic modulus than the β phase, so this 
microstructure with a strong elastic modulus contrast (similar to com -
posite materials) is beneficial to plasticity and work hardening rate 
[1,19] ; (3) The relatively “ soft ” SIM α ” phase hardens the matrix 
through the dynamic “ composite effect ” produced by its mechanical 
contrast with the β matrix, which is conducive to uniform plasticity 
[13,19] ; (4) The elastic anisotropy between the (332) < 113 > twin and 
the matrix causes a strong mechanical property contrast between them, 
which is manifested as high-density dislocations at the twin boundary in 
the transmission image, which will also cause additional strain hard -
ening [19,27] .
In addition, it is worth noting that in this experiment, the YS of the 
Ti-10Mo alloy is at a high level, which has almost reached 90% of the 
tensile strength (664 MPa). This is quite unusual, because it seems that 
most TWIP/TRIP alloys have a YS of less than 600 MPa [13,21,34,35] , 
such as Ti-12Mo and Ti-15Mo. Generally speaking, the improvement of 
the YS of the alloy is mainly achieved through solid solution strength -
ening and fine grain strengthening [16,36 – 38] , while the UTS and El 
mainly depend on the deformation mechanism [16] . First of all, the alloy 
used in this experiment is extra low interstitial (ELI) level, so the 
contribution of oxygen elements to solid solution strengthening may be 
relatively small. Secondly, as shown in Fig. 2 , the grain size of the alloy 
in this experiment is relatively large (about 200 μ m), so the fine grain 
strengthening effect is almost ineffective. We believe that the athermal ω 
phase formed during quenching may be beneficial to the YS of the Ti- 
10Mo alloy, because it is well known that the size and content of the 
athermal ω phase in metastable β titanium alloys determine whether it 
will affect the YS of the alloy. For example, some researchers pointed out 
that the growth of ω phase caused by air cooling conditions in meta -
stable β titanium alloys will cause the local elastic strain around the ω 
phase and β matrix interface to increase rapidly, become an obstacle to 
the movement of sliding dislocations, and thus strengthen the alloy 
[20,39] . In addition, compared with the athermal ω phase in Ti-12Mo 
[1] and Ti-15Mo [40] , the athermal ω phase observed in Ti-10Mo is less 
than Ti-12Mo but larger in size and the quantity and size are more ad -
vantageous than Ti-15Mo, which may indicate that the quantity and size 
of athermal ω phase in Ti-10Mo are just in an optimal range, so as to 
improve the YS of the alloy to a certain extent without causing severe 
impact on plasticity. What ’ more, the lattice distortion caused by va -
cancies in the grains also plays an important role in improving the YS of 
the alloy [41] . In this experiment, Ti-10Mo alloy is cooled to room 
temperature by water quenching. Therefore, a large number of va -
cancies at high temperature may be retained to room temperature 
because it is too late to diffuse to the equilibrium concentration during 
quenching. Therefore, a large number of quenching vacancies may also 
be beneficial to the increase of YS of the alloy.
In addition, similar to Ti-12Mo, from Fig. 3 (a and b), we can find 
obvious double yield phenomenon and the existence of a special rising 
stage (Stage II) of the work hardening rate curve, which makes the work 
hardening rate of the alloy continue to rise in a large strain range (about 
0.22) and finally makes the alloy have excellent work hardening per -
formance and elongation, while the Ti-15Mo [35] alloy with a single 
{3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning as the main deformation mechanism does not 
exist this special stage II. In fact, this is related to the evolution of the 
microstructure during the stretching process. The variation trend of the 
work hardening rate curve can be combined with the evolution process 
of the microstructure as follows: The deformation process of Ti-10Mo 
alloy during stretching can be divided into the initial stage of plastic 
deformation (Stage I in Fig. 3 (b)), the middle stage of plastic deforma -
tion (Stage II in Fig. 3 (b)) and the late stage of plastic deformation (Stage 
III in Fig. 3 (b)). At room temperature, due to the low CRSS of dislocation 
slip, dislocation slip is activated in large quantities in Stage I. As shown 
in Fig. 9 (e), a large number of dislocation entanglements produced by 
dislocation interactions can indeed be seen in the grains. A large number 
of dislocations interact with each other inside the grains, which in turn 
gradually reduces the mobility of dislocations [42] . As a result, although 
the work hardening rate is high at the beginning, with the increase of 
external stress, the dislocation entanglement, dislocation cells and other 
substructures produced by dislocation interactions will gradually tend to 
saturate and the work hardening rate will show a rapid decline. With the 
continuous increase of external stress, in the Stage II, due to the gradual 
increase of the shear stress of dislocation slip, the shear stress gradually 
reaches the CRSS of SIM α “ phase and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twin activation, so 
primary and secondary SIM α ” and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning were acti -
vated to further coordinate the deformation of grains, which makes the 
work hardening rate rise rapidly. J.Y. Zhang et al. [1] believe that the α “ 
martensites and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twins interfaces can act as obstacles to 
dislocation movement, which reduces the average free path of disloca -
tion slip and leads to a significant increase in the work hardening rate. 
Additionally, since the α ” phase is an orthogonal structure, its elastic 
modulus is much smaller than that of the β phase [43] , it is believed that 
the intertwined organization of these soft and hard phases contributes 
significantly to the work hardening rate of the material [1] . Further -
more, F. Sun et al. [5,35] also believe that although the α “ phase is 
softer, if the stress-induced β→ α ” transformation occurs in the plastic 
stage, it will improve the uniformity of plastic deformation of β titanium 
alloys and increase the strain hardening rate. What is more, SIM α “ may 
also promote strain hardening through new phase boundaries. There -
fore, we believe that Stage II with α ” martensite and {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 >
twinning as the main deformation mechanisms is the reason for the 
excellent mechanical properties of Ti-10Mo alloy at room temperature. 
Ti-10Mo alloy has a YS of 662 MPa, an UTS of 744 MPa and an El of 38%, 
which are superior to those of Ti-15Mo alloy with single {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 >
twinning as the main deformation mechanism. The strain hardening rate 
curve of Ti-15Mo alloy does not have the rising stage shown in Stage II 
[15] , which from another aspect illustrates the special role of stress- 
induced martensite α “ in the strain hardening and El of Ti-10Mo alloy. 
This is consistent with the conclusions of many researchers [35,44,45] . 
As deformation progresses to the Stage III, due to the limited space in -
side the grains, {3 3 2} < 1 1 3 > twinning and martensitic transformation 
tend to saturate, so the work hardening rate drops rapidly.
In summary, as predicted by the Bo- Md diagram, the Ti-10Mo alloy 
possesses both TWIP and TRIP dual effects, thus endowing the alloy with 
excellent work hardening rate and El at room temperature, while also 
having a higher YS. This adds new evidence and provides theoretical 
guidance for designing metastable β titanium alloy alloys with superior 
performance utilizing TWIP and TRIP effects.
