ABSTRACT
Two Nb-based alloys containing Ti, Mo and W were produced by Plasma Transferred Arc (PTA) and their 
microstructure and oxidation properties in the temperature range of 500 – 700 
◦
C were reported and compared 
with a pure Nb sample. The processed materials showed a homogeneous dendritic microstructure with all the 
elements in solid solution. Raman spectroscopy analysis allowed to identify the formation of a protective 
TiNb
2
O
7 
oxide layer, which provided the alloys protection against high temperature oxidation. It was concluded 
that the Nb-Ti-W and Nb-Ti-Mo alloys can be used as a competitive alternative for high strength, high- 
temperature applications.
1. Introduction
The first commercial uses of Niobium (Nb), in the early 20th century, 
were limited to alloying steel. Only a few kilos of niobium were avail -
able, since its extraction was limited as a by-product of tantalum refining 
[ 1 ]. World War II, and later the Cold War, sparked interest in developing 
new materials capable of withstanding high temperatures; to be applied 
in technologies such as jet engines, rocket nozzles, and directional 
thrusters. That ’ s when Nb stood out, as it retained a good degree of 
strength even at temperatures above 1050 
◦
C. On the other hand, pure 
Nb oxidizes quickly above 500 
◦
C since it forms non-protective scales of 
Nb
2
O
5 
[ 2 – 5 ]. For this reason, since the 1960s, various studies have been 
carried out seeking to improve resistance to oxidation at high temper -
atures. Initially, the objective was to use alloy elements that would form 
a protective oxide layer; testing hundreds of alloys [ 6 , 7 ]. Another 
alternative was the use of elements that acted as a barrier and hindered 
the diffusion of oxygen into the metal [ 2 , 8 – 11 ]. Although there are not 
many records, since most are protected because they are strategic ma -
terials, it is known that Boeing Company and Wah Chang Corporation 
established a cooperation to explore series of alloys; the first series were 
called “ C ” , and the most promising alloy was the C-103, with a nominal 
composition of Nb-10 %Hf-1 %Ti [ 1 ]. At that time, Hafnium was sepa -
rated from Zirconium, had few uses, and was accumulated as a by- 
product. Furthermore the defense spending as a percent of GDP were 
as high as 15 % [ 12 ], so the cost of the alloy was not so relevant. 
Nowadays, Hf can be up to 20 times more expensive than other re -
fractory elements ( Table 1 ), so it is necessary to look for other cheaper 
alternatives that also have good properties.
Recently, several studies have shown that the addition of Titanium 
(Ti), Molybdenum (Mo) or Tungsten (W) can improve the high tem -
perature strength and corrosion resistance of Niobium alloys [ 13 – 19 ]. 
Historically, processing W had been expensive and complicated, due to 
its high melting point, but new processing techniques have made it 
possible to obtain and process it in a simple way, to the point of being the 
most economical refractory element. Techniques such as Plasma 
Transferred Arc (PTA) allow melting W to form homogeneous Nb alloys; 
in addition, PTA can be adapted to additive manufacturing (AM) sys -
tems, making it possible to produce components with complex geome -
tries from the build-up of deposition of layers up on layers, without 
wasting material due to machining [ 20 – 23 ].
In this work, Nb-based alloys (which contain Mo, W and Ti) were 
processed by PTA; and its oxidation resistance, between 500 
◦
C to 
700 
◦
C, was evaluated by means of mass gain. Two alloys were assessed, 
Nb-20 %Ti-10 %W and Nb-20 %Ti-10 %Mo, and the effect of these el -
ements on the formation of a protective oxide layer was analyzed. The 
objective is to explore Nb alloys that offer better cost-benefit and that 
can be processed by additive manufacturing techniques, which would be 
doubly advantageous as it reduces both material waste and the cost of 
the alloy.

2. Materials and methods
Metal powders (99.9 % purity) were acquired from Stanford 
Advanced Materials; The morphology and particle size distribution are 
presented in Fig. 1 . The pure Nb (in cast condition) was donated by the 
CBMM (Companhia Brasileira de Metalurgia e Mineraç ˜ao). Based on the 
results of R. Smith [ 6 ]; two Niobium alloys, which presented good 
resistance to oxidation at high temperature, were selected to be pro -
cessed by PTA and evaluate their possible application in additive 
manufacturing. The composition of the metal powders used to produce 
the Nb-alloys is shown in Table 2 . To obtain the desired chemical 
composition (at. %), the corresponding weight of each metal powder 
was calculated. The powders were mixed in a Y mixer at 600 rpm for 12 
h. Before PTA deposition, the mixed powders were dried in an oven at 
70 
◦
C to remove moisture.
A STARWELD PTA coating systems from Kennametal, equipped with 
a Model 600 Torch was used. Two mini ingots (for each composition) of 
approx. 8 g each were processed by PTA using the parameters reported 
in Table 3 . The material was deposited on a copper plate, in four 
deposition stages of 1 min each and waiting for 5 min of cooling between 
each stage. Due to the high thermal conductivity of copper, the material 
deposited in the first layer did not completely melt (especially particles 
with a higher melting point, such as W). For this reason, after the last 
deposition, it was necessary to turn the ingot upside down to reprocess 
the first layer using the same parameters but without feeding material.
The mini ingots were cut into slices of approx. 2 mm thick, for 
characterization, and the slices were cut in a rectangular shape to 
facilitate the estimation of the area exposed in the oxidation tests, Fig. 2 . 
It is worth highlighting that the deposited materials had very low 
porosity, indicating that these alloys can be easily processed for future 
additive manufacturing applications. Microstructural characterization 
was performed on an Olympus BX51M light microscope, and a TESCAN 
VEGA3 LMU, Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) at the Electronic 
Microscopy Center of the Federal University of Paran ´a (CME/UFPR). 
The samples were grinding and polishing for metallographic analysis, 
the chemical etching used was: 3 mL of lactic acid, 1 mL of HNO
3 
and 1 
mL of HF; the surface was moistened with a cotton swab for 5 to 10 s, 
rinse in water, then alcohol, dried in air and inspected for degree of 
etching [ 24 ].
For the oxidation tests, all surfaces of the rectangular samples were 
sanded to 600 grit sandpaper, and ultrasonically cleaned with alcohol. 
Each individual sample was placed inside a ceramic crucible with a lid 
(previously cleaned and dried); The sample crucible assembly was 
weighed on a Shimadzu AUW 220D analytical balance, before the high 
temperature oxidation tests, and every 2 h during the tests. Pseudo- 
isothermal oxidation tests were carried out at 500 
◦
C and 700 
◦
C, in 
air, the assemblies were placed inside the furnace once the established 
temperature was reached. After the defined time (2 h) they were 
removed for weighing, and then returned to the hot furnace again and 
continued with the oxidation test, repeating the procedure until the 
determined total test time was met.
Finished the pseudo-isothermal test, the surface of the samples was 
analyzed in a WITec alpha 300R Confocal Raman microscope. All 
Raman spectra were acquired for 60 s at room temperature with a green 
laser; 532 nm excitation wavelength, 50 mW power, and 50 × objective 
lens. For each sample, Raman spectra were acquired at 3 random points 
on the sample surfaces; all spectra of the same sample were equivalent.
3. Results and discussions
The microstructure of both alloys, obtained by reflected light optical 
microscopy, is shown in Fig. 3 . Both alloys have a completely dendritic 
microstructure. The microstructure of the NbTi20Mo10 alloy ( Fig. 3 a, c) 
is coarser, compared to the NbTi20W10 ( Fig. 3 b, d). In both samples it 
was observed that the dendrites in the center of the ingots are coarser 
( Fig. 3 a, b), compared to the finer microstructure of the last molten 
material (either the 4th deposition, or the 1st that was remelted) that did 
not undergo thermal cycling. No difference was noticed in the micro -
structure of the material in the middle of the ingots; actually the 2nd and 
3rd depositions could not be differentiated, the size and morphology of 
the dendrites were the same. Clearly thermal cycles are responsible for 
the coarsening of the microstructure in the intermediate depositions; but 
when comparing both alloys, which were processed with the same heat 
input, the difference in the microstructure is due to metallurgical fac -
tors. All three elements, Nb, Mo and W, are completely miscible (have 
complete solubility) from room temperature to melting, forming a body- 
centered cubic phase (Im-3 m, space group) [ 25 ]. Due to the high 
melting point of W, compared to the other elements, the undercooling in 
the NbTi20W10 alloy is greater, this increases the number of nuclei 
formed and promotes grain refinement. Yukang et al., working with 
Ni
–
Cu alloys, showed that undercooling in the range of 0 – 60 K pro -
duces coarse dendrite, while undercooling of 180 K develops micro -
structure with fine dendrite [ 26 ].
Titanium is also miscible with other elements at high temperatures, 
but below 500 
◦
C (for our compositions) it stabilizes the hexagonal α -Ti 
phase creating a biphasic microstructure. Since Ti has the lowest melting 
point among the four elements, the alpha-Ti phase is the last to solidify, 
which is the interdendritic region (dark regions in Fig. 3 ). Senkov et al. 
reported a single-phase BCC microstructure in Nb-based alloys con -
taining Ti, Mo and W, which is the stable phase at high temperature and 
can be retained by rapid cooling; they also reported precipitation of Ti- 
rich phases at the grain boundaries, which is an indicator that the 
structure It was supersaturated with Ti [ 13 , 14 ].
Fig. 4 shows an image acquired in backscattered electrons (BSE) 
mode and the elemental mapping of the NbTi20W10 alloy. It can be 
observed that both Nb and W are homogeneously distributed in the 
dendritic grains, while Ti is more concentrated in the interdendritic 
region. A localized chemical analysis by Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spec -
troscopy (EDX), Fig. 5 , shows that the dendrites have the expected 
composition of the alloy, which proves that there was a total solubility, 
while the interdendritic region is rich in Ti, and has practically no W in 
solid solution. This could indicate that the first nuclei to form were rich 
in W, due to their high melting point, and from these the dendritic grains 
began to grow, so the liquid metal was left with a lack of W and an excess 
of Ti. The same behavior was observed in the NbTi20W10 alloy, the 
chemical composition line profile is shown in Fig. 6 . Table 4 shows the 
general chemical composition of both alloys, acquired in area by EDX.

It has been shown that the addition of 5 – 15 % (wt%) Nb to W im -
proves the oxidation resistance of W, a higher percentage of Nb provides 
better resistance [ 18 ]. It was also observed that the addition of 2 % Nb 
(at.%) improves the oxidation resistance at 800 
◦

addition of Mo and W reduce the interstitial solid-solubility of oxygen in 
Nb, with the effect of Mo being greater than that of W [ 19 , 29 ], which 
helps explain the better oxidation resistance results of the Mo alloy 
compared to that of W. On the contrary, oxygen solubility increases with 
the addition of Ti [ 29 ], but the greater affinity of this element with 
oxygen promotes the formation of Ti-based oxides protecting the Nb. Ti 
greatly reduce the diffusion coefficient of oxygen in Nb, while Mo and W 
have a negligible influence [ 30 ]. The synergy between these elements 
(Nb, Ti, W and Mo) is evident, the addition of alloying elements in solid 
solution distorts the crystal lattice, reducing oxygen solubility, diffu -
sivity and improving resistance to oxidation at high temperature.

4. Conclusions
• It is possible to process elemental metal powders of Nb, Ti, W and Mo 
by PTA to obtain Nb-Ti-W and Nb-Ti-Mo alloys. The process pa -
rameters resulted in samples with homogeneous chemical composi -
tion and low porosity, showing that their future applicability in 
additive manufacturing is promising.
• Nb-Ti-W and Nb-Ti-Mo alloys can be a suitable alternative with a 
better cost-benefit, compared to conventional Nb alloys.
• In the temperature range 500 – 700 
◦
C, Nb-20Ti-10W (at.%) and Nb- 
20Ti-10Mo (at.%) alloys have excellent oxidation resistance,
