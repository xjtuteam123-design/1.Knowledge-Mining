ABSTRACT
Tungsten heavy alloys are favoured for demanding components subjected to extreme operating conditions. In 
this study we investigate experimentally and via numerical predictions the effects of rotary swaging performed at 
selected temperatures on the material plastic flow, (micro)structure, and room and high temperature quasistatic 
and dynamic mechanical behaviour of the WNiCo powder-based pseudo-alloy. The results showed that the green 
sintered piece featured superior plasticity, but relatively low strength across the variety of testing conditions (e.g. 
room temperature yield strength of 800 MPa). Swaging at 900 
◦
C introduced dynamic recrystallization within the 
γ -phase NiCo matrix but did not remarkably affect the W agglomerates; the softened matrix and W agglomerates 
featuring accumulated strain provided the pieces swaged at 900 
◦
C with exceptional strength at room and 
elevated temperatures (room temperature yield strength of 1 650 MPa). On the other hand, swaging at 1 150 
◦
C 
introduced softening within the W agglomerates and secondary recrystallization of the NiCo matrix, by the effect 
of which this piece featured both, enhanced plasticity and strength. According to the numerical predictions, 
increasing the swaging temperature also facilitated the plastic flow and homogenized the imposed strain. 
However, the piece swaged at 1 150 
◦
C exhibited increased surface oxidation and thus deteriorated surface 
quality.
1. Introduction
Tungsten heavy alloys (THAs) feature favourable mechanical and 
physical properties, including high density, excellent thermal stability, 
extraordinary strength, sufficient ductility, and favourable corrosion 
resistance. Therefore, they are favoured for demanding components 
operating under extreme conditions, such as kinetic energy penetrators 
and radiation shields [1,2] . THA components to be used in the defence 
sector can be subjects to high-speed impact loading, and thus their dy -
namic mechanical behaviour and failure characteristics are of a high 
interest. Modern methods enabling the production of challenging ma -
terials (e.g. High Entropy Alloys [3] , Oxide Dispersion Strengthened 
alloys [4] , or composites [5] ) from original powders have been shown as 
highly advantageous to introduce superior properties of the final com -
ponents. THAs are typically produced by means of powder metallurgy, 
using methods such as mechanical alloying and liquid phase sintering 
[6,7] optimized to provide the final products with enhanced mechanical 
properties [8,9] . During the intense research and development of THAs, 
non-conventional procedures, such as spark plasma sintering [10 – 12] , 
microwave sintering [13,14] , and even additive manufacturing [15 – 19]
were investigated, too. However, green-sintered THAs exhibit poor 
mechanical (especially tensile) properties and can hardly be used under 
extreme conditions, such as at high strain rates. By this reason, ther -
momechanical post-processing under optimized conditions is usually 
favoured [20,21] .
Among the conventional and unconventional forming methods 
applied to enhance the mechanical and utility properties of THAs are, for 
example, methods based on cold rolling [22,23] , drawing [24,25] , 
extrusion [26,27] , rotary swaging [28 – 30] , and severe plastic defor -
mation (SPD), such as equal channel angular pressing (ECAP) [31,32]
and its modifications [33 – 37] , or high pressure torsion (HPT) [38 – 40] . 
The strength of THAs is primarily determined by the content of W, which 
typically varies between 90 and 97 wt%. The remaining volume, i.e. the 
γ -phase matrix, consists of alloying elements, the combination and 
volume fractions of which primarily influence the plastic properties. 
Among the most common THAs are the WNiCo [41,42] , WNiFe [43,44] , 
and WNiFeCo [45,46] systems.

However, the effects of selected preparation procedures on the 
enhancement of mechanical and physical properties of THAs are not 
equivalent for all the methods. Among the key factors influencing the 
final microstructures, properties, and lifetime of THA components are 
not only the intrinsic material properties (the W-W and W- γ interfacial 
properties, tendency to form precipitates, adiabatic shear bands, etc.), 
but also the applied thermomechanical processing parameters, i.e. 
selected technology, temperatures, and amount and homogeneity of the 
imposed strain [49,50] . Vice versa, the microstructure morphology, i.e. 
shapes and orientations of W grains (agglomerates), affect the defor -
mation behaviour of the particular THA. Specific texture orientations 
are preferred for demanding applications, such as in the defence, as 
favourably oriented grains provide increased resistance against forma -
tion of the mushroom-like effect, which deteriorates the penetration 
effect. Penetrators featuring partially twisted W fibres feature conve -
nient penetration properties (by up to 57 % increased penetration depth 
compared to penetrators without the twisted microstructures [51] ). The 
selected processing route is thus the dominant factor influencing the 
microstructures, and the final physical and mechanical properties of 
THA components [52] .
Another highly important factor influencing the mechanical prop -
erties is the selected processing temperature. Das et al. [53] used rotary 
swaging and documented that THAs are prone to exhibit dynamic strain

temperatures between 400

attributed to the presence of interstitial atoms [54] , especially C [55] . 
Nevertheless, detailed studies reporting the effects of processing tem -
perature and/or advanced thermomechanical processing on the me -
chanical properties of THAs are scarce. Generally, high forming 
temperatures are prone to support oxidation, introduce the development 
of microstructure instability, impart grain growth, influence the inter -
action of precipitation/recrystallization phenomena, and lead to 
degradation of plastic properties of a THA. On the other hand, low 
applied temperatures can result in technological issues during process -
ing, as W features a low number of available slip systems, and thus high 
flow stress and aggravated formability. Therefore, optimization of the 
processing temperatures and applied thermomechanical treatments, 
from the viewpoints of both the applied temperature and deformation 
ratio, is of a high importance.
The primary aim of this study was to characterize the effects of 
thermomechanical processing, performed with the use of rotary swag -
ing, on the quasistatic and high strain rate mechanical behaviour of a 
WNiCo pseudo-alloy in a wide range of temperatures. Rotary swaging 
[56,57] was selected as it is a versatile industrially applicable 
manufacturing technique. It is typically used to reduce the diameters of 
rods/tubes via a repeated action of rotating dies, although it can be 
applied to process various components of axisymmetric shapes. During 
swaging, small compressive strains are incrementally imparted to the 
work piece through hundreds to thousands of high speed impacts per 
minute, which helps to avoid large stress gradients. These advantages 
enable processing of metals and alloys that could hardly be deformed by 
conventional forming methods such as drawing, extrusion, or rolling. 
Thorough experimental examination involving thermomechanical sim -
ulations, Taylor anvil test, and detailed microscopy observations was 
herein performed to evaluate the effects of the selected processing routes 
and assess their suitability for production of THA bars with enhanced 
performance. The experimental investigation was supplemented with 
numerical prediction of the mechanical behaviour of the swaged pieces, 
including detailed characterization of the material plastic flow during 
the individual processing routes.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Surface quality
Figs. 3 a to 3 c show OM images depicting surface roughness of the 
green sintered and swaged WNiCo pieces, i.e. specimens A , B and C , 
respectively. The comparison of the Figures shows that specimen A 
featured surface of the lowest quality. In other words, the green sintered 
surface exhibited the highest roughness ( Fig. 3 a). The surface quality 
significantly improved after swaging at 900 
◦
C ( Fig. 3 b); specimen B 
exhibited 60 % improvement in roughness when compared to specimen 
A . Further increase in the swaging temperature to 1 150

deteriorated the surface quality and increased the roughness again, as

sintered surface of specimen A , to Fig. 4 b, depicting the hot swaged

following hypothesis arose: increasing the swaging temperature led to a 
more intense material plastic flow, which contributed to reduction/ 
elimination of surface defects originating from the sintering process. 
However, this hypothesis applied only partially, as increasing the tem -
perature above 900 
◦
C deteriorated the surface quality again. The pri -

also consists of the tangential and axial component. Nevertheless, the 
imposed strain is generally the highest along the work piece periphery, 
and then gradually decreases across its cross-section towards the axis.

3.3. Mechanical properties
The applied regimes of thermomechanical processing non-negligibly 
imparted differences in the mechanical behaviours of the samples. Their 
mechanical properties were at first evaluated via quasistatic testing by 
room temperature compression tests, then by examination of hot flow 
stress, and finally by dynamic TAT analysis performed at room 
temperature.
3.3.1. Room temperature compression tests
Fig. 6 shows that the green sintered piece ( specimen A ) exhibited 
relatively high room temperature plasticity when compared to the 
swaged pieces. On the other hand, both the swaged pieces exhibited 
substantially enhanced strength. The character of the curves acquired 
from the room temperature compression tests pointed to the occurrence 
of (sub)structure changes, as well as restoration processes. This hy -
pothesis was confirmed by the acquired values; the room temperature 
yield strength for specimen B reached to ~ 1 650 MPa. Specimen C and 
specimen A exhibited the yield strengths of 1 180 MPa and 800 MPa, 
respectively. Although specimen A exhibited the lowest observed yield 
strength, the room temperature deformation hardening was the most 
significant for the green sintered piece. On the other hand, specimen B 
and specimen C exhibited full consolidation and increase in dislocations 
density, i.e. development of substructure leading to (partial) 
deformation hardening, already during the rotary swaging.
3.3.2. Hot flow stress analysis
The acquired hot flow stress curves, depicted in Figs. 7 a and 7 b, 
reflect the combined effects of processing temperature and applied 
strain rate on the flow stress behaviours of the processed pieces at 
elevated temperatures. As can be seen, the deformation temperature had 
the major effect on the absolute flow stress values. During testing at 900 
◦
C, all the samples (especially the swaged ones) exhibited relatively high 
values of true flow stress. Nevertheless, increasing the testing temper -
ature to 1 150 
◦
C imparted significant decrease in the true flow stress (up 
to ~ 50 %), regardless the applied stain rate. Increasing the testing 
temperature also decreased the differences between the peak flow stress 
values of the analysed samples. In other words, increasing the testing 
temperature supported the development of dynamic softening (recov -
ery/recrystallization) primarily within the γ -phase matrix, which 
consequently decreased the peak flow stress values [68] .

attributed to the development of dynamic recovery [69] . In other words, 
the lattice defects, i.e. dislocations, originating from the sintering pro -

deformation-based testing. This effect was more evident for the higher

conditions, i.e. strain hardening accumulated during the particular 
swaging regime.

specimen B and specimen C exhibited substantial differences as regards 
the development of softening processes. Both the curves show that the 
activation of dynamic recrystallization (i.e. flow stress peak) was ach -

the higher testing temperature imparted steeper increase in the flow

On the other hand, at the lower testing temperature, specimen B 
exhibited a steady state like behaviour at a substantially lower imposed

testing at the higher temperature). This difference could most probably 
be attributed to the amount of energy stored within the WNiCo piece 
after swaging at 900 
◦

(accumulated energy) and testing (aggravated softening) temperatures 
introduced favourable conditions for the development of steady state on 
the flow stress curve. The higher testing temperature then supported the 
development of softening processes, by the effect of which the steady

In summary, the higher testing temperature supported partial 
development of softening processes (most probably static recovery). On 
the contrary, the lower testing temperature did not impart so high a 
decrease in the accumulated energy, which resulted in a more rapid 
establishment of the steady state. This fact confirms the hypothesis of 
the dominant occurrence of recovery [70] . Increasing the strain rate 
then generally suppressed these phenomena, as it tended to shift the 
establishment of steady state towards higher imposed strains. The 
selected strain rate influenced primarily the distribution of applied load 
between the tungsten agglomerates and matrix. Whereas for the NiCo 
phase the stress magnitude does not change significantly with increasing 
strain rate under quasi-static conditions, the change in flow stress for the 
W phase with changing strain rate is notable, i.e. the maximum flow 
stress of the THA becomes predominantly determined by the W 
component with increasing strain rate [71] .
3.3.3. Room temperature Taylor Anvil Test
Taylor Anvil Test (TAT) can advantageously be used to determine 
dynamic deformation behaviour of a material, as well as to provide basic 
data to create various constitutive models to be subsequently imple -
mented into numerical software to further perform complex simulations 
[72 – 74] . In our study, dynamic experiments were carried out at various 
strain rates to provide data on the dependences between flow stress and 
strain for all the prepared WNiCo specimens. Similar to the afore 
mentioned results, Figs. 8 a to 8 c depicting the TAT-deformed samples 
document that the selected processing route affected differently the 
dynamic behaviours of the processed pieces. The green sintered specimen 
A , featuring the highest plasticity, exhibited relatively consistent dy -
namic deformation behaviour. The impact end of the TAT-deformed 
specimen A exhibited only local occurrence of cracks. The 
TAT-deformed specimen A can thus be considered to exhibit the 
mushroom-type deformation mode [75] . On the other hand, certain 
inhomogeneity of material plastic flow was evident from the deformed 
non-circular cross-section of this specimen, see Fig. 8 a. The circular 
cross-sections of green sintered THA work pieces typically feature dis -
tortions due to gravity influencing the material during sintering 
[76 – 78] . Consequently, such distortions can impart differences in the 
plastic flow during deformation post-processing.
As for the effect of the rotary swaging regimes, Fig. 8 b shows that the 
TAT-deformed specimen B exhibited highly deformed impact end with 
frequent occurrence of cracks. On the other hand, this TAT-deformed 
specimen exhibited more homogeneous plastic flow than the TAT- 
deformed specimen A . The TAT-deformed specimen C then exhibited a 
quite homogeneous plastic flow and less pronounced cracks when 
compared to the TAT-deformed specimen B , see Fig. 7 c. Both the swaged 
specimens can thus be considered to exhibit tensile splitting after TAT 
[75] , however, both in different extents. The greatest differences were 
seen along the longitudinal shapes of the TAT-deformed specimens, 
specifically in their bulk volumes adjacent to the impact edges. The 
TAT-deformed specimen A and specimen C exhibited visible traces of 
plastic deformation in a direct vicinity of the impact end (visible 
bulging). However, the bulging was not observed for the TAT-deformed 
specimen B . This phenomenon could most probably be attributed to the 
intense deformation hardening introduced by the rotary swaging. The 
accumulated energy, together with relatively low swaging temperature 
of 900 
◦
C, aggravated the development of restoration processes within 
the TAT-deformed specimen B , as discussed also in Section 3.3.2 . In other 
words, swaging at 900 
◦
C supported deformation hardening at the 
expense of dynamic softening, by the effect of which the plasticity of 
specimen B decreased and thus the bulging effect was suppressed during 
the dynamic testing. The majority of the impact energy was therein 
consumed for the nucleation and development of numerous cracks, the 
occurrence of which was the most evident for the TAT-deformed spec -
imen B ( Fig. 8 b). [79]

typical for green sintered THAs, e.g. [81,82] . While the W phase 
exhibited the maximum texture intensity of ~ 4.2 x random, the 
maximum texture intensity of the γ -phase matrix was more than 8 x 
random.

grain growth, however, the majority of the grains underwent secondary 
recrystallization initiated by the mutual effect of accumulated energy 
and high temperature. This hypothesis is supported by the observed 
decreased intensity of the dominant texture orientation in the γ -phase 
matrix, and is also in agreement with the recently published findings 
[21] . The above discussed stress-strain behaviours of the individual 
specimens were non-negligibly related to the effects of the applied 
swaging routes on the interactions between the softer γ -phase matrix 
and harder W agglomerates.

microstructure and behaviour of a WNiCo powder-based pseudo-alloy at 
room and elevated temperatures. The most important results can be 
summarized as follows:
