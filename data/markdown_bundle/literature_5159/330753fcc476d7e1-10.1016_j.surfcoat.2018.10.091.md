Abstract: 
In this work, a composite coating prepared by laser cladding on a N i-17Mo-7Cr base d 
superalloy was thermal ly exposed at 1073 K for 240 h . A detailed electron microscopy 
examination was performed to characterize the microstructure of the aged coating. Results 
indicate that: the original Mo6Ni6C dendrites in the aged coating were coarsened while the 
coarsening layers were essentially the Mo -deficient and Cr -rich Mo6Ni6C, due to low 
diffusivity of Mo. Long-term thermal exposure promote d a great amount of nano -sized 
Mo6Ni6C and micro-sized Cr3C2 particles being precipitated in the interdendritic zones. In the 
aged coating, three types of Cr 3C2 were presented: discrete Cr3C2, Cr3C2 close to the graphite 
and Cr3C2 approaching to the Mo 6Ni6C. The transformation of Mo6Ni6C to Cr3C2 merely 
occurred in the newly formed Mo6Ni6C rather than in the original Mo6Ni6C dendrites, because 
deficiency of Mo and enrichment of Cr in the new Mo6Ni6C depressed their stability. Lastly, 
the mechanical behavior of the aged coating was characterized by Vickers hardness, 
nano-indentation and nano -scratch tests with the aim to unveil the effect of microstructural 
evolution on mechanical properties. Our findings provide basic information to understand the 
aged-induced microstructural modification and its effect on mechanical properties for a 
laser-induced coating prepared on the Ni-17Mo-7Cr based superalloy. The results obtained 
can not only apply to the investigated Ni-based superalloys, but also to other Hastelloy series 
superalloys. 
 
Keywords: Nickel based superalloys; Laser cladding; Aging; Microstructure; Mechanical 
property.

1. Introduction 
Nickel-based superalloys are extremely significant industrial alloys, which have been widely 
used in aircrafts, power generation turbine blades, vanes and discs, as well as a host of other 
nuclear power and chemical industry plant components characterized by challenging 
environmental requirement s [1] . The Ni-Mo-Cr based Hastelloy series all oys belong to the 
typical members of Ni-based alloys. Many types of Hastelloy series alloys, such as Hastelloy 
B [2], Hastelloy C276 [3], Hastelloy N [4] and Hastelloy X [5], were reported . The series 
alloys exhibit satisfactory high -temperature mechanical properties and excellent corrosion 
resistance. To broaden their application in harsh environments, improving the wear resistance 
is necessary. Surface modification is normally used to tune the surface properties, such as 
wear and corrosion resistance, friction coefficient, hardness, and hydrophobic characteristics 
[6-8]. The Ni-based Hastelloy series alloys after surface modification will find application in 
certain components like agitator blades, nozzles and slurry pump van es used in nuclear and 
chemical industries where both wear and corrosion resistance are simultaneously required 
[9-11]. Many reported technologies, i.e., thermal spraying (TS), physical vapor deposition 
(PVD), chemical vapor deposition (CVD), laser irradiation (LI) and laser cladding (LC), can 
be used to modify the surface of materials [12]. Among them, the LC method has been 
attracted great attention over past decades because of its unique features, such as low dilution, 
good metallurgical bonding and low distortion of substrate [13-16]. M. Hashim et al. [11] 
found that an improvement of hardness was achieved in the laser-treated zone coated on 
Hastelloy C276 alloy, yielding an enhancement in wear resistance up to four times compared 
to the base material. Some particles, such as SiC [17], TiC [18], WC [19], NbC [20], can also 
be incorporated in the coating through laser surface modification to tune its tribological 
property. The SiC particles , as reported in [9, 1 7, 21-22], are extensively used as the 
incorporated particles because they have excellent high-temperature mechanical properties 
and strong wear and corrosion resistance in severe chemical conditions. M. Ahmad et al. [9] 
once imported the SiC particles into the surface layer of Hastelloy C276 alloy, producing a 
significant surface hardening effect . To the best of our knowledge, less information is 
available for the laser surface modification of Hastelloy N alloy (a typical kind of Hastelloy 
series alloy) . Y.M. He et al. [23] introduced SiC particles as the incorporated phases to

Unlike the traditional SiC incorporation that needed to maintain the morphology of S iC 
particles in the coating after the LC, we adjusted the processing parameters to make the SiC 
particles decompose completely. A metal matrix composite coating including a great amount 
of hard reinforcements was then produced through the aid of triggering effect of Si. Although

1) lots of molybdenum carbides and chromium carbides were observed in the coating, but 
their nature has not yet been explicitly ascertained, 2) thermal exposure can stabilize the 
microstructure and mitigate the residual stresses in the laser -induced coating [24-27], which 
should be performed for the coating. For the composite coating produced on the Ni-17Mo-7Cr 
based alloy , however, effect of thermal exposure on its microstructure and mechanical 
behavior has not yet been reported. In fact, the solidification of m elt pool proceeds via the 
growth of dendrites during conventional casting, leading to rejection of alloy ing elements 
towards the interdendritic zones and then bring the inhomogeneity in chemical composition. 
The laser surface modification can aggravate the i nhomogeneity because laser-material 
interaction is super-fast (cooling rate: 103~1010 K/s) [28]. Such an inherently high cooling rate 
will detain extension of solubility of solute atoms , making the coating metastable. Besides, 
owing to large temperature differences experienced by the surface relative to the bulk of 
substrate during laser -material interaction , as well as the mismatch in materials properties 
between the coating and substrate [29-31], the residual stresses developed in the coating 
should also be considered. Thermal exposure can accommodate the residual stresses and 
stabilize the coating. 
 
Based on the above analysis , the composite coating prepared on the Ni -17Mo-7Cr based 
superalloy was thermally exposed at 1073 K for 240 h. The microstructural evolution and 
phase instability within the coating were studied through the state-of-art transmission electron 
microscopy and high -resolution transmission electron microscopy analysis. In addition, the 
mechanical behaviors of the aged coating were evaluated through Vickers hardness, 
nano-indentation and nano-scratch tests. In this work, primarily two aspects were emphasized: 
1) to identify the phases within the coating, and 2) to clarify the effect of age -induced 
microstructural modification on mechanical properties of coating . The work performed will

2. Materials and Methods 
The substrate used was Ni-17Mo-7Cr base d superalloy with the following composition of 
Ni-17.29Mo-6.96Cr-3.96Fe-0.63Mn-0.47Si-0.05C (wt.%), similar to that of Hastelloy N 
superalloy. The microstructure of as-received alloy consisted of Ni-based solid solutions and 
dispersed particles (molybdenum carbides [ 32]), as shown in Fig. 1 a. The SiC particles with 
an average diameter of 10 μm (Fig. 1b) were adopted as the coating materials. Before the LC, 
a layer of SiC particles with an average thickness of 1 mm were put on the substrate. A 3 kW 
transverse-flow CO2 laser system (Leo HJ-3000) was introduced to perform the laser surface 
modification. During the LC, the laser power, scanning speed, overlap ratio was set to 2 kW, 
150 mm/min, and 50%, respectively. The procedure of laser cladding was described in detail 
in Ref. [23]. After that, the samples obtained were thermally exposed at 1073 K for 240 h in a 
furnace with argon protection. The temperature of 1073 K was chosen because the alloy was 
routinely used in the temperature range of 873-1073 K , while its maximum service 
temperature was 1073 K [33]. The long duration of 240 h used during thermal exposure could 
guarantee new phases being precipitated sufficiently. After thermal exposure, the cross-section 
of the non-aged and aged samples was prepared by a standard metallographic polishing 
procedure. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Quanta 200F ) coupled with energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Bruker Nano XFlash Detector 5010 ) was used to perform the 
microstructural characterization and composition analysis for the coating . Two modes, i.e., 
secondary electron (SE) and back -scattered electron (BSE), were used during the SEM 
analysis. The coating was also investigated by transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI 
Tecnai G2 F30 ). The phase morphology was captured using bright -field and high-angle 
annular dark-field (HAADF) modes, while phase identification was performed by 
selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) and high-resolution transmission electron 
microscope (HRTEM) analysis. TEM samples with a thickness of 200 nm were prepared by 
focused ion beam (FIB, FEI Helios NanoLab 600i ). To evaluate the mechanical properties,

Before analyzing the effect of thermal exposure on the microstructure of the laser -induced 
coating, formation process of the coating should be clarified during laser cladding. It is seen 
from Fig. 4 that no SiC particles can be observed in the coating, suggesting that the SiC 
particles coated on the surface of Ni -17Mo-7Cr based alloy were decomposed completely. 
Note that the decomposition temperature of the SiC was approximately 2700 °C, while the 
melting temperature of Ni -17Mo-7Cr based alloy was only 1400 °C [32]. Considering this, it 
can be inferred that the surface layer of Ni -17Mo-7Cr based alloy in contact with SiC 
particles may melt prior to the decomposition of SiC . A detailed examination of the aged

substrate. 
 
The driving force of microstructural coarsening was generally the reduction of free energy 
achievable by minimizing the system's interfacial energy. The coarsening of precipitates has 
been reported in Ni-based superalloys [35-36]. According to the composition results shown in 
Table 1, the original Mo6Ni6C-1 could be expressed as (Ni0.41Mo0.32Si0.14Cr0.12Fe0.01)6C, while 
the coarsening layers around them were actually the (Ni0.44Mo0.20Cr0.20Si0.15Fe0.01)6C. It can be 
seen from Fig. 4b that some precipitates free zones (PFZs) can be clearly seen around the 
original Mo6Ni6C-1. The PFZs formation should be ascribed to the coarsening of Mo 6Ni6C-1 
dendrites, leading to the depletion of forming elements for nano-sized precipitates. The fact 
gives a hint that the diffusion of solutes m ight determine the coarsening behavior of 
Mo6Ni6C-1 in the interdendritic zones . A. Baldan [ 37] has also indicated that 
diffusion-controlled Lifshitz-Slyozov-Wagner (LSW) rather than interface -controlled 
trans-interface diffusion controlled (TIDC) coarsening mode predominated in the Ni -based 
superalloys. Based on this, it can be inferred that the coarsening of Mo 6Ni6C-1 dendrites was

Thermal exposure, as mentioned, produced a substantial amount of nano-sized particles in the 
HAZs and interdendritic zones . It is observed from the Fig. 4b that most of the particles 
indicated by the white box have the size of ≤1000 nm in length and ≤240 nm in width. Their

exceeded solubility limit far beyond that expected from the equilibrium phase diagram [41]. 
Before thermal exposure, approximately 3 wt.% Si (phase 2 in Table 1) were detected in the 
interdendritic zones. Enrichment of Si promoted and stabilized the precipitation of M 6C (M 
denotes metallic elements) during thermal exposure [42-43]. A great amount of nano-sized 
Mo6Ni6C-3 particles were therefore produced. Actually, the interdendritic zone was a complex 
multicomponent alloying system, in which many elements, such as Ni, Mo, Cr, Fe, Si and C, 
were distributed. Considering their concentration, the solvent was Ni, while Mo, Cr, Si, Fe 
and C acted as the solutes. Precipitation of the Mo6Ni6C should be involved with the diffusion

of Ni, Mo, Cr, Si and C atoms. Among them, the element C with its small radius diffuse d

Cr-rich M23C6 have been found to appear in the Ni -based alloys when the aging temperature 
was 973 K [48-49]. The thermodynamic information indicates that the standard Gibbs free 
energy for the formation of the Cr3C2, Cr7C3 and Cr23C6 were all negative at 800-1300 K [50]. 
In addition, free energy for the formation of Cr23C6 was the most negative, suggest ing that

this work, a sufficient amount of carbon (approximately 4 wt.% by EDS qualitative analysis) 
releasing from the decomposition of SiC was detained in the coating. Considering this , 
prevailing C favored the precipitation of the C-richest Cr3C2 in the aged coating. 
 
The Cr3C2-2 close to the graphite was formed because the graphite accommodated abundant 
carbon for their precipitation . T o produce Cr 3C2-3 adjoining to the Mo6Ni6C, there were 
usually two ways, nucleation or transformation. The nucleation process involve d the 
nucleation, growth and coarsening of the Cr3C2 around the Mo6Ni6C. The transformation, 
nevertheless, meant that the Mo6Ni6C first decomposed, and then transformed to the Cr 3C2. 
Considering the fact that part of the Cr3C2 grew along distinct crystal plane of the Mo 6Ni6C 
(Fig. 11e), the transformation rather than nucleation mechanism would govern the process. In 
fact, carbide transformation was quite common in the Fe, Ni or Co -based alloys [53]. 
However, the thermodynamic predicted that M6C kept stable below 1573 K [54]. Considering 
this, the investigated Mo6Ni6C would not degrade. However, carbides could tie up certain 
elements in the alloy that facilitate d their instability during aging or in service. The 
transformation of M 6C to M 23C6 has been reported [ 55]. As mentioned in section 3.1, the 
Mo6Ni6C-1, Mo6Ni6C-2 and Mo6Ni6C-3 appeared in the aged coating . The Mo6Ni6C-1 were 
the original phases while the latter two Mo6Ni6C belonged to the precipitated phases during 
thermal exposure. The composition results showed the Mo6Ni6C in Fig. 11a and 12a were 
actually the precipitated Mo6Ni6C rather than the original Mo6Ni6C-1 because they ha d a

lower content of Mo and higher content of Cr, see phase 3, 9 and 12 in Table 1. Based on this, 
it can be inferred that the transformation of Mo6Ni6C to Cr 3C2 merely happened in the 
precipitated Mo6Ni6C during thermal exposure. Deficiency of Mo and enrichment of Cr in the 
precipitated Mo6Ni6C leaded to the ir instability. During the process, a significant amount of 
atoms were released when the precipitated Mo6Ni6C were decomposed. The released Cr 
atoms react ed with C, producing the Cr 3C2 at the periphery of Mo6Ni6C. Similar to most 
precipitates, the Cr3C2 nuclei formed experienced the nucleation, growth and coarsening. As 
the concentration of Cr in the Mo6Ni6C was far lower than that in Cr3C2, the diffusion of Cr 
from the interdendritic zones was then the rate-limiting process governing the coarsening rate 
of Cr3C2. It should be mentioned that most of Cr was depleted during the precipitation of 
Mo6Ni6C, as demonstrated by the appearance of PFZs around the original Mo6Ni6C. One can 
then speculate that the growth of Cr3C2 approaching to the Mo6Ni6C was rather slow. The 
width of Cr 3C2 close to the Mo 6Ni6C was generally less than 1 μm (Fig. 8b), even the aged 
time arrived at 240 h. 
 
3.3 Mechanical properties of the coating 
Fig. 13 presents the results of Vickers micro-hardness distribution across the cross-section of 
coating before and after thermal exposure . The coating containing hard reinforcements, such 
as Mo6Ni6C and Cr3C2, always exhibits a higher hardness than that in the substrate, no matter 
whether the coating was thermally exposed . In addition, the micro-hardness shows a large 
fluctuation in the non-aged c oating. The coating, as mentioned, basically consists of the 
Mo6Ni6C and Ni (s.s) . When the testing position contacted the Mo6Ni6C hard phases, the 
hardness with a peak value appeared. After thermal exposure , a relatively homogenized 
micro-hardness can be clearly shown in the coating, because of the precipitation of Mo6Ni6C 
and Cr3C2 hard phases in the interdendritic zones, as has been demonstrated in Fig. 6, 7, and 
8.

the nano-scale elastic modulus and hardness for materials. The aged coating, as mentioned, 
was basically made up of the Mo6Ni6C equiaxed dendrites and interdendritic zones, in which 
a great amount of nano -sized Mo 6Ni6C and micros -sized Cr 3C2 particles, and few blocky

Thermal exposure imported a large number of hard particles (Mo6Ni6C and Cr 3C2) in the 
coating, being responsible for the enhanced elastic modulus and hardness in the aged 
interdendritic zones. 
 
Since a great number of new precipitated particles were imported in the aged coating, it is 
necessary to evaluate its local wear behavior. The nano-scratch tests were performed for the 
non-aged and aged coating , as shown in Fig. 15. For comparison, the original alloy without 
laser cladding was also studied. For the original alloy, the penetration depth increases 
constantly with an increase of scratch load. The penetration depth for the non-aged and aged 
coating, however, fluctuates frequently as the scratch load is increased, as is seen from Fig. 
15c. We took the aged coating as an example to explore the reason. The aged coating, as 
mentioned, contained four phases, i.e., Ni (s.s), Mo6Ni6C, Cr3C2 and graphite. Among them, 
the graphite usually acting as lubricants in resisting wear was the softest. Compared to the Ni 
(s.s), the Mo6Ni6C and Cr3C2 were significantly harder. We have reported that the Mo 6Ni6C 
had an average nano-scale hardness of 22.3 GPa [23], far higher than that (5.3 GPa) of Ni (s.s). 
The nano-indentation measurement for the Cr 3C2 was not performed in the work . However, 
this kind of carbide was reported to have a nano-scale hardness of 23.0 GPa [56]. Three cases 
may take place during the scratch tests. The first was that the scratch tip approached the 
blocky graphite. In that case the penetration depth (pointed out by arrow A in Fig. 15c) went 
deeper under the same load, compared to the original alloy. The second was that the scratch

showed a large fluctuation, see arrow B. The last one was that the scratch tip encountered the 
Ni (s.s). A small fluctuation indicated by the arrow C was presented, because the 
aged-induced nano-sized Mo6Ni6C or micro -sized Cr 3C2 hard phases in the Ni (s.s) had a 
strong ability to resist the scratch. That is, frequent fluctuation of the penetration depth should 
be ascribed to prevailing distribution of hard phases in the aged coating . Compared to the 
aged coating, some large fluctuations of penetration depth were also observed for the 
non-aged coating, but the appearance of small fluctuations w as less frequent. It is 
demonstrated from Fig. 4 that the non-aged coating also contained the hard original Mo6Ni6C 
dendrites but had less amount of nano-sized Mo6Ni6C, micro-sized Cr3C2 in the Ni (s.s). T he 
nano-indentation results have already indicated that the aged interdendritic zones had a larger 
nano-scale elastic modulus and hardness than that in the non -aged ones. The fluctuation of 
penetration depth meant that the penetration depth was recovered during the tests, suggesting 
the coating had an excellent scratch resistance . In fact , the investigated coating could be 
considered as a metal matrix composite material, in which Ni was the matrix while the 
Mo6Ni6C and Cr 3C2 being the reinforcem ents. In general, the volume fraction of 
reinforcements should be controlled below 50% in a laser -induced coating, to avoid cracks, 
pores or inhomogeneous distribution of reinforcements [57-58]. For the non-aged coating 
under investigation , the volume fraction of reinforcements was approximately 45.3% [ 23]. 
Thermal exposure increased the total amount of reinforcements to 55 vol.%, due to the 
coarsening of original Mo6Ni6C coupled with precipitation of nano-sized Mo6Ni6C and 
micro-sized Cr3C2 particles in the interdendritic zones. An increased amount of hard phases 
enhanced the plastic resistance in the aged coating.

on mechanical behavior was evaluated by Vickers hardness, na no-indentation and 
nano-scratch tests. The work performed will pave the way for understanding the 
microstructural evolution in a laser-induced coating during high-temperature thermal 
exposure, and building the microstructural modification with mechanical behaviors. The 
results obtained can not only apply to the Ni -17Mo-7Cr based Hastelloy N superalloy, but

4. Conclusions 
The following conclusions were drawn. 
1) The equiaxed dendrites in the coating were actually the Mo6Ni6C. The original Mo6Ni6C 
dendrites were coarsened during thermal exposure , while the coarsening layers were 
essentially the Mo-deficient and Cr-rich Mo6Ni6C. 
2) Thermal exposure produced a great amount of nano-sized Mo6Ni6C particles in the 
interdendritic zones. Compared to the original Mo 6Ni6C dendrites, these particles were also 
leaner in Mo and richer in Cr, similar to the coarsening layers around the original Mo6Ni6C 
dendrites. 
3) Due to carbon super-saturation, lots of Cr3C2 carbides were produced during thermal 
exposure. T hree types of Cr3C2 were exhibited in the aged coating: discrete Cr3C2, Cr 3C2 
close to the graphite and Cr 3C2 approaching to the Mo 6Ni6C. Part of Mo6Ni6C would be 
transformed to the Cr3C2 along specific crystal direction and plane . This transformation only 
took place in the newly precipitated Mo6Ni6C rather than in the original Mo6Ni6C dendrites. 
Deficiency of Mo and enrichment of Cr in the new Mo6Ni6C brought their instability. 
4) The results of Vickers hardness tests show that thermal exposure produced a relatively 
homogenized micro-hardness in the aged coating. The nano -scale elastic modulus and 
hardness of the aged interdendritic zones were measured to be 309.8 GPa and 12.9 GPa , 
which were higher than that ( elastic modulus: 246.8 GPa, hardness: 5.3 GPa) of the non-aged 
ones. The nano-scratch test show ed that the aged coating exhibited an excellent local scratch 
resistance, due to aging-induced increased amount of hard reinforcements (Mo6Ni6C and 
Cr3C2) in the coating.
