Abstract
The high-temperature strength of a Fe–25%Al–2%Nb alloy in both a solutionised state and a precipitated state has been de-
termined for temperatures up to 900 /C176C and compared with that of a solution hardened Fe 3Al alloy. Some comparisons of these
materials with previously reported Fe 3Al-based materials are also made. The Fe–25%Al–2%Nb material is capable of retaining
good strength to temperatures above 800 /C176C, but rapid coarsening of the Laves precipitate particles at higher temperatures leads to
strength loss at such temperatures. The presence of stable dispersed particles at the intermediate temperatures means that good
strength can be retained to very low strain rates.

1. Introduction
Much of the interest in iron aluminides over the last
about 20 years has grown from their possible appli-
cation as lower density replacements for stainless steels
at intermediate temperatures (500–600 /C176C) and in
various industrial oxidising or corrosive environments
[1–3]. Problems of limited ductility, related to hydro-
gen embrittlement by reaction with water (vapour)
[2,4], of diﬃcult fabrication requiring careful casting
and forming processing [5,6], and of poor high-
temperature strength [7–11], have, however, restricted
such applications.
In view of the outstanding corrosion, carburisation
and oxidation resistance of such iron aluminides [10,12–
15], attention has turned recently, in several countries, to
a reconsideration of these materials for very high-tem-
perature applications, above 800–1000 /C176C, where their
special chemical resistances make them obvious mate-
rials of choice, provided other weaknesses such as poor
high temperature or creep strength can be overcome.
The present report is a contribution to such research.
A very large body of research has been carried out
looking at the high-temperature plasticity [1,9] and creep
[7,8,11,16,17] of iron aluminides. However, for the most
part such studies have addressed topics such as the
strength anomaly at temperatures of 400–600 /C176C [18–
23], and the creep behaviour at similar temperatures
[11,16,17]. While many such studies have provided
valuable information, for example of the useful solution
hardening obtained by Mo and of the useful strength-
ening by particles such as niobium carbides [11] and
yttrium oxides [24], there is little information of strength
and creep behaviour at much higher temperatures.
The present study examines the behaviour of a Fe
3Al
alloy containing 2%Nb (atomic percent is given
throughout). This niobium content was chosen since it
was hoped that this quantity could be dissolved in solid
solution at high temperature [25] for subsequent pre-
cipitation. Such Nb solute would be expected to produce
stable Fe
2Nb Laves phase at temperatures below about
1000 /C176C [25], and it may be expected that such particles
would coarsen more slowly, due to the slow Nb diﬀusion
in the intermetallic matrix, than precipitate phases
such as the carbides studied previously [11,26]. A similar

2. Experimental details
The major part of the present study concerns the
structure and strength of a Fe–25%Al–2%Nb alloy
prepared by induction melting and drop casting to cy-
lindrical bar shape. Chemical analysis conﬁrmed the
nominal composition to within about /C6 0.2%. Some
comparative studies are also reported here on a cast
Fe
3Al-based alloy of composition Fe–28%Al–5%Cr–
1%Si, but also containing 1%Nb and 0.5%B to produce
borides for grain size reﬁnement. The Fe–Al–Nb alloy
(hereafter designated 25-2) had a slightly elongated
grain of size about 500 lm, while the Fe–Al–Cr–Si alloy
(hereafter designated 28CS) had a grain size of about
250 lm with micron-sized particles of niobium boride.
The 25-2 alloy was prepared in two heat treated
states, namely as-water quenched after a 1 h at 1300 /C176C
anneal (25-2–I) and after a subsequent 1 h at 900 /C176C
anneal (25-2-II). The ﬁrst state was expected to solu-
tionise all the Nb, while the second was expected to give
stable C14, Fe
2Nb precipitates [25,31].
Mechanical testing was carried out in compression on
cylindrical samples of diameter 3 mm and height 6 mm
to a strain of 2%. Tests were carried out in air, generally
at a nominal strain rate of 2 /C2 10
/C0 4/s, at temperatures
between ambient and 900 /C176C. In addition tests were
carried out at a variety of strain rates between 10 /C0 5/s
and 10 /C0 3/s, at 750 /C176C, to determine the strain rate sen-
sitivity at this temperature. Samples were removed
quickly from the furnace at the end of straining and
allowed to cool in air in an attempt to retain the de-
formation structure.
Thin foil samples were prepared after straining by
slicing disks about 0.2-mm thick and twin-jet electrop-
olishing using a 25% nitric acid in methanol mixture at
)30 /C176C and about 20 V. Thin foils were examined in
JEOL 2000FX and 2010FX microscopes.
3. Results
3.1. Initial microstructure
The as-cast 25-2 alloy showed a uniform distribution
of coarse particles, in the form of slightly elongated
blocky particles and needle particles, see Fig. 1(a),
spread throughout the grains and lining the grain
boundaries. Annealing at 1300 /C176C (state 25-2-I) led to
the dissolution of most, but not all these particles,
Fig. 1(b), where one of the typically micron-sized re-
maining particles, showing faulting along a direction
perpendicular to its long axis, is seen. The retention of
this small volume fraction of Laves phase is somewhat
surprising since the solubility of Nb in the Fe–Al matrix
is expected to be near 3% [25,31]. The most likely ex-
planation is that the original Laves phase dendrites
present in the cast material had not had suﬃcient time at
high temperature for complete dissolution. There was no
other evidence of precipitation in the water-quenched
material, according to TEM studies, both by selected
area diﬀraction and by dark ﬁeld imaging with various
diﬀracted beams. Quantitative analysis determines
about 0.5% volume fraction of the undissolved, coarse
particles. Chemical analysis by EDX in the SEM gave a
composition of 58%Fe–15%Al–27%Nb for the particles
and 73%Fe–26%Al–1%Nb for the matrix. These values
are close to those reported earlier on a similar alloy [25],
and correspond to the Fe
2Nb Laves phase, with Al
substitution, and to the Fe 3Al matrix. The composition
of the binary Fe 2Nb Laves phase in equilibrium with
Fe–Al at high temperature is close to Fe 3Nb, indicating
some substitution of Nb by Fe, and the shape of the
Laves phase homogeneity range in the ternary phase
diagram suggests the steady substitution of Fe by Al,
i.e., the Laves phase in equilibrium with Fe–Al evolves
from Fe
3Nb towards Al 3Nb [31]. The measured com-
position of the Laves phase is consistent with this, with
Al substituting mostly (all) for Fe in the binary Fe 2Nb
compound. These phase analyses were conﬁrmed by
electron diﬀraction, illustrated by the [1 /C222 1 0] diﬀraction
pattern of the hexagonal C14 phase in Fig. 1(c), with the
DO
3 structure of the matrix also demonstrated by the
APBs visible in Fig. 1(b). Following annealing at 900 /C176C
(state 25-2-II), elongated precipitates, also of C14 Laves
phase, were seen, Fig. 1(d), of size about 0.2 lmb y
0.5 lm. The needles were elongated along a h110 i di-
rection of the matrix, corresponding to [0 0 0 1] of the
Laves phase. Fig. 1(e) shows a diﬀraction pattern at
[0 0 1] matrix with additional spots at 1 1 0 (matrix)
corresponding to 1 0 1 0 of the Laves phase. A complete
analysis of the precipitation process and the formation
of the Laves phase particles will be published elsewhere
[32]. Quantitative analysis showed a number density of
about 1 /C2 10

to about 2.5% by volume. For both materials, strong

3.2. Mechanical test results
Compression tests were carried out to about 2% strain
in each case. There was little sign of any yield point
discontinuity, with the elastic stage smoothly evolving
into the plastic stage. At low temperatures the stress–
strain plot was approximately linear, and at tempera-
tures above about 600 /C176C was parabolic with gradually
less hardening, until at 900 /C176C the stress remained con-
stant after 1–2% strain. No strain softening was ob-
served for any temperature or strain rate, so there was
no evidence of large scale recrystallization or structural
coarsening. The 25-2-I material (but not the 25-2-II)
showed load serrations after about 1% strain, increasing
in strength with strain, when tested at 250 /C176C (not at
room temperature nor 500 /C176C), and this is believed to be
evidence of dynamic strain ageing – the Portevin LeC-
hatelier eﬀect – possibly due to the large amount of Nb
solute. Similar serrations, indicating unstable obstacles
to initial easy deformation, have occasionally been ob-
served in Fe–Al alloys tested at high temperatures, either
examined as initial yield or strain-age yield points, or
examined as continuous dynamic strain-age yield points
[33–35]. The dependencies of yield drop values on strain,
test temperature, and material state or composition have
been used to argue that twinning mechanisms, carbon or
other solute, or intrinsic partial/super-dislocation pin-
ning structures may be responsible for the observed ﬂow
instabilities. While no further studies have been carried
out to conﬁrm this suggestion – that Nb solute is playing
an important role in producing the serrations – the
presence of serrations at intermediate temperatures in
the Nb-solutionised 25-2-I material, but not in any other
of a range of similar Fe
3Al alloys, leads to the present
suggestion of the important role of the Nb solute in
creating these serrations.

4. Discussion
4.1. Analysis of mechanical strength
The ﬂow stress behaviour of the present materials can
be characterised, with reference to Fig. 2, by the high
ﬂow stress of both the 25-2 materials (over that of 28CS)
up to 500 /C176C, and then the higher stress of the 28CS for
the temperature range 600–900 /C176C. At the same time,
comparing 25-2-I and 25-2-II, it is seen that 25-2-I is
stronger at room temperature, 25-2-II becomes stronger
over the range 250–500 /C176C, and then 25-2-I becomes
stronger again at higher temperatures. These relative
strengths can be analysed by considering the relative
importances of solution and particle hardening, with
28CS being hardened only by solution eﬀects, 25-2-I
hardened by solute and ﬁne precipitates, and 25-2-II
hardened mostly by ﬁne Laves particles.

materials with values for binary Fe–30%Al single crystal
[36], and with oxide dispersion strengthened Fe–40%Al
[37,38] (MA40). The ﬂow stress values for the single
crystal should be multiplied by the Taylor factor, a value

strengthening contribution of the 5%Cr and 1%Si solute,
since the few boride particles in this material were too
large and separated to have any noticeable eﬀect. The
TEM study shows that the eﬀect of solute is to inhibit
dislocation recovery to such an extent that dislocation
rearrangement into subgrains does not occur, as seen in
Fig. 7, in contrast with Figs. 5(c) and 6.
The high strength of the 25-2-I at room temperature
is due to the strong solution hardening of almost all the
2%Nb in solution. This statement is based on the ab-
sence of any evidence of Nb precipitation, as Laves
phase or other, during quenching, while the strength-
ening contribution due to the few, large, undissolved
Laves particles is negligible (calculated as 4 MPa using
suitable material parameters [38]). Since there is no
structure change (no precipitation) evident up to 500 /C176C,
the strength reduction for 25-2-I up to this temperature
must be due to thermally activated easier dislocation
glide. At such temperatures the dislocations become
dissociated as single 1 =4h111 i dislocations, see Fig. 4,
and clearly trailing APB faults is no more diﬃcult than
for the simpler alloys. At temperatures of 600 /C176C and
above, Nb precipitates out as ﬁrstly L2
1 phase and later
as stable Laves phase. The measured ﬂow stress of this
material is now determined by the considerable loss of
solution hardening and gain of particle hardening. As-
suming precipitation of Nb at 750 /C176C leading to 2.5–5%
volume of particles of the size observed in Fig. 5(b) (i.e.
considering that not all the 2%Nb in the alloy was ini-
tially solutionised and that some may remain in solution
at 750 /C176C), the Orowan strengthening contribution ex-
pected is about 80–110 MPa.
The room temperature strength of the 25-2-II cannot
be explained by particle strengthening by the 2.5% vol-
ume of 0.2–0.5 lm Laves particles, which are expected
to lead to an Orowan strengthening of about 30 MPa,
and presumably Nb solute remaining from the prepa-
ratory 900 /C176C anneal is responsible for considerable
solution hardening. Considering that most, but not all,
the 2%Nb is initially dissolved by the 1300 /C176C anneal
(a value of about 1.85% is deduced from the remnant
about 0.5% by volume of Laves phase particles), and
that the Nb solubility at 900 /C176C is about 0.5% [31], the
solution hardening expected in the 25-2-II material is of
the order of 160–210 MPa relative to the Fe–30%Al
binary, as compared with 300–400 MPa for the 25-2-I
material (Fig. 2). Taking into account also of the minor
strengthening of the Laves phase particles in the 25-2-II
material, this material would be expected to be some
100–150 MPa softer than the as-quenched 25-2-I. While
not in exact agreement with the room temperature
strength data measured (Fig. 2), the above explanation
is reasonably convincing in conﬁrming that solution
hardening remains an important contributor to the
strengthening in the 25-2-II material. The high-temper-
ature behaviour of this material is determined by the
loss of this smaller amount of Nb solute, and the Laves
particles present, which contribute about 30 MPa to
strength. For this reason, this 25-2-II material remains
softer than the 25-2-I once such solute loss has occurred.
Again, as for the case of 28CS, the presence of solute or
many ﬁne particles acts not only to strengthen but also
to inhibit dislocation recovery processes leading to the
formation of sub-grain structures, compare Figs. 5(a)–
(c) and 6.
This comparison of strength variation and dislocation
structures is further conﬁrmed by examination of the
strain rate sensitivity data given in Table 1. At 750 /C176C,
where these tests were performed, alloy 28CS showed a
stress exponent n of 3.5, which is a typical value for de-
formation controlled by viscous glide of dislocations [40].
At the same time, since there are no strengthening parti-

4.2. Development of high-temperature strength in Fe
3Al
alloys
The previous discussion has compared the high-tem-
perature strength of the two 25-2 materials with the
28CS alloy and examined the strengthening contribu-
tions of solution hardening and of particle hardening. In
a general context, it is of interest to examine other
published data on the strength of Fe
3Al at very high
temperatures to understand in which directions future
eﬀort should be concentrated for the development of
high-temperature strength alloys.
A ﬁrst factor to consider is the role of Al content on
high-temperature behaviour, as examined in Fig. 8. Here
and later, throughout, we shall consider data obtained
with strain rates of the order of 10
/C0 4/s, and where nec-
essary data have been adjusted based on the strain rate
dependency reported by the authors concerned. Com-
parisons made in Fig. 8 and subsequently assume that
other factors such as the (relatively large) grain size or
impurity content are not very diﬀerent between materi-
als compared, or that the eﬀect of such parameters is not
signiﬁcant when small diﬀerences do exist [7]. Fig. 8
examines the inﬂuence of Al content in some binary
Fe
3Al and FeAl alloys [17,41]. Data from both sources
are relevant for coarse-grain cast materials at strain
rates of 10
/C0 4/s, and are self-consistent both with respect
to the ﬂow stresses shown and with respect to the low
stress dependences reported. Fig. 8 shows that the Al
content plays a signiﬁcant role in determining the high-
temperature ﬂow stress, and a signiﬁcant strength im-
provement is obtained on increasing the Al content to
about 30%, but that thereafter there is little strength
gain at higher Al contents. These alloys show low stress
exponents under these test conditions, conﬁrming that
viscous dislocation glide, or perhaps climb, is the im-
portant controlling mechanism. These results are also
consistent with other work reported by Whittenberger
[7], which show little dependence of strain rate param-
eters on Al content for alloys containing more that
40%Al.
The importance of solute additions is indicated in
Fig. 9 for several binary and ternary solid solution
alloys, with the present data on alloy 28CS included
for comparison. Data shown are again taken from
several sources [17,27,41–44] to show general trends.
The importance of solution hardening has also been
emphasized elsewhere [11]. Fig. 9 shows the inﬂuence
of Cr, Mo, Nb, Ti, and Cr–Si solute additions on
high-temperature strength of alloys containing about
30%Al. Cr addition is seen to have a rather small
inﬂuence, showing sometimes a small increase in
strength [17] or else a small decrease in strength [42].
On the other hand, Mo is shown to produce a sig-
niﬁcant strength increase [11,43]. Accordingly, it
would appear that the signiﬁcant strength increase of
the present 28CS alloy is largely due to the 1%Si
solute addition. Another signiﬁcant point is the low
stress exponent reported for these solid solution alloys
[17,41,42], as reported for the present 28CS alloy,
again conﬁrming viscous glide/climb as the important
mechanism.

5. Summary and conclusions
The present study has examined the microstructure
and high-temperature ﬂow stress of a solution-hardened
Fe
3Al(Cr,Si) alloy and a Fe 3Al(Nb) alloy heat treated to
both a solutionised state and to a precipitated state. The
Fe3Al(Nb) material in its precipitated state contains a
large number of 1/4 lm sized Laves phase particles. The
solutionised Fe 3Al(Nb) material shows precipitation of
ﬁne L2 1 phase particles at temperatures near 750 /C176C,
which transform to coarser C14 Laves particles at higher
temperatures. Based on the structural changes at high
temperature it appears that an upper temperature limit
for retaining ﬁne particles in the Fe
3Al(Nb) alloy is
about 750–800 /C176C.
Flow stresses determined by compression testing
show high strength over the temperature range of room
temperature to 500 /C176C, followed by a rapid drop in
strength over the 500–700 /C176C range. Strength at tem-
peratures of 750–850 /C176C and above are still highly sen-
sitive to the alloy and microstructure considered.
The relative importance of high-temperature
strengthening by solute eﬀects and by second-phase
particle eﬀects has been considered for the materials
examined here, and also in relation to previously re-
ported studies on similar Fe
3Al based alloys. Strength-
ening by second-phase particles is seen to be important
for endowing the material with a high-stress sensitivity
of the strain rate, which is clearly of value in retaining
high-material strength under low strain rate, creep-like
conditions. Accordingly the solution-strengthened
Fe
3Al(Cr,Si) alloy, which shows high strength under
fast straining conditions, becomes weaker than the
