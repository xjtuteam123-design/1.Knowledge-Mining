BRIEF COMMUNICATION
Solid-State Joining of Dissimilar Ni-Based
Superalloys via Field-Assisted Sintering Technology
for Turbine Applications
CHARIS I. LIN, NAMIKO YAMAMOTO, DEREK S. KING, and JOGENDER SINGH
Friction welding or electron/laser beam welding of nickel superalloys compromises the
microstructure with localized melting and heat-aﬀected zones (HAZ). In this work, Field-As-
sisted Sintering Technology (FAST) is studied as an alternative method for solid-state diﬀusion
bonding of superalloys CM247LC and Inconel 718. Bonding microstructures and room-tem-
perature tensile strengths are evaluated. Preliminary results suggest that FAST bonding leads to
an interface with a compositional gradient without producing a HAZ and without losing tensile
strength.

THE thrust-to-weight ratio in gas turbine engines is a
critical factor in both commercial and military sectors.
This factor can be increased by developing turbine blade
and disk alloys of higher strength and/or thermal
stability, but also by optimizing the bond between the
blades and disk in the turbine blade-disk assembly to
decrease the overall weight. High-pressure turbine disks
are generally made of polycrystalline nickel (Ni)-based
superalloy for high strength and toughness, while
turbine blades are made of single crystal Ni-based
superalloy for high creep resistance. [1] Because conven-
tional welding leads to elemental segregation and
precipitation of unwanted intermetallic phases, the
blades and disks must be joined either mechanically or
via solid-state joining. Solid-state joining is preferred, as
it can lead to weight savings of up to ~30 pct compared
to a mechanical ﬁr-tree joint. [2]
Direct joining of dissimilar Ni-based superalloys has
been achieved through linear friction welding, resulting
in predictable and consistent formation of strong
interfaces, but with heat-aﬀected zones. Dissimilar
Ni-based superalloys joined using linear friction welding
include Inconel 718 and Inconel 713LC, [3] LSHR and
Mar-M247,[4] and Inconel 718 and Mar-M247. [5] By
increasing processing temperatures and durations,
material interfaces free of oxides and carbide particles
were achieved. [3,6] It was reported that linear fric-
tion-welded LSHR and Mar-M247 had bonding
strength equal to or greater than that of the weaker
Mar-M247 material. [6] However, the linear friction
welding process formed a heat-aﬀected zone (HAZ)
and dissolved all c¢ precipitation near the interface. [3,7]
Localized melting due to the friction weld also leads to
signiﬁcant recrystallization on both sides of the material
interface, resulting in a ﬁne-grained microstructure
within the recrystallization areas. [3,5,6] Preheating of
the material before welding improved its weldability and
led to a thicker layer of recrystallized c grains and c¢
precipitates, but plastic deformation of the material
beyond the ﬁne-grained layer may still lead to decreased
performance.[8] Residual stresses from the friction weld-
ing process were also investigated, with higher residual
stresses observed in materials with larger c¢ volume
fractions, and post-weld heat treatments were required
to relieve residual hoop stresses below 400 MPa. [9] The
main drawback of linear friction welding is the forma-
tion of a HAZ associated with residual stresses that
contributes to a reduction in mechanical properties.
In this study, ﬁeld-assisted sintering technology
(FAST) is studied as an alternative method for
solid-state joining of Ni-based superalloys. Unlike
conventional diﬀusion bonding, heating is directly
applied during FAST solid-state joining via electric
current ﬂow through the material, leading to rapid Joule
heating. Conventional diﬀusion bonding of the same
Ni-superalloy material on either side of the bonding
interface has been previously explored, whether without
an interlayer, [10–12] with a powder Ni-superalloy inter-
layer,[13] with a Ni foil interlayer of 2 to 10 lm
thickness,[10,12,14] or with a 3 lm electroplated Ni
layer.[11] The addition of an interlayer decreased

higher strengths. Diﬀusion bonding of dissimilar Ni
superalloys without an interlayer has also been explored
in the literature, [15,16] resulting in a diﬀusion bonding
zone with a gradient in elemental composition and
precipitate morphology. However, studies of diﬀusion
bonding of two solid Ni-based superalloy components
under an electric ﬁeld, whether with or without an
interlayer, are limited, while the kinetics of diﬀusion are
expected to be similar to that of diﬀusion bonding.
Previously, the authors have demonstrated FAST join-
ing of powder and solid Ni-based superalloy compo-
nents. Ni-based superalloy CM247LC powder was
simultaneously sintered and joined to a solid Inconel
718 component, resulting in a diﬀusion bonding zone
with no heat-aﬀected zone or localized melting. [17]
Similar results are expected with solid-state joining of
two solids, but such study in the literature is currently
limited to Inconel 718 joined to stainless steel 316L [18]
and a brief study of Inconel 718 joined to Inconel 718,
with limited microstructure analysis. [19] The eﬀect of
FAST solid-state joining on interface microstructure
and bonding strengths of dissimilar Ni-based superal-
loys remains to be studied.
In this work, the solid-state joining of the polycrys-
talline Ni-based superalloys CM247LC and Inconel 718
using FAST was studied, with the goal of gaining
knowledge about the diﬀusion mechanisms at play
during FAST solid-state joining of polycrystalline Ni
superalloys with diﬀering elemental compositions (see
Table I). Nb is included in Inconel 718 for grain
boundary strengthening and for formation of the c¢¢
phase, but is not included in CM247LC. CM247LC, on
the other hand, includes a signiﬁcantly higher wt pct of
Al to form the c¢ phase. The diﬀerences in alloying
content lead to a large disparity between the two
materials’ mechanical properties; for instance, a higher
hardness is observed in CM247LC (553 HV after
solution treatment and aging of conventionally cast
material [20]) than Inconel 718 (335 HV after annealing
and aging [21]).
CM247LC rods of 15 mm diameter and 40 mm length
were cut from a sintered 100 9 100 9 25 mm 3 plate
using wire electrical discharge machining (EDM), with a
surface ﬁnish of less than 40 lm surface roughness. The
CM247LC plate was sintered from CM247LC powder
(105-149 lm particle size) using FAST with 50 /C176 C/min
heating rate, 45 MPa axial pressure, 1275 /C176 C sintering
temperature, and 30-minute holding time. Forged and
hot-rolled Inconel 718 bar was purchased (Rolled
Alloys) and machined to rods of 15 mm diameter and
40 mm length. The CM247LC rods were joined to the
Inconel 718 rods within a graphite die, using a FAST
unit with a 25 metric ton loading capability (FCT
Systeme GmbH) (see Figure 1(a)). Volumetric heating
was applied through temperature-controlled current
ﬂow through the graphite die and the Ni-superalloy
material. During the joining process, a 50 /C176 C/min
heating rate, 45 MPa axial pressure, 1225 /C176 C joining
temperature, and a 15-minute holding time were
applied, as shown in Figure 1(b).
The microstructure and mechanical properties of the
joined rods were evaluated to determine the eﬀect of
FAST processing. The microstructure of the joined rods
was evaluated using optical microscopy, scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM), and electron dispersive spec-
troscopy (EDS). As shown in Figure 2(a), a diﬀusion
bonding zone was formed between the CM247LC and
Inconel 718 materials instead of a sharp interface line.
The Kalling’s 2 etchant etched preferentially on the
CM247LC side of the joined interface, revealing an
equiaxed grain structure with large grains in the order of
60-70 lm in size. No voids were observed between the
two materials, suggesting complete diﬀusion bonding.
When inspected using SEM (see Figure 2(b)) and EDS
(see Figure 3), precipitation formation and a gradual
elemental composition transition were observed across
the interface; Al wt pct gradually decreased and Fe
wt pct gradually increased from the CM247LC side to
the Inconel 718 side. Such gradual transition can be
associated with the c¢ phase of CM247LC, Ni 3Al. As
noted above, the Kalling’s 2 etchant preferentially etches
the c¢ phase, exposing the c¢ precipitates on the
CM247LC side, then the large c phase patches in the
midsection of the diﬀusion bonding zone, and ﬁnally the
more continuous c phase in the Inconel 718 side (see
Figure 2(b)). Note that the etchant does not reveal the
grain boundaries, c¢¢ phase, or carbides that are expected
to be present within the Inconel 718 side.
Microstructure variation can be observed not just
across the interface, but also along the length of the
interface. The wave-like interface line and precipitate
content variation along the line, as observed in
Figure 2(a), can be indirectly attributed to the surface
roughness of the rods and the associated c¢ phase
content variation along the interface length. The
machined rod surfaces are not perfectly ﬂat, and thus,
in the initial stages of FAST bonding, the contact
surface area and stress distribution are expected to be

stresses, diﬀusion (of Al, for example) is enhanced,
resulting in local diﬀusion bonding zones of greater
thickness and/or a greater volume fraction of c¢

The interface microstructure between the two dissim-
ilar Ni-based superalloy parts formed by the FAST
solid-state diﬀusion process is similar to that by con-
ventional diﬀusion bonding; a heat-aﬀected zone was
not formed, unlike the interface microstructure formed
by friction welding. Unlike linear friction welding, large
plastic deformations were not seen near the interface in
the current study, and a region of small grains due to
recrystallization after melting was not observed on
either side of the material interface, due to the process-
ing below the melting temperature. Instead, a
~20-lm-long diﬀusion bonding zone was observed at
the interface of the two materials, with a gradient of
precipitation and elemental composition. A similar
diﬀusion bonding zone has been previously observed
after solid-state diﬀusion bonding of the Ni-based
superalloys DD3 (single crystal) with GH4159 (poly-
crystalline) without the application of axial electric
current, where Liu et al. also observed a ~20 lm
diﬀusion bonding zone after bonding at 1150 /C176 C for 1
hour with a bonding pressure of 30 MPa, although the
shear strength of the bond was lower than that of the
parent materials. [16] Khalikov et al. also observed
elemental diﬀusion and no heat-aﬀected zone during
the diﬀusion bonding of the Ni superalloys VKNA-25
(single crystal) with EP975 (polycrystalline), though a
sharper interface was seen. Thus, the diﬀusion seen in
ﬁeld-assisted solid-state joining may be similar to that of
some conventional diﬀusion bonding processes, depen-
dent on the materials to be joined, but with shorter
processing times (15-minute holding time in the current
study). The shorter processing times enabled in the
FAST process can be attributed to either the enhanced
diﬀusion from the application of electric current or the
more rapid and homogenous temperature rise from the
resulting Joule heating.
The interfacial gradient of elemental composition and
precipitation observed in this work, or in the previous
conventional diﬀusion bonding work, is attributed to
the compositional diﬀerences between the two Ni-based
superalloy parts that were bonded. In contrast, when
two of the same Ni-based superalloy (Inconel 718) were
bonded (970 /C176 C for 1 hour, 32 MPa) in a study by Zhang
et al. ,[24] instead of a diﬀusion bonding zone with
elemental and precipitation gradient, a straight, sharp
material interface line, interrupted periodically by
shared grains, was observed. Similar interface formation
was observed during the diﬀusion bonding of the same
parts (Alloy 600, Alloy 617, and Haynes 230). [25,26]
Thus, the formation of a diﬀusion bonding zone
correlates with a gradient of elemental composition,
and the chemical composition of the materials to be
joined is expected to impact the thickness of the
diﬀusion bonding zone.
The joining strengths and elastic moduli of the joined
specimens were determined using room-temperature
tensile testing and analysis of stress-strain curves,
following the ASTM E8 standard for joining strengths
(round specimen 2, gage length 45 mm) and the ASTM
E111 standard for elastic modulus. The tensile proper-
ties of the joined specimens were compared with those of
the parent materials: CM247LC and Inconel 718 rods
that underwent the same FAST processing as the joined
rods, with a 50 /C176 C/min heating rate, 45 MPa axial
pressure, 1225 /C176 C joining temperature, and 15-minute
holding time. Three joined specimens for each conﬁgu-
ration were tested, and tensile properties are listed in

The FAST-sintered and FAST-processed parent
CM247LC materials have comparable UTS to that of
directionally solidiﬁed and heat-treated CM247LC
(1260 /C176 C/2h + 1079 /C176 C/4h + 871 /C176 C/20h) and exhibit
over ~20 pct greater UTS than conventionally cast and
heat-treated CM247LC. [27] The high tensile strength of
the FAST-processed material is likely a result of a
combination of 1) small grain sizes due to the shorter
processing times and 2) decreased amounts of porosity
and inclusions with the FAST process compared to
conventional casting.
The tensile properties of the joined specimens are
comparable to that of FAST-processed Inconel 718, and
fracture occurred within the weaker Inconel 718 mate-
rial, suggesting that diﬀusion across the diﬀusion
bonding zone led to a bonding strength at least as high
as the parent material strength. As seen in Table II, the
FAST heat-treated CM247LC material has a higher
tensile strength and lower ductility than those of Inconel
718. The yield strength of the joined specimens was
marginally higher than that of the weaker Inconel 718
material, as expected; because plastic deformation
occurred mostly within the Inconel 718 material, which
is half the length of the FAST heat-treated base
material, the elongation of the joined specimens for
the same stress will be smaller than that of the Inconel
718 base specimens, shifting the 0.2 pct oﬀset yield
strength to a higher value. For the same reason, the
measured elastic modulus of the joined CM247LC and
Inconel 718 rods was marginally higher than that of the
weaker Inconel 718 material. The FAST-joined parts’
mechanical behavior can be attributed to the strong
diﬀusion bonding zone formed between the two dissim-
ilar parts. If two parts of the same material were joined
via FAST, the joined interface is hypothesized to be
weaker and with a sharper interface line. The chemical
composition of materials to be joined via FAST must be
carefully considered to produce the desired bonding
interface microstructure.
A similar high tensile strength has been observed
using the linear friction welding process. As shown by
Mahaﬀey et al. , after processing parameter optimiza-
tion, room-temperature tensile testing near the center of
the interface in linear friction-welded LSHR and
Mar-M247 resulted in fracture within the Mar-M247
material, with strain localized within the Mar-M247
material.[6] The high bonding strength in both the FAST
and friction welding processes suggests that complete
bonding across the interface area is possible for both
processes. However, with the linear friction welding
process, a heat-aﬀected zone with high residual stresses
and a layer of dynamically recrystallized grains were
observed at the material interface, which are expected to
negatively impact high-temperature creep and fatigue
properties. The high strength suggests the possibility of
FAST to complement or fully replace conventional
post-processing heat treatments with a one-step process
inside of the FAST system. With FAST bonding, grain
recrystallization at the interface was avoided. More
research is needed in the FAST heat treatment of
diﬀerent metals.

This study demonstrated that CM247LC and Inconel
718 can be joined via ﬁeld-assisted solid-state joining,
and their bonding behaviors are as described below:
/C15FAST joining resulted in the formation of a diffu-
sion bonding zone with a gradient of elemental
composition between the two materials, which is
similar to the interface formed by conventional
diffusion bonding.
/C15The c¢ phase volume fraction decreased from the
CM247LC to the Inconel 718 side of the interface,
due to the compositional gradient. The interphase
precipitate volume fraction also varied along the
length of the material interface, due to local differ-
ences in diffusion times during the bonding process.
/C15Unlike friction welding, the FAST bonding did not
exhibit a layer of recrystallization at the bonding
zone or extensive plastic deformation away from the
interface.
/C15Tensile failure occurred away from the interface, and
the resulting tensile strength was at least as high as
the weaker parent material.
The high tensile strength suggests that FAST is a
viable alternative for solid-state joining of dissimilar
Ni-based superalloys. However, the FAST joining
process can lead to decreased strength in the parent
materials due to grain growth and dissolution of the c¢
phase. This weakening due to FAST processing needs to
be addressed, possibly through in situ post-processing
heat treatments within the FAST system; such in situ
precipitation treatment has previously been demon-
strated by Mondet et al. with a magnesium alloy. [28]
Future research includes studying the eﬀect of the FAST
process on high-temperature properties, the eﬀect of
post-processing heat treatments on FAST-joined spec-
imens, and the use of FAST to replace post-processing
heat treatments in Ni alloys. More research is also
needed regarding the eﬀects of the electric current on
diﬀusion between Ni-based superalloys compared to
conventional diﬀusion bonding, such as possible elec-
tromigration eﬀects.
