1. Introduction 
Metallic materials with high strength and excellent toughness are 
highly demanded for cryogenic applications, such as space exploration, 
liquefied gas storage, fusion, high-energy physics and superconductivity 
[ 1 , 2 ]. The face-centered cubic (FCC) high-entropy alloys (HEAs) and 
medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) have received considerable attentions 
due to their enhanced mechanical properties at cryogenic temperatures 
[ 3 – 12 ]. For example, it was reported that an equiatomic CoCrFeNiMn 
HEA presented exceptional damage tolerance with a tensile strength 
above 1 GPa and a fracture toughness exceeding 200 MPa m
1/2 
at 77 K 
[ 4 ], comparable to the best cryogenic steels like some austenitic stain -
less steels and high-Ni steels. However, a major drawback of these 
single-phase alloys is their low yield strength, usually being 150 – 300 
MPa at room temperature, which limits their applications as structural 
materials [ 3 , 13 , 14 ]. Classical strengthening approaches, such as solid 
solution strengthening [ 15 , 16 ], grain refinement [ 17 , 18 ], precipitation 
strengthening [ 19 , 20 ] and cold working [ 21 ], have been introduced to 
improve the room temperature yield strength of these FCC structured 
HEAs and MEAs. 
The addition of interstitial atoms (e.g., carbon and boron) [ 14 – 16 , 
22 – 24 ] has proved to be one of the effective strengthening methods. 
Wang et al. [ 14 ] have showed that the room temperature strength and 
ductility of an FCC structured FeNiMnAlCr HEA could be simultaneously 
improved by carbon alloying. We have also developed a heavy carbon 
alloyed (Fe

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructures 
The XRD patterns in Fig. 1 a show that all the HEAs exhibit a single 
FCC phase. Figs. 1 b and c presents typical backscattered electron (BSE) 
images of the 3.3C and 0.4Ti HEAs, respectively. The average grain size 
of the 3.3C HEA is approximately 65 ± 6 μ m. In addition, a few M
23

consistent with our previous reports [ 24 , 35 ]. The addition of 0.4 wt% Ti 
has significant influence on the microstructure. The grain size of the 
0.4Ti HEA is refined to about 18 ± 3 μ m, and some micron-sized TiC 
precipitates form and distribute in the FCC matrix in a strip-like 
arrangement ( Fig. 1 c). Moreover, numerous square nano-sized pre -
cipitates are also observed in higher magnification BSE image (the inset 
of Fig. 1 c). The average size and volume fraction of the nano-sized 
precipitates are about 67 ± 3 nm and 1.8%, respectively. The crystal 
structure of the nano-sized precipitates is further analyzed by TEM. 
Fig. 1 d shows a bright field (BF) TEM image of the precipitates in the 
0.4Ti HEA. The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) 
patterns in the inset of Fig. 1 d confirm that the precipitates are TiC. After 
cold rolling and annealing treatment, a large number of dislocations and 
some nanotwins appear in the 0.4Ti-45CR HEA ( Figs. 1 e and f). 
3.2. Tensile properties at 293 K and 77 K 
Fig. 2 a shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the three 
HEAs at 293 K and 77 K, respectively. The results indicate that the yield 
strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) are both improved by 
Ti-doping. Compared to the 3.3C HEA, the YS and UTS of the 0.4Ti HEA 
at 293 K increase by 23% and 17%, from 447 MPa and 888 MPa to 551 
MPa and 1038 MPa, respectively. When decreasing the temperature to 
77 K, the YS and UTS are further increased by 12% and 20%, from 881 
MPa and 1234 MPa to 987 MPa and 1477 MPa, respectively. Notably, Ti- 
doping not only enhances the strengths of the 3.3C HEA at both room 
and cryogenic temperatures, but also recovers its deteriorated cryogenic 
ductility. The 3.3C HEA has a maximum elongation of 72.5% among the 
present HEAs at 293 K, but it shows a degraded elongation of 21.4% at 
77 K. In contrast, the elongation of the 0.4 Ti HEA only slightly drops 
from 54.9% to 52.1% correspondingly. Consequently, the present 0.4Ti 
HEA exhibits an excellent combination of cryogenic YS (~1 GPa) and 
ductility (~52%), which surpasses most traditional HEAs [ 28 , 29 , 38 – 41 ] 
and is comparable to the newly developed ultrafine-grained CoCr -

3.3. Deformation microstructures of the 3.3C and 0.4Ti HEAs 
The microstructures of the 3.3C and 0.4Ti HEAs are examined using 
EBSD after tensile fracture at both 293 K and 77 K, as shown in Fig. 3 . 
Only FCC phase can be indexed in all deformation microstructures, 
indicating that no strain induced phase transformation [ 25 ] occurs 
during tensile deformation at both temperatures. Figs. 3 a and c clearly 
show that the two HEAs exhibit similar deformation microstructures at 
293 K: a large number of high angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) and 
deformation twins. The fraction of twins in the 3.3C and 0.4Ti HEAs are 
approximately 55.3% and 41.6%, respectively, which demonstrates that 
their excellent room temperature ductility might be attributed to the 
TWIP effect [ 25 , 26 ]. However, their deformation microstructures are 
quite different at 77 K. As shown in Fig. 3 b, apart from some annealing 
twins, deformation twins rarely appear in the 3.3C HEA. Obviously, the 
suppressed twinning activities at low temperature should be responsible 
for the strong ductility deterioration in this alloy. In contrast, numerous 
deformation twins with the fraction of about 42.6% again formed in the 
0.4Ti HEA at 77 K ( Fig. 3 d), similar to the case at 293 K. Consequently, 
the 0.4Ti HEA exhibits the same level of excellent ductility at both 
cryogenic and room temperatures.

4.2. Effect of carbon and Ti addition on the deformation mechanism 
It is well documented that low temperatures can promote twinning 
by reducing stacking fault energy (SFE) in TWIP steels and some HEAs 
[ 4 , 46 ]. For example, the equiatomic CoCrFeNiMn HEA shows excep -
tional damage tolerance at cryogenic temperature, and the improvement 
of its mechanical properties is attributed to a transition from planar-slip 
dislocation activity at room temperature to mechanical nano-twinning 
with decreasing temperature, which results in steady strain hardening 
[ 4 ]. Similarly, the C-free Fe
40
Mn
40
Co
10
Cr
10 
(0C) HEA was also found to 
enhance twinnability due to the reduced SFE with decreasing temper -
ature [ 28 , 47 ]. At 77 K, deformation induced FCC-HCP martensitic 
transformation was also observed as well as deformation twinning, 
which provides strong evidence that decreasing temperature decreases 
the SFE and the thermodynamical stability of the FCC phase [ 28 , 47 ]. 
However, the present 3.3C HEA displays an opposite trend with the 
temperature decreasing from 293 K to 77 K, where deformation twining 
is dominant at 293 K but absent at 77 K. Deformation induced FCC-HCP 
martensitic transformation is not observed at 77 K too. The above ob -
servations cannot be simply understood from the temperature effect on 
the SFE (as in the C-free alloys), which indicates that the interstitial 
carbon must activate a different twinning (/martensitic transformation) 
mechanism. Usually, carbon is considered as a strong austenite stabilizer 
and increases SFE [ 48 ], thus will suppress deformation twinning. 
However, many researches have also shown that the carbon addition 
notably promotes deformation twinning in high-Mn TWIP steels [ 49 , 
50 ], HEAs and MEAs [ 24 , 51 , 52 ]. Recently, Xie et al. [ 53 ] proposed an 
novel twinning mechanism to understand the controversial effects of 
carbon on deformation twinning. Their result shows that carbon diffu -
sivity along partial dislocation core is highly dependent on the charac -
teristics of the partial, which can result in different pinning effects on the 
leading and trailing partial dislocations through the formation of atomic 
pairs or short-range orders. Consequently, when a trailing partial ex -
periences stronger pinning effect than the leading one in a mixed type of 
dislocation (e.g., 60
◦
dislocation), the resulted unbalanced pinning ef -
fect will assist stacking fault and twinning formation under external 
stress, which explains the effect of carbon addition on improving twin -
ning activities in many alloys despite that carbon strongly increases the 
SFE and the stability of the FCC phase. Clearly, the above mechanism is 
temperature dependent, i.e., optimized when carbon diffusion at dislo -
cation core is notably higher than diffusion in bulk. At cryogenic tem -
perature such as 77 K, either diffusion in the dislocation core or bulk is 
difficult and the above unbalanced-pinning assisted twinning mecha -
nism is not expected to operate. Consequently, only dislocation slips are 
present in the heavy C-alloyed alloy due to its high SFE at 77 K, which 
cannot provide sustained high work-hardening ability and leads to

5. Conclusions 
In summary, the influence of carbon and minor Ti doping to the 
mechanical properties of the (Fe

HEA at room

improved at both temperatures without showing plasticity degradation 
at cryogenic temperature as typically observed in the undoped one. The 
enhanced yield strength is due to the improved strengthening effect by 
the grain boundary and precipitation, while the superior plasticity at 
cryogenic temperature is due to the recovery of deformation twinning, 
which is related with the unbalanced-pinning effect assisted twinning 
mechanism in C-contained alloy. It is important to emphasize that the 
present 0.4 Ti HEAs also have a larger size of thickness of 20 mm in sheet 
and there will be enough room to optimize the strength and ductility by 
cold working as shown in Fig. 2 b. Moreover, this 0.4Ti HEA has lower 
cost than other equiatomic CoCrFeNiMn, FeNiCoCr HEAs and FeCoNi, 
VCoNi MEAs, etc, because it contains larger amount of cheap Fe and Mn 
and smaller amount of noble Co and Ti. Therefore, it will have greater 
potential for cryogenic structural materials application.
