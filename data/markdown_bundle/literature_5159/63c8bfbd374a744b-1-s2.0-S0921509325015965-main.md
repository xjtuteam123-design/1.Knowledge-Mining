1. Introduction
Currently, new opportunities are raised by leveraging the out-of- 
equilibrium solidification conditions of laser beam powder bed fusion 
(LB-PBF) to develop processable aluminium alloys specifically dedicated 
to high-temperature applications, see e.g. Refs. [ 1 – 10 ]. Various alloy 
design strategies have been employed in the literature to develop new 
compositions with good mechanical properties at elevated temperatures 
[ 11 ]. Michi et al. have defined three different alloy families depending 
on the strategy employed [ 11 ]. The first approach involves promoting 
the formation of a high-volume fraction of fine, thermally stable 
intermetallics [ 1 – 4 ]. Developing near-eutectic compositions has been 
identified as a promising strategy for producing microstructures rein -
forced by a fine network of intermetallic particles. The second family 
uses ceramic particles for reinforcement instead of intermetallics 
[ 12 – 14 ]. While the idea is analogous, from an industrial point of view, 
using ceramics as reinforcing particles presents processing challenges 
such as the incorporation of the reinforcing particles into the powder 
feedstock. The third family aims to promote solute trapping of elements 
that can produce a high-volume fraction of strengthening precipitates 
during post-fabrication heat treatment. Zr and Sc have been the two 
most widely used solutes to develop alloys belonging to this family.

2.2. Microstructural characterization
Samples for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were 
prepared by standard polishing procedures: mechanical grinding with 
SiC abrasive papers up to grade P1200, followed by successive polishing 
with a 9 μ m, 3 μ m and 1 μ m diamond suspensions. A final polish was 
performed with a 0.03 μ m colloidal silica solution for 15 min. SEM 
observations were made in the plane containing the building direction 
using a Zeiss Gemini SEM 500 FEG equipped with an electron back 
scattered diffraction (EBSD) camera. The fraction and sizes of the 
intermetallic particles were estimated based on SEM back scattered 
electron (BSE) micrographs. The images were segmented using the 
ImageJ software. A threshold was then applied to measure the area 
fraction of intermetallics and their size was estimated using the Feret 
diameter. The variations measured on at least three different SEM-BSE 
micrographs taken in the SR condition were considered to estimate 
the error bars. Low magnification EBSD maps (440 × 340 μ m) were ac -
quired using a 200 nm step size. The EBSD maps were cleaned using a 
neighbour orientation correlation followed by a grain dilation. These 
options are available in the EDAX OIM Analysis software. From the 
EBSD data, columnar grains were segmented by considering only the 
grains with an aspect ratio < 0.7 and a size > 2 μ m. The average columnar 
grain size was then estimated using the average equivalent diameter 
(diameter of a sphere whose projected area is the same as that of the 
average columnar grain) and the surface fraction of columnar grains 
determined by the fraction of points in the partition. Error bars were 
estimated based on the variations measured on three different EBSD 
maps collected in the SR condition. High magnification EBSD maps (55 ×
42 μ m) were acquired with a step size of 50 nm and then cleaned using 
the same procedure. Equiaxed grains were segmented by considering 
only the grains with an aspect ratio > 0.9 and a size < 1 μ m. The average 
equiaxed grain size was then estimated using the average equivalent 
diameter.

The specimens were systematically field evaporated in voltage- 
pulsed mode, at a temperature of 40 K with a pulsing rate of 125 kHz, 
a pulse fraction of 20 %, and a detection rate of 1 ion detection per 1000 
pulses on average. The data were analysed and reconstructed using the 
AP Suite 6.1 software and the composition of the solid solution was 
determined using the distribution of isolated atoms for the determina -
tion of the matrix composition (DIAM method) described in Ref. [ 28 ]. 
The DIAM method is particularly relevant to measure the solid solution 
composition in presence of nanoscale precipitates. It is based on the 
calculation of the relative amount of isolated solute atoms (i.e. not 
surrounded by similar atoms) as a function of a distance d in the APT 
dataset. First, the bulk composition of the specimen is extracted from the 
AP Suite 6.1 software. Then, the first nearest neighbour distribution is 
calculated and extracted for each solute. This distribution is then 
compared to a random distribution of solute atoms in order to determine 
the matrix composition. The interested reader is referred to Ref. [ 28 ] to 
get a more detailed description of the DIAM method. The main advan -
tage of this method is that it does not depend on any user parameter 
which contrasts with other methods relying on cluster selection algo -
rithms. Besides, it works equally well regardless of the size, morphology 
and distribution of the precipitates, since it only recognizes them as local 
solute-rich regions. The average solid solution composition was deter -
mined from the results of at least 3 specimens from the two different 
locations: fine-grained zones (FZ) and coarse-grained zone (CZ) within 
the melt pool for each condition. Note that the solid solution composi -
tion can be locally affected by the presence of grain boundaries or large 
intermetallic particles. In this case, a region of interest is defined in the 
APT sample away from such microstructural features. A schematic 
illustration of a typical region of interest within the fine-grained zone 
(FZ) and the coarse-grained zone (CZ) from which APT samples were 
extracted using FIB is presented in Fig. S2 provided in Supplementary 
Materials. Particular attention has been paid the extraction of APT 
samples from the centre of a FZ and from a columnar grain located in the 
CZ, respectively.

4. Discussion
4.1. Thermal stability of the microstructure after 4h at 300 
◦
C (SR)
The results presented in Section 3.1 show that no significant micro -
structural evolution occurs, even at the atomic scale. The only variation 
detected was a slight decrease in hardness that was attributed to the 
relaxation of residual stresses and static recovery. The thermal stability 
of the microstructure, exemplified by the solid solution composition that 
remains nearly unchanged after 4h at 300 
◦
C ( Fig. 4 a – d) and the absence 
of nanoscale Mn or Zr-rich precipitates ( Fig. 3 a – b), is related to the 
nature of the supersaturated solutes. Mn and Zr are slow-diffusers while 
solutes having a higher diffusivity in Al such as Ni and Cu are mostly 
consumed by the intermetallic particles, see Fig. S3 . This result is worth 
highlighting because a heat treatment at 300 
◦
C for 4h in traditional Al 
alloys from the 2xxx, 6xxx, or 7xxx series would, on the contrary, 
severely alter the solid solution composition and precipitation state. The 
comparison with the Al-Mn-Mg-Sc-Zr alloy from the literature is inter -
esting because such a heat treatment (4h at 300 
◦
C) would nearly 
correspond to the peak-aged. Indeed, the peak-aged was reached after 
5 – 6h at 300 
◦
C [ 9 , 24 – 26 ]. This was correlated to the profuse precipi -
tation of nanoscale L1
2
-Al
3
Sc with a limited Zr content entering in their 
composition, and the concentration in Mn in the solid solution that was 
reported to be equal to that measured in the as-built microstructure 
[ 25 ]. Such considerations show that microstructural evolutions 
observed upon ageing may thus be drastically different in the 
Al-Mn-Mg-Sc-Zr alloy compared to the Al-Mn-Ni-Cu-Zr alloy. For 
example, in the latter Sc-free alloy, one needs 192h at 300 
◦
C to reach 
the hardness peak caused by the formation of nanoscale L1
2
-Al
3
Zr pre -
cipitates. This difference in ageing response between the 
Al-Mn-Mg-Sc-Zr and our Al-Mn-Ni-Cu-Zr alloy results from the higher 
diffusivity of Sc compared to Zr in FCC-Al. For example, the diffusion of 
Sc in FCC-Al is four orders of magnitude greater than that of Zr at 300 
◦
C, 
see Ref. [ 32 ]. This means that L1
2
-Al
3
Zr nanoscale precipitates require a 
higher temperature and/or longer ageing times than L1
2
-Al
3
Sc pre -
cipitates to be formed.

temperatures
As expected for a precipitation-strengthened alloy, the yield strength 
trend at room temperature is such that the yield strength reaches its 
highest value in the peak-ageing condition. In the presence of nanoscale 
Zr-rich precipitates (ageing conditions: SR + 1h/400

◦

nanoscale Zr-rich precipitates are expected to be cut by dislocations; 
however, the increase in temperature reduces their ability to alter 
dislocation motion due to the aforementioned two thermally activated 
deformation mechanisms.
4.2.3. Role of Mn in solid solution
The measurements of the evolution of the matrix composition using

yield strength of the SR sample compared to that of the aged specimens

◦

solution decreased drastically after ageing, by a factor of 2 in the FZ, and 
by a factor of 4 in the CZ. This depletion in Mn in the matrix is 
accompanied by the formation of Mn-rich precipitates [ 15 ], which 
correspond to the intragranular precipitates observed in the SEM images 
of the aged samples ( Fig. 9 b – d and Fig. 10 b – d). This suggests that Mn 
can be much more beneficial for the elevated-temperature strength 
when trapped in solid solution as in the SR sample, than when Mn is 
consumed by the formation of precipitates. This key result also suggests 
that Mn atoms in solid solution strongly affects dislocation motion at 
elevated temperature.

4.3. Recommendations for high strength at elevated temperatures in AM 
alloys
The present results show that the relative contribution of the 
different strengthening mechanisms varies with temperature. Conse -
quently, the microstructure must be optimized depending on the oper -
ating temperature. This work yields two recommendations for achieving 
high strength at elevated temperatures: (i) preserving the elongated 
morphology of the intermetallic network and (ii) retaining a significant 
amount of slow-diffusing solutes that strongly interact with dislocations 
in the solid solution. Manganese (Mn) was found to be a good candidate 
because it is a slow diffuser in aluminium, has a high interaction energy 
with dislocations and can be retained in solid solution in significant 
amounts, well beyond its maximum equilibrium solubility, via solute 
trapping by leveraging the LB-PBF processing conditions.
Interestingly, the formation of thermally stable L1
2
-Al
3
Zr nanoscale 
precipitates has long been recognised as a way to increase the high- 
temperature resistance of castable Al alloys [ 32 , 40 ]. However, in the 
present study their contribution to elevated-temperature yield strength 
appears relatively limited compared to the other two mechanisms pre -
viously described, especially when their radius is < 2 nm because of 
thermally activated deformation mechanisms such as cross-slip and 
dislocation climb. Therefore, depending on the relative importance of 
intermetallics, solid solution and nanoscale precipitates, promoting the 
formation of nanoscale precipitates with a radius typically < 2 nm via 
ageing heat treatment at 400 
◦
C could be indirectly detrimental, as this 
process leads to the globularisation of elongated intermetallic particles 
and a decrease in Mn in the matrix. These two factors were identified as 
providing the greatest strengthening effect between 150 and 300 
◦
C in 
the Al-4Mn-3Ni-2Cu-1Zr (wt.%) alloy.
The negative effect of the peak-ageing treatment on the high- 
temperature strength evidenced in this work in the Al-4Mn-3Ni-2Cu- 
1Zr (wt.%) may not necessarily apply to all aluminium alloys designed 
for LB-PBF. It will depend on the nature of the different solutes, espe -
cially (i) their ability to be retained in solid solution in the aluminium 
matrix after a peak-ageing treatment, (ii) their ability to interact with 
dislocations and hinder dislocation climb, and (iii) their relative diffu -
sivity relatively to that of Zr, which is consumed to form nanoscale 
precipitates upon ageing. However, the present results demonstrate that 
the heat treatment that leads to the best room-temperature mechanical 
properties is not necessarily the same treatment that leads to the best 
mechanical properties at elevated temperatures.
5. Conclusion
In this work, the influence of ageing on the high-temperature yield 
strength of the Al-4Mn-3Ni-2Cu-1Zr (wt.%) alloy was investigated and 
the links with the microstructural evolutions occurring upon ageing 
have been established. The main conclusions drawn from this work can 
be summarized as follows. 
• The as-built microstructure remains stable after a stress-relief heat 
treatment at 300 
◦
C for 4h. No major microstructural change was 
observed from the melt pool size down to the nanoscale (the solid 
solution composition is unchanged compared to the AB condition).
• At room temperature, the materials aged at 400 
◦
C have a superior 
yield strength in comparison with their SR counterpart, as expected 
for an alloy strengthened by L1
2
-Al
3
Zr precipitation. However, at 
elevated temperatures, typically > 150 
◦
C, the SR microstructure 
outperforms the three studied ageing conditions. The reduction in 
yield strength with temperature is larger for the peak-aged micro -
structures compared to their stress-relieved counterpart (absence of 
nanoscale Zr-rich precipitates).
• Nanoscale L1
2
-Al
3
Zr precipitates (radii ranging between 1 and 1.5 
nm) bring a substantial contribution to the room-temperature 
strength. However, their contribution to the high-temperature 
strengthening appears to be minor.
• The supersaturation of the solid solution in Mn and the elongated 
morphology of the network of intermetallics in the CZ in the SR 
condition are the two microstructural characteristics identified as 
being beneficial for maintaining a high yield strength at elevated 
temperatures in this LB-PBF-designed alloy.
The conclusions drawn from this study emphasize the importance of 
optimizing the heat treatment of Al alloys designed for AM depending on 
their operating temperature. It is also crucial to pay greater attention to 
the supersaturations resulting from out-of-equilibrium solidification 
conditions in LB-PBF, particularly with regard to solutes such as Mn that 
interact strongly with dislocations and can hinder dislocation climb.
