ABSTRACT 
Including ductile solid-solution phases into NiAl intermetallic alloys can toughen these materials, but based on 
conventional alloying strategy, this approach either degenerates the strength or has no visible improvement in 
the room-temperature (RT) ductility. Here we show a feasible strategy to improve the RT ductility of NiAl alloys 
by replacing conventional dilute solid solutions with complex concentrated ( i.e. , high-entropy) solid solutions as 
the ductilizing phases. The resulting alloy possesses a composite nanostructure, which combines B2-structured Ni 
(Al, Fe) phase with body-centered-cubic (BCC) and face-centered-cubic (FCC) structured high-entropy phases 
(HEPs) in different scales. We find that the applied stress is mainly partitioned to the B2 phase, contributing the 
alloy to high strength, while the ductile BCC and FCC HEPs, although bearing low loading, cooperate with the 
stress-induced martensitic transformation to accommodate the deformation, enhancing the sample ductility. 
Accordingly, our alloy presents superior tensile properties in a wide temperature range, including high strength 
( > 1500 MPa at RT and > 360 MPa at 700 
◦
C) and good ductility ( > 9% at RT and ~8% at 50 
◦
C), which 
achieves a simultaneous improvement in the ductility and strength compared with the conventional multiphase 
NiAl alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Intermetallic alloys based on B2-structured NiAl phase are attractive 
materials for advanced structural applications because of their low 
density, high melting temperature, high heat conductivity, and excellent 
oxidation resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. Unfortunately, the poor ductility at RT hin -
ders the application of these materials. Based on the conventional alloy 
design strategy, a common approach for improving the toughness of 
NiAl alloys is to include ductile single-principal-element, dilute 
solid-solution phases into them, i.e. , to develop multiphase NiAl alloys. 
However, this approach either degenerates the strength, such as NiAl-Fe 
[ 4 , 5 ] or has no visible improvement in the RT ductility, such as 
NiAl – Cr/Mo [ 6 , 7 ]. As a result, achieving the desired strength ‒ ductility 
balance remains a significant challenge for the NiAl alloys designed by 
the conventional alloying strategy. 
Different from the conventional alloys that are based on one or two 
principal elements, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) generally contain at least 
four principal elements in (near-)equimolar ratios [ 8 , 9 ]. Such alloys are, 
therefore, also defined as complex, concentrated alloys [ 8 , 10 ]. This 
unique alloy design philosophy offers HEAs many superior mechanical 
properties, such as high-temperature (HT) strength [ 11 ] and 
transformation-induced plasticity [ 12 , 13 ]. In most reported HEAs, the 
systems based on FCC-structured high-entropy solid solutions composed 
of 3d transition elements (Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni) commonly exhibit excel -
lent ductility and toughness [ 8 ]. For instance, the HEA CoCrFeNiMn 
presents exceptional ductility and toughness in a wide temperature 
range [ 14 ]. Other alloys based on such high-entropy solid solutions also 
possess good strength ‒ ductility combinations [ 15 – 21 ]. For example, an 
excellent balance between strength (yield strength > 1.8 GPa) and 
ductility (fracture strain > 9%) was achieved in a 
precipitation-hardening HEA, which consists of an FCC high-entropy 
solid-solution matrix and Ni
3
Al-type L1
2 
nanophases [ 15 ]. These 
studies imply that compared with the alloys based on simple, dilute solid 
solutions, the systems containing high-entropy solid solutions stand a 
chance for achieving better strength ‒ ductility combinations. 
Considering the exceptional properties of high-entropy solid solu -
tions, replacing the simple, dilute solid solutions with high-entropy ones 
as the ductilizing phases of multiphase NiAl alloys can be a feasible 
strategy for improving their mechanical properties. As an example of 
this alloy design strategy, we developed a multiphase NiAl alloy

3. Results 
3.1. Composite nanostructure with multiscale high-entropy phases 
Fig. 1 shows the SEM images of the microstructure of the as-cast 
alloy. The grains with a size of about dozens of micrometers contain 
two regions. Within every grain, a region (Region I) with densely 
dispersed spherical nanoparticles ( Fig. 1 ) exists in the core. In contrast, 
the other (Region II) exhibits a two-phase nanostructure and may 
contain some dark grey lamellar phases ( Fig. 1 ). At the grain boundaries 
(GBs), continuous lamellar phases with a thickness of ~1 μ m enclose the 
grains. The combination of these regions leads to a unique composite 
nanostructure. 
Fig. 2 a shows the detailed microstructure of Region I. The spherical 
particles can be seen more clearly in the high-angle annular dark-field 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images. 
The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) of the matrix presents a 
B2-type pattern, whereas that of the spherical particles demonstrates a 
diffraction pattern of the disordered BCC structure. The EDS results 
( Table 1 ) indicate that the matrix is the B2 Ni(Al, Fe) phase, whereas the 
BCC particles are Cr-rich, which are coherent with the Ni(Al, Fe) matrix 
according to high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image ( Fig. 2 b). In addition 
to the Cr-rich particles, there are also ultrafine nano-precipitates 
dispersing in the matrix. It should be noted that the SAED pattern of 
these nano-precipitates is shown in the upper right panel of Fig. 2 a 
because they are too fine to be characterized solely. The SAED analysis 
indicates that these nano-precipitates are FCC-structured phase and 
keep a Nishiyama Wassermann type orientation relationship

Such a relationship was also reported in the Ni – Al – Cr-based alloys by 
Gale et al. [ 26 ]. According to the SAED pattern, the lattice parameter of 
these FCC-structured nano-precipitates is about 0.351 nm, which is 
consistent with that of pure Ni (~0.3517 nm) [ 27 ]. Additionally, the 
STEM-EDS results ( Table 1 ) show that the Ni content in the Ni(Al, Fe)

are Ni-rich. However, their lattice parameter is less than that of the 
FCC-structured phases calculated from the HEXRD spectrum (~0.3601 
nm), indicating that there also exist other FCC-structured phases in our 
alloy and the volume fraction of these Ni-rich nano-precipitates is too

and an ordered BCC-structured (B2) Ni(Al, Fe) matrix with a coherent 
interface. 
The microstructure in Region II is inverse to that in Region I, i.e. , the 
continuous matrix becomes the disordered BCC phases ( Fig. 2 c). As 
shown in Table 1 , these BCC-structured phases contain multi-principal 
elements in near-equimolar ratios. In other words, the matrix phases 
in Region II are CrFeNi-rich, Al-containing BCC-structured HEPs. This 
finding confirms that our strategy for including HEPs in NiAl alloys is 
valid. Similar to Region I, the interface between the two phases is 
coherent ( Fig. 2 d). Accordingly, Region II is mainly a two-phase nano -
structure that the B2-structured Ni(Al, Fe) phases (in nanoscale) are 
enclosed by continuous disordered BCC-structured HEP with a coherent 
interface.

3.2. Mechanical properties in a wide temperature range 
Fig. 4 a and b show the tensile stress – strain curves of the current alloy 
in a wide temperature range ( 50 – 700 
◦
C). At RT, the alloy exhibits a 
large fracture strain greater than 9%, an excellent ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) greater than 1500 MPa, and a yield strength (YS) of 
~850 MPa. This stress strain curve presents three stages. The first is the 
elastic stage, where strain increases linearly with increasing stress. At 
stage II, the slopes of the curve decrease, but the material still remains 
high strain-hardening capacity. Interestingly, a nearly linear relation -
ship between stress and strain occurs again in the tensile curve when the 
engineering stress is greater than 1300 MPa (stage III). This unique 
tensile behavior contributes to the adequate strain-hardening capacity 
until fracture (UTS/YS close to 1.8). At a low temperature of 50 
◦
C, the 
alloy still exhibits good ductility (elongation after fracture of ~8%), 
high UTS and YS (~1500 MPa and ~970 MPa, respectively). It should be 
noted that the ductility at 50 
◦
C was defined by the elongation after 
fracture rather than the fracture strain because of the lack of exten -
someter during testing. Also, the mechanical properties of the alloy are 
competitive at elevated temperatures. At 600 
◦
C, the YS and UTS of the 
alloy are about 467 MPa and 620 MPa, respectively. At a higher tem -
perature of 700 
◦
C, usable YS and UTS of 326 MPa and 364 MPa, 
respectively, still remain. These superior properties exhibit the potential 
of our alloys for structural applications in a wide temperature range. 
In the RT tensile fractography ( Fig. 4 c), a feature similar to inter -
granular fracture is found. This observation seems to be inconsistent 
with the excellent ductility of this alloy. However, careful research on a 
high-magnification image reveals that the tensile fractography includes 
two fracture modes – cleavage fracture within the grains and ductile 
fracture at the GBs ( Fig. 4 d). These findings indicate that the inter -
granular fracture, which is attributed to the intrinsic brittle of NiAl GBs 
and difficult to be alleviated by microalloying [ 28 ], is suppressed in our 
alloy. Therefore, the lamellar GB FCC HEP should play an essential role 
in the excellent ductility of our alloy.

4. Discussion 
The mechanical properties of alloys are sensitive to their micro -
structures. The microstructure of our HEP ductilizing NiAl alloy is a 
unique composite nanostructure. Within every grain, Region I in the 
core presents a morphology of numerous nanoparticles dispersing in the 
Ni(Al, Fe) matrix. In contrast, Region II exhibits a spinodal-like structure 
composed of nanoscale Ni(Al, Fe) phases and a continuous BCC HEP 
matrix. Every grain is enclosed by the continuous lamellar GB FCC HEPs. 
Owing to the difference in the type, size, shape, and distribution of the 
constituent phases, these regions can contribute variously to the tensile 
behavior. 
Region I is considered to contribute primarily to the high strength in 
a wide temperature range ( > 1500 MPa at RT and > 360 MPa at 700 
◦
C), 
because this region contains mainly the B2 Ni(Al, Fe) phase that is 
partitioned to the main loading ( Fig. 7 b), and strengthened by dispersed 
Cr-rich nanoparticles ( Fig. 8 g). Also, the lack of independent slip sys -
tems in B2-structured phases [ 22 , 28 , 38 ] causes the fact that the plastic 
deformation in the B2 Ni(Al, Fe) phase depends mostly on the 
stress-induced martensitic transformation ( Fig. 8 g, h, and j), which af -
fects slightly on the macro-ductility and toughness [ 38 , 41 ], leading to a

HEPs at GBs. More importantly, the orientation relationship enables the 
easy slip transfer from GB FCC HEPs to adjacent grains, hence the 
compatible deformation among different grains, contributing to the 
excellent balance between strength and ductility.

strength and ductility. It should be noted that the conventional multi -
phase NiAl alloys with good strength ductility combinations were 
mostly produced by deformation or rapid solidification. Therefore, their 
good ductility was attributed to fine grains [ 5 , 43 , 50 ], which unavoid -
ably degenerates the HT properties. Additionally, these processes are 
generally difficult to fabricate the actual components with large sizes 
and complex geometries ( e.g. , hollow blades), restricting the industrial 
application of these materials. 
In comparison, the excellent mechanical properties of our alloy are 
attributed to the superior ductility of multiscale high-entropy solid so -
lutions, the composite structure consisting of various multiphase nano -
structures, as well as the stress-induced martensitic transformation. As a 
result, a satisfactory strength ductility combination can be obtained in 
the as-cast alloy. In addition, the as-cast alloy with an equiaxed structure 
has exhibited good HT strength. As shown in Fig. 10 b, the YS of our alloy

2.1
, which contains 
the B2 NiAl phase and FCC-structured HEP in lamellar eutectic structure 
and has many advantages, including good liquidity, castability, and 
ductility (~17% at RT, as shown in Fig. 10 a) [ 20 , 21 ]. However, the 
definition of HEAs requires the concentration of each component within 
5 – 35 at.%, which intrinsically limits the selectable range of composi -
tions and the volume fraction of the NiAl phases related to the alloy 
strength. Different from the alloying strategy of eutectic HEA, our 
strategy aims to introduce HEPs as the ductilizing phases of multiphase 
NiAl alloys rather than to develop a HEA. Consequently, the designed 
alloy is not restricted to the requirement of the near-equiatomic initial 
composition. It has more possibilities to optimize the constituent phases 
by composition design (including type, content, shape, distribution, 
etc .), as long as the final microstructure contains HEPs. For instance, the 
highest element concentration in the AlCoCrFeNi
2.1

Ni (close to the upper concentration limit of HEAs), while that in our 
alloy is 40 at.% and can be further increased in future if necessary. These 
advantages make our alloy more competitive than the eutectic HEA in a 
wide temperature range ( Fig. 10 b). 
These comparisons indicate that our alloying strategy has a more 
significant potential for the actual applications than NiAl-based/ 
containing alloys. Compared with the conventional multiphase NiAl- 
based alloys, our alloy replaces the single-principal-element, dilute 
solid-solution phases with the multi-principal-element, near-equia -
tomic/concentrated ones (HEPs) that have unique ductile behavior and 
excellent strength ductility combinations in a wide temperature range. 
This change results in a simultaneous improvement in the RT ductility 
and HT strength (or operating temperature). Compared with the NiAl- 
containing eutectic HEA, our alloy has more chances to optimize its 
properties because our alloy is not a HEA. Its composition is not limited 
to the composition range of HEAs (5 – 35 at.%). This difference gives our 
alloy sufficient strength in a wide temperature range. Therefore, our 
alloy can be superior to both the conventional multiphase NiAl alloys 
and the NiAl-containing eutectic HEA because our alloy design strategy 
takes advantages of both the design philosophies of conventional alloys 
and HEAs.

5. Conclusions 
This work introduces multiscale HEPs into NiAl alloys to improve 
their properties. The resulting alloy presents a composite nanostructure 
that contains B2 Ni(Al, Fe) phase and multiscale HEPs. The martensitic 
transformation occurs in both the B2 phase and BCC HEP. Unlike the 
martensite dominating deformation in the B2 phase, the BCC HEP de -
forms via both dislocation motion and martensitic transformation, 
leading to a good plastic deformability that can accommodate the 
deformation inside grains. Moreover, the K – S-type orientation rela -
tionship between the ductile GB FCC HEP and the adjacent grains en -
ables the easy slip transfer from one to the other. Hence, the compatible 
deformation among different grains enhances the sample ductility. The 
large loading to the B2 phase and the high internal stress required for 
uniform homogeneous deformation contribute to increased strength and 
UTS/YS ratio. Benefiting from the unique composite nanostructure with 
multiscale HEPs and the stress-induced martensitic transformation, our 
alloy presents the highest UTS at RT ( > 1500 MPa), competitive HT 
strength ( > 360 MPa at 700 
◦
C), and good ductility at low temperatures 
( > 9% at RT and ~8% at 50 
◦
C). These excellent properties confirm 
that improvement in the RT ductility of NiAl alloys without severely 
losing the strength is valid by including HEPs. This HEP-containing NiAl 
alloy has significant advantages over almost conventional multiphase 
NiAl alloys that contain simple, dilute solid-solution phases, suggesting 
that the systems containing high-entropy solid solutions stand a chance 
for achieving better strength ‒ ductility combinations.
