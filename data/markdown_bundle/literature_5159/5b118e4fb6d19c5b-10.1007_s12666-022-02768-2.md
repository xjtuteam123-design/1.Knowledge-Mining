1 Introduction
To cater for the requirement of the structure materials oper-
ating at elevated temperatures in aerospace and petrochemi-
cal industries, high-temperature alloys have drawn consid -
erable attention. Thereinto, Ni-based high-temperature 
superalloys possess the most elevated service temperature. 
However, due to its melting point of ~ 1300 °C, the potential 
application temperature of Ni-based superalloys is limited at 
the range of 850–900 °C [1 –3]. Notably, with the boosting 
development of the modern technology, Ni-based superal-
loys are actually on the basis of a common and straight-
forward idea: one principal element combined with small 
amounts of other elements to optimize the properties [4, 5], 
which has been labelled as “mature technology” due to the 
limited possibility in expanding the rims of performance 
[6]. To overcome the essential drawbacks, new alloys are 
needed to design by a distinct route. Fortunately, in the past 
several years, a fundamentally novel idea of multi-principal 
element alloys (MPEAs) proposed by Cantor significantly 
reinvigorates the discovery of new alloys [7], and at the same 
time a similar idea is also independently presented by Yeh 
termed as high entropy alloys (HEAs) [8 ]. Soon after the 
introduction of MPEAs and HEAs, refractory HEAs, com-
monly composed of IVB-VIB elements, have been explored 
for elevated-temperature applications [9 , 10]. Among the 
reported refractory HEAs, HfNbTaTiZr with a single BCC 
structure has drawn much attention due to its excellent plas-
ticity exceeding 50%. However, its room-temperature (RT) 
yield strength is only about 929 MPa [11].
To improve the yield strength of HfNbTaTiZr with high 
plasticity, a series of common methods have been widely 
utilized in MPEAs/HEAs. Thereinto, refinement strengthen-
ing has drawn much attention due to its benefit in enhanc-
ing yield strength without sacrificing plasticity. Chen et al. 
Abstract Two refractory CrHfNbTa 0.5 Zr and 
 CrHfMo0.5Ta0.5Zr alloys were designed and prepared by 
a vacuum arc-melting based on HfNbTaTiZr, exhibiting a 
dendritic structure with different morphologies and phase 
components. The round/elliptical dendrites surrounded with 
the intertwined Cr-rich Laves matrix and Zr-rich BCC net-
work structure of the as-cast CrHfNbTa0.5Zr unprecedently 
improved the yield strength to 1590 ± 22 MPa, 1.7 times 
more than 929 MPa of HfNbTaTiZr, and retained a prom-
ising plasticity of ~ 5%. In contrast, CrHfMo0.5Ta0.5Zr was 
barely satisfactory either in strength or plasticity, due to the 
lath-like dendrites composed of the Mo-rich Laves phase 
with a high-volume fraction of ~ 70%. A common BCC to 
HCP phase transformation occurred in the two alloys origi-
nated from the instability of (Zr, Hf)-rich BCC phase and 
the relatively high self-diffusivities and interdiffusion of Zr 
during the annealing. The hard HCP phase further increased 
the yield strength of the alloy-Nb but deteriorated the plas-
ticity of the two alloys.

[12] significantly decreased the grain size of HfNbTaTiZr 
alloy to 38 μm and unfortunately found that the contribu-
tion from refining grains to the yield strength is somewhat 
limited, exactly only ~ 30 MPa. Besides, as well known, 
compositional modification, such as elemental substitution 
or minor addition, is also a conventional and effective way 
to optimize the material performance [13, 14]. Accordingly, 
adjusting the alloy composition swiftly catches research-
ers’ eyes in the realm of MPEAs and HEAs with the same 
purpose mentioned. For instance, Fazakas et al. [14] used 
Cr with low density to substitute the heavy element Ta in 
HfNbTaTiZr, decreasing the density to 8.012 g/cm 3 and 
successfully increasing its yield strength to 1375 MPa. The 
attractive combination of strength and density is attributed 
to the incorporation of low-density Cr and high-strength 
Cr-containing Laves phases (Cr2Hf, Cr2Nb). Likewise, Mo 
replaced Ta in HfNbTaTiZr, obviously improving its yield 
strength up to 1719 MPa in the as-cast HfMoNbTiZr alloy 
[15].
In the present work, inspired by the motivation of enhanc-
ing the yield strength and decreasing the density, Cr and Mo 
were selected to add to the most concerned HfNbTaTiZr 
alloy, facilitating the formation of AB2-type Laves phases 
with elements Hf and Zr, which have been confirmed benefi-
cial to the strength improvement [16]. To avoid the increas-
ing difficulty in metallurgy, Ti was firstly substituted by Cr 
due to the relatively low Ti-containing alloy’s high-temper-
ature stability, and meanwhile Ta with the highest density 
(16.68 g/cm3) was halved in the newly designed alloy. Sub-
sequently, based on the new alloy CrHfNbTa0.5Zr, Mo was 
further used to replace Nb alternatively, considering their 
similar density and the modulus of Mo three times over Nb. 
The modulus is commonly positively related to the strength 
of materials. The phase components, microstructure, and 
mechanical properties of the two alloys were intensively 
investigated and found that the as-cast CrHfNbTa0.5Zr not 
only possessed an outstanding yield strength, 1.7 times more 
than that of HfNbTaTiZr but also had promising plasticity 
of ~ 5%.
2 Experimental Procedures
Alloy ingots of CrHfNbTa 0.5 Zr (alloy-Nb) and 
 CrHfMo0.5Ta0.5Zr (alloy-Mo) were prepared by melting the 
highly pure bulk elements (> 99.9 wt%) under a titanium 
getter argon atmosphere. More than four times melting with 
electromagnetic stirring was carried out to ensure the com-
positional homogeneity of all alloy ingots. Ingot pieces of 
10 g were sliced by electrical discharge wire-cutting and 
then melted again to pour into the copper mould with a rod-
like cavity. To further homogenize the microstructure, the 
rod-like samples were annealed at 1273 K for 20 h under 
vacuum conditions.
The crystal structure of the as-cast and homogenized 
samples was identified using a PANalytical_X’Pert X-ray 
diffractometer with Cu K/u1D6FC radiation with a scanning rate 
of 3°/min. The density of the two alloys was measured by 
Archimedes’ principle, and presented in Table  1. Before 
morphological characterization using a field emission 
scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Zeiss Sigma 500) 
combined with the energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS, 
Oxford), the samples were cut from the rod-like ingots and 
underwent polishing and etching in aqua regia with the mole 
ratio of HCl: HNO3 equal to 3:1. The secondary electron 
mode was used to capture images under an electron high 
tension voltage of 20 kV , a diaphragm diameter of 60 μm 
and a working distance of ~ 8.5 mm. The specimens with 
3.7 mm in diameter and ∼ 5.6 mm in height were com-
pressed to 50% height reduction using a computer-controlled 
mechanical testing machine (Instron, Norwood, MA) at RT. 
The compressive repeatability was ensured by multiple tests, 
according to which the standard deviation can be acquired. 
The compression was performed at a constant ramp speed 
of 0.0056 mm/s, which corresponded to an initial strain rate 
of 0.001  s−1 in compression tests.

To uncover the effect of Cr/Mo incorporation on alloys’ 
microstructure, SEM images of the two as-cast alloys are 
shown in Fig. 2. At low magnification, the two alloys both 
exhibited a typical dendritic structure but with different 
morphologies in the as-cast state (Figs.  2a, e). For the 
alloy-Nb, the round/elliptical dendrites with blunt edges 
(marked as D) irregularly embedded into the interdendrites 
(marked as ID), as shown in Fig.  2b. Furthermore, it is 
noted that the interdendrites were composed of two dis-

network structure (Fig.  2c). To trace the elemental dis-
tribution of the different regions, a concentration profile 
of each element along the ‘Line 1’ labelled in Fig.  2c is 
displayed in Fig.  2d. Three distinct categories of distribu-

determined by its area fraction in Fig.  2e using the ImageJ 
software. In addition, some acute pores could be seen in 
the interdendrites (green arrows in Fig.  2f), which may be 
because there was insufficient molten metal to feed into

Laves phase [16], Cr also participated in the formation of the 
Mo-rich Laves phase and exhibited a slightly high content 
in the dendrites. Subsequently, Zr with the lowest melting

morphological change of the dendrites in the two homog-

interdendrites of the as-homogenized alloy-Nb changed from 
two regions in the as-cast state to three regions, including a 
dark grey region without distinct boundary, a grey bounded 
region, and a broken network marked with ‘Region 4–6’ 
in Fig. 4b. However, for the as-homogenized alloy-Mo, the 
interdendrites remained unchanged compared to those of the 
as-cast alloy, as shown in Fig.  4e. To identify the chemi-

mapping confirmed that the elemental distribution of the 
as-homogenized alloy-Nb was much different from that of 
the as-cast one. First, the dendrites were rich in Hf, Nb, 
and Zr with contents of 31.1 ± 0.3, 26.6 ± 0.5 and 25.6 ± 0.7 
at%, corresponding to the ZrNb-type BCC1’ phase. Sec-

contained a higher content of Cr up to ~ 50 at%, the grey

Hf, and Nb. Therefore, they corresponded to the Cr2Hf-type 
Laves’, (Zr, Hf)-rich HCP, and NbTa-type BCC2’ phases

3.3 Compressive Properties
The RT compression tests were carried out to determine the 
effect of elements Cr/Mo on the mechanical properties of the 
HfNbTaTiZr base alloy. The engineering stress–strain curves 
of the as-cast and as-homogenized samples of alloy-Nb and 
alloy-Mo are shown in Fig.  6. Detailly, the yield strength 
(σ0.2) of the as-cast alloy-Nb was 1590 ± 22 MPa, even 1.7 
times more than 929 MPa of HfNbTaTiZr alloy [11]. It also 
showed a high work-hardening response of σ 0.2/σb = 0.75 
after yielding, leading its fracture strength up to a maximum 
value of 2112 ± 20 MPa. The yield strength of the as-homog-
enized alloy-Nb increased a little compared to that of the as-
cast one, while the plastic strain decreased from ~ 5% for the 
as-cast state to ~ 3%. By replacing Nb with Mo, the alloy-Mo 
exhibited a typical brittle fracture behaviour, which directly 
resulted in decreased fracture strengths for the alloy. Specifi-
cally, the fracture strength decreased from 2112 ± 20 MPa of 
alloy-Nb to 1675 ± 25 MPa of alloy-Mo for the as-cast state, 
while it decreased from 1850 ± 20 MPa to 1281 ± 23 MPa 
for the homogenized state (Table 3).
To reveal the origination of the difference in the mechani-
cal properties, the schematic diagram of the samples under

4 Conclusions
In summary, two refractory MPEAs, CrHfNbTa0.5Zr and 
 CrHfMo0.5Ta0.5Zr were designed and intensively discussed 
from their phase components, microstructure, and mechani-
cal properties. The following conclusions are reached:
(1) The two alloys both exhibited a typical dendritic struc-
ture but with different morphologies and phase compo-
nents in the as-cast state. The alloy-Nb was composed 
of the round/elliptical HfZrNb3-type BCC dendrites 
surrounded with the intertwined Cr2Zr-type Laves 
matrix and Zr0.686Nb0.314-type BCC network structure. 
The alloy-Mo was composed of lath-like dendrites and 
interdendrites with the Mo2Hf-type Laves phase and 
 Hf9Mo-type BCC phase, respectively.
(2) The as-cast alloy-Nb unprecedently improved the yield 
strength to 1590 ± 22 MPa and retained a promising 
plasticity of ~ 5% due to the introduction of the Cr-
rich Laves matrix and the Zr-rich BCC network struc-
ture in the interdendrites, while the as-cast alloy-Mo 
exhibited a brittle fracture with the fracture strength of 
1675 ± 25 MPa due to the lath-like dendritic structure 
composed of the hard Mo-rich Laves phase with a high-
volume fraction of ~ 70%.
(3) After the homogenization treatment, the network struc-
ture in the interdendrites of the alloy-Nb decomposed 
into the grey bounded region of (Zr, Hf)-rich HCP 
phase and the broken network of NbTa-type BCC phase 
and the obvious increased contents of Zr and Hf in the 
interdendrites of the alloy-Mo promoted the BCC to 
HCP phase transformation.
(4) The (Zr, Hf)-rich BCC to HCP phase transformation 
originated from the instability of (Zr, Hf)-rich BCC 
phase and the relatively high self-diffusivities and inter-
diffusion of Zr, which increased the yield strength of 
the alloy-Nb slightly but was harmful to the plasticity 
and fracture strength of the two alloys.
