Development of high strength, high ductility
and high creep resistant iron aluminide
David G. Morris*, Maria A. Mun˜oz-Morris, Jesus Chao
Department of Physical Metallurgy, Centro Nacional de Investifaciones Metalurgical (CENIM), CSIC, Avenida Gregorio del Amo 8, E-28040 Madrid, Spain
Available online 1 April 2004
Abstract
Iron aluminides have been examined over many years, especially since the 1960s with renewed attention since the 1980s. Several clear
characteristics of these materials have been demonstrated, some good and some less attractive. Properties seen are those such as reasonable
strength from room temperature to about 500 8C; acceptable but not good ductility at room temperature dependent in part on environmental
sensitivity; poor creep and high temperature strength above 500 8C; excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance; cheap constituent elements
but difﬁcult thermomechanical forming. This article reviews advances in strengthening and improving ductility of FeAl over the last decade
or so, concentrating especially on activities of the authors’ groups, and reviews also possibilities for exploiting the excellent
oxidation/corrosion resistance at high temperatures by improving the high temperature creep behaviour.

1. Introduction
The majority of the studies on iron aluminides have
examined ordered structure, strengthening and plastic
deformation, and oxidation or corrosion behaviour of alloys
based on binary alloys with composition near Fe
3Al, with Al
contents of 25–30%, or based on FeAl, with Al contents of
about 35–40%. Strengthening has been modiﬁed by control
of Al content, grain size or shape, by solute additions such
as Cr or Mo, and by second phase or precipitate forming
additions such as Nb, Zr, C and B [1–10] . To a large extent,
these materials were examined with a view to replacement
of high temperature or stainless steels, and to a large extent
this effort has been unsuccessful, as evidenced by the few
applications of iron aluminides today. Major reasons for this
lack of success are the difﬁculties of processing
iron aluminides [11,12], their low ductility at ambient
temperatures which has been related to embrittlement by
hydrogen produced by chemical reactions between fresh
metal and atmospheric water vapour [13,14], and the loss of
strength at temperatures above about 500–600 8C [1,2,15]
which has been variously related to changes of dislocation
types to simple k100l perfect dislocations at high
temperature [16–19] , the high diffusivity of the open bcc
structure, and the dissolution or coarsening of strengthening
carbide particles [6,7]. Despite their relatively low density
and excellent oxidation [2,20–22] and high temperature
corrosion [2,23,24] resistance, these deﬁciencies have
meant that iron aluminides are not at all the much-vaunted
cheap to produce, high strength, high temperature materials.
Alternative approaches to alloy and material development,
which may lead to a material with a sufﬁciently good raft of
properties for engineering application are outlined here.
2. High strength FeAl
One domain of possible interest in iron aluminides is for
room temperature applications where very high strength,
combined with fairly low density and high elastic moduli, is
required. Addressing such interest, the last decade or so
has seen the development of a very high strength
(above 1000 MPa yield stress), mechanically alloyed
version of Fe–40Al [15,25–28] . This material contains
40% Al (atomic percent is used throughout this article,
unless speciﬁcally stated otherwise), chosen to ensure low
density, good strength and ductility, and good oxidation/
corrosion resistance. There are, in addition, small amounts
of Zr and B for removing carbon impurities and improving
grain boundary adhesion [25–28] . In addition, 1 wt% Y

understood in terms of its very strong k110l ﬁbre texture.

initial batches of materials contained higher levels of
impurity). As a ﬁnal point, it has been noted that
higher material processing temperatures lead to stronger
k110l textures [36], and these may have been the origin of
the stronger texture in the CH materials.
In conclusion, it has been shown that optimised
mechanical alloying, powder consolidation and subsequent
thermomechanical processing are capable of producing
material with yield strength over 1000 MPa. Matrix strength
contributes nearly 300 MPa, strengthening by the ﬁnest
grains contributes up to almost 350 MPa, and dispersoid
particles then contribute up to about 450 MPa.
3. High ductility FeAl
It has become clear that several common-sense aspects
must be respected in order to obtain reasonable ductility in
iron aluminides: avoiding the near-stoichiometric FeAl
composition, restricting environmental attack as far as
possible, and also including B addition to improve grain
boundary strength and avoid brittle intergranular fracture.
Apart from these alloy design and application aspects,
there seems to be few alloying or structural recipes to follow
in order to improve ductility [37]. The only clear
microstructural parameter of importance [37] is reﬁnement
of the grain size, for a given strength value, and in this sense
the mechanically alloyed materials discussed above show
excellent levels of tensile ductility, 5–10%, for their high
strengths [37,38].
For these very high strength materials it has been shown
that tensile failure occurs by crack propagation from the
surface, with the initial slow crack growth determined by
environmental attack before ﬁnal, uncontrolled rapid failure
[38,39]. As such, fracture mechanics and local stress
intensity factors at the crack tips correctly describe the
failure process. Good ductility (5–10%) is obtained for
those cases where crack nucleation is difﬁcult, and it is here

4. High temperature creep strength
The intrinsic creep strength of FeAl is very low. Even
though the material is ordered (or perhaps may be
disordered when considering off-FeAl-stoichiometric
compositions and creep at very high temperatures) the
diffusivity is sufﬁciently high in the open bcc-base lattice
and there are no strong, intrinsic dislocation barriers to make
dislocation motion difﬁcult [41]. Clearly then, creep
strength depends on the availability of a suitable dispersion
of ﬁne and stable second phase particles.
Most of the work reported on creep of iron aluminides
makes use of solute or carbide additions to improve the
creep strength in the 500–600 8C range [2,6,7]. A little
attention has also been given to oxide particles in
mechanically alloyed materials [42,43], similar to those
discussed in the earlier parts of this article, and these may
offer hope for creep resistance at much higher temperatures.
In addition, there has been some work reported using rapid
solidiﬁcation methods to introduce ﬁne dispersions of
boride particles, which also considerably improve high
temperature creep behaviour [44,45]. The remainder of this
section reviews the work reported in the literature on
Fe
3Al–FeAl alloys with respect to their high temperature
creep behaviour, and especially to the inﬂuence of different
varieties of second phase additions.
The second phases of importance for ensuring good creep
resistance can be characterised by their stability at high
temperature, the solubility of the second phase constituents
at these temperatures, dispersoid particle size, which should
be as ﬁne as possible, and volume fraction of second phase,
typically in the percent range. For example, much of the
work reported on creep resistance of iron aluminides deals
with carbide second phases present in volume fractions of
approximately 1% [2,6,7]. Very ﬁne particles are obtained
by precipitation from a solutionised state, limiting the
possible volume fraction of second phase, and their limited
stability at high temperatures means that they dissolve or
coarsen during creep testing at temperatures above
500–600 8C. Somewhat more stable are the boride phases
that have been studied, but here it is limited solubility in the
molten alloy state that restricts the volume fraction of
dispersoid particles possible [44,45]. At the other extreme of
high stability are oxide particles, such as the Y
2O3 particles
introduced by mechanical alloying [25–28,42,43] , which
are too insoluble for preparation by melt–solidiﬁcation
techniques, but which are hence expected to have excellent
high temperature stability.

may also show intermediate levels of high temperature
solubility and low temperature stability.
Even though the yttria dispersoid particles are expected
to show excellent stability against coarsening at high
temperatures, studies have shown [15,29,32,46] that sig-
niﬁcant particle coarsening occurs at high temperatures such
as those used for material processing, and that the yttria
particles are not as stable in the FeAl matrix as observed in
an FeCr matrix [47], see Figs. 2 and 3. These oxide particles
show excellent stability against coarsening up to about
1100 8C for moderate times, but for longer times and higher
temperatures quickly become very much coarser.

matrix to produce mixed (Y, Al, Fe) oxides [15,46]. In the
case of the FeCr matrix there is a change from yttrium oxide

An alternative to the use of very ﬁne and stable particles,

restricted high-temperature long-time stability, is to make
use of intermetallic second phase particles such as Laves

for the carbides and oxides discussed above, but they may
be present with larger particle sizes following their
formation during solidiﬁcation [48]. These phases may
nevertheless be interesting for endowing creep resistance
for two reasons: (i) they can be produced in larger volume
fraction than for the more stable (limited solubility) oxide
and carbide phases since their component elements have
good solubility in the molten state, and this can compensate
their somewhat coarser size to produce good strengthening;
(ii) their stability can nevertheless be good [49,50]
since their coarsening is controlled by diffusion of a
metal (Ta, Nb, W, etc.) and not by diffusion of interstitial
(C, B, etc.).

need for exotic processing methods. The Laves phase
reinforcing particles are rather large following casting but
the volume of second phase is nevertheless sufﬁciently high
to ensure good creep strength.
5. Summary
The present article has reviewed attempts made over the
past years to achieve especially good mechanical properties
in iron aluminides.
(i) Very high strength has been shown to be possible in
mechanically alloyed materials, with strengthening
produced in almost equal portions by solution alloying
of the matrix, dispersed particle (oxide) hardening, and
ﬁne grain size hardening. Textures obtained after
processing these PM materials play an additional role
of modifying the strength of the grain boundaries, as
these change from being high-angle varieties in weakly
textured materials to low-angle varieties in strongly
textured materials.
(ii) Good ductility in iron aluminides has been shown to
be possible by reﬁning the grain size to very low
values—near the micron. These grain sizes are easily
obtained for the mechanically alloyed materials, but
are diﬁcult to obtain by conventional thermo-mechan-
ical processing. Such ﬁne grain sizes are unstable at
very high temperatures, and grain coarsening leads to a
loss of the good ductility.
(iii) Creep strength in iron aluminides depends on the
presence of a ﬁne distribution of small and stable par-
ticles. Fine carbides obtained by solutionising-precipi-
tation are satisfactory only at creep temperatures
