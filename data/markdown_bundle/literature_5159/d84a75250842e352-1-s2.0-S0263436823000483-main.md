ABSTRACT 
There is considerable interest in the adoption of additive manufacturing for processing refractory metals. The 
layer-wise fabrication approach enables opportunities for producing complex geometries which cannot be 
otherwise be achieved via powder metallurgy. However, the processing science is still in its nascent stages and 
structure – property relations are relatively unexplored. Fundamental research is needed to further develop the 
technology and enable the fabrication of refractory metals for high temperature applications. Here we focus on 
the processing of pure tungsten using electron beam melting additive manufacturing. Experimentally we develop 
a suitable processing window for achieving high density crack free material. Microstructural analysis reveals that 
the microstructure generally consists of a columnar structure with a ( 111 ) build direction fiber preference, 
although, fiber switching was observed. Process induced deformation is believed to drive the formation of 
subgrains whose boundaries exhibit a high dislocation density. High temperature tensile testing reveals that the 
material exhibits excellent properties closer to that of annealed tungsten. Significant mechanical anisotropy was 
observed to be present which is likely driven by strong crystallographic texture. 
1. Introduction 
The high liquidus of refractory metals makes this class of materials 
attractive for extreme high temperature applications. Tungsten, in 
particular, exhibits the highest melt temperature at 3422
◦
C . Unfortu -
nately, tungsten ’ s ductile to brittle transition temperature (DBTT) is well 
above room temperature making it difficult to process [1] . The combi -

limits manufacturing options to powder metallurgy approaches [2,3] . 
However, recent advances in the processing of high temperature non- 
weldable Ni-based superalloys, γ -TiAl, and some refractory metals via 
electron beam melting (EBM) additive manufacturing (AM) indicates 
that perhaps analogous processing of tungsten may be possible [4 – 12] .

layer is preceded by a preheating step during which the electron beam is 
defocused and rapidly scanned over the build area. Surface temperatures 
achieved by preheating can exceed 1000

process induced thermal stresses. In addition, the entire process occurs 
under vacuum which improves the metallurgical quality of fabricated

not be suppressed [13] . This was also observed in another LPBF tungsten

comparing the efficacy of EBM and LPBF for processing pure tungsten,

crystallographic texture was shown to be controlled locally via manip -

Tungsten is a desirable material for extreme high temperature ap -
plications such as fusion energy generation. Processing via AM offers 
novel opportunities to unlock areas of the design space inaccessible 
through powder metallurgy. A review of the current state of the art in -
dicates that LPBF processing is challenging. While EBM offers promise, 
currently, even the most successful attempts have had difficulty in 
obtaining material free of defects. This work presents recent findings on 
the processing of pure tungsten via EBM AM. An exhaustive experi -
mental effort was performed to establish the process-structure re -
lationships. High density crack-free material was obtained across 
various settings and microstructures consist of columnar grains with 
either ( 001 ) , mixed ( 001 ) / ( 111 ) , or fully ( 111 ) BD fiber textures. Mi -
croscopy reveals that the as-fabricated material consists of small sub -
grains with low angle grain boundaries. High temperature tensile testing 
reveals that the as-fabricated material exhibits good strength and 
ductility. When compared against the available data in the literature the 
material behavior most closely resembles tungsten in the annealed state. 
These findings demonstrate that pure tungsten may be processed via 
EBM AM and that the synthesized material exhibits good structural 
integrity.

EBM AM tungsten [17] . Prior work on EBM AM processing of Ti6Al4V 
demonstrated these defects nucleate and grow from the sidewalls of the 
component [42] . The proposed mechanism is a nucleation and growth 
mechanism. In Ti6Al4V, under the considered settings, chimney 
porosity appeared to nucleate from dimples on the sides of the printed 
part. During raking the pores were seen to be obstructed by the depos -
ited powder. However, despite all the powder being assumed to melt, 
the pores reappeared. The argument made is that the melted powder 
forms a liquid film which has a free surface both above and below the 
film. Surface tension causes the film to rupture and the pore to persist 
across layers. Here, in the X-BD cross section of Fig. 2 , it is clear that 
indeed the pores do have a tendency to persist across layers. Further -
more, from the XY cross section, pores indeed are often times filled with 
partially melted material. The morphology of the chimney porosity 
across in situ near-IR images is also shown in the bottom of Fig. 2 . 
Interestingly, chimney porosity development has a transient nature 
which depends on processing conditions. Shown in Fig. 3 are in situ 
near-IR micrographs obtained in two samples at various heights during 
the build. Images corresponding to layers in immediate succession are 
shown as well as images 8 mm apart. The porosity in sequential images 
spatially appears in the same locations, a clear characteristic of chimney 
porosity, but is completely eliminated in sample No. 11 in later layers. It 
is noted in prior work on Ti6Al4V and pure copper that the beam size 
and energy density can be used to mitigate against chimney porosity 
[42,43] . Therefore, it is believed that healing in sample No. 11 is due to 
the transient nature of heat transfer during the AM process. Initially, 
deposition occurs on a rather large build plate which acts as a heat sink. 
Later in the build, parts are surrounded by a relatively insulative powder 
bed and heat can only conduct away through the previously printed 
part; the build plate is “ far away ” . Hence, as the build progresses, the 
fusion process dynamics evolve, and chimney porosity may be alleviated 
under certain process settings.

4. Discussion 
This work has demonstrated that EBM AM is a suitable 
manufacturing route for processing pure tungsten. To the best of our 
knowledge, this is the first demonstration of AM fabricated tungsten 
exhibiting good high temperature tensile properties. This technological 
advancement is the first step towards alleviating a previously existing 
manufacturing bottleneck. The established process science may enable 
future fabrication of complex geometry tungsten components. Towards 
this end a few example complex geometries are shown in Fig. 16 . In both 
parts a hexagonal tile geometry can be found which is surrogate repre -
sentation of a tile which potentially could be used in the diverter or inner 
wall of a Tokamak fusion reactor. The tiles are mounted on additively 
manufactured rods, not made from tungsten, with internal passages for 
flowing cooling gases to the tiles. 
Analogous to what other works have previously reported, tungsten 
also exhibits a fiber texture switch with differing process settings. In this 
work surface or volumetric energy density was not predictive of this 
transition. Even linear energy density is not predictive; traversing the P /

Mechanical testing revealed that the material behavior is generally

higher temperatures are needed to recrystallize the material [16,50] . 
Besides this, the material in the present work was demonstrated to 
exhibit significant plastic anisotropy. Similar behavior has been recently 
observed in EBM AM pure molybdenum [12] . This behavior is funda -
mentally driven by the crystallographic anisotropy present in the ma -

High spatial and angular resolution orientation imaging demon -
strated that the as-fabricated material exhibits a sub-grain structure 
consisting of equiaxed low angle grain boundary cells. This has previ -
ously also been observed in molybdenum [34] and tantalum [31] . STEM 
and HREBSD analysis demonstrates that indeed the EBM AM fabricated 
material has a high dislocation density. This supports the argument that 
the subgrains form due to plastic deformation at elevated temperature. 
While there are some disparities between the intensity and size of sub -
grains across process settings, it is not immediately clear if there are any 
systematic trends. It could be hypothesized that process settings, which 
affect stresses and hence plastic deformation, influence the dynamic 
recrystallization process and hence the subgrains ’ physical characteris -
tics. Matters become complicated when considering the cooling effect 
previously mentioned. Parts printed last have a different “ effective ” 
preheat temperature and hence may exhibit a different subgrain struc -
ture. Additional work is needed to understand the influencing factors 
driving the formation of these subgrains and their potential impact on 
material properties. 
The anisotropic inelastic behavior exhibited by the material is a 
feature unique to EBM AM material which is driven by crystallographic 
anisotropy. In order to further develop a thorough understanding of the 
mechanical behavior, and eventually higher order properties such as 
creep and fatigue, both experimental and numerical work is needed. A 
fundamental understanding of the crystal scale plasticity behavior is 
needed to understand the aggregate continuum scale response. 
Advanced experiments, for instance in situ neutron diffraction, coupled 
with crystal plasticity models, would be well suited towards developing 
these key structure – property relationships.

5. Conclusions 
In this work the process-structure – property behavior of EBM AM 
fabricated pure tungsten was investigated. Through an exhaustive set of 
fabrication experiments a process window was identified within which 
highly dense, crack-free, material could be obtained. Analogous to ob -
servations made in other refractory systems the material obtained in this 
study exhibited a pronounced “ texture switch ” from ( 001 ) to ( 111 ) with 
decreasing speed/current. High spatial resolution EBSD microscopy 
revealed that the as-fabricated material contains ∼ 10 20 μ m scale 
subgrains. High angular resolution EBSD microscopy revealed that these 
low angle grain boundaries have a high dislocation density between 
∼ 10
15
 10
16
/, m
 2 
in some cases. The presence of a high dislocation 
density was confirmed via STEM. Mechanical testing reveals that the 
tensile strength exhibits properties closer to that of recrystallized 
tungsten whereas the elongation is closer to wrought tungsten. The 
material response is high anisotropic owing to the underlying crystal -
lographic anisotropy. The results demonstrate that EBM AM is a suitable 
manufacturing route for processing pure tungsten.
