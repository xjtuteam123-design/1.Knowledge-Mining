abstract
Direct hot forging of heavily-alloyed Ni-based superalloy castings usually ends up with
severe cracking due to serious macro-segregation. To address this issue, we propose to use
hot extrusion as the replacement technology to process this kind of superalloys. An
advanced cast and wrought Ni-based superalloy, GH4151, was thus subjected to hot
extrusion. It was found that the cast GH4151 ingot can be successfully processed by hot
extrusion without cracking. Extensive twinning happened and played a crucial role in
accommodating large and sudden macroscopic plastic deformation during hot extrusion.
Dynamic recrystallization happened through twinning and grain boundary bulging, leading
to signiﬁcant grain reﬁnement. Along the radial direction, the central region shows a
relatively larger grain size than the outer region, which is believed to be associated with the
less extent of recrystallization in the central region. Despite a certain microstructural
scatter, the hot-extruded GH4151 demonstrates superior tensile strengths and stress
rupture properties as compared with a number of the state-of-the-art disc superalloys.
With proper heat treatment, the microstructure was further reﬁned and the strengths were
further enhanced. The present study indicates that hot extrusion is capable of processing
heavily-alloyed Ni-based superalloys and producing desired microstructure that allows for
acquisition of excellent mechanical properties.

1. Introduction
The increased demand for higher inlet temperatures of the
aeroengine turbines and higher engine efﬁciency has driven
continued development of high-strength Ni-based
superalloys with heavy alloying [ 1]. This is accompanied by
increasingly reduced hot workability in cast and wrought
(C&W) Ni-based superalloys due to increasingly serious
segregation issue and increased amount of strengthening
phases in cast ingots [ 2e5]. When directly subjected to hot

from severe cracking [ 5]. The most common solution to this
issue nowadays is to resort to powder metallurgy (PM)
approach [6e10]. Powder particles of heavily-alloyed Ni-based
superalloys are produced and then consolidated by hot
isostatic pressing (HIP) to achieve good chemical homogeneity
and reﬁned microstructure, which is followed by either hot
extrusion or isothermal forging to break down defects such as
prior powder particle boundaries (PPBs) formed during HIP.
This route has been proven to be effective in producing high-
performance heavily-alloyed Ni-based superalloys. However,
the complex processing route and equally bad ﬂy-to-buy ratio
make the approach very expensive in manufacturing aero-
engine components. Recently, Pan et al. [ 11] proposed a
completely new processing route to conquer the cracking
issue of high-grade C &W Ni-based superalloys during forging.
They introduced hot extrusion as a transitional process be-
tween casting and forging of a new generation heavily-alloyed
Ni-based superalloy, GH4151, and successfully suppressed
cracking and produced desired microstructure and properties
during the ultimate hot forging process [ 11]. With the full
compressive stresses imposed on all radial directions of the
cast ingots, hot extrusion has effectively suppressed crack
development in the ingots and produced both chemically
homogenized and microstructurally reﬁned billet for subse-
quent forging [ 11].
Owing to the superior processing advantages of hot
extrusion over hot forging in processing advanced Ni-based
superalloys, here in this study, we aim to develop hot extru-
sion as a replacement thermomechanical processing tech-
nology for hot forging (instead of being only a transitional
process). Type GH4151 Ni-based superalloy ingots will be
directly and only subjected to hot extrusion and ﬁnal heat
treatment to see whether the microstructural evolution and
mechanical property development can meet industrial appli-
cation requirements. The microstructural development and
homogeneity throughout the hot-extruded billet will be thor-
oughly investigated. So will be the inﬂuence of heat treatment
on microstructure and mechanical properties. Moreover, it is
envisaged that cast ingots would also undergo extensive
plastic deformation and dynamic recrystallization (DRX)
during hot extrusion (just like hot forging [ 12,13]). The micro-
deformation mechanism and recrystallization behaviour of
cast Ni-based superalloys during rapid hot extrusion (happens
in 20 e30 s), however, remain unknown. Thus, in-depth
investigation will also be launched to study the DRX mecha-
nism during hot extrusion. Development of understanding on
this fundamental science would help tailor microstructure
and thus mechanical properties through process control. The
mechanical properties and deformation mechanism of the
hot-extruded GH4151 will also be examined.
2. Materials and methods
GH4151 superalloy whose chemical composition is summa-
rized in Table 1 is used in the present study. Cast ingots of
GH4151 were prepared by vacuum induction melting (VIM),
electro-slag reﬁning (ESR) and then vacuum arc remelting
(VAR) prior to a high-temperature homogenization annealing
treatment. A multi-step heating strategy which involves up-
heating to and dwelling at several temperatures between 900
and 1170
/C14 C has been adopted for the homogenization treat-
ment. A total dwell time at high temperatures reached 50 h.
The homogenized ingots were then subjected to hot forging
and hot extrusion, respectively. The hot extrusion was con-
ducted at 1120
/C14 C with a 260 MN extrusion force and an
extrusion ratio of 4:1. A two-pass hot extrusion was conducted
in order to promote plastic deformation and reﬁne grain
structure. After hot extrusion, a billet with a diameter of
200 mm and a length of 800 mm was obtained ( Fig. 1 a). Cy-
lindrical samples with a diameter of 13 mm and a length of
75 mm were sectioned from different regions of the billet (i.e.,
peripheral and core positions of bottom regions; see Fig. 1aec)
along the extrusion direction by electrical discharge
machining (EDM). Some of the samples were then subjected to
a standard heat treatment, e.g., solution treatment at 1135
/C14 C
for 4 h followed by air cooling and then annealing at 830 /C14 C for
6 h followed by air cooling and ﬁnally ageing at 760 /C14 C for 6 h
followed by air cooling.
Samples with a dimension of 10 /C2 10 /C2 2m m 3 were cut
from the homogenized ingot, the hot-extruded billet and the
heat-treated samples, respectively, which were then ground
using SiC papers from 200 grit up to 2500 grit before being
polished using 1.5 mm diamond suspension. The samples were
then chemically etched in a solution containing 50 g CuCl
2,
100 mL CH3OH and 100 mL HCl and electrolytically etched in a
solution which contains 150 mL H 3PO4, 100 mL H 2SO4 and 15 g
CrO3 for microstructural characterization. The samples were
examined using a Leica DM4000 M optical microscope (OM)
and a Zeiss Sigma 300 scanning electron microscope (SEM)
which was equipped with an Oxford electron backscattered
diffraction technique (EBSD) detector and an energy disper-
sive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) studies
were also performed on some of the hot extruded samples by
using an FEI Tecnai G2 F20 TEM microscope. TEM disc speci-
mens with a diameter of 3 mm and a thickness of 150 e200 mm
were machined from the hot extruded sample which were
then ground into 50 e60 mm in thickness. The specimens were
then electro-polished to perforation using a twin-jet electro-
polisher (RL-1). A polishing solution containing 5% perchloric
acid and 95% ethanol was used to prepare the TEM specimens.
Both hot-extruded samples and heat-treated samples were
machined into smooth cylindrical specimens with a gauge

3.2. Deformation and dynamic recrystallization
behaviour
To understand the deformation and DRX behavior during hot
extrusion, Kernel average misorientation (KAM) map and
deformation-recrystallization (Def-REX) maps of the samples
obtained from different regions of the hot-extruded GH4151
billet have been derived based on the EBSD data using Chan-
nel 5 software. The internal average misorientation angles
(IAMA) within each grain were measured and then used to
distinguish different types of grains. Grains with IAMA
exceeding 10
/C14 are deﬁned to be deformed. Grains with IAMA
between 1/C14 and 10/C14 are deﬁned as sub-structured while those
with IAMA less than 1 /C14 are classed as recrystallized [ 14]. The
related analysis results are shown in Fig. 10. It is clear that the
GB primary g0 particles tend to show higher KAM values as
compared with their surrounding regions. Since geometrically
necessary dislocation (GND) density is proportional to KAM
values [15,16], regions with higher KAM values mean that they
would contain a higher density of GND. According to the
recrystallization map, the majority of GB primary g
0 particles
are identiﬁed to be deformed g0. The majority of g grains are
demonstrated to be fully recrystallized while the rest are sub-
structured. The sample from the top central region seems to
contain a higher area fraction of sub-structured grains than
the other samples as shown in Fig. 10f. In general, the present
material has experienced a fully DRX throughout the whole
billet during hot extrusion. This is also underpinned by the
fact that the hot extruded samples all show signiﬁcantly
smaller grain sizes than the as-cast ingot ( Fig. 2).

as-cast ingots causes signiﬁcant tensile-shear stresses along
the radial directions of the ingots. This could easily lead to
bulging and cracking to the superalloy ingots that have
inherent poor hot workability. In contrast, the cast ingots
during hot extrusion would experience full compressive
stresses along all radial directions which suppress cracking
development and help close cracks if there is any crack
formed. With this unique processing characteristic, hot
extrusion can effectively overcome the alloying-formability
trade-off in C &W superalloys.

4.2. Microstructural evolution and dynamic
recrystallization mechanisms
The current experimental results also demonstrate that with
hot extrusion, the as-extruded billets show highly consistent
microstructures along the length direction. Actually, samples
obtained from the same radial positions along the longitudi-
nal direction show similar grain sizes and g
0 distribution and
sizes ( Figs. 5 and 7 ). This indicates that the current hot
extrusion has been highly consistent and stable in processing
different parts of the ingots along the length direction.
Nonetheless, there exists a gradient microstructure along the
radial direction in the hot-extruded billet, i.e., from the pe-
ripheral regions towards the core regions, the grain size, the
secondary g
0 precipitate size and the volume fraction of the GB
primary g0 continue to increase. This could be the combined
result of the microstructural inhomogeneity in the original as-
cast ingot and the varied extents of recrystallization in
different radial parts of the hot-extruded billet. The core re-
gion of the as-cast ingot shows much larger grains than the
peripheral region ( Fig. 2 ) but the extent of recrystallization in
the core region tends to be much less complete during hot
extrusion (Fig. 11). This inevitably resulted in larger grains and
fewer GB primary g
0 in the central region of the hot-extruded
billet. After hot extrusion, the peripheral regions may have
experienced higher cooling rates than the centre and thus
developed relatively ﬁner secondary g
0. The central regions
which may have undergone relatively lower cooling rates
tended to develop larger and more irregularly-shaped sec-
ondary g
0. The central regions of the hot-extruded billet were
found to demonstrate predominantly higher texture levels
than the peripheral regions, which, according to the EBSD IPF
colour maps in Fig. 8 , seems to be due to grain coarsening in
the central regions which tend to reduce the diversity of grain
orientations. The varied extents of recrystallization in
different regions may be due to varied stresses or strains
experienced along the radial direction during hot extrusion. It
is highly likely that the central regions may have experienced
less macroscopic plastic deformation and had lower driving
force for recrystallization. To better understand the origin for
the varied recrystallization levels and microstructural in-
homogeneity along the radial direction in the hot-extruded

It is noted that during hot extrusion, the GB primary g

High densities of twins were observed in the hot-extruded
samples ( Figs. 9 and 11 ). Given that macroscopically hot
extrusion happens almost instantaneously (ﬁnishes in
20e30s), it is reasonable to believe that twinning has been the
main micro-deformation mechanism involved in hot extru-
sion. Only when large-scale twinning was activated could
large and rapid macroscopic plastic deformation be accom-
modated at the microscopic scale. Dislocation slipping may
have also been involved in the deformation process but it
usually induces a much slower macroscopic plastic defor-
mation and a smaller plastic strain in a short timescale. Grain
boundary bulging was also found to have occurred and led to
formation of new g grains at GBs. Since local twin boundary
bulging and grain boundary bulging are usually the two forms
of discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) [ 19], their
presence indicates that the recrystallization during hot
extrusion happened mainly through the DDRX mechanism.
To be mentioned, the DDRX mode is highly distinct from the
continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) mode which is
characterized by progressive transformation of LAGBs to
HAGBs with accumulative strain and continuous absorption
of dislocations [ 20] and is often observed during forging of
C&W superalloys such as GH5188 and Inconel 625 [ 21,22].
Zhang et al. [ 23] concluded that CDRX occurs in a slow and
continuous manner but DDRX happens in a rapid and
discontinuous way. Given that hot extrusion happens highly
rapidly, the DDRX mode has obviously been preferred during
hot extrusion. In general, it is obvious that twinning has
played a signiﬁcant role in accommodating sudden intense
macroscopic plastic deformation and in DRX process during
hot extrusion.

sources for dislocation multiplication, leading to a steady
and progressive work hardening that is good for strength.

room-temperature tensile deformation have been extensively
reported in other superalloys [ 25e27]. To be mentioned, the
formation of stacking faults and L-C locks within the present
GH4151 sample during deformation should be associated with
its high content of Co ( >14 wt.%), which according to previous

observed in other Ni-based superalloys such as RR1000 [ 28].
The increased number of planar defects at high temperature is
believed to result from increased dislocation decomposition
reactions favored by thermal activation.
5. Conclusions
/C15 With hot extrusion, the heavily-alloyed GH4151 cast ingots
can be successfully processed without cracking.
/C15 During extrusion, the DRX mainly happened through
twinning and grain boundary bulging, leading to signiﬁcant
grain reﬁnement.
/C15 The central region of the hot-extruded alloy billet shows
relatively larger grain size and fewer primary g
0 than the
peripheral region, which is attributed to the less extent of
recrystallization in the central region. The central region
also contains much larger secondary g
0, which could be due
to relatively lower cooling rate in the centre after hot
extrusion.
/C15 The hot-extruded GH4151 shows superior tensile strengths
and stress rupture properties over many state-of-the-art
Ni-based disc superalloys. Proper heat treatment led to
further microstructural reﬁnement and to improvement in
strengths.
/C15 The hot-extruded GH4151 deformed mainly by dislocation
slipping through g channels and tertiary g
0 precipitates and
by formation of stacking faults in secondary g0 at both
room temperature and 650 /C14 C.
