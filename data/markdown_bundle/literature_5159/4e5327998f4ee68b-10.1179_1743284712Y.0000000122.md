Structural evolutions and mechanical
behaviours of carbides in Nb–Ti–C alloys
W. Q. Wei *1, H. W. Wang 2, C. M. Zou 2,J .Q .Q i 2 and Z. J. Wei 2
The structural evolution of the (Nb, Ti)C and its effect on the mechanical behaviours of Nb–(21,
35)Ti–4C and Nb–25Ti–8C (at-%) alloys have been comprehensively investigated. The alloys
consist of Nb solid solution (Nbss) and (Nb, Ti)C both for the as cast and the heat treated
samples; however, the secondary Nbss precipitates within the (Nb, Ti)C after heat treatment and
the composition of the secondary Nbss is mainly the Nb. The driving force of Nbss precipitation
from (Nb, Ti)C is summarised as two aspects: the lack of C atoms in (Nb, Ti)C induced by the fast
cooling rate; and the difference of thermodynamic stability of TiC and NbC. The small sized long
strip secondary Nbss, which adopts a well defined orientation relationship between the (Nb, Ti)C,
is obtained in the case of low driving force. On the condition of high driving force, the
microstructure of the Nbss precipitations becomes large sized massive and layered. The
brittleness of (Nb, Ti)C is reduced by the secondary Nbss precipitations proved by
nanoindentation tests.
Keywords: Nb–Ti–C alloy, Nbss precipitation, Mechanical behaviours
Introduction
Further improvement of the performance of the gas
turbines and missile technologies requires getting better
fuel efﬁciency in producing power or thrust, reducing
weight and increasing reliability.
1 Compared with the
conventional Ni based or Co based superalloys, Nb
based superalloys and their composites are considered to
be novel high temperature structural materials.
2–4 As the
nature of refractory metal, pure Nb has relatively low
density and high melting temperature (2468 uC), as well
as excellent ductility at room temperature. However, the
strength of Nb decreases substantially when the
temperature rises above 900 uC. To resolve this problem,
it is documented that the antiplastic deformation
behaviour of Nb solid solution (Nbss) alloys at high
temperature can be enhanced through solid solution
method.
5,6 In recent years, the studies on the micro-
structures and mechanical behaviours of Nb based
alloys, with Ti, Zr, Al and Si as primary doping
elements draw much attention. It is reported that the
Ti doped Nbss alloys have excellent mechanical proper-
ties at both low and high temperatures.
7,8 In addition,
Nbss with enhanced phases (such as carbides, 9–12
Nb3Al13,14 or Nb 5Si3
15) are also explored. For example,
the superalloys with homogeneous distribution of
reinforced carbide particles can offer outstanding
mechanical properties at high temperature.
9 For these
alloys, the mechanical properties are strongly dependent
on the structural evolution and the thermal stability of
enhanced phases while they work at high temperatures.
The previous study has investigated that the a-Ti
precipitations within TiC after heat treatment for Ti–
6Al–4V/TiC composites exhibit long strip morphology
with inner lamellar structure.
16 For the Nb–Ti–C and
Nb–Zr–C alloys, the Nbss-like precipitates within
carbides ((Nb, Zr)C or (Nb, Ti)C) have also been
observed by Jiao et al. ;
9,11,12,17 however, the mechanism
of structural evolution is not clear. A comprehensive
understanding for both the mechanism of structural
evolution and the thermal stability of the enhanced
phases will enable better design and fabrication of Nb
based superalloys. Nevertheless, few studies were
dedicated to this end and more deeply insights into this
ﬁeld were undoubtedly needed.
Nb–Ti–C alloys, which have carbide as the enhanced
phases, have potential for application at working
temperatures .1100uC.
18 Consequently, in this paper,
structural evolution of the carbide and its effect on the
mechanical behaviours of Nb–Ti–C alloys before and
after heat treatment processes are studied, favouring
the exploration of novel Nb superalloys with pro-
mising mechanical properties at both low and high
temperatures.

Results and discussion
Phase identification
Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns obtained from the as
cast and the heat treated samples. For the as cast
samples (Fig. 1 a), some sharp crystalline diffraction
peaks for Nb–(21, 35)Ti–4C and Nb–25Ti–8C alloys
were detected. These peaks can be indexed to be a body
centred cubic (bcc) structured Nbss phase (PDF: 35-
0789) and a face centred cubic (fcc) structured (Nb, Ti)C
phase (PDF: 47-1418). For the heat treated samples
(Fig. 1 b), the diffraction patterns of the alloys are the
same as those of the as cast samples, revealing the
existence of identical phases before and after heat
treatments.
It should be noted that the ratios of diffraction
intensities between the (Nb, Ti)C phase and the Nbss
matrix relatively decrease after heat treatment, suggest-
ing that the fraction of the (Nb, Ti)C carbide becomes
smaller for the heat treated samples. The lattice
parameters of (Nb, Ti)C calculated from the XRD data
are listed in Table 2. As can be seen, these parameters
are smaller than that of the (Nb, Ti)C phase (4 ?415 A˚)
and mostly larger than that of the TiC phase (4 ?327 A˚).
On the other hand, there exists a trend that the lattice
parameter of heat treated sample becomes even smaller
than that of as cast sample. To understand these, it is
documented that the (Nb, Ti)C phase is an isomorphous
mixture of the pure NbC and pure TiC, indicating that
the lattice parameter of the (Nb, Ti)C phase depends on
the atomic ratio between the Nb and Ti elements in the
carbide.
19,20 Therefore, the decrease in lattice parameter
for the (Nb, Ti)C phase suggests a relative decrease in
Nb atoms in the (Nb, Ti)C carbide after heat treatment.

Discussion on structural evolution
The secondary Nbss precipitations within (Nb, Ti)C
carbide during heat treatment can be summarized as the
splitting process of Nb and Ti atoms from carbide.
Through the structural evolution of the secondary Nbss
within the Nb– (21, 35)Ti–4C and Nb–25Ti–8C alloys, it
can be found that there is a relationship between the
sizes and shapes of the precipitations and the composi-
tions of the alloys. For example, the large sized massive
and layered Nbss precipitations are obtained in Nb–
21Ti–4C and Nb–25Ti–8C alloys, which have relatively
higher fraction of the Nb atoms within the carbide. On
the contrary, for the Nb–35Ti–4C alloy with a lower
fraction of Nb ( see Table 3) within the carbide, the small
sized Nbss precipitations exhibit long strip morphology
and have a well deﬁned OR with the (Nb, Ti)C carbide.
In order to analyse the driving force of the structural
evolution, it is noted that the composition of secondary
Nbss is mainly Nb atoms. Taking the transformation of
NbC during heat treatment as an example and according
to the Nb–C binary phase diagram, one can see that the
eutectic transition temperature of Nbss and NbC is
2338uC and the atomic percentage of C within the NbC
phase is y28?8%. Furthermore, the solubility of C
within NbC increases as the decreasing of solidiﬁcation
temperature, e.g. the atomic percentage of C within NbC
is y35% at 1100uC. This deviation of C solubility can be
canceled by two aspects: the C atoms dissolving into the

the secondary Nbss precipitations can be caused by two
reasons: the lack of C atoms due to the fast cooling rate
(non-equilibrium solidiﬁcation); and the different ther-
modynamic stability of TiC and NbC. The structural
evolution of the secondary Nbss is determined by the
synergetic effect of the above two aspects. As a result of
the lowest Nb fraction within the carbides in these three
alloys ( see Table 3, i.e. the lowest driving force), the
small sized long strip Nbss forms for the Nb–35Ti–4C
alloy during heat treatment. The forming process can be
understood by the following: the secondary Nbss

Effects of Nbss precipitation on mechanical
behaviour of (Nb, Ti)C
In order to study the effect of the secondary Nbss
precipitation within the (Nb, Ti)C on the mechanical
behaviours of the (Nb, Ti)C, nanoindentation tests were
carried out. Figure 7 a shows the BSE–SEM images of
the Berkovich imprint of the interface between the Nbss
and the (Nb, Ti)C for the as cast Nb–21Ti–4C alloy. It is
clearly to see that a slight pile-up is obtained at the edge
of the Nbss matrix; however, it is not visible at the (Nb,
Ti)C side. Furthermore, there are some obvious cracks
generating on the carbide surface (inset). The interface is
clear and no cracks form around it after deformation,
suggesting a strong interfacial bonding. Figure 7 b is the
BSE–SEM image of the interface imprint of Nb–21Ti–
4C alloy after heat treatment at 1100 uCf o r7 2 h ,
together with a highlighting SE image of the imprint
(inset), in which the pile-up can be observed around the
imprint. In contrast with Fig. 7 a, no evident cracks are
noted on the surface of the carbide. These results suggest
that the brittleness of (Nb, Ti)C can be reduced by Nbss
precipitating after heat treatment.

Conclusions
Based on the microstructural analysis, the OR analysis
of Nbss precipitations and (Nb, Ti)C carbide, and the
nanomechanical tests, the following conclusions can be
drawn.
1. The alloys consist of Nbss and (Nb, Ti)C both for
the as cast and the heat treated samples. Two types of
(Nb, Ti)C carbide are obtained in the as cast alloys, i.e.
the large sized primary carbide in Nb–25Ti–8C alloy and
the small sized eutectic carbide in each alloys. The (Nb,
Ti)C precipitates within the Nbss, and the secondary
Nbss precipitates within the (Nb, Ti)C after heat
treatment. The composition of the secondary Nbss is
mainly the Nb atoms.
2. The morphology of the secondary Nbss is deter-
mined by the driving force of precipitation during heat
treatment, which includes two aspects: the splitting of
metal atoms from carbides, namely the lack of C atoms
induced by the fast cooling rate; and the thermodynamic
stability of TiC and NbC. The morphology of the
secondary Nbss is small sized long strip in the case of
low driving force, and the precipitations adopt a well
defined OR between the (Nb, Ti)C and the Nbss. On the
condition of high driving force, the microstructure of the
Nbss precipitations becomes large sized massive and
layered.
3. The secondary Nbss precipitations within (Nb,
Ti)C can effectively reduce the brittleness of the carbides
of Nb–Ti–C alloy. It is evidenced that (Nb, Ti)C is an
effective reinforcement for Nb based alloys, which is
proved by nanoindentation tests.
